The **Epic of Gilgamesh** is the great epic of [[mesopotamian-religion|Mesopotamia]] — the oldest surviving
great work of literature — a meditation on **friendship, mortality, and the futile human quest to escape
death**, built around the legendary king **Gilgamesh** of Uruk. It also contains the **Mesopotamian Flood**
story, a famous parallel to [[genesis|Genesis]] (see [[genesis-ancient-near-east|the ANE comparison]]).

## The Story (in detail)

**Gilgamesh and Enkidu.** Two-thirds god and one-third man, **[[gilgamesh|Gilgamesh]]** the king of Uruk is a
magnificent but oppressive tyrant. To check him, the gods create his equal, the wild man **Enkidu**, raised
among the animals. After a titanic wrestling match the two become **inseparable friends**, and together they
adventure — slaying the forest-guardian monster **Humbaba** and the **Bull of Heaven** (sent by [[ishtar|
Ishtar]] when Gilgamesh spurns her advances).

**Death and the quest.** For these offenses the gods condemn **Enkidu to die**. Devastated by his friend's
death and terrified by his own mortality, Gilgamesh abandons his kingdom and **wanders the wilderness seeking
the secret of eternal life**. He journeys to the ends of the earth to find **Utnapishtim**, the one man made
immortal by the gods.

**The Flood.** Utnapishtim tells Gilgamesh his story: long ago the gods resolved to **destroy humanity with a
great flood** (because their noise disturbed [[enlil|Enlil]]); but the god **[[enki|Ea]]** warned Utnapishtim,
who **built a great boat**, loaded it with his family, craftsmen, and animals, and survived the deluge. When
the waters receded he **released birds** to find land and offered **sacrifice**, and the gods (regretting the
flood) granted him **immortality**. The parallels with Noah are striking.

**The failure.** Utnapishtim sets Gilgamesh a test — to stay awake seven days — which he fails at once,
proving he cannot conquer even sleep, let alone death. He is told of a **plant of rejuvenation** at the
bottom of the sea; he retrieves it, but a **serpent steals it** while he bathes (and sheds its skin, renewed).
Gilgamesh returns to Uruk **empty-handed but wiser** — finding his immortality only in the **enduring walls of
his city** and his name. The epic's lesson: **death is the human lot; meaning lies in deeds, friendship, and
what one leaves behind.**
