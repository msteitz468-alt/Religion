# Yijing (Scripture of Change)

The **Yijing** (易經, "Scripture of Change") — also romanized as *I Ching* and known in its
pre-canonical form as the **Zhouyi** (周易, "Zhou Changes") — is one of the oldest and most
influential texts in East Asian religious and intellectual history. It is the first of the **Five
Classics** (*Wujing*) of [[confucianism|Confucianism]] and one of the most-commented texts in world
literature. Roger Ames: "Perhaps no single text can compete with [it] in terms of the sustained
interest it has garnered from succeeding generations of China's literati, and the influence it has
had on Chinese self-understanding."

The text's graphic core — 64 six-line figures (hexagrams) composed of solid (yang) and broken (yin)
lines — dates to the early Zhou dynasty (c. 11th–9th c. BCE) and was originally a **divination
manual** for Bronze Age statecraft. Over the following centuries it accumulated philosophical and
ethical appendixes (the "Ten Wings") and became the master text of Chinese cosmology, the supreme
resource for [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucian]] metaphysics, and the source of the most influential
single-text commentarial tradition in Chinese history. See [[adler-original-meaning-yijing|Adler
2020]] for the full English translation of [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] *Zhouyi benyi*.

## Canon and Reception

The Yijing occupies a distinctive position in the Chinese textual tradition: it is simultaneously
a **divinatory oracle**, a **cosmological treatise**, and a **moral handbook**, and it has been
claimed by Confucians, Daoists, and Chinese Buddhists alike.

**In Confucianism**: The Zhouyi was among the Five Classics (*Wujing*) from the earliest Confucian
school. [[confucius|Confucius]] was traditionally said to have worn out the leather bindings of his
copy by reading it so often, and was credited with composing the philosophical appendixes (the Ten
Wings). Modern scholarship rejects Confucian authorship of the Ten Wings, dating them to the 3rd–2nd
c. BCE, but the attribution was central to the text's canonical authority. Under **Han Wudi** (r.
140–87 BCE), the Yijing was the first of the Five Classics in the Imperial Academy curriculum.
[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] synthesis of the two interpretive schools (xiangshu/yili) in the *Zhouyi benyi*
(1188) became the standard reading for 700+ years.

**In Daoism**: The Yijing's cosmology — yin-yang, the Supreme Polarity (*taiji*), the ten thousand
things arising from the dao — was readily assimilated into Daoist metaphysics. Wang Bi's 3rd-c.
commentary read the Yi through a distinctly Daoist lens (see [[daodejing|Daodejing]]).

**In Buddhism**: Although the Yijing is not canonical for any Buddhist school, it was absorbed into
Chinese syncretic thought. Chan/Zen literature and Chinese Buddhist cosmological speculation draw on
its vocabulary.

## Structure

### I. The Zhouyi (the Basic Text, pre-canonical)

The graphic and textual core of the received Yijing:

**A. The 64 Hexagrams**

Each hexagram (*gua* 卦) is a six-line figure in which each line is either solid/unbroken (yang; ——)
or broken (yin; – –). The 64 hexagrams represent all possible configurations of yin and yang in six
positions — "all possible configurations of change in nature and in human life" (Adler). They are
traditionally arranged in 32 complementary pairs following either the **Fuxi sequence** (a priori,
based on binary generation) or the **King Wen sequence** (the received canonical order used in
divination).

Each hexagram is composed of two **trigrams** (*bagua*, Eight Trigrams), upper and outer / lower and
inner. The Eight Trigrams each have a name, an image (Heaven, Earth, Thunder, Wind, Water, Fire,
Mountain, Lake), and multiple symbolic correlates catalogued in the Shuogua appendix. The trigrams
were attributed to the mythic sage-king **Fuxi** as the primordial symbols of yin-yang change.

**B. Textual Layers**

Three strata of written text were added to the hexagram diagrams:

1. **Hexagram names** (*guaming*) — attributed to King Wen (mid-11th c. BCE); some appear
   functional tags, some more evocative.
2. **Hexagram statements** (*guaci*, "Judgments") — terse oracular pronouncements for each hexagram;
   attributed to King Wen. They include four key recurring terms: *yuan* (originating/supreme),
   *heng* (penetrating/success), *li* (appropriate/advantageous), *zhen* (correct/to divine).
3. **Line statements** (*yaoci*) — one oracular statement per line (6 per hexagram = 384 total);
   attributed to the Duke of Zhou (King Wen's son).

These three layers together constitute the Zhouyi in its earliest documentary form, probably
reaching written form in the early–mid Zhou period (9th–8th c. BCE).

### II. The Ten Wings (the Appendixes, late Zhou–Han)

Ten philosophical appendixes traditionally attributed to Confucius but actually composed anonymously
c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE. They "Confucianized" the text, overlaying the divinatory function with a
moralized, ethical-cosmological reading. Major components:

1–2. **Tuanzhuan** (Commentary on the Judgments) — philosophical commentary on each hexagram
   statement; interprets the hexagrams in terms of yin-yang structure and Confucian virtue.

3–4. **Xiangzhuan** (Commentary on the Images) — divided into the **Larger Image** (commentary on
   the two trigrams of each hexagram) and the **Smaller Image** (comments on individual lines). The
   Larger Image is the "most Confucian" part (Rutt): it draws a behavioral lesson for the *junzi*
   from the imagery of the combined trigrams.

5. **Wenyan** (Commentary on the Words of the Text) — commentary on the first two hexagrams only
   (Qian and Kun); connects their virtues to the Confucian cardinal virtues.

6–7. **Xici zhuan** (Treatise on the Appended Remarks; also the **Great Treatise**, Dazhuan) —
   the most philosophically rich appendix. It does not comment on specific hexagrams but provides a
   general theoretical account of the Yi's cosmological mechanism, the relationship between yin-yang
   and the moral order, the role of divination, and key statements such as: "The alternation of yin
   and yang is called the Way" (A.5.1) and "Generation and regeneration are the meaning of change"
   (A.5.6). It was the primary source of Neo-Confucian metaphysical vocabulary (esp. the
   [[taiji|Supreme Polarity]]).

8. **Shuogua** (Discussion of the Trigrams) — systematic catalogue of the trigrams' symbolic
   correlates (Heaven, ruler, jade, round; Earth, mother, cloth, square; Thunder, eldest son, dragon,
   black; etc.); its first three sections resemble the Xici and may have been part of it originally.

9. **Xugua** (Sequence of Hexagrams) — brief rationales for the canonical ordering of hexagrams;
   the only appendix Zhu Xi declines to comment on.

10. **Zagua** (Assorted Hexagrams) — very brief paired comparisons of hexagrams in a non-canonical
    order; Zhu Xi comments on some.

**The Shiyi (*Divination Ritual*)**: Zhu Xi appended detailed instructions for the milfoil-stalk
divination ritual to his *Zhouyi benyi*; it is based on the method described in Xici A.9.

## The Bronze Age Context (vs. the Confucian Reading)

Modern scholarship (Waley 1933; Kunst, Shaughnessy, Rutt, Redmond) has recovered the original Bronze
Age meaning of the Zhouyi, prior to the Ten Wings' Confucianization. Key findings:

- The text was composed for **royal divination** in the early Zhou court — deciding the advisability
  of military expeditions, royal marriages, feudal alliances, and sacrifices.
- Many terms that post-Han readers took as moral concepts were originally ritual: *heng* originally
  meant the "receipt" of a sacrificial offering by an ancestor spirit; *zhen* meant "to divine."
- The word *fu* (孚), which Han interpreters read as "honesty/sincerity," originally meant
  "captive/prisoner of war."
- The "superior person" (*junzi*) appears only 15 times in the Zhouyi but 87 times in the Ten Wings
  — showing how thoroughly the Ten Wings moralized the original text.

## Interpretive Schools

### The Two Schools: Xiangshu vs. Yili

From the Han dynasty, Yijing interpretation divided into two major schools:

- **Xiangshu** (象數, "Image and Number"): focuses on the graphic elements — the yin-yang
  relationships of the lines, their numerological values, the trigrams' symbolic correlates, and
  cosmological diagrams (the River Chart / Hetu, the Luo Writing / Luoshu, the Fuxi sequences).
  Major Han practitioners: Jiao Yanshou, Meng Xi, Jing Fang, Yu Fan. Song-dynasty representative:
  **Shao Yong** (1012–1077).
- **Yili** (義理, "Meaning and Principle"): focuses on the written text to derive moral principles
  for the *junzi*; minimizes the graphic/numerological elements. Major exponent: **Wang Bi**
  (226–249), whose commentary became the "orthodox" Tang-dynasty version. Song representative:
  **Cheng Yi** (1033–1107).

### Zhu Xi's Synthesis: "The Yi Was Originally Created for Divination"

[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] (1130–1200) broke from both Wang Bi and Cheng Yi by insisting that "the Yi was
originally created for divination" (*Yi ben wei bushi er zuo*) — a claim he derives from Fuxi's
original intention and finds stated in the Xici ("in divining we honor its prognostications"). This
hermeneutical principle has two immediate implications:

1. The **images** (yin-yang line structure, trigram symbolism) must receive equal attention with the
   text — against Wang Bi's and Cheng Yi's textualism.
2. The Yi was created **for everyone** (farmers, merchants, artisans as well as literate *junzi*),
   not only the educated. Moral guidance is accessed *through* divination, not by reading the text
   independently of the oracle.

Zhu Xi synthesized the xiangshu approach (from Shao Yong) with the yili approach (from Cheng Yi):
his commentary begins by explaining the yin-yang structural relationships of each hexagram and then
draws the moral implications, always grounding the moral in the cosmological.

His "original meaning" (*benyi*) is, as Adler notes, quite different from the modern historical-
critical "original meaning" (Bronze Age divination for statecraft). Zhu Xi's "original" means the
original *moral and cosmological* intention of the Sages — not a Bronze Age archaeological fact.

## Key Hexagrams

- **Qian** (Heaven, hexagram 1): pure yang; the originating principle; the Way of creative
  generativity. Its four virtues (*yuan/heng/li/zhen*) are the cosmological-moral root.
- **Kun** (Earth, hexagram 2): pure yin; the completing principle; the Way of responsive simplicity.
- **Fu** (Return, hexagram 24): one yang returning at the base after total yin — the moment of
  "incipient creation," the turning of the cosmic cycle. Zhu Xi's most theologically charged
  hexagram: "In Fu is seen the mind of Heaven and Earth." The analogue: the moment between stillness
  and activity, the origin of moral creativity in the human mind.
- **Xici A.5.1–9**: The doctrinal core: "The alternation of yin and yang is called the Way.
  Carrying it out is good. Completing it is the nature. ... Generation and regeneration are the
  meaning of change."

## The Xici's Cosmological Framework

The Xici's key cosmological scheme (central to Neo-Confucian thought):

- **Supreme Polarity** (*taiji*) generates the **Two Modes** (yin and yang);
  the Two Modes generate the **Four Images**; the Four Images generate the **Eight Trigrams**.
- Yin and yang are not substances but *modes* of *qi*'s constant fluctuation and transformation.
- The cosmos is ordered by *li* (principle); *qi* is ordered by *li*; human nature (*xing*) =
  *li* as received by persons — hence "human nature is good" (Mencius / Zhu Xi).
- The phrase *sheng sheng* ("generation and regeneration") = the incessant creativity of Heaven and
  Earth, which is also the ground of human moral creativity (*ren*, humaneness). This is the Yijing's
  contribution to Confucian moral cosmology.

## Commentary Tradition

The Yijing has the largest commentarial tradition of any Chinese text:

| Commentator | Dates | School | Major Work | Approach |
|-------------|-------|--------|------------|----------|
| [[wang-bi\|Wang Bi]] | 226–249 | Wei-dynasty; yili | *Zhouyi zhu* | Daoist-inflected; "forget the image, get the idea"; texts over diagrams |
| Cheng Yi | 1033–1107 | Song Neo-Confucian; yili | *Yizhuan* | Moral principle; the text as guide to righteous action |
| Shao Yong | 1012–1077 | Song; xiangshu | *Huangjijingshishu* | Numerological; Fuxi's a priori sequence; the "Supreme Number" system |
| [[zhu-xi\|Zhu Xi]] | 1130–1200 | Cheng-Zhu; synthesis | *Zhouyi benyi* (1188) | Divination as hermeneutical key; synthesis of xiangshu and yili |

The Qing-dynasty compilation *Zhouyi zhezhong* (1716, Kangxi reign) paired Cheng Yi's and Zhu Xi's
commentaries as jointly normative; it remained the standard edition through the imperial period.

## Textual Notes

- The Mawangdui silk manuscript (discovered 1973) provides an alternative pre-Han version of the
  Yijing text with significant differences — including the *Yizhi yi* text that combines the Shuogua
  and Xici materials in a different arrangement.
- Zhu Xi restored the **original structure** of the text (appendixes separate from hexagrams), but
  most editions follow the **collated arrangement** (introduced by Wang Bi) in which the Tuanzhuan
  and Xiangzhuan are printed under each hexagram for ease of reading.
- In Adler's translation (Columbia 2020) the collated arrangement is used; Adler apologizes to Zhu
  Xi but defends the decision on grounds of readability.
