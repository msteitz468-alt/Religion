Job is the first Wisdom literature text in the wiki and the most theologically radical book of the Hebrew Bible. It addresses suffering, theodicy, and the nature of disinterested worship through the frame of a non-Israelite sage subjected to extreme, undeserved affliction. The characters — Job and his three companions — are from Uz (probably Edomite territory), Teman, Shuah, and Naamah. They use the divine names *El, Eloah,* and *Shaddai* almost exclusively; the covenant name YHWH appears primarily in the narrative frame and the Yahweh speeches.

This is a Wisdom text, not a narrative text about Israel's history. It operates in a different theological register from the Former Prophets: no exodus, no covenant, no redemptive history. Its question is universal — can a person maintain genuine, disinterested fear of God when stripped of every earthly blessing? — and its primary interlocutor is the whole human race.

## Place in Canon

**Protestant/Hebrew Bible**: Job appears in the third division, the Writings (*Kětûvîm*). Ashkenazic manuscripts order it Psalms-Proverbs-Job; Sephardic manuscripts order it Psalms-Job-Proverbs. Protestant Bibles follow the Vulgate order placing it at the head of the poetical books (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Canticles).

The canonicity of Job was never seriously questioned — it passed into the canon without controversy, unusual for a book that makes such radical theological moves.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

**The Hebrew text.** [[john-hartley|Hartley]] describes Job's Hebrew as "one of the most obscure in the OT," attributing the difficulty to "many rare words and textual disturbances," and observes that "the ancient versions testify to the fact that many passages were unintelligible even to the earliest translators" (pp. 3–4). His working policy is MT-default: he judges that "the versions offer numerous variant readings, but only a few are useful for the restoration of the original reading," and he resists emendation undertaken to satisfy metrical or strophic theories — "Those who arrange the speeches in strophes are often forced to emend the text quite freely and in so doing must omit many verses as secondary. Such wholesale reordering of the text does not seem warranted" (p. 35).

**Septuagint.** The oldest Greek text is shorter than the MT by roughly 400 lines. Hartley reproduces Driver-Gray's tabulation of the omissions by section — prologue 1; chs. 7–14, 23–29; chs. 15–21, 59; chs. 22–31, 124; chs. 32–37, 114; 38:1–42:6, 43; epilogue 3 — and reads that distribution as decisive: because "the number of omissions increases significantly in the trite third cycle and in the wordy Elihu speeches, and because many of the lines omitted are recurring lines and thoughts, a more likely explanation is that the Greek translator abridged the speeches intentionally" (p. 4). The rival explanation he reports and rejects is that "the book of Job had been translated into Greek before its development was complete" — i.e. a shorter Hebrew *Vorlage*. On the translator's character Hartley cites Orlinsky, who "has demonstrated that its translator strove to render the Hebrew into Greek as accurately as possible" and who rejects the theological-bias explanation of the variants; Hartley qualifies Orlinsky, holding that the translator "on occasion… slanted the translation toward God's exalted, unassailable perfection."

**11QtgJob.** The oldest known Targum of Job comes from Qumran Cave XI. Its date is disputed: J. van der Ploeg and A. van der Woude date it on paleographic grounds to the 1st century A.D., while Pope and Sokoloff hold that "the style of the language is closer to the 2nd century B.C." (p. 5) — Hartley reports the dispute without adjudicating it. The text runs from the middle of ch. 17 to 42:11 and is fragmentary, better preserved from ch. 32 on, with extensive passages at 37:10–42:11; whether it originally ended at 42:11 is unclear. Its chief variants are at 42:3, where it reads 40:5 in place of the MT text, and at 37:16–18, where the line order is 16a, 17a, 16b, 18 with 17b omitted; Hartley also notes a demythologizing tendency, as at 38:7, rendered "When the morning stars shone [MT sang] together and all the angels [MT sons] of God shouted in unison."

Text-critically the most consequential fact for this book is that, on Hartley's report, "the Qumran Targums supports the M.T. including the order of chs. 24–27" (p. 5). That is a hard constraint on the elaborate transpositions proposed for the third cycle — see [[job-third-cycle-integrity|Controversy: the integrity of the third dialogue cycle]].

**Other versions.** The rabbinic Targum "follows the MT closely but has some of its own idiosyncrasies," among them readings that interpret verses in the light of Israel's history and added theological explanations (1:6, 15; 20:26–28). The Syriac Peshitta was translated directly from the Hebrew; Hartley judges that it "offers insight into some obscure words and difficult passages" and that its text-critical value "will increase" as the MT tradition is better understood. Jerome translated Job into Latin from the Greek at the end of the 4th century, then redid the work from the Hebrew, engaging a rabbi of Lydda "who opened the rabbinic tradition to him"; Hartley therefore allows the Vulgate some value for recovering the Hebrew.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

Job is not read within a single confessional interpretive system in this wiki's sources; the frameworks in play are (a) the book's own internal hermeneutic — the friends' deductive application of inherited doctrine to a particular case, which the book stages in order to break — and (b) the modern commentator's method.

[[john-hartley|Hartley's]] method is **message-focused rather than linguistic**: he states that the commentary's purpose is "to concentrate on interpreting the book's message rather than to write a linguistic commentary." Its most visible apparatus is the **"Aim" section** appended to the end of each speech, in which Hartley states what that speech contributes to the argument of the whole — the device that carries most of his synthetic reading. He works with form-critical categories but subordinates them to literary setting, laying down an explicit rule: "the author of Job has used these genres freely to his own purpose. Thus the setting in the book is far more important in determining the function of each genre than its setting in life" (pp. 42–43), with 15:20–35 as his worked example — a description of the fate of the wicked that normally teaches moral order but here functions as a warning to Job.

On the internal hermeneutic, Hartley reads the book as licensing correction of received doctrine: "the traditions of the fathers, though worthy of great respect, must not be accepted uncritically… The book of Job thus posits that inherited dogma can be corrected through new insights that God leads a person, even one younger than the elders, to discover" (Excursus on 12:7–25, pp. 216–217).

## Literary Structure

Job is one of the most formally complex books in the OT. [[john-hartley|Hartley]] defends single authorship and treats all major units as integral to the final work.

### Prose-Poetry Frame

The book's outer shell is a prose epic (chs. 1-2; 42:7-17) enclosing a long poetic dialogue (chs. 3-42:6). This combination is unusual and intentional. Hartley argues the author took an ancient "Epic of Job" (real oral tradition with patriarchal-era features) and reworked it as the frame for the dialogue. Evidence for an ancient substratum: wealth counted in animals and servants (1:3; 42:12); the family head offers sacrifice (1:5); Sabeans and Chaldeans as wandering tribes (1:15, 17); the *qəśîṭâ* as a currency unit (42:11; cf. Gen 33:19); the symmetrical 2-4 structures typical of ancient epic.

The resulting tension between the *patient* Job of the prose (who endures silently, 1:21-22; 2:10) and the *defiant* Job of the dialogue (who accuses God and demands litigation) is an *intended* literary tension, not a contradiction between sources.

### Formal Structure

```
I.   Prologue (1:1-2:13)
       A-A' — Introduction of Job / Introduction of three friends
       B-B' — First and second heavenly scenes (divine council)
       C-C' — Job's misfortune / Job's physical affliction

II.  Job's Curse-Lament (3:1-26)
       Job curses the day of his birth; opening move of legal complaint

III. The Dialogue (4:1-27:23)
       Three cycles of speeches: Eliphaz → Job, Bildad → Job, Zophar → Job
       [Third cycle disrupted in transmission; see below]

IV.  Hymn to Wisdom (28:1-28)
       An interlude judging the comforters' wisdom as bankrupt

V.   Job's Avowal of Innocence (29:1-31:40)
       A formal legal summons compelling God to respond

VI.  Elihu Speeches (32:1-37:24)
       Four speeches; prepares Job for the theophany

VII. Yahweh Speeches (38:1-42:6)
       Two speeches from the whirlwind; Job's two responses

VIII. Epilogue (42:7-17)
       Job vindicated; friends rebuked; restoration
```

### The Third Cycle Problem

Bildad's third speech (25:1–6) is abnormally short and lacks both introduction and conclusion; there is no third speech from Zophar; and 24:18–24 is nearly unintelligible and argues against Job's own complaint in 24:1–17. Hartley makes the minimum possible change: he joins 27:13–23 to 25:1–6 as Bildad's third speech and holds that Zophar never gave one, the author having lengthened Zophar's second speech "as a signal that Zophar would not speak again." He rejects the larger reconstructions of Gordis and of Hölscher/Duhm as still leaving "very rough transitions," and states the governing principle: "To regain a part of the third cycle and to avoid a highly speculative reconstruction that, if incorrect, would only be further from the original production, only a minor change is made in the commentary" (p. 26). Full positions, including the 11QtgJob constraint, at [[job-third-cycle-integrity|Controversy: the integrity of the third dialogue cycle]].

### The Hymn to Wisdom (ch. 28)

The Hymn to Wisdom does not connect smoothly to the preceding speeches, but Hartley argues it is integral and rightly placed. Crucially, he does not read it as any speaker's utterance: "Since its abstract, reflective tone does not match well any of the speakers, it is taken to be a piece that stands outside the dialogue. It functions as a bridge between the dialogue and the groups of speeches that are coming" (p. 373). He rejects the relocations proposed by Buttenweiser (before the Yahweh speeches) and Szczygiel (after Job's final response), judging that "their failure serves to point out that this hymn is presently in the best place possible within the book of Job," and he rejects Gordis's suggestion that a scribe inserted an early piece of the author's here. Against the objection that the hymn preempts the Yahweh speeches, Hartley argues the reverse: the anticipations "are intended to prepare Job and the audience to hear Yahweh's words."

In Hartley's Aim for the chapter, the hymn "marks the end of the dialogue" and delivers the verdict on the comforters — "It judges the efforts of the comforters to teach Job wisdom as a failure" — while authenticating "Job's turning away from his comforters to petition God directly" and indicating in advance the *method* Yahweh will take. He ties it by inclusio to the prologue and the ending: "In this manner the hymn links the opening characterization of Job as 'one who feared God and shunned evil' (1:1, 8; 2:3) with his submissive response to Yahweh's word in the end (42:1–6)" (p. 384). See [[wisdom-literature|Wisdom Literature]] for the chapter's account of wisdom itself.

### The Elihu Speeches (chs. 32-37)

Hartley reports that "the majority consider them to come from a later hand," on grounds of Elihu's absence from prologue and dialogue, stylistic divergence, divine-name and pronoun preferences, and more Aramaisms; Budde, Snaith, and Gordis are the defenders of authenticity he names. He argues against interpolation chiefly from the interpolator's own incoherence — an interpolator "could have adjusted those places to accommodate his inclusion of Elihu" — and from the author's habit of dropping characters (the Satan, Job's wife). He nonetheless leaves the authorship question open: "Whether they are the work of the author of Job or of a final redactor is difficult to ascertain." His functional verdict is unambiguous: "Clearly, then, the Elihu speeches are an integral part of the final edition of the work." Positions and evidence at [[elihu-speeches-authenticity|Controversy: the authenticity of the Elihu speeches]].

Elihu's key contribution: **disciplinary suffering** — God uses suffering to turn a person from potential or actual error (33:12-22; 36:16-21), not as punishment for past sin. He also introduces the concept of a **mediating angel** who proclaims "a ransom has been found" and delivers the sufferer from Sheol (33:23-28).

### The Yahweh Speeches (38-42:6)

Two speeches from the whirlwind, each with a parallel structure:

| Element | Speech 1 | Speech 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Prosaic introduction | 38:1 | 40:6 |
| Exhortation to Job | 38:2-3 | 40:7 |
| Interrogation of Job | 38:4-39:30 | 40:15-41:26 |
| Challenge to Job | 40:2 | 40:8-14 |

The challenge to Job forms a chiasm across the two speeches (40:2 / 40:8-14), intensifying the rhetorical pressure. The first speech counters Job's claim that God fails to administer the world justly; the second challenges Job to demonstrate superior power and justice — if he cannot, his case is forfeit.

The two-speech shape is disputed. **Fohrer** treats Behemoth (40:15–24) and Leviathan (40:25–41:26) as secondary additions, relocates 40:8–14 to the close of the first speech, and reconstructs a single answer from Job out of 40:3–5; 42:2, 3ab, 5, 6. **Westermann** argues on form-critical grounds that "a theophany, unique by its very nature, can consist of only one speech," classes the genre as a hymn of praise transformed into disputation, and takes 40:7–14 as heading a second *division* rather than a second speech, with 40:15–24 and 41:4–26 later additions "due to their purely descriptive nature." Hartley defends two integral speeches on five grounds: the book's twos/threes/fours architecture ("The two speeches from Yahweh with Job's two answers make a set of four. This set of four matches the four speeches from Elihu"); the stylistic match of the Behemoth portrait to the ostrich (39:13–18) and of the Leviathan portrait to the horse (39:19–25) and birds of prey (39:26–30); comparable expansions elsewhere (12:12–25; 24:1–17; 28:15–19; 30:2–8); a rhetorical explanation of the length ("Yahweh kept speaking, as he waited for his words to move Job to change his attitude"); and the distinct business of each speech. He also holds that a divine word belonged to the oldest epic stratum, and that the speeches are dramatically required, being "anticipated in Job's repeated demand that God justify him (13:22; 16:18–21; 19:25–27)."

## The Central Theological Question: Theodicy

The structural key to the book is the heavenly council scene (1:6-12; 2:1-7). The *haśśāṭān* ("the adversary/accuser" — a title, not a proper name; the definite article marks a function, not a personal name) poses the challenge: Job fears God only because God has hedged him with prosperity. Remove the blessings, and he will curse God to his face. God accepts the test.

This frames the book's governing question: **Is disinterested fear of God — worship that persists regardless of circumstances — possible?** The entire drama is a test of that proposition. Job's endurance vindicates it. But the author is honest about what disinterested faith *looks like* under maximum pressure: it looks like Job's lament and lawsuit, not patient silence.

### Job's Legal Case

Job's speeches follow a formal progression. He begins with lament, then moves to litigation language. He wants:
1. A hearing before God (13:13-27; 23:3-7)
2. A credible witness to testify on his behalf (16:19-22)
3. A redeemer who will vindicate him (19:25-27)

Hartley reads this as a rising sequence rather than a set of alternatives, running from the angel/holy one (5:1) through the arbiter (9:33–34), witness (16:19), and friend or interpreter (16:20) to the redeemer (19:25), and finally identified for Job by Elihu at 33:23. On Hartley's own commentarial method, see **Hermeneutical Frameworks** above.

His avowal of innocence (chs. 29-31) is a formal oath — an ancient legal device that compels the opposing party to respond or concede. By swearing it, Job forces God's hand. God *must* answer.

## The Yahweh Speeches: Non-Answer or Deeper Answer?

The Yahweh speeches are the book's interpretive crux. They do not address Job's specific charges. They do not explain why he suffered. They ask Job where he was when God laid the foundations of the earth, set the boundaries of the sea, designed the constellations, fed the raven, controlled the wild ox.

Hartley's reading: this is not a non-answer but a reframing. The argument's logic:

> If God exercises such fine-grained wisdom over creation — caring for the timing of birth pangs, governing the rain, understanding the binding of the Pleiades — then power and wisdom (justice) are *unified* in God's administration of the cosmos. No human being has a sufficient vantage point to accuse God of unjust governance without being able to answer these questions. God's speeches offer Job grounds for *trust*, not an explanation for his suffering.

The two primordial creatures — **Behemoth** (40:15-24) and **Leviathan** (40:25-41:26) — represent the hostile, chaotic forces that threaten cosmic order. If Job cannot master these, he cannot rule more justly than God. The challenge collapses Job's case not by answering it but by exposing the epistemological limits of any human accusation against divine governance.

## Job's Response: Recantation or Surrender?

Job 42:1-6 is one of the most disputed passages in the OT. The key verb is *em'as* (42:6): traditionally translated "I despise myself" or "I retract [and repent]." Hartley reads it as Job surrendering his *lawsuit* — his legal case against God — rather than confessing hidden sin. Job does not admit wrongdoing; he abandons the litigation after God has persuaded him that no human perspective is adequate to judge divine governance.

The epilogue then confirms: God says Job "has spoken rightly" (*dibartem ʾēlay neḵôn*) while condemning the three friends (42:7). This apparently contradicts the Yahweh speeches, where God rebukes Job for "darkening counsel" (38:2). Hartley's resolution: Job spoke rightly in *holding to his innocence* and in *affirming God as his Witness and Redeemer* (16:19; 19:25-27), even as he was wrong in accusing God of unjust governance of the world. The friends spoke wrongly in applying a mechanical retribution theology that could only interpret Job's suffering as punishment for sin — which would have confirmed the Satan's hypothesis that all worship is transactional. See [[job-42-6|Controversy: Job 42:6 — Recantation or Surrender?]].

## Key Themes

### 1. Righteous Suffering

The book's foundational tenet: an upright person *may suffer terribly without having sinned*. Calamity is not necessarily evidence of wrongdoing. This directly challenges the "doctrine of double retribution" (righteous = blessed; wicked = punished) that the three comforters articulate.

### 2. The Qualified Retribution Doctrine

Hartley insists the book does not *reject* retribution but *qualifies* a simplistic version:
- The comforters' error: treating retribution as a mechanical formula applicable to every individual case
- Their deeper error: tempting Job to seek God for material gain (repent → restore prosperity), which would confirm the Satan's cynical view of human worship
- Elihu's modification: suffering can be *disciplinary*, turning a person from potential error; it need not be *punitive*

### 3. Theodicy and Divine Justice

Job raises the issue of theodicy by pointing to the undeniable prosperity of the wicked and the suffering of the innocent (21:7-33; 24:1-17). God's answer: the universe is larger and more complex than any human vantage point allows. God manages the cosmos with wisdom that transcends human comprehension — suffering in one case does not disprove just governance of the whole.

### 4. Encounter with God as the Deepest Answer

The most profound resolution in the book is not intellectual but experiential: "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (42:5). Job's encounter with God — being addressed personally by the Creator of the cosmos — is itself the answer. Not an explanation, but a *relationship* with the personal God. Hartley: "the profoundest personal answer to undeserved suffering resides in the divine-human encounter."

### 5. The Mediating Angel and Ransom (33:23-28)

Elihu's speech introduces the most soteriologically developed passage in Job: a mediating angel (*malʾāḵ*, a "messenger/interpreter") proclaims that "a ransom (*kōper*) has been found" and the sufferer's life is redeemed from Sheol. This is the only passage in Job with explicit redemption-ransom structure. Hartley notes its prophetic significance for NT atonement theology.

### 6. Job as Precursor to Isaiah's Suffering Servant

Hartley makes the striking claim that **Isaiah was influenced by Job**, not vice versa. Job served as one of Isaiah's models in constructing the portrait of the Suffering Servant (Isa 40-55). The Servant, like Job, suffers innocently; the Servant's silent endurance contrasts with Job's vocal protests but shares the same paradigm of the righteous sufferer vindicated by God. If Hartley's dating is correct (Job = 7th century; Isa 40-55 = late 7th or 6th century), this is chronologically possible.

## Scholarship and Historiography

### Authorship

The book is anonymous. Hartley profiles the author as an Israelite wise man — "a highly educated person and a devout servant of Yahweh; he may be numbered among the great wise men of ancient Israel" — writing in a dialect "distinct from the Hebrew of Jerusalem… closer to Aramaic," possibly multilingual, versed in mining technology (28:1–11, which Hartley calls "the most detailed passage about mining preserved in ancient Hebrew"), zoology, and Canaanite myth, and at odds with the received answers of his day: "His unwillingness to accept standard answers no doubt brought him into conflict with the established priesthood and the scribes."

Competing accounts Hartley reports as positions:

| Position | Holder(s) as Hartley reports them |
|---|---|
| Mosaic authorship | recorded only as the tradition motivating the Peshitta's placement of Job after Deuteronomy; no holder named, no endorsement |
| The author was an Edomite, a descendant of Esau | unnamed ("it has been suggested"); Hartley judges it untestable for lack of Edomite literary remains |
| Job was translated into Hebrew from another language (Arabic or Aramaic) | traced to Ibn Ezra (12th c.) and later scholars |
| A Jew at Tema wrote the book **in Arabic**, 552–542 B.C. | Guillaume; Hartley: the Arabic insights "are not frequent enough and consistent enough for the book to have been composed in Arabic" |
| The MT is a partial translation of a lost 6th-c. Babylonian Aramaic original, worsened by Masoretic vocalization | Tur-Sinai; Hartley: "his ingenious insights are buried amidst many wild speculations" |

On literary unity Hartley lays out three approaches — single author revising over a lifetime (Snaith, Gordis); a collection of independent pieces by many authors (Irwin); an author who composed the bulk with later accretions (Fohrer) — and declares his own premise: "This commentary assumes one author for the reasons given below." He also cautions that "apart from manuscript evidence, the criteria for identifying secondary passages are highly subjective."

On the prose frame, Hartley follows what he calls the majority view — that "the author has taken an old epic account and reworked it to serve as the framework for the series of speeches" — citing N. Sarna's demonstration of an epic substratum, and rejecting A. Alt's narrower reconstruction (ch. 1 + 42:11–17) as "too restrictive." Fohrer's full reconstruction of the epic's adaptation is reported at length but not adopted.

### Date

| Date | Holders named by Hartley |
|---|---|
| Early 7th century B.C., Hezekiah's time | Andersen |
| Mid-6th century B.C., after the fall of Jerusalem | Terrien, Guillaume |
| 4th–3rd century B.C., the second-temple era | Dhorme, Fohrer, Gordis |
| 7th century B.C. | Hartley's own conclusion |

Hartley rebuts the late date by arguing that Aramaic–Hebrew interplay reaches back at least to the 9th century, that "the term the Satan in the prologue functions as a title, not as a proper name as in the late books of Chronicles and Zechariah," and that individual responsibility is attested pre-exilically. He rejects dating the book late merely because it challenges retribution: "the doctrine of retribution was well entrenched in different ages… it is fallacious to assume that Job was written quite late primarily because it challenges the doctrine of retribution." Against an exilic date he argues that the Exile was read as national punishment while Job treats an innocent individual, that a book of that era would allude to the Exile, and that an exilic author would not have located hero and friends in **Edom** given post-586 Judean hostility (Ezek. 25:12–14; 35:1–15; Ps. 137:7). His positive case for the 7th century rests on Canaanite/Ugaritic contacts, the revival of wisdom interest under Hezekiah (Prov. 25:1), 8th-century socio-political conditions, and the heavenly-council scenes resembling Micaiah's 9th-century vision (1 K. 22:19–23) more than Zech. 3.

**[TENSION — recorded, not resolved]** Hartley's Isaiah-priority argument yields a *different* terminus from his stated conclusion. Arguing that Isaiah depends on Job, he writes that this "would lead to a date for Job no later than the second half of the eighth century" (p. 19), while his concluding formulation is that "the interplay between this book and other OT books, especially Isaiah, can best be accounted for by placing this work in the seventh century B.C." (p. 20). The commentary does not reconcile the two.

### The direction of the Job–Isaiah relation

Hartley holds that the direction of dependence between Job and other OT texts is usually undeterminable in principle — "Job may be dependent on other texts, other texts may be dependent on Job, or parallel texts may be mutually dependent on an unknown third source" — but treats the Isaiah case as decidable, citing **Pfeiffer and Terrien**: "it seems most likely that the author of Job wrote before Isaiah, for he only alludes to the vicarious merit of innocent suffering; Isaiah develops this theme fully." He also notes that Job knows no individual resurrection (14:7–17), so that "in Isaiah the thought of victory over death is developed further than in the book of Job." His theological payoff — attributed to him, not to the text — is that "the message of the book of Job prepared the people to understand and receive Isaiah's bold new message."

## Genres

Hartley rejects both single-genre proposals he surveys. **Richter** identifies the book as a lawsuit, mapping its sections onto stages of legal procedure (pre-trial hearing chs. 4–14; formal trial chs. 15–31; Elihu's appeal chs. 32–37; God as litigant chs. 38–41; withdrawal of the complaint 42:1–6). **Westermann** calls it a dramatized lament with legal language incorporated. Hartley judges that "Richter's study is too one-sided," and that "'dramatized lament' is a descriptive term, not a literary genre." His own verdict: the book "is *sui generis*… both an epic and a wisdom disputation."

The constituent forms he identifies: **lament** (description of suffering, fear of death, justification of lamenting); **complaint** (against friends, against God); **lawsuit**; **petition**; **hymnic lines in praise of God**; **avowal of innocence**; **affirmation of trust**; **wisdom instruction**; **disputation** (the mode of the friends' speeches, the Elihu speeches, and the Yahweh speeches alike); **theophany**; and **animal portraits**, which he calls "an infrequent genre in the OT" (38:39–39:30). Job's two speeches outside the dialogue are a **curse-lament** (ch. 3 — curse against the day of birth, 3:3–13, plus lament, 3:14–26) and an **oath of innocence** (ch. 31) embedded in an expanded lament structure.

*Terminological caution:* Hartley's discussion uses "lawsuit," "litigation," "legal language," and "litigant," but he does **not** use the technical term *rîb* in the Introduction. Do not attribute *rîb* terminology to him.

## ANE Parallels

Hartley's controlling verdict governs everything in this section: "the author may have been influenced by the rich literary tradition of the ancient Near East about suffering, but more in format than in substance." Beside the comparanda tabulated below he adduces the Egyptian **"Admonitions of Ipu-wer"** (protest at social upheaval, but its author "desire[s]… more for a stable social order than for moral justice"); the Egyptian **"A Dispute over Suicide"** (a man debating death with his *ba*); a **Sumerian poem of the afflicted righteous man** of the early 2nd millennium, whose sufferer "never raises the question of divine justice"; an **Akkadian text from Ugarit** in which "the god who has cast him down is the very god who will raise him up"; a **fragmentary 16th-century Akkadian text** in which the god declares the sufferer's heart innocent — "the points of contact are amazing" — and in which, inversely to Job, the friend supports rather than accuses; the Indian legend of **[Hariscandra]**, tested by a wager between "the gods **[Vasishta]** and Shiva" (Hartley's characterization); the Egyptian **"Book of the Dead"**, which he thinks may have influenced the construction of Job's oath of innocence in ch. 31; and the **Ugaritic** corpus generally, whose parallels with Job he calls "too specific to be accidental." The "Babylonian Ecclesiastes" is the acrostic dialogue of the sufferer **[Shaggil-kinam-ubbib]**, whose name Hartley glosses "May Esagil declare the righteous pure."

Major parallels examined by Hartley:

| Text | Origin | Key similarity | Key difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Protests of the Eloquent Peasant" | Egypt, 21st c. BCE | Long speeches from an offended party; demand for justice | Social complaint, not theodicy; resolved by clever petition |
| "I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom" ("Babylonian Job") | Mesopotamia, 1st mill. BCE | Inexplicable suffering of a righteous man; healing by divine dream | No accusation against the deity; no lawsuit; resolved by exorcism |
| "Dialogue about Human Misery" ("Babylonian Ecclesiastes") | Mesopotamia, ca. 1000 BCE | Dialogue format between sufferer and friend; God's ways remote | Only two speakers; gods created humans with perverse nature; no theodicy resolution |
| Ugaritic Akkadian sufferer | Ras Shamra | Sufferer expects Marduk to restore despite affliction | Takes no adversarial posture; no litigation |

Job's uniqueness: four speakers (literary breakthrough), the format of a formal lawsuit against God, integration of lament and disputation into a unified dramatic structure, and the severity of the theodicy challenge. The author was likely familiar with the ANE tradition but transformed it substantially.

## Literary-Theological Cruxes

**The *haśśāṭān*** (chs. 1-2): A title (*haśśāṭān* = "the adversary/accuser"), not a personal name. The definite article marks a function: a heavenly "prosecuting attorney" who tests human loyalty. In later books (1 Chr 21:1; Zech 3:1-2) and in the NT the name is used differently. The Job figure is closer to a heavenly quality-assurance function than to the cosmic evil being of later theology.

**Job 1:9 — *ḥinnām***: The Satan asks whether Job fears God *ḥinnām*, "for nought." Hartley makes the word the pivot of the whole book, tracking three turns of it: the Satan's insinuation that "all good deeds spring from selfish motives" (1:9); Yahweh's counter-use at 2:3, where the accusations prove to be the thing that is "without cause"; and Job's own use at 9:17, where he "perceives that God has afflicted him *ḥinnām*, without cause" — "Whereas the Satan doubted Job's motive for being righteous, the trial leads Job to doubt the justice of God's actions."

**Job 9:33 — the arbiter/umpire**: Job wishes for "a heavenly umpire, one who could lay his hand on both God and himself and effect a reconciliation" — the idiom meaning, on Hartley's reading, "to ensure that both parties abide by the resolution." Hartley judges the wish impossible within Job's world ("there is no official who could require God to act in a set way") but revelatory: "his search for vindication is essentially a search for God again to make himself known to him." Hartley then adds an explicitly Christian extension of his own, not of Job's meaning: "Job's thinking is working with the same inner logic that stands behind the core teaching of the NT, that Jesus Christ, God's Son, has become the Mediator, the Arbiter, between God and man" (p. 192).

**Job 13:15**: Hartley adopts the *lōʾ* reading and translates "If he were to slay me, I would have no hope. Nevertheless, I will defend my ways to his face," rejecting the AV's familiar "Though he slay me, yet will I trust him" as "inaccurate" — the AV rendering, he argues, "goes against the grain of Hebraic thought that teaches that a person's primary opportunity to praise and serve God is in this life" and "suggests irrational trust." His formula: "Job's faith is bold, not blind. It is searching for the justice behind the display of God's power."

**Job 14:14**: Hartley holds that Job envisions no individual resurrection — "His view here is closer to a concept of resuscitation than to resurrection" — and dates full resurrection doctrine explicitly to the Christian era: "this doctrine becomes central to biblical faith only after the resurrection of Christ, for it gains its validation in Christ's triumph over death." This is Hartley's own confessional periodization, not a claim of the text.

**Job 16:19 — the heavenly witness**: Job's affirmation of a witness in heaven; Hartley treats it as one station in a rising sequence — angel/holy one (5:1), arbiter (9:33–34), witness (16:19), friend/interpreter (16:20), redeemer (19:25).

**Job 19:25-27 — "I know that my Redeemer lives"**: One of the most debated texts in Job. Hartley reads it as the climax of the sequence above, and notes the shift from wish to knowledge — Job "now speaks with assurance." The identity of the *gōʾēl* (God himself, or an arbiter other than God), the timing of the vindication, and the long Christian reception line are treated at [[job-19-25-redeemer|Controversy: the Redeemer of Job 19:25]].

**Job 27:4 — the refusal of a false confession**: "The *ʾim* implies an imprecation: 'May God curse me if I do such a thing.'" Hartley reads the verse as Job's oath never to speak falsely, and as the anticipation of ch. 31: "Both Job's resolve and his use of an oath pave the way for his final oath of innocence."

**Job 28:28**: Hartley resolves the old complaint that the verse is a pious anticlimax by a grammatical argument — "Up to this point 'wisdom' has had the definite article, but in this verse it is without it… The fact that this word is without the article indicates that it is the practical side of wisdom human beings may acquire." He insists the resulting wisdom is "not irrational": "It is intelligible, for it is the portal into the vast resources of God's wisdom." *(Note an inconsistency in Hartley's own citation: his translation at 28:28 reads "the fear of God," but at p. 484 he quotes the verse as "the fear of the Lord.")*

**The Elihu speeches and Job 33:23-28**: The mediating angel who finds a ransom is the most soteriologically specific passage in the book. Hartley catalogues six proposed identities for the mediator — "(1) another human being, e.g., a covenant friend, a prophet, or a teacher; (2) the sufferer's own conscience; (3) one of the angelic host; (4) the heavenly witness mentioned in 16:19; (5) the special angel or messenger of Yahweh…; (6) the concealed Christ" — and adopts the fifth, not the sixth: reading "one among a thousand" as "having very restrictive force," he concludes "this mediating angel is a very special heavenly creature. He may be identified with 'the angel of Yahweh.'" He adds that "Elihu is no doubt showing Job who his witness (16:19) or his redeemer (19:25) really is."

**Job 42:6**: see [[job-42-6|Controversy: Job 42:6 — Recantation or Surrender?]].

**Job 42:7 — "you have not spoken of me what is right"**: Hartley registers the tension with 38:2 himself, already at the earlier verse: "This divine rebuke, nevertheless, stands in tension with the coming statement in which Yahweh will affirm that Job has spoken rightly about God (42:7)." His resolution turns on the *magnitude and kind* of the two charges: "Whereas Yahweh has accused Job of darkening knowledge (38:2), his charge against the friends is much stronger. Job has been genuinely groping for the truth, but the friends have spoken falsely in their attempt to defend God." He glosses the friends' *nəḇālâ* as "folly… the denial of God's goodness and redemptive activity in the affairs of mankind."

## NT Connections

- **James 5:11**: "You have heard of the steadfastness of Job" — the NT's direct citation of Job's endurance as a model of patient faith. James reads Job as the paradigm of perseverance under trial.
- **Rom 11:34-35**: Paul quotes Job 41:11 ("Who has given to me first, that I should repay?") to argue that God's ways are unsearchable — a direct use of Job's theodicy framework to describe the mystery of divine election.
- **1 Cor 1:19**: Paul cites Job 5:13 ("He catches the wise in their craftiness") to ground his theology of the cross against the wisdom of the world.
- **Heb 4:12-13**: "Everything is laid bare before him" — the language of total divine knowledge echoes Yahweh's speeches on divine omniscience.
- **Isaiah's Suffering Servant (Isa 40-55)**: Hartley argues Job provided the model for Isaiah's portrait; the Servant songs draw on the paradigm of the innocent sufferer who is ultimately vindicated.
