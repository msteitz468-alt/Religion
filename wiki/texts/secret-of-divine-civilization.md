# Secret of Divine Civilization (*Risáliy-i-Madaniyyih*)

*Populated from Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (Oneworld, 2000). All claims from that source unless marked.*

## Overview

The *Secret of Divine Civilization* (Arabic: *Asrár ul-ghaybiyya li asbáb ul-madaniyya*; Persian: *Risáliy-i-Madaniyyih*, "The Treatise on Civilization") is a treatise by [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]] on Iranian modernization. Written in 1875, the work was lithographed in Bombay in 1882 and received wide circulation in Iran. It was published anonymously and not identified as a specifically Bahá'í work — presumably to avoid prejudicing readers against its contents. The first English translation was published in 1910 in London as *The Mysterious Forces of Civilization* (lines 28031–28046).

## Argument

The main thrust of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's argument is the urgent need for Iran to adopt reform measures on Western models. Ancient Iran had been a powerful empire, honoured for its culture and civilization. By contrast, the country was in a wretched and degenerate state, pitied by others for its backwardness. Injustice and misgovernment had led to its decline (lines 28047–28057).

The Iranians should arise out of their torpor and resolve to transform their country and its institutions by:
- Promoting education (regarded as being of particular importance), industry, commerce, technology, arts and sciences
- Establishing just laws and protecting the rights of individuals equally before the law
- Ending corruption and the absolute powers of local governors, particularly their power to administer the death penalty
- Strengthening relations with other countries, including the great powers and friendly governments
- Increasing trade linkages and developing national resources and infrastructure
- Strengthening the army, ensuring soldiers were well housed and fed, officers well trained, and armaments up to date

(Lines 28058–28082.)

## Response to Islamic Objections

Some Iranians objected that such reforms involved copying the practices of non-Muslims and were hence un-Islamic. 'Abdu'l-Bahá responded that this was not valid: there were many Islamic precedents for the adoption of foreign practices, and many elements of European civilization were in any case derived from Islamic roots during the medieval period. Reforms such as the introduction of assemblies of consultation could be given Quranic justification. The rapid progress of Japan and the continuing backwardness of China indicated the contrast between nations that modernized and those that did not (lines 28083–28100).

## Distinctive Features

In the context of 19th-century reformism in the Middle East the book is unusual in advocating borrowing from the West whilst at the same time emphasizing the fundamental role of religion. Both religious bigotry and Western secularism are rejected. 'Abdu'l-Bahá also stressed the importance of spiritual qualities; outlined the characteristics required of religious leaders; and commented on civilization, government, law, mission, peace and wealth (lines 28100–28112).

## Historical Reception

The book was written when genuine reform of Iran seemed possible — Mírzá Husayn Khán was still politically influential and Násiru'd-Dín Sháh had just made his first visit to Europe (1873). The reform process effectively petered out in the late 1870s, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá evidently decided not to pursue his original intention of writing other books on related themes — specifically education. Apart from its specifically Iranian context, the treatise can be seen as a general Bahá'í prescription for developmental reform in any society (lines 28113–28132).

## Canon and Reception

Regarded as authoritative scripture produced by 'Abdu'l-Bahá (as an "authenticated" text). Its anonymous original publication means it was received first as a general reform tract, only later as a Bahá'í text.
