# Stromata (Miscellanies)

The third and most substantial work of [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]]'s trilogy —
eight books of deliberately unsystematic ("patchwork") theological and philosophical reflection. The
title *Stromateis* means "patchwork" (or "carpets"): "LET THESE NOTES OF OURS... BE OF VARIED
CHARACTER — AND AS THE NAME ITSELF INDICATES, PATCHED TOGETHER — PASSING CONSTANTLY FROM ONE THING TO
ANOTHER, AND IN THE SERIES OF DISCUSSIONS HINTING AT ONE THING AND DEMONSTRATING ANOTHER" (Bk IV).
"For those who seek for gold," he quotes Heraclitus, "dig much earth and find little gold." The
scattered form is deliberate: truth is sown so that only the worthy reader reconstructs it. Translated
by William Wilson (Book III's sexually explicit material is given in an anonymous *Library of Christian
Classics* English rendering where the Wilson edition leaves it in Latin).

## The Project: Faith, Philosophy, and the Christian Gnostic

The Stromata develops Clement's central, defining program: that the mature Christian advances from
**faith (pistis)** to **knowledge (gnosis)** and on to **love**, and that Greek philosophy is a
God-given preparation for this ascent — not the enemy of faith but its propaedeutic.

### Philosophy as schoolmaster, handmaid, and covenant (Books I, VI)
The signature thesis: "before the advent of the Lord, philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for
righteousness... For this was a schoolmaster to bring 'the Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews,
'to Christ'" (Bk I, l. 11933 — applying Gal 3:24's *paidagogos*). "God is the cause of all good
things; but of some primarily, as of the Old and the New Testament; and of others by consequence, as
philosophy." Philosophy is "the handmaid of theology," figured by the Hagar/Sarah allegory (secular
culture the handmaid, wisdom the wife: "I embrace secular culture as youthful, and a handmaid; but
thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife," l. 12013). In Book VI the point is sharpened:
"the same God that furnished both the Covenants was the giver of Greek philosophy to the Greeks" —
philosophy "a covenant peculiar to them," a "stepping-stone to the philosophy which is according to
Christ." "Rightly, then, to the Jews belonged the Law, and to the Greeks Philosophy, until the
Advent." Yet it yields only partial knowledge: philosophers, "while naming God, do not know Him." See
[[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy|harmony of religion and philosophy]].

### The theft of the Greeks (Books I, II, V, VI)
A structural counter-theme: the Greeks "plagiarized and falsified" their best dogmas, which are
"older" because derived from Moses and the prophets (Bk II, l. 14365). The Greeks are "thieves" who
"have taken without acknowledgment their principal dogmas from Moses and the prophets." Greek
philosophy is itself barbarian in origin (Pythagoras a Tuscan, Orpheus a Thracian), and "Plato does
not deny that he procured all that is most excellent in philosophy from the barbarians." Plato's
*Timaeus* is turned against the Greeks: "O Solon, Solon, you Greeks are always children. And no Greek
is an old man." Book VI extends this to a long catalogue of Greeks plagiarizing one another and
recasting Hebrew miracles as Hellenic prodigies.

### Faith as the indemonstrable first principle (Books II, VIII)
"The first cause of the universe can be apprehended by faith alone" (l. 14563); "Faith, then, is not
established by demonstration." Faith is "the foundation of rational choice," "as necessary to the
Gnostic, as respiration... is to life." Knowledge and faith are reciprocal: "neither is knowledge
without faith, nor faith without knowledge." The order of virtues runs "faith... fear, and hope, and
repentance... lead us to love and knowledge." Book VIII, a treatise on logic, grounds this
epistemologically: "all demonstration is traced up to indemonstrable faith" (l. 29121) — since first
principles cannot themselves be demonstrated without infinite regress, "there must be something that
is self-evident." Against [[gnostics|Basilides and Valentinus]], who made faith a *natural* endowment
of the elect, Clement insists faith is a free, voluntary act (see [[freedom|free will]]).

### The true Christian Gnostic (Books II, IV, VI, VII)
Clement's most distinctive construct: he reclaims the word "Gnostic" from the heretics for the
perfected orthodox believer. The true Gnostic does good "out of love, and for the sake of its own
excellence," not from fear of punishment or hope of reward; he would, if the two could be separated,
"without the least hesitation choose the knowledge of God" over salvation itself (l. 21246). He
attains [[apatheia|apatheia]] (passionlessness, not mere Stoic moderation) and is "assimilated to
God": "man, when deified purely into a passionless state, becomes a unit"; "made a god going about in
flesh" (l. 28811); destined "to sit on thrones with the other gods that have been first put in their
places by the Saviour." His whole life is unceasing prayer — "converse with God" (l. 27500) — and he
alone is "truly pious," though persecutors call him atheist. See [[gnosis|gnosis]] and
[[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]].

## Apophatic Theology (Book V)
Book V contains a *locus classicus* of Christian negative theology. The First Cause "is neither
genus, nor difference, nor species, nor individual, nor number... without form and name." We name God
("the One, or the Good, or Mind... or Father") only "for want," as "points of support," since "each
one by itself does not express God." By abstraction we "may reach somehow to the conception of the
Almighty, **knowing not what He is, but what He is not**" (l. 22901); the First Cause is "above both
space, and time, and name, and conception." God is known "only by His own power," by grace and "the
word alone that proceeds from Him." See [[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]].

## Marriage, Asceticism, and the Heretics (Book III)
Book III is a two-front polemic defending moderate, procreative marriage *and* voluntary celibacy as
both God-given, against (a) the antinomian libertines who say all is permitted and (b) the
world-hating encratites who call marriage evil. "We may divide all the heresies into two groups" — the
indifferentist and the over-ascetic. Against the encratites: "if anyone dares to call marriage
fornication, he again falls into blasphemy against the law and the Lord." Against the libertines:
true continence is from love of God, not display. It is the single richest early source for several
Gnostic and ascetic teachers, often quoting their lost works verbatim (see below).

## Symbolism and the Discipline of Concealment (Book V)
Book V argues that all who spoke of divine things — "both Barbarians and Greeks — have veiled the
first principles of things, and delivered the truth in enigmas, and symbols, and allegories." The
Egyptian *adyta* and the Hebrew *veil* alike hid the sacred; "almost the whole Scripture gives its
utterances in this way." Truth seen through a veil is "grander and more imposing; as fruits shining
through water." A mystic exegesis of the Tabernacle (the veil-colours as the four elements, the
cherubim, the high-priest's robe) and of Pythagorean symbols models the method. Clement claims an
**unwritten apostolic tradition** of gnosis transmitted "to a few" — "the gnosis itself... having
been imparted unwritten by the apostles."

## Martyrdom (Books IV, VII)
Martyrdom is "perfection, not because the man comes to the end of his life as others, but because he
has exhibited the perfect work of love" (l. 19713). True (gnostic) martyrdom is a whole pure life
witnessing "by life and word," "shedding faith as blood." But Clement firmly condemns rash,
**voluntary martyrdom**: those who rush to give themselves up "banish themselves without being
martyrs" and make themselves "an accomplice in the crime of the persecutor"; the Gnostic surrenders
only "on God really calling them."

## Book VIII — Logic and First Principles
Often regarded as a separate methodological treatise, Book VIII expounds demonstration ("discourse...
producing belief in points disputed, from points admitted"), its distinction from syllogism,
definition by genus and species, the ten categories, and a Stoic-flavoured taxonomy of causes
(procatarctic, synectic, co-operating, *sine qua non*). Its theological payoff is the grounding of
faith as the indemonstrable first principle on which all demonstration rests.

## Hermeneutical Method

Allegorical and symbolic exegesis is the master key — the patriarchs read as types
(Abraham/Sarah/Hagar = faith/wisdom/secular culture; Isaac "self-taught"; Jacob the "Exerciser"),
with [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] cited by name as authority. The reading proceeds "according to the
syllables" (gnostic unfolding) rather than "according to the letter," governed by "the ecclesiastical
rule" — "the concord and harmony of the law and the prophets in the covenant delivered at the coming
of the Lord" (see [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]]). Clement is programmatically **eclectic**: "whatever
has been well said by each of those sects... this eclectic whole I call philosophy." Knowledge of
truth is veiled to provoke inquiry and to exclude the unworthy ("nor are the mysteries of the word to
be expounded to the profane"). See [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement (commentator)]].

## Figures, Groups, and Heretical Sources

The Stromata is a major primary source for second-century "Gnostic" and ascetic teachers, often
preserving verbatim fragments of otherwise-lost works:
- [[valentinus|Valentinus]] and the Valentinians (incl. **Heracleon**, quoted on the two confessions;
  Valentinus's *Letter to Agathopus* on Jesus' continence);
- [[basilides|Basilides]] and his son Isidore (the martyrdom theodicy from the *Exegetics*; faith and
  election as "natural"; quoted from Isidore's *Ethics*);
- the [[carpocratians|Carpocratians]] — Carpocrates and his son Epiphanes, whose *Concerning
  Righteousness* (community of wives) Clement quotes and refutes;
- Marcion and the Marcionites (birth as evil, the Creator condemned);
- Tatian and the Encratites; Julius Cassianus ("originator of the Docetae"); the Nicolaitans,
  Antitactae, and the followers of Prodicus and Simon.

Against all of these Clement insists on the unity of the one God of Law and Gospel and the antiquity
of "the true Church, that which is really ancient" against later heresies dated to Hadrian and
Antoninus. [[moses|Moses]], Plato, and Pythagoras recur as the authoritative ancients; a long
florilegium of Greek poets is marshalled as witnesses to one God.

## Canon and Reception

A patristic work, authoritative but non-scriptural in Eastern Orthodoxy and Catholicism. Indispensable
as (a) the charter of Christian engagement with Greek philosophy, (b) a primary witness to lost Gnostic
literature, and (c) the seedbed of Alexandrian theology developed by [[origen|Origen]]. The Stromata
also preserves Clement's treatment of disputed and apocryphal texts — the *Epistle of Barnabas* and
the *Shepherd of Hermas* cited as authoritative, the *Gospel of the Hebrews*, the *Gospel of the
Egyptians*, and the *Preaching of Peter* quoted as sources.
