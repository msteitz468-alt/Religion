# Paedagogus (The Instructor / Tutor)

The second work of [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]]'s trilogy, in three books.
Where the [[protrepticus|Protrepticus]] converts and the projected *Didascalus* (never written,
its place taken by the [[stromata|Stromata]]) would teach doctrine, the *Paedagogus* trains
conduct: the [[logos-christology|Logos]] himself is the *paidagogos* (tutor) who forms the moral
life of the newly converted. Following Plato's tripartition (character, actions, passions), Clement
assigns character to the Protrepticus and makes the Paedagogus treat Christ "teaching us to act
morally and to control our passions." Translated by William Wilson; Homer is cited over sixty times.

## Book I — The Logos as Tutor

The Word is named "Tutor (or Pædagogue, or Instructor)" (l. 5375): "The Instructor being
practical, not theoretical, His aim is thus to improve the soul, not to teach, and to train it up
to a virtuous, not to an intellectual life" (l. 5376). The Word "first exhorts, then trains, and
finally teaches" (l. 5402) — a three-stage pedagogy, the Instructor finally handing the disciple
over to the Teacher ("now... it is time for me to cease... and for you to listen to the Teacher,"
l. 10978).

The Tutor is "sinless, blameless, and with a soul devoid of passion; God in the form of man... a
spotless image; to Him we are to try with all our might to assimilate our souls" (l. 5405) — see
[[apatheia|apatheia]] and [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]]. Believers are "the children"; Book I
Ch. V catalogues Scripture's figurative names for them (children, lambs, colts, chickens, doves)
and reads them as one system, with etymologies (παιδεία/παιδαγωγία, l. 5624; νήπιος as "newly
gentle," l. 5671). Baptism is illumination: "Being baptized, we are illuminated; illuminated, we
become sons; being made sons, we are made perfect; being made perfect, we are made immortal"
(l. 5797).

Against the [[gnostics|Gnostics]] who deride simple faith as childish, Clement insists faith is
itself perfection and salvation is equal for all ("neither male nor female: for ye are all one in
Christ Jesus," l. 5890). A long meditation makes the Word the milk, father, mother, and nurse of
the child, and the Church "at once virgin and mother" (l. 6080), shading into eucharistic
reflection ("Eat ye my flesh, and drink my blood"). The Word is the same God who disciplines and
saves: punishment is medicinal, "the surgery of the passions of the soul" (l. 6464), and
[[freedom|free will]] keeps God blameless — "Each one of us, who sins, with his own free-will
chooses punishment" (l. 6536). The old covenant of fear is fulfilled in the new covenant of love:
"fear is turned to love" (l. 6366).

## Books II–III — Concrete Ethics

Books II–III translate the Logos-as-tutor into a remarkably detailed code of daily conduct — among
the richest sources for Christian *Lebensführung* in the early Church. Topics: eating ("eat that we
may live," not "live that we may eat," l. 5169) and the love-feast vs. mere "suppers"; drinking
(water the natural drink; wine in moderation; against the [[gnostics|Encratites]] — "Let this be
held fast by us against those that are called Encratites," since Christ drank wine, l. 7759); costly
vessels; conduct at feasts (the famous allegory of the Psalm 150 instruments — "man is truly a
pacific instrument," l. 7940); laughter ("Smiling even requires to be made the subject of
discipline," l. 8032); filthy speech; table manners; ointments and crowns (against garlands: "it
were irrational in us, who have heard that the Lord was crowned with thorns, to crown ourselves
with flowers," l. 8462); sleep and watchfulness; sexual ethics (Bk II Ch. X, rendered in Latin in
Wilson's edition: procreation the sole legitimate end of intercourse; condemnation of same-sex acts,
adultery, intercourse with the pregnant or menstruant, and abortion/contraception); clothing
(plain, white; women veiled, l. 9145); shoes; jewellery and gold (a communitarian property ethic:
"All things therefore are common, and not for the rich to appropriate an undue share," l. 9242).

Book III opens with self-knowledge as the way to God and likeness: "the greatest of all lessons to
know one's self. For if one knows himself, he will know God; and knowing God, he will be made like
God" (l. 9388); "Men are gods, and gods are men... God in man, and man God" (Heraclitus, applied at
l. 9415). It then treats cosmetics (condemned — the painted woman as a gorgeous Egyptian shrine
concealing a beast), male grooming (sharp condemnation of depilation and hair-dyeing as effeminacy;
the beard "the token of the superior nature," l. 9721), companions, baths, and — prefiguring
[[quis-dives-salvetur|Quis Dives Salvetur]] — wealth ("it is not he who has and keeps, but he who
gives away, that is rich," l. 10017; "The Christian Alone Rich"). It closes with a compendium of the
Christian life, a "Religion in Ordinary Life" (honest trade, no oaths in the market, conduct in
church), a prayer to the Paedagogus, and the **"Hymn to Christ the Saviour"** — among the earliest
extant Christian hymns with traditional named authorship ("Bridle of colts untamed, / Over our wills
presiding," l. 11039).

## Hermeneutical Method

Clement reads chiefly for the moral (tropological) sense, blending literal and allegorical. He draws
ethical lessons from Mosaic ritual law allegorically — and notably keeps the moral conclusion while
*rejecting the literal natural-history premise* (the hyena/hare zoology, Bk II Ch. X), treating
allegory's validity as independent of the factual claim. He is self-aware about genre, twice
catching himself lapsing from the "pædagogic" into the "didactic" register and pulling back (l. 8503).
Greek philosophy and the comic poets are deployed as ethical corroboration, with Plato "the disciple
of the barbarian philosophy" (i.e. dependent on Moses, l. 8921). See
[[clement-of-alexandria|Clement (commentator)]].

## Canon and Reception

A patristic work, authoritative but non-scriptural; valued in Eastern Orthodoxy and Catholicism. Its
detailed ethic of moderation, dress, food, and wealth made it a quarry for later Christian moral
theology and for the history of early-Christian daily life.
