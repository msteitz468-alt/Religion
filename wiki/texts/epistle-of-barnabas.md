# Epistle of Barnabas

## Textual Overview

*The Epistle of Barnabas* (*Barn.*) is an anonymous pseudonymous document — its attribution to Barnabas (Paul's missionary companion in Acts) is rejected by virtually all modern scholars. The author was an anonymous Christian teacher who used the epistolary framework as a literary convention. The document is not a genuine letter but a polemical essay — or, in its own self-description, a "short letter" that shares with its readers *gnōsis* (knowledge) that the author has received.

**Date**: After AD 70 (the destruction of the Jerusalem temple is referenced explicitly at 16.3–5 as a past event), and before ca. 135 CE (the author seems to know of plans to rebuild the temple, which Hadrian's plans — later scrapped in the Bar Kokhba aftermath — would suggest). Most proposals fall between 96–132 CE; the reign of Nerva (96–98) or Hadrian (117–138) is most common.

**Place of origin**: Alexandria is the most probable location, based on:
- Affinities with Alexandrian Jewish hermeneutics (especially Philo's allegorical method)
- The document's earliest witness is Clement of Alexandria (who cited it as authoritative, with a status approaching scripture)
- *Codex Sinaiticus*, the famous 4th-century Alexandrian biblical manuscript, includes *Barnabas* after Revelation and before the *Shepherd of Hermas*

A Syrian origin has also been proposed.

## Structure and Content

Two major sections are united by a shared apocalyptic-ethical perspective:

### Part One: Allegorical Interpretation of the Mosaic Law (1.1–17.2)

The author's central argument: **Israel forfeited God's covenant by idolatry** (4.8; 16.1–2) and disobedience; the **Mosaic laws were always intended to be read spiritually/allegorically**, not literally; therefore **Christians are the true and intended heirs of the covenant**, not Israel. "Their covenant was shattered" (4.8) — not because God changed his mind, but because Israel misread him from the beginning.

Key allegorical readings:
- **Dietary laws** (10.1–11): The prohibition on pork does not mean avoid eating pig — it means avoid people who behave like pigs (forget the Lord when well-fed; remember him when in need). The prohibition on eagle, hawk, kite means avoid associators with tyrants. The prohibition on hare means avoid pederasts. Etc.
- **Circumcision** (9.1–9): Abraham's 318 servants in Gen. 14:14 are read numerologically — 318 in Greek = 18 + 300, and 18 = ΙΗ (the first two letters of Jesus/Iēsous), while 300 = Τ (the letter shaped like the cross). Therefore, Abraham's circumcision was always a type of Jesus and the cross.
- **The covenant** (4.6–8; 14.1–4): Moses received the covenant on Mt. Sinai, but Israel broke it immediately (the golden calf). Moses shattered the tablets. "Their covenant was shattered in order that the covenant of the beloved Jesus might be sealed in our hearts."
- **The Temple** (16.1–10): The physical temple was always a mistake — God does not dwell in human-made houses (quoting Isa. 66:1). The real temple is the heart. Now, through the Lord's grace, "we are the temple of God."

The hermeneutical method is thoroughgoing allegory — the most radical application of allegorical reading in the early Christian corpus. Unlike Philo, who maintained both literal and allegorical meanings of the Torah, the author of *Barnabas* eliminates the literal: Israel *never* had the right reading.

### Part Two: The Two Ways (18.1–21.9)

A "Two Ways" section structurally similar to the *Didache*'s (chs. 1–6) and probably sharing a common antecedent source:
- **Way of Light** (19.1–12): A list of positive duties: love God, your neighbor; confess your sins; share with those in need; be humble; obey your masters
- **Way of Darkness** (20.1–2): Evil angels govern it; the vices that characterize it

The Two Ways section functions as the ethical application of the allegorical argument: since Christians now have the covenant, here is how to live as covenant people.

## Major Themes

**Radical supersessionism**: *Barnabas* is the most thoroughgoing statement of supersessionism in the Apostolic Fathers. Israel did not have, and never had, a genuine covenant relationship with God — they lost it immediately, by idolatry. This is different from the more common "fulfillment" supersessionism (Christians complete what Israel began) and represents an extreme form: Israel was always wrong.

**Allegorical hermeneutic**: The document is an extended display of what Holmes calls "the earliest and arguably most extreme example of allegorical exegesis" in early Christianity. The Mosaic laws are entirely reread as prophecies and types of Christ and the church. This is "a good early example of what became the dominant method of interpreting the Bible in the early and medieval church" (Holmes, p. 372).

**Apocalyptic urgency**: The Two Ways framework is set within an apocalyptic worldview: the present evil age is ending; the "age to come" is near; Christians must live in covenant purity because judgment is imminent (4.9; 21.3).

**The question of real Jewish opponents**: Scholars debate whether *Barnabas* is responding to actual Jewish pressure on the Christian community (e.g., a Jewish renaissance threatening to draw Christians into synagogue observance) or whether the "Jews" function as a literary foil in an internal Christian identity-construction exercise. The weight of recent scholarship (Miriam Taylor, Miriam Hvalvik) tends toward the latter: the anti-Jewish rhetoric serves internal Christian self-definition more than it reflects actual conflict with historical Jews.

## Hermeneutical Framework

*Barnabas* operates almost exclusively within the **allegorical** mode — tradition-specific to early Christian Alexandrian practice. The movement is: (1) cite an OT text; (2) reject its literal sense as Israel's misunderstanding; (3) provide the true spiritual/prophetic/typological meaning as pointing to Christ or the church.

This is the patristic tradition that produced Origen's *Commentary on Numbers* and much of the Alexandrian exegetical corpus — but *Barnabas* is cruder and earlier than the systematized Alexandrian allegorism of Origen and Clement.

## Canonical Status and Manuscript Tradition

*Barnabas* was among the most disputed early texts regarding canonicity:
- **Cited as scripture by Clement of Alexandria** — the earliest witness to the document and the most prestigious endorsement it received
- **In Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century) — one of the two oldest complete Greek Bibles, *Barnabas* appears after Revelation and before the *Shepherd of Hermas*; its presence in Sinaiticus does not guarantee canonical status but indicates high authority
- **Explicitly excluded by Eusebius** (*Church History* 3.25): listed among the "rejected" (*nothoi*) writings
- **Origen**: Sometimes cited it as scripture, sometimes not

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Covenant transfer or covenant replacement?**: The most theologically explosive question. Does *Barnabas* say that Christians *replace* Israel in the covenant (covenant transfer to a different people — supersessionism) or that Israel *never had* the covenant (the covenant was always intended for Christians alone)? The text seems to say the latter: "their covenant was shattered" on the day Moses threw down the tablets — before Israel ever received it properly.

**Barn. 4.6 — "Our covenant remains valid"**: A textually uncertain and interpretively disputed passage. Holmes emends the text to read that the *Christians* must not think their covenant is irrevocably secure (i.e., avoid presumption), not that Israel's covenant was or is valid. The alternative readings have significant theological implications.

**Barn. 9.7–9 (The 318 servants and the cross)**: A tour de force of numerological allegory. Whether this represents the author's genuine method throughout or a rhetorical display of a technique that should not be universalized has been debated. It is certainly representative of what the author means by allegorical reading.

**The Two Johns problem does not apply here, but the "Two Ways" problem does**: Was the Two Ways section (chs. 18–21) an integral part of the original document or appended later? Holmes treats it as original, bound together with the polemical essay by the shared apocalyptic ethical framework.
