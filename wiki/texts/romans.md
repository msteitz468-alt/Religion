# Letter to the Romans

Paul's most theologically comprehensive letter, written from Corinth c. AD 55–58 to the house churches of Rome before his planned visit and subsequent mission to Spain. The longest Pauline letter and the most contested in interpretation history; Luther called it "the chief part of the New Testament, and truly the purest gospel." Romans has generated more commentary and more hermeneutical controversy than any other NT letter, and has been a decisive force in every major theological shift from Augustine through the Reformation to the present.

## Authorship, Date, and Occasion

Pauline authorship is uncontested. The amanuensis was Tertius (16:22). Date: between the Gallio proconsulship datum (AD 51–52) and Paul's Jerusalem arrest — most likely the three-month stay in Corinth (Acts 20:2–3), winter AD 55–58. The letter was carried by Phoebe of Cenchreae (16:1–2).

The original scope of the letter is debated: chapter 16 (whose many greetings have raised questions about a Roman vs. Ephesian destination) is accepted as original by most scholars. A fourteen-chapter Marcionite recension circulated early. The doxology (16:25–27) is authentically Pauline.

## Occasion and Purpose

[[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (BECNT, 1998) argues for multiple, interlocking purposes:

**Immediate**: Resolve tensions between Jewish and Gentile Christians in Rome. After Claudius expelled Jews in AD 49 and Nero allowed their return (AD 54), the Roman house churches were strained. Jewish Christians returning to churches that had developed in their absence encountered Gentile Christians less observant of food laws and the Sabbath — the "weak" and "strong" of 14:1–15:13.

**Strategic**: Secure Rome as a support base for the Spanish mission (15:22–24).

**Theological**: Defend and explicate the Pauline gospel against suspicions current in Rome. Romans is not a general doctrinal summary (it omits the Lord's Supper, developed ecclesiology, and eschatology), but a targeted exposition of the gospel with respect to the Mosaic law, Israel's status, and Jew-Gentile unity — precisely the areas of contention.

**Ultimate**: The glory of God. Schreiner argues inductively that every section drives toward this end (1:5 "for the sake of his name"; 11:33–36 doxology; 15:7–13 Jews and Gentiles together glorifying God; 16:27 closing doxology). "The glory of God is the central theme that permeates the letter."

## Structure

Schreiner's eight-part outline organized around the *dikaiosynē theou* (righteousness of God):

1. **The Gospel as Revelation of God's Righteousness** (1:1–17) — salutation, thanksgiving, theme (1:16–17)
2. **God's Righteousness in Wrath against Sinners** (1:18–3:20) — universal human sinfulness: Gentiles (1:18–32), Jews (2:1–3:8), all (3:9–20)
3. **The Saving Righteousness of God** (3:21–4:25) — justification by faith; God's righteousness in Jesus' death (3:21–26); Abraham as paradigm
4. **Hope as Result of Righteousness by Faith** (5:1–8:39) — Adam and Christ (5:12–21); freedom from sin (ch. 6); the law and the flesh (ch. 7); life in the Spirit and hope (ch. 8)
5. **God's Righteousness to Israel and Gentiles** (9:1–11:36) — the problem of Jewish unbelief; election; "all Israel will be saved" (11:26); closing doxology
6. **God's Righteousness in Everyday Life** (12:1–15:13) — total dedication to God (12:1–2); love and nonretaliation; submission to governing authority (13:1–7); the weak and strong
7. **Extension of God's Righteousness through the Pauline Mission** (15:14–16:23)
8. **Final Summary** (16:25–27) — doxology; framing bookend with 1:1–7

## Major Themes

**The righteousness of God** (*dikaiosynē theou*, 1:17). The controlling concept of the letter; both forensic (a gift — the believer's righteous status before God's court) and transformative (a divine saving power). The two cannot be separated: "God's forensic declaration is effective, so that those who are pronounced righteous are also transformed by God's grace." For the full debate, see [[righteousness-of-god|The Righteousness of God]].

**Justification by faith alone**. Romans 3:21–4:25 is Paul's most developed argument for the doctrine. Righteousness is a divine gift received through faith, not earned through "works of the law." Abraham is the paradigm: he was reckoned righteous before circumcision (4:9–12) and apart from works (4:3–5). See [[justification|Justification]].

**The New Perspective on Paul**. Schreiner's commentary is an explicit engagement with E. P. Sanders's covenantal nomism hypothesis (Judaism = getting in by grace, staying in by works) and James Dunn's claim that "works of the law" = Jewish ethnic identity markers. Schreiner defends the traditional Protestant reading: "works of the law" refers to human achievement/merit broadly, not merely boundary markers. See [[new-perspective-on-paul|The New Perspective on Paul]].

**Sin, death, and the law** (chs. 1–3; 5–7). Universal human sinfulness (1:18–3:20) establishes the need for God's saving righteousness. The law reveals and even provokes sin (5:20; 7:7–11) but cannot deliver from it. The resolution comes through Christ (8:1–4) and the Spirit (8:1–17), fulfilling what the prophets foresaw (Jer 31:31–34; Ezek 36:26–27).

**The "wretched man" of Romans 7**. The identity of the "I" in 7:14–25 ("I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do") is one of the letter's major interpretive cruxes. See [[romans-7-identity|Romans 7 — The Identity of the "I"]].

**Israel and the Gentiles** (chs. 9–11). The theological heart of Romans addresses whether God has been faithful to his covenant promises to ethnic Israel in light of Israel's widespread unbelief. Schreiner's reading: (1) God never promised salvation to every ethnic Israelite — there has always been an elective remnant (9:6–29); (2) Israel's present hardening is temporary and serves the inclusion of Gentiles (11:11–24); (3) at the end of history, the mass of ethnic Israel will be saved — "all Israel will be saved" (11:26). See [[all-israel-saved|"All Israel Will Be Saved" (Rom 11:26)]].

**The weak and the strong** (14:1–15:13). Schreiner rejects the view that this is generic paraenesis reworked from 1 Cor 8–10. The "weak" in Rome were Jewish Christians maintaining Torah observances (food laws, Sabbath); the "strong" were primarily Gentile Christians. Paul's primary exhortation is directed to the strong: accept the weak without requiring them to abandon scruples. Unity for the sake of the joint glorification of God (15:7–13) is the ultimate goal.

**Submission to governing authority** (13:1–7). All civil authorities are God-ordained; believers are to submit for both prudential (fear of punishment) and conscience reasons. The authority's right to "bear the sword" (capital punishment) is affirmed. This is a general norm, not an absolute rule — Paul is sketching the typical obligation, not endorsing totalitarianism.

**Glory of God as ultimate motive**. Schreiner's distinctive contribution: the entire soteriological argument is anchored not in human benefit but in God's glory and name. This is established from OT background (Ps 143; Dan 9; Ezek 36:20–32) and confirmed throughout Romans.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

1. **Romans 1:17 — "righteousness of God"**: Forensic gift, divine saving power, or covenant faithfulness? See [[righteousness-of-god|The Righteousness of God]].
2. **Romans 1:17 — Hab 2:4 citation**: Does *ek pisteōs* modify "righteous" ("the righteous-by-faith shall live") or "live" ("the righteous shall live by faith")? Schreiner: latter, but both describe the same reality.
3. **Romans 3:25 — *hilastērion***: Propitiation (turning away divine wrath) or expiation (cleansing sin)? Schreiner: propitiation.
4. **Romans 5:12 — "death spread to all because all sinned"**: Did all humanity sin *in* Adam (federal headship) or *because of* Adam's sin (natural transmission)? Schreiner follows Cranfield and Moo in the federal reading.
5. **Romans 7:14–25**: Regenerate or unregenerate? See [[romans-7-identity|Romans 7 — The Identity of the "I"]].
6. **Romans 9 — election**: Does Paul teach unconditional individual election to salvation, or corporate/historical election? Schreiner: unconditional individual election.
7. **Romans 11:26 — "all Israel"**: Ethnic Israel in a future mass conversion, the elect remnant throughout history, or the whole people of God (church + Israel)? See [[all-israel-saved|"All Israel Will Be Saved" (Rom 11:26)]].
8. **Romans 13:1–7**: Does Paul's call to submission forbid civil disobedience in any circumstance? Schreiner: No — this is a general norm qualified by Acts 5:29.

## Relationship to Paul's Other Letters

Schreiner operates from the assumption that all 13 Pauline letters are authentic, including the Pastorals, and draws on parallels throughout. Romans is not a comprehensive summary of Pauline theology (no Lord's Supper, no detailed Christology, undeveloped ecclesiology) but the fullest treatment of the specific themes Paul needed to address for the Roman situation.
