The **Qur'an** (*al-Qur'an*, "the Recitation") is the central scripture of [[islam|Islam]], believed
by Muslims to be the literal, verbatim word of God (*Allah*) revealed to the Prophet
[[muhammad|Muhammad]] through the angel Gabriel (Jibril) between 610 and 632 CE, in Arabic. It is universally canonical
across all Islamic sects — its `canon_scope` is the simplest in the wiki, holding for
[[sunni-islam|Sunni]], [[shia-islam|Shia]], and [[sufism|Sufi]] Islam alike — even as the
**hadith** canon that interprets it divides them sharply.

## Structure

The Qur'an comprises **114 *suras*** (chapters) of unequal length, each (but one) opening with the
*basmala* ("In the name of God, the Merciful, the Compassionate"), and totaling **6,236 *ayat***
(verses, lit. "signs"). After the short opening sura (*al-Fatiha*), the suras are arranged **roughly
longest-to-shortest**, not chronologically — a non-narrative ordering that is itself an interpretive
challenge. Suras are traditionally classified as **Meccan** (earlier, shorter, eschatological and
theological) or **Medinan** (later, longer, legal and communal), a distinction central to *tafsir*
and to the theory of **[[naskh|abrogation]]** (*naskh*).

## Composition, Collection, and Text

Muslim tradition holds that the revelations were memorized and written piecemeal during Muhammad's
life and collected into a single authoritative codex (*mushaf*) under the third Caliph **Uthman**
(c. 650), with variant codices suppressed. The agreed **consonantal skeleton** (*rasm*) is stable; the
later addition of vowel and diacritical marks, together with the orally-transmitted recitation
systems, generates the licensed variant readings — the **[[qiraat-variant-readings|qira'at]]** — the
wiki's first dedicated Islamic controversy page. Historical-critical scholarship (the Sanaa palimpsest,
the revisionist debate) is recorded there without adjudication.

The **critical reconstruction** ([[bell-watt-introduction-to-the-quran|Bell & Watt]], following the
Nöldeke–Schwally *Geschichte des Qorāns*) treats the tradition of an **Abu Bakr** "collection" as
largely a back-projection and the **Uthmanic recension** (via **Zayd ibn Thabit**, privileging the
Quraysh dialect) as the first authoritative text — while concluding, moderately, that it faithfully
preserves "what Muhammad delivered" and raising "no serious question of its authenticity." The
traditionalist counter-history — that the text was perfectly memorized and transmitted from the start
and that the orientalist reconstruction is methodologically bankrupt — is argued by
[[al-azami-history-of-the-quranic-text|M. M. Al-Azami]]; the two are held in tension on the
[[qiraat-variant-readings|qira'at controversy page]] (Contradiction Protocol).

## Major Themes

- **[[tawhid|Tawhid]]** — the absolute oneness and transcendence of God; the cardinal doctrine, and
  the point of sharpest contrast with Christian Trinitarianism (Q 112; Q 4:171).
- **Prophecy and the seal** — a single chain of prophets from Adam through Ibrahim, Musa, and Isa to
  Muhammad, the *khatam al-nabiyyin* (seal of the prophets); earlier scriptures genuine but corrupted
  (*tahrif*).
- **Judgment, resurrection, paradise and hell**; **mercy** (the divine names al-Rahman al-Rahim);
  **law and covenant** (the Medinan legislation).

## Key Interpretive Cruxes (cross-tradition)

These are the loci the sequencing plan flags as immediate cross-tradition controversy generators:

- **[[crucifixion-in-the-quran|The crucifixion of Jesus]]** (Q 4:157): "they slew him not nor
  crucified him, but it appeared so unto them" — a direct contradiction of the Gospel passion
  narratives.
- **The near-sacrifice** (Q 37:99–113): the son is unnamed in the text, but mainstream Islamic
  tradition identifies him as **[[ishmael|Ishmael (Isma'il)]]**, not Isaac — see
  [[akedah-interpretation|the Akedah controversy]].
- **Isa (Jesus)** (Q 3, Q 19): virgin-born, Messiah, word and spirit from God, a prophet — but **not**
  divine and **not** the son of God (Q 4:171; Q 5:72–75).
- **Tahrif** — the claim that the [[genesis|Tawrat]] and [[gospel-of-john|Injil]] were altered, set
  against their integrity in [[judaism|Judaism]] and [[christianity|Christianity]].

## Hermeneutics

The Qur'an is read through **[[tafsir|tafsir]]** — classically *bi'l-ma'thur* (tradition-based, of
which [[al-tabari|al-Tabari]]'s *Jami' al-bayan* is the exemplar) vs. *bi'l-ra'y* (reason-based) — and
along a *zahir* (exoteric) / *batin* (esoteric) axis, the latter the domain of [[sufism|Sufi]] and
Shia *ta'wil* (see [[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]]).

## Reception and Translation History

A central theme of [[lawrence-quran-a-biography|Lawrence's *biography* of the Qur'an]] is that the
book lives less as a read text than as a **recited** one — *al-Qur'an* means "the Recitation," and
*aya* means both "verse" and "sign/miracle," so Lawrence calls it **"a Book of Signs."** On this view
the written Arabic is already a "second-order" revelation relative to what Muhammad *heard*, and any
**translation a "third-order" revelation** — the theological root of the long Muslim reticence about
translating the Qur'an at all (a translation is a *tafsir*, an "interpretation," never the Qur'an).

The text's afterlife runs through successive interpretive communities:

- **Earliest material witnesses**: Qur'anic verses inscribed in the **Dome of the Rock** (Jerusalem,
  692) are among the oldest dated Qur'anic texts.
- **The classical commentators**: the Sunni *tafsir bi'l-ma'thur* of [[al-tabari|al-Tabari]] (d. 923),
  set against the Shi'ite Imami *ta'wil* descending from [[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]], and later
  the Sufi esoteric reading of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]] and Rumi.
- **The Latin/Christian reception**: **[[robert-of-ketton|Robert of Ketton]]**'s *Lex Mahumet
  pseudoprophete* (1143), the first complete Latin translation — polemical in title yet dependent on
  Muslim commentaries; printed in 1543 and the channel through which Europe first read the Qur'an.
- **Modern readings**: South Asian rationalist-modernists (Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Muhammad Iqbal),
  African-American Islam (W. D. Mohammed), and militant absolutism (Lawrence reads bin Laden's
  univocal Qur'an as heir to the early Khawarij) — illustrating the standing tension between a
  **plurivocal** (historically reinterpreted) and a **univocal** (timeless, single-meaning) Qur'an.
- **Gender-inclusive (feminist) tafsir**: the reformist current of [[fazlur-rahman|Fazlur Rahman]]'s
  "double movement" and [[amina-wadud|Amina Wadud]]'s [[wadud-quran-and-woman|*Qur'an and Woman*]]
  rereads the text holistically (a "[[tawhid|hermeneutics of tawhid]]") to argue that women's
  inequality is an androcentric *reading* imposed on the Qur'an, not its teaching — the flashpoint
  being [[quran-4-34-male-authority|Q 4:34]] ([[qiwama|qiwama]], *daraba*). See
  [[islamic-feminism|Islamic feminism]].

## Collection and Text History (Bukhari Evidence)

*Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, Vol. 6, Book 61 (Virtues of the Qur'an), Nos. 502–582. M. Muhsin Khan trans.*

### First Collection — Abu Bakr (after Battle of Yamama, 12 AH / 633 CE)

**No. 509** (Zaid bin Thabit, narrated by his son):

> "Abu Bakr sent for me [i.e. Zaid] at the time of the Battle of Yamamah [where many Quran-reciters were killed]. Umar was with him. Abu Bakr said, '…the Quran reciters are being killed in the battles… I suggest you order that the Qur'an be collected.' … I [Zaid] said, 'How will you do something which Allah's Apostle did not do?' Abu Bakr said, 'By Allah, it is a good project.' Umar kept on urging me to accept his proposal till Allah opened my chest for it and I agreed with Umar's opinion."

Zaid collected Quranic material "from parchments, scapula, leaf-stalks of date palms, and from the memories of men." He found the last two verses of Surah Al-Tauba (9:128–129) **only with Khuzaima bin Thabit al-Ansari** and included them.

The compiled codex was given to Abu Bakr, passed at his death to Umar, and at Umar's death to his daughter Hafsa.

### Standardization — Uthman (c. 25 AH / 645 CE)

**No. 510** (Zaid bin Thabit, narrated in the same chain):

Uthman learned that Companions in different regions were reciting with different readings, causing disputes. He sent to Hafsa for the codex, ordered Zaid and three Qurayshi men to copy it. **Key editorial instruction**: "If you (Zaid) differ with Abdur-Rahman bin Hisham, Abdullah bin Az-Zubair and Said bin Al-As in any point in the Qur'an, then write it in the dialect of Quraish as the Qur'an was revealed in their tongue."

Uthman then distributed copies and "ordered that all the other Qur'anic materials, whether written in fragmentary manuscripts or whole copies, be burnt." This is the origin of the standard Uthmanic text.

### The Seven Ahruf (Modes of Recitation)

**No. 513** (Umar bin al-Khattab): Umar heard Hisham bin Hakim reciting Surah al-Furqan in a mode different from what the Prophet had taught him. Umar grabbed Hisham and brought him to the Prophet: "I heard this man reciting Surat Al-Furqan in a way other than the one you taught me." The Prophet said: "Recite it, O Hisham." He recited it and the Prophet said: "Thus has it been revealed." Then Umar recited his version and the Prophet said: "Thus has it also been revealed." Conclusion: **"This Quran has been revealed to be recited in seven different ways (ahruf)."**

**No. 514** (Ubai bin Ka'b): Gabriel taught the Prophet the Quran in one mode; Muhammad requested additional modes for the ease of his community. Each time he asked, Gabriel increased by one, until seven. The Prophet then said: "…all of them are sufficient and adequate."

**Purpose of the Seven Ahruf** (stated explicitly in the hadiths): ease (*takhfif*) for the community.

**No. 582** (Abdullah bin Masud): Warning against division over variant recitations:
> "I saw the Messenger of Allah recite it (a verse) in a certain way, and men taught it in a different way. I was so grieved that I became rough in speech." The Prophet's advice: "Whoever recites [the Qur'an] in the mode he has heard is correct."

### Qira'at Variant — Ibn Masud and Al-Mu'awwidhatain

**No. 560** (Zirr bin Hubaish from Ubai bin Ka'b): A significant canonical controversy:
> "Ibn Masud struck the (two suras of Refuge, i.e. Surah al-Falaq and Surah An-Nas [113–114]) out of his Mushaf, saying, 'These two are not from the Book of Allah.'"
Ubai explicitly contradicted him, asserting the Prophet himself had taught him these two suras. The disagreement is recorded without adjudication; the Uthmanic text includes them. This is one of the clearest instances of a Companion-level textual dispute in the Sunni hadith corpus.

### Order of Revelation

**No. 515** (Aisha): A foundational pedagogical principle about the Quran's composition:
> "The first things that were revealed thereof were the Suras with short verses dealing with Paradise and Hell. When the people came to embrace Islam, the Verses regarding legal and illegal things were revealed."

This establishes a **two-phase canonical logic**: (1) doctrine and eschatology first, to build faith; (2) legal injunctions second, once the community was grounded. Aisha's example: if the first verse had been "do not drink wine," people would never have stopped; if it had been "do not commit adultery," people would never have stopped.

### Virtues of Specific Suras

- **Al-Fatiha** (No. 528): "greatest Surah" (*Umm al-Kitab*, "Mother of the Book")
- **Al-Ikhlas** (Nos. 533–534): equivalent to one-third of the Quran in spiritual weight
- **Ayat al-Kursi** (2:255) and the last two verses of Baqara (Nos. 530, 530a): protection for the night
- **Al-Baqara** (No. 531): The Prophet recommends reciting daily because "al-Shaytan does not come near a house in which Surat al-Baqara is recited"

### Khawarij Identified by Their Recitation

**Nos. 577–578** (Abu Said al-Khudri; Sahl bin Hunaif): A proto-Kharijite group is described:
> "They recite the Quran in a very impressive way but it does not exceed their throats (they have no inner faith)."

The marker of the Khawarij in Bukhari is excessive Quran recitation without inward faith — "they go out of Islam as an arrow darts out of a game."
