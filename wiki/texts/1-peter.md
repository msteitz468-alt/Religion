# The First Letter of Peter

A circular "Diaspora letter" addressed "to the elect, foreigners of the dispersion of Pontus,
Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1:1) — the Christians of northern Asia Minor — written to
encourage believers suffering social hostility for their faith. Often called "the most condensed NT
résumé of the Christian faith and of the conduct it inspires" (Clowney); Luther ranked it among the
"noblest books in the New Testament," on a par with Romans and John. Its governing concept is
Christian identity as **"foreigners and resident aliens"** (*parepidēmoi* and *paroikoi*, 2:11) in a
hostile world, and its governing ethic is that **it is better to suffer than to sin**, following the
pattern of the suffering and vindicated Christ (2:21).

[[karen-jobes|Karen H. Jobes]]'s BECNT commentary (2005) makes three distinctive contributions: (1)
a **new historical theory** — the recipients were converted elsewhere (probably Rome) and *displaced
to Asia Minor by Roman colonization*, rather than evangelized in situ; (2) a sustained reading of 1
Peter against its **Septuagint** matrix (Peter cites the OT in context, not as proof-texts); and (3)
a **syntax-critical** argument that the Greek shows "bilingual interference" consistent with a
Semitic-speaking author — undercutting the standard "Greek too good for a fisherman" objection to
Petrine authorship. See [[karen-jobes|Jobes]], [[peter-apostle|Simon Peter]], and
[[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter: Authorship, Date, and the Colonization Theory]].

## Authorship and Date

The letter claims to be from "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ" (1:1). The modern critical majority
holds it pseudonymous (written c. AD 75–95 by a "Petrine circle" in Rome); Jobes defends authentic
Petrine authorship (or very close to it) and an early date within Peter's lifetime, possibly the
early 50s, possibly the mid-60s before his Neronian martyrdom (c. AD 64–67). She argues the four
classic arguments for pseudonymity are inconclusive because each (the persecution, the church
structure of "elders," the affinity with Paul, the "Babylon" of 5:13) fits *any* point in the first
century, and she contests the assumption that epistolary pseudonymity was an accepted, non-deceptive
literary device for *personal correspondence* (as opposed to wisdom/apocalyptic). The full debate,
including the colonization theory, is at [[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter: Authorship, Date, and the
Colonization Theory]].

## Recipients and Situation

- **Geography**: five regions of northern/central Asia Minor (1:1), most lying outside Paul's
  attested mission field (Paul was forbidden Bithynia, Acts 16:7).
- **Jew or Gentile?** The modern consensus is predominantly Gentile (cf. "the futile way of life
  inherited from your ancestors," 1:18; the vice list of 4:3). Jobes argues it makes little
  difference: Peter addresses them indiscriminately *from within the traditions of biblical Israel*,
  assuming deep familiarity with the LXX — which her colonization theory helps explain (Greek-speaking
  Roman converts steeped in the Greek OT).
- **The suffering**: not official, state-sponsored persecution (no martyrdom-by-policy in view) but
  *sporadic, local, unofficial social hostility* — slander, malicious talk, false accusation, loss of
  honor and standing (1:6; 2:12; 3:16; 4:4, 14–16). The "fiery ordeal" (4:12) is the testing of faith
  (cf. Seneca's *ignis aurum probat*), not Nero's literal fires.

## Genre and Unity

A unified work of **personal correspondence** in the form of a **Diaspora letter** (closest parallel:
the Epistle of Jeremiah; cf. 2 Macc. 1; 2 Bar. 78–87) — written by a recognized spiritual authority
urging a holy way of life on a scattered people. Jobes (with the current consensus) rejects the older
source-critical theories that 1 Peter was a **baptismal homily/paschal liturgy** later framed as a
letter (baptism is mentioned only once, 3:21, and is not discourse-prominent) and that 4:11 marks a
seam between two documents (the doxology punctuates a unit; the persecution does not intensify after
4:12). Its primary source is the **Septuagint**; claims that it embeds pre-formed hymns/creeds
(notably 2:21–25; 3:18–22) are judged unprovable — the material is too thoroughly integrated into
Peter's own thought.

## Structure (Jobes's outline)

- **1:1–2** — Greeting to the Christian Diaspora; the trinitarian shape of election (foreknowledge of
  the Father, sanctifying work of the Spirit, the covenant in Christ's blood)
- **1:3–2:10** — *Reassurance for God's people*: opening doxology (new birth into a living hope);
  joy amid suffering; the Christian's advantage over prophets and angels; "be what you are"; the
  identity of God's people (living stones; "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation," 2:9)
- **2:11–4:11** — *Live godly lives as God's people*: commendable conduct (the household code,
  2:13–3:7); the inner qualities of righteous living; suffering unjustly for the name of Christ
  (including the Christ-hymn of 2:21–25 and the "spirits in prison," 3:18–22)
- **4:12–5:11** — *Consolation for the suffering flock*: the fiery ordeal; final exhortations to
  elders and community; resist the devil
- **5:12–14** — Closing: Silvanus, "Babylon," Mark; "this is the true grace of God — stand fast in it"

## Major Themes and Theology (Jobes)

1. **Foreigners and resident aliens.** The controlling self-understanding: Christians are *resident
   aliens* whose new allegiance to God in Christ marks them off from society. See
   [[christian-exile-diaspora|Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity]].
2. **Suffering for the name.** The destiny of Christ is the destiny of the Christian; a godless world
   rejects believers for the same reasons it rejected Christ. The church is to be an *alternate
   society / colony* of brotherly love.
3. **God.** Theocentric *and* christocentric. Divine **fatherhood** is the controlling concept (God as
   Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, 1:3); God is merciful but the impartial **Judge** of every life
   (1:17); judgment "begins with the household of God" (4:17).
4. **Christ.** The focal point is specifically Jesus' *rejection and suffering unto death*: basis of
   the new covenant, price of redemption, substitutionary sin-bearing (2:24), reconciliation (3:18) —
   and his **resurrection and ascension** as the proclamation of victory over all powers (3:22).
5. **Salvation.** Past (redemption accomplished), present (sanctification), and **future** (salvation
   "ready to be revealed at the last time," 1:5) — genuine faith tested and confirmed through trials.
6. **Eschatology.** Inaugurated; "the end of all things is near" (4:7) functions as urgency, not
   datable timetable; the present suffering is *itself* God's judgment beginning with his house, and
   eschatological glory is present in suffering (the Spirit of glory rests on the insulted, 4:14).
7. **Early, creative theology.** Jobes (with Schlatter, R. P. Martin, Marshall) stresses that 1 Peter
   is *not* a footnote to Paul but independently creative and "antiquated" in the good sense — close
   to the crucifixion. Three distinctive contributions: the **identification of Jesus with the Isaiah
   53 Suffering Servant** (Peter exegetes Jesus' death in light of Isa 53, not vice versa — see
   [[servant-songs|Servant Songs]]); the **spirits in prison / cosmic victory** (3:18–22); and the
   **living stones / spiritual house** imagery (2:4–8).

## Narrative Frameworks and Typology

1 Peter relies heavily on two primary narrative matrices to form the identity of its suffering readers:
1. **The Passion Narrative (Christ as Exemplar)**: In 2:21-25, Peter presents a concentrated narrative sequence of Jesus' trial and crucifixion, modeling his silent endurance of unjust suffering as the template for believers. Drawing heavily on the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53, Christ's refusal to retaliate becomes the ethical paradigm for the persecuted church.
2. **The Enoch-Noah Tradition**: In 3:19-20, Peter utilizes the rich pseudepigraphal tradition of 1 Enoch and Genesis 6 to interpret Christ's ascension. The "spirits in prison" are understood as the rebellious Watchers (fallen angels) from the days of **[[noah|Noah]]**. Christ's proclamation is a declaration of cosmic victory over these rebellious powers. Furthermore, the Flood narrative provides the typological basis for Christian baptism: just as Noah was brought safely through the waters of judgment, baptism saves the believer through the resurrection of Christ. **[[enoch|Enoch]]**'s legacy as the proclaimer of doom to the Watchers is typologically assumed by the ascending Christ.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. Christ and the "spirits in prison"; baptism (3:18–22).** The most disputed passage. Jobes
follows the Dalton–Selwyn line: the three participles (put to death — made alive — *went*) trace
death → resurrection → **ascension**; the resumptive "having gone into heaven" (3:22) shows the
"going and preaching" of 3:19 is Christ's *ascension proclamation of victory*, not a descent into
hell. The "spirits in prison" are the **disobedient fallen angels of Noah's day** (the 1 Enoch
background), not the souls of the dead; the message is a proclamation of doom/victory, not an offer
of salvation. The Noah–baptism typology (3:20–21) is the point: "baptism now saves you" through the
resurrection. Jobes rejects both the *descensus ad inferos* reading and Grudem's "Christ preached
through Noah" view. See [[spirits-in-prison-1-peter-3|Christ and the Spirits in Prison]].

**2. "The gospel preached to the dead" (4:6).** Jobes rejects any link to 3:19 and any
postmortem-second-chance reading. "The dead" = the same group as in 4:5 (all humanity subject to
judgment): those who heard the gospel while alive but are *now dead*. The point: physical death does
not exempt anyone from judgment, nor does it nullify the gospel for those who embraced it. See
[[spirits-in-prison-1-peter-3|the controversy page]].

**3. The household code (2:13–3:7).** Submission "to every human institution" (2:13), then to
masters (slaves, 2:18–25), husbands (wives, 3:1–6), and a reciprocal word to husbands (3:7). Jobes
reads it within the larger question of *Christ and culture*: neither sectarian withdrawal (Elliott)
nor straightforward assimilation (Balch) but a *differentiated, selective* engagement (Volf) —
accommodating, rejecting, subverting, and transforming culture piecemeal. Slaves who suffer unjustly
are *paradigmatic for all Christians* (slaves of God). On wives: Jobes (writing as a woman scholar)
stresses the suffering in view is verbal abuse and loss of standing, *not* domestic violence — the
text neither sanctions abuse nor counsels staying in an abusive marriage, and the instruction to
husbands (3:7) "delicately prohibits" it. See [[1-peter-christ-and-culture|Christ and Culture in 1
Peter]].

**4. "Babylon" (5:13).** Jobes argues "Babylon" = Rome, but *not* as the apocalyptic code-word of
Revelation (1 Peter is not apocalyptic and is notably *positive* toward the state, 2:13–17). It
functions as the closing *inclusio* of the letter's Diaspora/exile frame (Rome could be called
"Babylon" from 63 BC onward), and may also veil Peter's location.

## Relation to Other Books

- **Paul (esp. Romans, Ephesians)** — real affinities, but Jobes (with most) attributes them to
  shared early Christian tradition (perhaps Roman), not literary dependence; 1 Peter is no
  "deutero-Pauline" derivative.
- **Isaiah 53** — the substructure of Peter's Christology (2:21–25). See [[servant-songs|Servant
  Songs]].
- **The Johannine Epistles / Revelation** — also addressed to Asia Minor late in the century, but
  with very different (heresy-conscious, anti-Roman) tone — a foil that, for Jobes, points to 1
  Peter's earlier date.
- **2 Peter** — shares the apostolic name but is widely held to be by a different hand; not covered
  here.
