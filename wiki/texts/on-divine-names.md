# On the Divine Names (*Peri Theion Onomaton*)

**On the Divine Names** is the longest and most systematic treatise of [[pseudo-dionysius|
Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]], addressed to "Timothy." Its purpose is to expound the
various names applied to God in Scripture — not as descriptions of the divine essence
(which is beyond all names) but as expressions of God's "beneficent progressions" (*proodoi*)
outward to creation. The work is the primary vehicle for Dionysian cataphatic theology and
the treatment of evil as privation.

Parker's 1897 translation covers thirteen chapters (*capita*) with multiple sections each.

## Structure and Contents

### Caput I — Purpose and Method; Superessential Theology

Caput I establishes the governing rule of the treatise: divine names may be explored only from
"those things divinely revealed to us in the sacred Oracles," never from unaided human wisdom.
The two poles are set immediately: God is both "Nameless" and "Many-Named." Nameless because
"the superessential Illimitability is placed above things essential," and the divine essence is
"inconceivable to all conceptions"; Many-Named because all positive names — Good, Beautiful, Wise,
Being, Life, Power — are drawn from God's "beneficent progressions" (*proodoi*) outward to
creation, not from the essence itself. The key term introduced here is *Agnosia* — "supra-knowledge"
of the divine superessentiality, which "surpasses every expression and every knowledge" (Section I).
Section IV describes the eschatological hope: when "incorruptible and immortal," we shall be
"fulfilled, through all-pure contemplations, with the visible manifestation of God covering us with
glory, in most brilliant splendours, as the disciples in the most Divine Transfiguration." Section VI
surveys the range of scriptural names — He is "He, Who is — the Life — the Light — the God — the
Truth" and simultaneously "in a gentle breeze... in minds... in souls... in bodies... not one of
things existing." Section VII articulates the regulative principle: all things "are around It and
for It," and It is "the 'all in all'" — sustaining, directing, and perfecting the whole.

Parker's framing (Preface): he treats the work as authentic 1st-century apostolic text dedicated by
Dionysius to "Timothy" (identified with Marcellus of Toledo, renamed Timothy by Dionysius). He
defends the Greek philosophical vocabulary as a tool, not an authority: "Plato and Aristotle are not
authorities here." Luther's charge ("Platonising, rather than Christianising") is dismissed as
reflecting "a very meagre acquaintance with Dionysius."

### Caput II — Divine Union and Distinction; the Trinity

Caput II argues that every divine Name applies to the *whole* Godhead — not to Father alone, or Son
alone, but collectively, "not partitively, but as applied to the whole and entire and complete and
full Godhead" (Section I). The Good, Being, Life, Light, Lordship are all sung of the entire Trinity.
Sections IV–V distinguish "Divine Unions" (the hidden, unrevealed superessential sublimities) from
"Divine Distinctions" (the goodly progressions and manifestations outward). The central illustration
is lamps in a room: their lights "are both wholly distinct in each other throughout, and keep the
distinction from each other specifically and perfectly maintained, being one in distinction and
distinct in union" (Section IV). The Trinitarian persons share all common names but are distinguished
by their incommunicable properties: "The Father is sole Fountain of the superessential Deity, since
the Father is not Son, nor the Son, Father" (Section V). Sections IX–X quote Hierotheus's
*Theological Elements* on the mystery of the Incarnation: the Word is "super-natural in things
natural... super-substantial in things substantial," and "the newest of all new things" who became
man without alteration. The deification of human beings is stated here: "the godlike become both
gods, and sons of gods, and fathers of gods," accomplished "spiritually, i.e. incorporeally,
immaterially, intellectually" (Section VIII).

### Caput III — Prayer; Hierotheus; the Analogy of the Ship and Rock

Caput III opens with instruction on prayer: we do not draw God down to us but elevate ourselves
toward God, "as if a luminous chain being suspended from the celestial heights, and reaching down
hither, we, by ever clutching this upwards, first with one hand, and then with the other" (Section I).
The ship-and-rock analogy makes the same point: "we do not draw the rock to us, but ourselves, in
fact, and the ship, to the rock." Prayer is "conducting ourselves to, and making ourselves one with"
the divine presence. Section II–III describes Hierotheus, Dionysius's chief teacher after Paul, whose
*Theological Elements* are so exalted that Dionysius treats them as "certain second Oracles, and next
to the Anointed of God." The present treatise exists to expand and make accessible Hierotheus's
compressed formulations. Section III gives an account of Hierotheus's behavior at the Dormition of
the Virgin — her body described as the "Life-springing and God-receptive body" — where Hierotheus
was "wholly entranced, wholly raised from himself, and experiencing the pain of his fellowship with
the things celebrated, and was regarded as an inspired and divine Psalmist." The witnesses include
"James, the brother of God, and Peter, the foremost and most honoured pinnacle of the Theologians" —
Parker reading this as historical documentation of apostolic-era authorship.

### Caput IV — Good, Light, Beauty, Love, Ecstasy, Jealousy; Evil as Non-Being

Caput IV is the treatise's most expansive chapter, covering the cluster of names around the Good and
its implications for the nature of evil.

**The Good**: God as the Good is compared to the sun — "not as calculating or choosing, but by its
very being, enlightens all things able to partake of its light in their own degree" (Section I). The
Good is cause of angels' immortality, souls' indestructibility, the life of all creatures including
irrational animals and plants, and even the being of inorganic matter. "Even the non-existing itself
aspires to the Good above all things existing" (Section III).

**Light**: God is celebrated as spiritual Light, filling "every supercelestial mind with spiritual
light" and expelling "all ignorance and error from all souls" (Section V). The sun is "a brilliant
likeness of the Divine Goodness... a most distant echo of the Good" (Section IV). The metaphor of
light "gathering and turning to itself all things existing" mirrors the Good's power of attraction.

**Beauty**: The Good is identical with the Beautiful. Beauty is "Cause of all beautiful things,"
their "adaptations, friendships, and inter-communions"; it is that which "calls (καλοῦν) all things
to Itself (whence also it is called Beauty) (κάλλος), and as collecting all in all to Itself"
(Section VII). Beauty is the exemplary, creative, and final cause of all existence simultaneously.

**Three movements**: Divine minds (angels) move circularly (united to the Beautiful and Good without
beginning or end), directly (advancing to succour subordinates), and spirally (remaining fixed in
identity while providing). Souls move circularly (unified convolution of intellectual powers into the
One), spirally (illuminated discursively through logical steps), and in a straight line (conducted
from multiplied external symbols to unified contemplation) (Sections VIII–IX).

**Love/Eros**: Divine Love (*eros*) "is extatic, not permitting (any) to be lovers of themselves,
but of those beloved" (Section XIII). Paul's "I live no longer, but Christ lives in me" (Gal. 2:20)
illustrates ecstatic love. Even the divine Author "by the beautiful and good love of everything,
through an overflow of His loving goodness, becomes out of Himself, by His providences for all
existing things" (Section XIII). God is called "Jealous" because of this vast love. Love moves in
an eternal circle: "from the Good, from the Good, and in the Good, and to the Good, and ever
advancing and remaining and returning in the same and throughout the same" (Section XIV). Sections
XV–XVII excerpt Hierotheus's *Hymns of Love*, defining love as "a certain unifying and combining
power, moving the superior to forethought for the inferior, and the equals to a mutual fellowship."

**Evil as Non-Being**: Sections XIX–XXXV constitute the most sustained Dionysian treatment of evil.
The argument proceeds negatively: evil is not from the Good; is not in things existing; is not in
God, not in Angels, not in demons by nature, not in souls, not in irrational creatures, not in
bodies, not in matter. The governing principle: "The Evil qua evil makes no single essence or birth,
but only, as far as it can, pollutes and destroys the subsistence of things existing" (Section XX).
Evil has a "parasitical subsistence, coming into being for the sake of the Good, and not of itself"
(Section XXXI). The positive formulation: "all things which are, in so far as they are, both are
good, and from the Good; but, in so far as they are deprived of the Good, are neither good, nor do
they exist" (Section XXVII). Demons are evil "by reason of their weakness as regards their action
according to nature," specifically by "a turning aside and a stepping out of things befitting
themselves, and a missing of aim, and imperfection and impotence" (Section XXIII). The comprehensive
definition: "The Evil is privation and failure, and want of strength, and want of proportion, and
want of attainment, and want of purpose; and without beauty, and without life, and without mind,
and without reason, and without completeness, and without stability, and without cause, and without
limit, and without production" (Section XXXII). See [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation]].

### Caput V — Being (*to On*); Divine Being as Cause of All Essences

Caput V celebrates God as "He, Who veritably is" — the superessential, sustaining Cause of all
potential being. The key claim is ontological priority: God is "not relatively a Being, but
absolutely and unboundedly, having comprehended and anticipated the whole Being in Himself" (Section
IV). As "King of the ages," all being "is, and is sustained, in Him and around Him." The chapter
also treats the *exemplars* (Platonic forms reconceived): they are "the methods in God, giving
essence to things that be, and pre-existing uniformly," which theology calls "predeterminations, and
Divine and good wills, which define and produce things existing" (Section VIII). The analogy of the
monad (all numbers pre-exist within it unitarily) and the circle-center (all radii coexist in the
center, united to each other, diverging only as they move outward) illustrate how God holds all
things in superessential unity (Section VI). Everything — life, wisdom, similitude, order, union —
first participates in Being before being a source of anything; God "pre-possessing even pre-existence
and super-existence, and super-possessing being, pre-established all being" (Section V).

### Caput VI — Life

Caput VI celebrates God as the Eternal Life "from which comes the self-existing Life, and every
life." The angelic "immortal immortality" is sustained entirely from this divine Life; even the
immortality of the angels is not from themselves but "from the vivifying Cause forming and sustaining
all life" (Section I). The life of demons is likewise sustained by the same source. The chapter
announces the promise of bodily resurrection: God "promises to transfer even our whole selves —
souls, and bodies their yoke-fellows — to a perfect life and immortality" (Section II), dismissing
objections from "Antiquity" as failing to reckon with "the all-powerful nature of the Divine Life."
The divine Life is "super-living, and life-springing... Cause both of all life, and is generative,
and completive, and dividing of life" (Section III) — to be celebrated from every form of life.

### Caput VII — Wisdom, Mind, Word, Truth, Faith

Caput VII celebrates God as above all wisdom, Mind, Logos, Truth, and Faith. God is "superfull of
wisdom, and of His understanding there is no number, but He is fixed above all reason and mind and
wisdom" (Section I). Paul's "foolishness of God is wiser than men" (1 Cor. 1:25) is read not merely
as divine superiority but as an instance of the theological practice of denying privation-terms in
an elevated sense. God's knowledge is not derived from things but precedes them: "by knowing Itself,
will know all things... not as learning existing things from existing things, does the Divine Mind
know, but from Itself, and in Itself, as Cause, it pre-holds and pre-comprehends the notion and
knowledge, and essence of all things" (Section II). The highest mode of knowing God is through
*agnosia*: "the most Divine Knowledge of Almighty God, which is known, through not knowing (ἀγνωσία)
during the union above mind; when the mind, having stood apart from all existing things, then having
dismissed also itself, has been made one with the super-luminous rays" (Section III). God as *Logos*
(Section IV): not merely provider of reason and mind but the One who "anticipated the causes of all,
solitarily in Himself, and because He passes through all, as the Oracles say, even to the end of
all things." Faith is "the enduring foundation of the believers, establishing them in the truth, and
the truth in them, by an unchangeable identity." The *Logos* and *Faith* together secure the
"unmoved, unchangeable identity" of those united to divine Truth.

### Caput VIII — Power, Righteousness, Salvation, Redemption; Inequality of Powers

Caput VIII celebrates God as Power, Justice, Preservation, and Redemption. God pre-has "every power
in Himself" and "produces everything as beseems a Power inflexible and unencompassed" (Section II),
distributing power to all beings without any single being utterly deprived. The chapter engages
polemically with "Elymas the Magician" (Simon Magus) who objected that God's omnipotence conflicts
with Paul's statement that God "is not able to deny Himself" (2 Tim. 2:13) — Dionysius resolves
this by noting that denial of truth is "a falling from the existent," and God, being Truth, cannot
fall from Himself. God as Justice distributes "things suitable to all, both due measure, and beauty,
and good order, and arrangement" to each being according to its nature (Section VII); it is not a
failure of justice when pious persons suffer, because divine Justice does not "pamper and destroy the
bravery of the best by the gifts of earthly things" (Section VIII). Justice as Preservation guards
"the essence and order of each, distinct and pure from the rest" (Section IX). Justice as Redemption
does not permit "things really being to fall away to non-existence," raises from evil, fills up
"the leaking good," and arranges "disorder and deformity" — it is the ontological ground of God's
providential care (Section IX).

### Caput IX — Great, Small; Same, Other; Like, Unlike; Rest, Motion; Equality

Caput IX examines pairs of apparently contrary predicates that the Oracles apply to God, resolving
the apparent contradiction by reading each predicate in the supereminent mode appropriate to the
Cause of all. God as **Great**: greatness "overflows, and extends itself outside of all greatness...
an infinite and without measure and without number" (Section II). God as **Little/Fine**: "leaves
behind every mass and distance, and penetrates through all, without hindrance... piercing through to
a dividing of soul and spirit, and joints and marrow" (echoing Heb. 4:12) (Section III). God as
**Same**: "superessentially everlasting, inconvertible, abiding in itself, always being in the same
condition and manner; present to all in the same manner" (Section IV). God as **Different**: present
to all providentially while remaining self-identical; the manifold shapes of divine visions signify
"certain things are different from the phenomena under which they appear" — a hermeneutical
principle for symbolic theology (Section V). God as **Similar and Dissimilar** simultaneously:
creatures are similar to God through participation ("the received imitation of the inimitable") but
God remains "similar to none" as Cause surpassing caused (Sections VI–VII). God as **Standing and
Moving**: "remains Himself, in Himself, and is abidingly fixed in unmoved identity" (Standing,
Section VIII); yet "brings into being and sustains everything, and provides in every way for
everything" (Moving). The three kinds of divine movement — straight, curvative, circular — signify
"the unswerving and the undeviating progression," the "steady progression and the productive
condition," and "the turning to Him of the things which proceeded from Him" (Section IX).

### Caput X — Omnipotent; Ancient of Days; Eternity and Time

Caput X addresses two pairs: Sovereign Lord / Ancient of Days, and Eternity / Time. God as
**Sovereign Lord** (*Pantokrator*): "an all-controlling basis, binding and embracing the whole,
and establishing and supporting, and tightening, and completing the whole" (Section I); also desired
and beloved by all, "placing upon all the voluntary yokes, and the sweet pangs of the Divine and
Sovereign, and indissoluble love of the Goodness itself." God as **Ancient of Days** (Dan. 7:9):
because "He is of all things both Age and Time — and before Days, and before Age and Time" (Section
II). The mystic visions show God as both old (the "Ancient" from the beginning) and young (never
growing old). On **Eternity and Time** (Section III): the Oracles use both terms flexibly —
sometimes "eternal" means merely imperishable or unchangeable, sometimes "time" refers to the
spiritual. The true distinction: "things spiritual are spoken of and denoted by Eternity, and things
subject to generation by time." God is above both, and is to be celebrated as "Author of every time
and eternity... Ancient of days, as before time, and above time," whose "kingdom, a kingdom of all
the Ages."

### Caput XI — Peace; Being Itself, Life Itself, Power Itself

Caput XI opens by celebrating God as **Divine Peace** — that which "unifies all, and engenders, and
effects the agreement and fellowship of all" (Section I). Peace preserves the "idiosyncrasy" of each
being — its own proper distinctiveness — without confusion, while at the same time binding all into
one harmonious unity: "making all things of one family, by the unities, the identities, the unions,
the conjunctions of the Divine Peace" (Section II). Even beings that appear to delight in strife and
division are shown to be seeking an obscure image of peace — the gratification of desire sought as
the pacification of tumult (Section V). Christ's "peaceful stream of love towards man" reconciles
"us to Himself, in Spirit, and through Himself and in Himself to the Father" (Section V).

Section VI addresses the terminological distinction between God as "self-existent Life" and as
"Mainstay of the self-existent Life." The resolution: God is named from things existing as Cause
("self-existent Life"), and named as above all existing things as the Superessential ("Mainstay of
the self-existent Life"). The providential powers that issue forth from God — the self-existent
essentiation, living, deification — are the participable forms through which existing things
participate in being, life, and God. The Cause itself remains unparticipating.

### Caput XII — Holy of Holies; King of Kings; Lord of Lords; God of Gods

Caput XII explains the biblical superlatives (Dan. 7:9; Rev. 17:14; 19:16). Holiness is defined as
"purity free from every pollution, and all perfect, and altogether unstained"; Kingdom as "the
assignment of every limit and order, and ordinance and rank"; Lordship as "the perfect possession,
in every respect, of the Beautiful and Good; and a true and unswerving stability"; Deity as "the
Providence watching over all, and with perfect goodness both circumscribing and grasping all"
(Section II). God surpasses even these attributes in the superlative form: as "Cause surpassing all"
It is "above Holiness, and Lordship, and Kingdom, and most simplex Deity" (Section III). The
duplication of names (Holy *of Holies*, etc.) signals the ontological difference between God and the
highest creatures who bear these names: the higher angelic orders are themselves called "holy" or
"kings" or "gods," but as channels through which inferior ranks receive divine gifts (Section IV).

### Caput XIII — Perfect and One

Caput XIII is the culminating chapter, addressing the "most difficult subject in the whole discourse."
God as **Perfect**: "not only as self-perfect... but also as super-perfect, as beseems His
pre-eminence over all, and limiting every infinitude, and surpassing every term, and by none contained
or comprehended; but even extending at once to all, and above all" (Section I). God as **One**:
"uniquely all, as beseems an excess of unique Oneness, and is Cause of all without departing from
the One" (Section II). The numerical analogies are exhausted: everything participates in the one —
the many-by-parts is one in the whole; the many-by-species is one in genus; the many-by-progressions
is one by source. Yet God is "not One, as one of many, but before every one and multitude, and
determinative of every one and multitude" (Section II).

The chapter culminates in apophatic transcendence of all names: "Whilst celebrated as Unit and Triad,
the Deity above all is neither Unit nor Triad, as understood by us or by any other sort of being...
the hiddenness above every expression and every mind... Nor has It a Name, or expression, but is
elevated above in the inaccessible" (Section III). The theologians "have given the preference to the
ascent through negations, as lifting the soul out of things kindred to itself... and at the furthest
extremity attaching it to Him" — the transition to the *Mystical Theology* is explicitly announced:
"I will now pass to the Symbolic Theology, with God for my Guide" (Section IV).

## Key Theological Positions

1. **Cataphatic names describe progressions, not the essence.** All divine names drawn from Scripture
   refer to God's "beneficent progressions" (*proodoi*) outward to creation — God's effects, not the
   divine superessentiality itself, which is beyond all names and conceptions.

2. **God is Nameless and Many-Named simultaneously.** The apophatic and cataphatic are not
   alternatives but two sides of the same epistemological situation: the divine essence is
   unspeakable; the divine energies are knowable under many names.

3. **The Good is identical with the Beautiful.** Both function as the final, exemplary, and
   efficient cause of all things; "all things aspire to the Beautiful and Good, on every account,
   and there is no existing thing which does not participate in the Beautiful and the Good" (IV.VII).

4. **Love (eros) is ecstatic and circular.** Divine Love moves outward from the Good to creation and
   returns — "an everlasting circle whirling round in unerring combination, by reason of the Good,
   from the Good, and in the Good, and to the Good" (IV.XIV). It is ecstatic because it carries the
   lover out of self into the beloved.

5. **Evil is pure privation — non-being.** Evil has no substance, no power, no nature of its own;
   it is a "parasitical subsistence" dependent on the Good it lacks. Everything that *is*, in so far
   as it *is*, is good and from the Good.

6. **Hierarchical mediation.** Divine gifts pass from God through graded intermediaries (angels,
   souls, bodies), each receiving and transmitting according to its capacity. This principle
   structures both the angelic hierarchy (treated in [[celestial-hierarchy|Celestial Hierarchy]])
   and the analysis of divine names (each name corresponds to a mode of providential outflow).

7. **Deification (*theosis*) as goal.** Human beings are called to participate in the divine
   nature through a progressive ascent — purification, illumination, unification — culminating in
   "wholly of God, and not of ourselves" (VII.I). See [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]].

## Relation to Neoplatonism

The text itself is aware of the overlap with Platonic/Neoplatonic vocabulary and addresses it
explicitly. Parker's preface argues that Jahn's comparative study of Plato and Dionysius was
undertaken "for the purpose of exploding the puerile supposition that such complex writings as these
could have been evolved from the elementary treatises of Proclus and Plotinus. Most probably, some
of the lost writings of Dionysius are in part preserved in those writers and in Clement of Alexandria;
but Dionysius is the Master, not Pupil." Parker thus inverts the standard scholarly judgment
(Proclus as source → Dionysius) and claims priority for Dionysius.

The structural parallels to Neoplatonism embedded in the text include:

- **Procession and return (*proodoi* / *epistrophe*)**: the entire framework of divine names as
  "beneficent progressions" outward, and Love as the return-movement of all things to the Good,
  maps onto the Neoplatonic triad of Remaining–Procession–Return (Monē–Proodoi–Epistrophē).
- **The One beyond Being**: God as "before every one and multitude, and determinative of every one
  and multitude" (XIII.II) parallels Plotinus's *One* that surpasses *Being* (Nous).
- **The Beautiful as First Principle**: Caput IV's identification of the Beautiful with the Good
  as the ultimate cause and object of all desire echoes Plato's *Symposium* (Diotima's ladder) and
  Plotinus (*Enneads* I.6, "On Beauty").
- **Exemplars**: Caput V's treatment of divine "predeterminations" as exemplars (*paradeigmata*)
  pre-existing in God recalls Platonic Forms, but reconceived as God's own "good wills" rather than
  eternal archetypes external to God.
- Parker explicitly refutes the Platonic doctrine of Exemplars: "on the question of Exemplars and
  some other points, the opinions of Plato are expressly refuted" (Preface), pointing to Caput V's
  reworking of the concept within a Christian theistic framework.

## Reception and Influence

Parker's 1897 preface notes: (1) the text was known in Alexandria by 250 CE (Dionysius of
Alexandria's scholia); (2) parallels with Sankara and Rāmānuja's Vedāntic distinctions (Brahman as
"absolutely One" vs. "non-dual with qualification") may reflect transmission through Pantaenus's
mission to India ca. 193 CE. Parker explicitly hopes the *Divine Names* will be "instrumental in
bringing India to the Christian Faith." These are Parker's claims, not endorsed by modern scholarship.

The modern scholarly consensus dates the corpus to ca. 485–530 CE and identifies heavy dependence
on Proclus's *Elements of Theology* and *Platonic Theology* (a position the text's own preface
reverses). The work's reception includes: citation at the Second Council of Nicaea (787); extensive
use by [[thomas-aquinas|Thomas Aquinas]] in the *Summa Theologiae* (especially on divine names and
the five ways); foundational influence on Byzantine theology via Maximus the Confessor and John of
Damascus; and significant influence on Meister Eckhart and the German mystical tradition. The
[[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]] is typically read as the apophatic complement to the
cataphatic project of the *Divine Names*.

## Rolt's Translation and Reading (1920)

C. E. Rolt's *[[rolt-dionysius|On the Divine Names and the Mystical Theology]]* (SPCK, 1920)
provides a second complete English translation of all thirteen chapters, in notably more fluent
prose than Parker's. The chapter scheme and doctrine are identical to Parker's text above; Rolt's
distinctive renderings include **"the Undifferencing and the Differentiation in Divinity"**
(Ch. II; Parker's "Unions and Distinctions"), **"Very Being," "Very Life," "Very Power"** for the
*autoeinai / autozōē* group (Ch. XI), and **"Outlines of Divinity"** for the *Theological
Outlines*.

Rolt's Introduction reads the treatise philosophically: the divine Names apply to the Godhead
*as manifested* — "God is but an Appearance of the Absolute" (a formula Rolt borrows from
F. H. Bradley) — while the **Super-Essence** (= the **Supra-Personal Absolute**) is beyond all
names. He reconstructs the *proodoi* of the Names into a metaphysics of **two emanating streams**
(the Universal Stream of "Very Being/Life," and the Particular Stream identified with
Non-entity), each creature holding "two sides to its existence," one in the Super-Essence and one
in itself. On Caput IV he restates [[evil-as-privation|evil as non-being]] (his "zero in
mathematics" image) but faults Dionysius for under-emphasizing evil's ugliness and "the Cross of
Christ." See [[rolt-dionysius|Rolt's edition]] for the full reconstruction and for
Sparrow-Simpson's reception history.

## Canon and Reception

**On the Divine Names** is not scripture in any tradition. It functions as a patristic
theological authority:
- **Authoritative for**: Catholic and Eastern Orthodox theology (cited at Council of Nicaea
  787; extensively by Thomas Aquinas; foundational for Byzantine theology via Maximus Confessor)
- **Disputed by**: Protestant tradition; Luther characterized the corpus as "Platonising
  rather than Christianising"
- **Authorship controversy**: See [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius]] for the dispute over
  dating and attribution
