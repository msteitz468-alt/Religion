# Tattvartha Sutra

The *Tattvartha Sutra* (also known as *Tattvartha-dhigama Sutra*; abbreviated *TS*) is the foundational philosophical treatise of Jainism, composed by **Umasvati** (also spelled Umasvami). It is the only Jain text accepted as authoritative by both the [[digambara|Digambara]] and [[svetambara|Svetambara]] communities, making it the closest thing to a pan-Jain philosophical canon.

## Canon and Reception

The *Tattvartha Sutra* occupies a unique position in Jain canonical history. Dundas cites it throughout his "Doctrine" chapter (*TS* 1.4, 2.41–3, 5.17, 5.19–20, 6.1–2, 6.23, 8.1, 10, 10.5, and 23.25) as the authoritative source for the systematisation of Jain ontology — the [[jiva]], [[ajiva]], the *tattvas*, karma theory, rebirth, and liberation. It is cited alongside Digambara commentators (Akalanka's *TSRV*) and in contexts that presuppose cross-community acceptance.

The fact that both Digambara and Svetambara communities accept it is not without intra-tradition nuance: there is a Digambara recension and a Svetambara recension with minor textual differences (not in Dundas's account of this chapter). The text is cited as *TS* in the Digambara commentary tradition (*TSRV* = *Tattvartha-sloka-vartika* by Akalanka, and Dundas also cites *TSRV* by the commentator Siddhasena Gani in Svetambara context).

There is a sectarian dispute noted by Dundas specifically regarding the kevalin's experience of "feeling karma": the Digambaras denied that a kevalin's feeling karma could bring about an effect such as hunger (lines 687–688), a position that involves a reading of the *Tattvartha Sutra*'s doctrine of the non-harming karmas.

## Umasvati

Umasvati is described by Dundas as drawing "on canonical sources" (*TS* 6.1–2, lines 420–421). The *Tattvartha Sutra* represents a pivotal moment of systematisation: "the 'Exposition of Explanations', which probably represents a transitional stage leading to the formulation of the classical Jain teachings as represented by Umasvati" (lines 467–469). The date of Umasvati is disputed (not in Dundas's account of this chapter).

## Content Overview

Dundas cites the *Tattvartha Sutra* in the following thematic areas (all references are to Dundas's Chapter 4 slice):

### The *Tattvas* (Real Categories)
Umasvati "presumably subsumes meritorious and morally negative action under either *asrava* or the binding of karma within his list which contains only **seven reals** (*TS* 1.4)" (lines 373–374), compared with the more common nine-*tattva* scheme.

### The *Ajiva* Entities
- *Dharma* as motion-medium: *TSRV* on *TS* 5.17 (lines 320–322)
- *Pudgala* providing the bodily dimension for the *jiva*: *TS* 5.19–20 (lines 329–331)
- Space: *TSRV* p. 477 (line 338)

### Karma Theory
- Physical, mental and verbal activity (*yoga*) as cause of karmic influx: *TS* 6.1–2 (line 421)
- The five causes of karma-binding (false belief, lack of discipline, carelessness, passions, *yoga*): *TS* 8.1 (lines 424–426)
- *Tirthankara*-name karma as arising through exceptional past circumstances: *TS* 6.23 (line 520)

### Rebirth and Gestation
- Subtle and luminous bodies facilitating rebirth: *TS* 2.41–3 (line 641)
- Types of birth: *TS* 23.25 (line 671)

### Deliverance
- *Moksha* defined simply as "release from all karma": *TS* 10.5 (lines 675–676)
- Full account of liberation and the *siddha* realm: *TS* 10 (line 719)

## Hermeneutical Context

The *Tattvartha Sutra*'s style is that of a concise philosophical *sutra* drawing on the Svetambara canonical corpus and systematising it in Sanskrit — a form that enabled cross-sectarian acceptance. The commentary tradition on the text is substantial: Akalanka's *TSRV* (*Tattvartha-sloka-vartika*) is the principal Digambara commentary cited by Dundas. Umasvati's own *bhashya* (commentary) is also attested (not discussed in this chapter's slice).

The text is associated with the tradition of embedding Jain doctrine within standard logical procedures: "later medieval writers attempted to demonstrate [the omniscient teachings embodied in authoritative scripture were] in full conformity with standard logical procedures" (lines 65–68) — the *Tattvartha Sutra* belongs to this rationalising strand.
