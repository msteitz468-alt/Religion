# Letters of John (1, 2, 3 John)

Three letters associated with the Johannine tradition, likely written from or around Ephesus in the Roman province of Asia. The author identifies himself only as "the elder" in 2 and 3 John; 1 John is anonymous. All three letters address a community or group of communities in theological and ecclesiastical crisis.

## Overview

**1 John** (5 chapters) is not formally a letter — it lacks an opening greeting and an address — and functions more as a homily or circular pastoral address. It is the longest and theologically densest of the three. Its stated purpose: "I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God so that you may know that you have eternal life" (5:13). The governing concern is assurance — assuring loyal believers, destabilized by the claims of the secessionists, that they are genuine Christians.

**2 John** (13 verses; one of the two shortest NT books) is a real letter to a local church (addressed metaphorically as "the chosen lady and her children"). It warns against extending hospitality to itinerant teachers who carry the secessionist christological denial.

**3 John** (14 verses; the shortest NT book) is a real letter to an individual, Gaius, praising his hospitality to legitimate traveling preachers and denouncing a local leader, Diotrephes, who has resisted the elder's authority and refused to receive the missionaries.

## Authorship and Historical Setting

The author refers to himself as "the elder" (ho presbyteros). Early Christian tradition (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Dionysius of Alexandria, Jerome, Papias as cited by Eusebius) unanimously ascribes 1 John to John the apostle, the son of Zebedee, and regarded the same John as "the elder." Eusebius, noting Papias's reference to both "John" (in the apostolic list) and "the elder John" (separately), suggested that the latter — distinct from the apostle — may have been the author of 2 and 3 John and perhaps Revelation.

Three positions on authorship:

1. **Apostle John**: the author is John the son of Zebedee, known also as "the elder" due to his age and revered status. Supported by the strongest early tradition and by the claim to eyewitness status in 1:1–4, which [[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]] and [[colin-kruse|Kruse]] both take as literal. Kruse: the eyewitness language of 1:1–4 (hearing, seeing, touching "the Word of life") is so strong that "spiritual" or "representative" alternatives (Bultmann, Brown, Schnackenburg) do not do justice to the text.

2. **John the Elder** (Papias's second John): a venerated figure associated with the Johannine tradition, distinct from the apostle, perhaps a disciple of the apostle. Supported by the Papias fragment and by scholars who find the difference in style and perspective between Gospel and Epistles too significant.

3. **Johannine school**: the letters emerged from a circle of writers sharing a common tradition rooted ultimately in the apostle's authority. Adopted by Brown, Schnackenburg in his later work, and others unwilling to assign direct authorship to the apostle.

Both ingested commentaries favor the apostolic identification or at minimum accept the eyewitness language at face value. [[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]] (NICNT, 1978): probably John the apostle known as "the elder," or a close associate; the Revelation was written by a different John. [[colin-kruse|Kruse]] (PNTC, 2020): apostolic authorship is the fairest reading of the internal evidence; the Papias problem is real but not decisive.

**Relation to the Gospel of John**: strikingly close in vocabulary and theology. Both Kruse and Marshall favor common authorship or at least very close proximity. Kruse's working hypothesis: the Gospel existed in early form before the Epistles; the secession prompted 1 John, followed by 2 and 3 John. The Epistles often apply Johannine Gospel concepts differently — notably *paraklētos* (in the Gospel = the Holy Spirit; in 1 John 2:1 = Jesus as heavenly advocate) — requiring care in cross-referencing.

**Location**: Asia Minor, almost certainly in or around Ephesus. Consistent with the tradition connecting John with Ephesus.

## The Secessionist Crisis

Both letters 1 and 2 John are written in response to a schism. A group of believers had left the author's community (1 John 2:19: "they went out from us, but they did not belong to us") and were now actively trying to win remaining members over to their position through itinerant preaching (1 John 2:26; 4:1–3; 2 John 7). The author calls these people **antichrists** (see [[antichrist]]).

For the detailed dispute about the secessionists' identity and teaching, see [[johannine-secessionists|The Johannine Secessionist Crisis]].

Summary of secessionist teaching as reconstructed from the author's rebuttals:

- **Christological denial**: they denied that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, come in the flesh (2:22; 4:2–3; 2 John 7). This is the decisive, irreconcilable point.
- **Denial of the atoning blood**: they denied that Jesus came "by blood" — that his death was salvifically necessary (5:6). This is the anti-Cerinthian emphasis.
- **Sinlessness claims**: they claimed to be without sin and to have fellowship with God (1:6, 8, 10) while not keeping his commands.
- **Lack of brotherly love**: they did not show love to the believing community they had left (2:9–11; 3:14–15; 4:20).
- **Spirit claims**: they appealed to spiritual revelation as authorizing their "advanced" teaching (4:1; 2:20, 27).

## Major Themes

**Right belief about Jesus**: The entire corpus is saturated with Christological insistence. Jesus Christ has "come in the flesh" (4:2; 2 John 7); he came "by water and blood" (5:6); he is "the atoning sacrifice [*hilasmos*] for our sins" (2:2; 4:10); he is the Son of God (5:5). Correct Christology is not optional — it is the dividing line between true and false Christianity.

**Hilasmos (propitiation/expiation)**: 1 John 2:2 ("he is the *hilasmos* for our sins, and not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world") and 4:10 ("he loved us and sent his Son as the *hilasmos* for our sins"). The translation is debated: "propitiation" (turning away divine wrath; Marshall, Kruse, Stott, Morris) vs. "expiation" (cleansing sin; Dodd, RSV). Both ingested commentaries prefer propitiation.

**Love for fellow believers**: The love command is "from the beginning" (2:7; 2 John 4–6) and is the mark distinguishing true Christians from those claiming fellowship with God while hating their brothers. The standard is Christological: "we ought to lay down our lives for our brothers and sisters" (3:16).

**Assurance of eternal life**: The primary pastoral purpose of 1 John. The author is not encouraging self-testing to see if one qualifies, but assuring genuinely believing people that they already have eternal life. The "tests of life" (Robert Law's classic title) function positively — as grounds for assurance — not as a qualifying examination.

**God as light and love**: The two great theological declarations: "God is light; in him there is no darkness at all" (1:5) and "God is love" (4:8, 16). Both have ethical consequences: those in fellowship with light must walk in light; those who know love must love one another.

**The Spirit as witness**: The Spirit is given as assurance of God's presence (3:24; 4:13) and as the means of recognizing truth (4:6). Testing the spirits (4:1–6): the confession "Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" is the criterion.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. The sinlessness paradox**: 1:8, 10 say "if we claim to be without sin, we deceive ourselves" and "if we claim we have not sinned, we make him out to be a liar." But 3:6, 9 say "no one who lives in him keeps on sinning" and "no one who is born of God will continue to sin." These passages appear to contradict each other. Resolution: the 1:8/10 texts address false claims of sinlessness (directed against the secessionists); the 3:6/9 texts describe the fundamental directional reorientation of the person born of God — habitual, characterizing sin is incompatible with being born of God, but individual acts of sin remain possible (hence 2:1–2: "if anybody does sin, we have an advocate with the Father"). Marshall and Kruse both hold this reading.

**2. Water and blood (5:6)**: "He did not come by water only, but by water and blood." The secessionists (on a Cerinthian-type reading) held that Christ came "by water" = the baptism of Jesus, when divine power descended on him; but denied that Christ was present in the crucifixion. John insists on "water *and* blood" — both the baptism and the atoning death — as the moments of Christ's coming. This verse is among the most anti-Docetic in the entire NT.

**3. The structure of 1 John**: No consensus exists. Major proposals (Law's "spiral," Brooke's two-thesis scheme, Dodd's broad sections, Schnackenburg's three sections, Feuillet's parallel structure) all face objections. Marshall resolves: the letter is "a series of connected paragraphs whose relation to one another is governed by association of ideas rather than by a logical plan" — not illogical, but not susceptible to large-scale outline. Kruse structures around the secessionists' claims being successively tested.

**4. Sin unto death (5:16–17)**: "There is a sin that leads to death. I am not saying that you should pray about that." The "sin unto death" is debated: apostasy? denial of Christ? the secessionists' specific heresy? deliberate, final rejection of God? Marshall is cautious and leaves multiple readings open.

**5. Diotrephes (3 John 9–10)**: A local leader who "loves to be first," has refused the elder's authority, rejected his letter, and will not receive traveling preachers — even excommunicating those who do. Is he a heretic or merely a power-seeker? Marshall (followed by Kruse): the ecclesiastical reading is correct — no doctrinal issue is raised against Diotrephes; he is not a secessionist but a leader resisting outside authority. Kasemann's reversal (Diotrephes is the orthodox defender, the elder is the Gnostic) is rejected by both.

**6. Authorship** — see above.

## Structure

**1 John** (Marshall's outline):
- Prologue: the Word of life (1:1–4)
- Walking in the light (1:5–2:2)
- Keeping his commands (2:3–11)
- The new status of believers and their relation to the world (2:12–17)
- A warning against antichrists (2:18–27)
- The hope of God's children (2:28–3:3)
- The sinlessness of God's children (3:4–10)
- Brotherly love as the mark of the Christian (3:11–18)
- Assurance and obedience (3:19–24)
- The spirits of truth and falsehood (4:1–6)
- God's love and our love (4:7–12)
- Assurance and Christian love (4:13–5:4)
- The true faith confirmed (5:5–12)
- Christian certainties (5:13–21)

**2 John**: Address and greeting (1–3) | Living in truth and love (4–6) | Beware of false teaching (7–11) | Final words (12–13)

**3 John**: Address and greeting (1–4) | Praise of Gaius (5–8) | Denunciation of Diotrephes (9–10) | Commendation of Demetrius (11–12) | Final greetings (13–15)

## Canonical History

1 John was widely received early; Papias (Eusebius, HE 3:39:17) used it; Polycarp's Philippians 7 alludes to it (c. AD 110–140); Irenaeus cites it as John the apostle's (c. AD 180). Its authority was "never questioned" according to Eusebius (HE 3:24:17; 25:2). 2 and 3 John faced more dispute — their brevity kept them obscure; 3 John has its first clear attestation only in Origen (mid-3rd c.). In the Syriac church, 2 and 3 John entered the canon only in the Philoxenian version (AD 508). All three were eventually accepted in the East and West on the assumption of apostolic authorship.
