The **Egyptian Book of the Dead** — properly the **"Book of Going Forth by Day"** — is the most famous text
of [[egyptian-religion|ancient Egyptian religion]]: a collection of ~190 **spells, hymns, and instructions**
written on papyrus and buried with the dead to guide and protect them on the perilous journey through the
underworld (**Duat**) to eternal life.

## Content and Use

Not a single fixed book but a **personalized selection** of spells, copied for each individual and placed in
the tomb. Its spells equip the deceased to pass the gates and demons of the Duat, to know the secret names
that grant power, to avoid the "second death," and above all to survive the **[[maat|weighing of the
heart]]** — including the famous **"Negative Confession"** (Spell 125), in which the dead declare their
innocence of a long list of sins before the 42 judges. Successful, the deceased "goes forth by day,"
identified with [[osiris|Osiris]] and free to move among the living and the gods.

## Place in the Tradition

It is the New Kingdom heir of the older **[[pyramid-texts|Pyramid Texts]]** (royal, Old Kingdom) and Coffin
Texts (Middle Kingdom) — and marks the **democratization** of the afterlife: eternal life, once a royal
privilege, became available to anyone who could commission a copy. The ethical self-examination of the
Negative Confession is a notable early articulation of moral accountability after death.
