# Letters of Origen

Three letters survive among Origen's writings in the Schaff/ANF edition:

1. **Julius Africanus to Origen** — on the Susanna episode in the Greek Daniel (lines 10476–10520)
2. **Origen to Julius Africanus** — Origen's defense of the Greek additions to Daniel as canonical (lines 10521–11032)
3. **Origen to Gregory (Thaumaturgus)** — on Christian use of Greek philosophy; the "spoils of Egypt" (lines 11033–11119)

## Letter of Julius Africanus to Origen (On Susanna)

**Correspondent and occasion**: Julius Africanus (c. 160–240 CE), the Christian chronographer and bibliographer, addresses Origen after a discussion in which Origen had cited the Susanna episode in Daniel as genuine prophecy. Africanus writes to argue that this pericope — along with Bel and the Dragon at the end of Daniel — is a "more modern forgery" absent from the Hebrew text and therefore not part of the authentic Scriptures.

**Text-critical arguments**. Africanus marshals several lines of evidence. First, the mode of prophecy: in the rest of canonical Daniel the prophet operates through "visions, and dreams, and an angel appearing to him, never by prophetic inspiration" (line 10488–10489), whereas in Susanna Daniel is seized by the Spirit and cries out directly — a different and uncharacteristic mode. Second, and most technically, the crucial punning condemnations on the two elders depend on Greek wordplay: *prinos* (holm-tree) / *prisein* (to saw asunder); *schinos* (mastich-tree) / *schisthenai* (to rend asunder). As Africanus writes: "Now, in Greek, it happens that holm-tree and saw asunder, and rend and mastich-tree sound alike; but in Hebrew they are quite distinct. But all the books of the Old Testament have been translated from Hebrew into Greek" (lines 10498–10501). A narrative whose central judicial proof depends on Greek wordplay cannot, Africanus concludes, have been composed in Hebrew and translated. Third, a historical objection: captives in Babylon — "lost and won at play, thrown out unburied on the streets" — could not plausibly have held capital jurisdiction or possessed estates with "spacious gardens" (lines 10502–10509). Fourth, and decisively for Africanus: "this section, along with the other two at the end of it, is not contained in the Daniel received among the Jews" (lines 10511–10512). Fifth, no prophet before Susanna's Daniel quotes another prophet word for word — yet this text has Daniel quote Exodus: "The innocent and righteous shall you not slay" (lines 10515–10516). Finally, the style differs from canonical Daniel. Africanus closes with a characteristic rhetorical flourish: "I have struck the blow; do you give the echo; answer, and instruct me" (lines 10518–10519).

**Hermeneutical significance**. Africanus's letter is an early instance of text-critical method applied to canon — the argument that a passage absent from the Hebrew Vorlage and dependent on Greek wordplay cannot be genuinely Semitic in origin. His approach anticipates Jerome's later preference for the *Hebraica veritas* and directly challenges Origen's practice of using the Greek LXX tradition in the Churches without qualification.

## Origen's Letter to Julius Africanus

**Occasion and scope**. Written from Nicomedia (where Origen had little time), this is Origen's point-by-point defense of the Greek additions to Daniel — Susanna, the Prayer of Azariah/Song of the Three Youths, and Bel and the Dragon. The letter is one of the most important early documents on Christian canon formation and the authority of the Septuagint.

**The governing principle: the Church's canon vs. the Jewish canon**. Origen opens by reframing the entire dispute. The question is not whether these passages exist in the Hebrew Bible — he acknowledges they do not. Rather: "what needs there be to speak of us... as if these copies were not in use in our Churches?" He points out that such divergences between the Hebrew and Greek traditions occur throughout the Old Testament. He has personally collated copies — the labor that would produce his *Hexapla* — and found the differences pervasive: additions in Daniel (the Song of the Three Youths), in Job, in Esther (neither Mardochaios's nor Esther's prayers are "found in the Hebrew"), in Jeremiah (transposed and variant readings), in Genesis ("God saw that it was good, when the firmament was made, are not found in the Hebrew"), and in Exodus. He used critical signs: "I marked, for the sake of distinction, with the sign the Greeks call an obelisk... those passages in our copies which are not found in the Hebrew" (lines 10614–10615). This is the same method he employed in the Hexapla.

**The central argument: do not replace the Church's Scriptures with the Jewish text**. Origen refuses Africanus's implied conclusion — that the Church should adopt the shorter Hebrew canon. He writes rhetorically: "Are we to suppose that that Providence which in the sacred Scriptures has ministered to the edification of all the Churches of Christ, had no thought for those bought with a price, for whom Christ died?" (lines 10625–10628). He invokes Proverbs: "You shall not remove the ancient landmarks which your fathers have set" (lines 10631–10632). The Church's Greek text, however it came to differ from the Hebrew, is the text God has providentially given the Churches; ejecting it to please the Jews would discredit the brethren. Moreover, Origen notes a practical apologetic concern: he studies the Hebrew in order to avoid "in my controversies with the Jews... quot[ing] to them what is not found in their copies" (lines 10643–10644), but this is different from surrendering the Church's canon.

**On the wordplay (prinos/schinos)**. Origen admits uncertainty. He personally consulted "not a few Jews" asking for the Hebrew equivalents of *prinos* and *schinos*, and they could not provide them because the trees were not mentioned in their Scriptures. One learned Jew said: "If you can adduce a passage in any Scripture where the schinos is mentioned... you will find there the words you seek" (lines 10668–10670). Origen therefore declines to affirm or deny that a Hebrew equivalent exists. He proposes a more nuanced answer: elsewhere in Scripture Hebrew etymological puns were rendered in Greek with corresponding Greek puns (e.g., *ish*/*isha* in Genesis: "She shall be called *woman* [*essa*], because she was taken out of her husband [*is*]," lines 10901–10908). The translators of Susanna may have done the same — "they contrived it so that they found out some Greek words... which either corresponded exactly to the Hebrew form... or presented some analogy to it" (lines 10896–10900).

**On the historical objections**. Origen answers Africanus's claim that captives could not have held capital jurisdiction by citing the situation of Jews under Roman rule: "the Romans rule, and the Jews pay the half-shekel to them, how great power by the concession of Caesar the ethnarch has; so that we... know that he differs in little from a true king! Private trials are held according to the law, and some are condemned to death" (lines 10964–10969). Similarly, wealthy captives are attested — Tobias, Mardochaios, Neemias, Dachiacharus — demonstrating that not all exiles were destitute. As for Joakim's garden and mansion: "why should we wonder that one Joakim had garden, and house, and property, whether these were very expensive or only moderate" (lines 10956–10957)?

**On Daniel's mode of prophecy**. Africanus had complained Daniel never prophesies by inspiration in the canonical book. Origen replies by citing Hebrews 1:1 — "At sundry times, and in various manners, God spoke in time past unto the fathers by the prophets" — and then demonstrating from Genesis that Jacob prophesied by dreams, by angelic appearance (the wrestling, lines 10810–10823), and by direct inspiration at his deathbed blessing. "We need not wonder, then, that Daniel sometimes prophesied by inspiration... sometimes, as you say, by dreams and visions, and at other times by an angel appearing unto him" (lines 10832–10834).

**On prophets quoting each other**. Origen offers a striking parallel: Isaiah and Micah share the "mountain of the Lord" passage "almost word for word" (lines 10984–11005). Chronicles overlaps with Psalms 105 and 96. The law about the Sabbath burden appears in both Jeremiah and Moses. The argument that prophets never quote each other is simply false.

**On the suppression of the passage by Jewish authorities**. Origen offers a tradition-history argument: the passage may have been genuine but suppressed by Jewish leaders embarrassed by stories of wicked elders and corrupt judges. He cites the Isaiah martyrdom (sawn asunder), known from tradition but absent from the Hebrew, as a parallel: "they hid from the knowledge of the people as many of the passages which contained any scandal against the elders, rulers, and judges, as they could... some of which have been preserved in uncanonical writings" (lines 10708–10711). Jesus himself cited the murder of Zacharias son of Barachias "between the temple and the altar" — an event unattested in any known OT book (lines 10759–10760).

**Hermeneutical significance**. Origen's letter is a landmark in the history of the biblical canon debate: it defends the LXX tradition against text-critical reduction to the Hebrew, articulates a providential account of textual transmission, and — crucially — marks the difference between *scholarly* knowledge of the Hebrew text (for apologetics against Jews) and *ecclesial* authority of the Greek text (for the life of the Church). It also documents Origen's own *Hexapla* methodology. The letter shows that canon questions for Origen are determined by ecclesiastical use and divine providence rather than by Semitic philology alone.

## Origen's Letter to Gregory Thaumaturgus

**Correspondent and occasion**. Origen writes to Gregory (later called Thaumaturgus, "the Wonderworker"), his most distinguished pupil, urging him to subordinate his considerable intellectual gifts entirely to Christianity while drawing upon Greek learning in a carefully controlled way. The letter is brief (lines 11033–11119) but programmatic — it contains the famous "spoils of Egypt" metaphor that became a standard patristic justification for the Christian appropriation of classical education.

**The core argument: philosophy as preparation for Christianity**. Origen opens by praising Gregory's natural ability — natural gifts "might make of you a finished Roman lawyer or a Greek philosopher, so to speak, of one of the schools in high reputation" (lines 11038–11040). But Origen's wish is different: "I am anxious that you should devote all the strength of your natural good parts to Christianity for your end" (lines 11040–11042). The specific instruction: "extract from the philosophy of the Greeks what may serve as a course of study or a preparation for Christianity, and from geometry and astronomy what will serve to explain the sacred Scriptures, in order that all that the sons of the philosophers are wont to say about geometry and music, grammar, rhetoric, and astronomy, as fellow-helpers to philosophy, we may say about philosophy itself, in relation to Christianity" (lines 11043–11049). The liberal arts — the classical *enkyklios paideia* — are thus reoriented: as they serve philosophy among the Greeks, so philosophy itself serves Christianity.

**The "spoils of Egypt" typology**. Origen develops an extended allegory from Exodus: God commanded Israel to ask their Egyptian neighbors for "vessels of silver and gold, and raiment" (lines 11052–11054), and these Egyptian materials were used to construct the Tabernacle furnishings — the ark, the cherubim, the mercy seat, the candlestick, the showbread table. Origen reads this as a figure: "the children of Israel gained this from their dwelling [in Egypt], that they had no lack of such precious material for the utensils of the service of God" (lines 11065–11067). Similarly, Christians who dwell among Greek learning should take what is useful and devote it to the service of God. "Now the sacred Scripture is wont to represent as an evil the going down from the land of the children of Israel into Egypt, indicating that certain persons get harm from sojourning among the Egyptians, that is to say, from meddling with the knowledge of this world" (lines 11076–11079) — but the Israelites who came out with Egyptian gold did great good.

**The warning: the danger of Greek philosophy**. Origen immediately qualifies the metaphor with a cautionary example. Ader the Idumean was fine while in Israel, but when he fled to Egypt and married into Pharaoh's court, he returned only to make the golden calf. "Not many take from Egypt only the useful, and go away and use it for the service of God; while Ader the Idumean has many brethren. These are they who, from their Greek studies, produce heretical notions, and set them up, like the golden calf, in Bethel, which signifies God's house" (lines 11090–11093). The heretics who misuse philosophy have set up their "own imaginations in the Scriptures, where the word of God dwells" (lines 11094–11096). Greek learning, then, is to be used instrumentally and under the control of Scripture — not adopted wholesale or allowed to displace Christian categories.

**The concluding exhortation**. The letter closes with a three-fold Scriptural exhortation to Gregory regarding the reading of Scripture: seek, knock, and ask in prayer — "Apply yourself thus to the divine study, seek aright, and with unwavering trust in God, the meaning of the holy Scriptures, which so many have missed" (lines 11109–11111). Prayer is "of all things indispensable to the knowledge of the things of God" (lines 11112–11113). The Saviour's own command is "not only, Knock, and it shall be opened to you; and seek, and you shall find, but also, Ask, and it shall be given unto you" (lines 11114–11115).

**Hermeneutical significance**. The letter is one of the clearest ancient statements of what patristic tradition called the *spoliatio Aegyptiorum* — the justified expropriation of pagan learning for Christian use. Origen's position is neither simple rejection of Greek philosophy nor uncritical adoption: philosophy is a *propaedeutic* that must be taken into the service of Scripture, not allowed to rule it. The same tension that runs through Origen's broader hermeneutical program — the Word of God as the organizing center into which all else must be brought — is articulated here in miniature.
