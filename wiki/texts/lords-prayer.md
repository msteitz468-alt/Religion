# The Lord's Prayer

## Textual Overview

The Lord's Prayer stands at Matt 6:9-13, within the [[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]], with
a shorter parallel at Luke 11:2-4.

[[rt-france|R. T. France]] observes that the prayer lands at the centre of the discourse —
fifty-four verses before it, forty-eight after — but arrives there "by means of what is in effect a
literary digression." On his reading Matthew has interrupted his own carefully balanced tripartite
unit on almsgiving, prayer and fasting (6:2-4, 5-6, 16-18) with an extended discussion of prayer at
vv. 7-15, inserting sayings material paralleled in Luke 11:2-4 and Mark 11:25.

France analyses the Matthean form as an address followed by two balanced triads: "Three clauses
about God and his worship ... Three petitions for our own needs." He argues that this structure
itself refutes the "Gentile" view of prayer condemned in vv. 7-8: God's honour, kingdom and purpose
come first, and only then our needs. He reads the three third-person imperatives as "in effect a
doxology, an act of worship, associating the praying community with God's purpose in the world,"
which subordinates the second-person petitions that follow to God's will rather than our desires.

France stresses that the prayer is addressed to the disciples corporately and is phrased throughout
in the plural: "It is the prayer of a community rather than an individual act of devotion" — though
he adds it would equally guide the private storeroom prayer of v. 6. He notes what the prayer
*omits*: "There is no explicit confession of sin, no direct thanksgiving for blessings already
received, no intercession for the needs of the world" — nor for persecutors, despite 5:44. He allows
these may be developed by meditation, but holds the starting point is worship and petition.

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

### Address: "Our Father in heaven"

France holds that Luke's simple "Father" reflects the Aramaic vocative *'abbā'*, which he takes to
be [[jesus|Jesus]]' distinctive approach to God in prayer, subsequently shared by disciples (Rom
8:15; Gal 4:6). He judges that "Matthew's addition of 'our' makes the echo of the form *'abbā'* less
obvious," though the implication of privileged access is equally clear. He observes that Jesus in
Matthew prays to "my Father" and "never in the form 'our Father' which he here teaches his disciples
to use," and finds here "the raw materials for a theological system which posits a unique filial
relationship for Jesus and a derivative relationship for God's other 'children'" — a doctrine
Matthew lets emerge by implication rather than developing.

On "in heaven," France argues it does double duty: "It not only underlines the metaphorical nature
of the concept but also prescribes the disciple's attitude to God" — all-powerful and wholly
trustworthy, yet to be approached with reverence.

### The three God-clauses

**"Hallowed be your name."** France argues the clause does not ask that God's name be *made* holy —
"it is holy already" — but "that people may recognize and acknowledge its holiness by giving God the
reverence which is his due," the petition being itself an expression of that reverence. He explains
"name" as "a recurrent OT term for God himself as he is perceived and honored by people," citing Isa
29:23 and the prophetic concern that the name not be profaned.

**"Your kingdom come."** France calls this "perhaps the most clearly futuristic reference to God's
kingdom in Matthew," read in the light of the mustard seed and yeast, where a hidden present kingdom
prepares a future public demonstration. His formulation: the petition asks not that God *become*
king but "that his actual kingship *de jure* may be fully implemented *de facto* as people submit to
his sovereignty." See [[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]].

**"Your will be done."** France holds the third clause builds on the second: "the essence of the
coming of God's kingship is that he is duly obeyed and his purpose fulfilled," noting that even
Jesus must submit his own will at 26:42. He argues the closing words "as in heaven so also on earth"
"can be seen to apply to all three of the preceding clauses"; Luke lacks them on the first two, but
attaching them to the third makes their relevance obvious.

France draws the practical corollary: "To pray such a prayer is, of course, to be committed oneself
to honor God's name, accept his kingship, and do his will."

He also criticizes Hagner's paraphrase converting the third-person imperatives into second-person
("Set apart your holy name; Bring your eschatological kingdom; Cause your will to be fulfilled"),
arguing that "To speak as Hagner does simply of 'the divine passive' runs the danger of obscuring
this human dimension" — hallowing, accepting kingship and doing God's will all involve human
response, including that of those praying.

### "Give us today our bread for the coming day" — the *epiousios* crux

France reads v.11 as the simplest expression of the trust later expounded in 6:25-33: "Even bread,
the most basic of survival rations, comes by God's daily provision ... and is thus a proper subject
for prayer." He proposes this petition as the cue for Matthew's inclusion of the treasure-and-anxiety
material later in the chapter.

The Greek adjective *epiousios* occurs nowhere else in extant Greek literature. France catalogues
the options:

| Option | Basis | France's assessment |
|---|---|---|
| "continual" / "for our need" | Syriac versions | reported |
| "daily" | earlier Latin versions | reported |
| *supersubstantialis*, "supernatural" | Jerome's Vulgate coinage | reported as a coinage, "probably meant 'supernatural'" |
| *epi* + *ousia*, "needed for existence" | modern derivation | canvassed |
| *epi tēn ousan (hēmeran)*, "for the present day" | modern derivation | canvassed |
| *hē epiousa (hēmera)*, "the following day" | modern derivation | "most recent interpreters prefer" this; France follows it |
| from *epienai*, so that the *bread* is what is "coming" | eschatological interpreters | canvassed and rejected |

France's own solution turns on the time of utterance: in the morning *hē epiousa* means the day then
beginning, later in the day it means "tomorrow" — hence his rendering "for the coming day." "The
bread requested is therefore for the near future, which may be 'today' or 'tomorrow' depending on
the time of utterance."

He rejects the eschatological reading — "bread for tomorrow" as shorthand for eschatological
provision, the messianic banquet, or the hoped-for return of manna (2 Bar. 29:8; Sib. Or. 7:149; Rev
2:17) — on three grounds: "bread" is literal elsewhere in Matthew even when deployed symbolically;
the everyday sense implied by the more linguistically probable derivation requires that meaning
absent contextual signals to the contrary; and "today" — still more Luke's "every day" — sits
uncomfortably with an eschatological perspective. Of Hagner's paraphrase, "Give us today the
eschatological bread that will be ours in the future," France remarks that it "has added a lot to
the actual wording of the Greek!"

France does suggest the petition "would remind a Jewish hearer of the provision of manna in the
wilderness, enough for each day at a time, with an extra supply before the sabbath (Exod 16:4-5)."
He endorses Carson on "the precarious lifestyle of many first-century workers who were paid one day
at a time and for whom a few days' illness could spell tragedy," and flags that such immediate
dependence "seems remote from our experience" in modern Western culture — but not to much of the
world today, nor to Jesus' itinerant disciples.

### "Forgive us our debts"

France holds the point of v.12, and of the comment at vv.14-15, is that "forgiveness is a reciprocal
principle, a point which will be more fully underlined in the parable of 18:23-35." He rules out a
monetary understanding of debt, "since it is debts to God for which forgiveness is asked," and notes
that vv.14-15's substitution of "offenses" (and Luke's "sins") gives the prosaic but correct
interpretation. He observes that Matthew, unlike Luke, keeps the debt metaphor in both halves,
ensuring a close parallel between God's forgiveness and ours; and he stresses that "it is the
debtors rather than the debts which we have forgiven; our concern, like God's, is to be with
personal relationships."

On whether human forgiveness is strictly a *prior condition* of divine forgiveness, France holds the
*hōs* leaves the question open, and suggests manuscript variation in the verb's tense may reflect
ancient uncertainty on this very point. He declines to settle it: "perhaps precision on this point
is not necessary." What matters is whether the forgiveness sought is mirrored in the attitude of
those praying — "To ask to be forgiven while oneself refusing to forgive is hypocritical." He
reports Jeremias's proposal that *aphēkamen* represents an Aramaic perfect with performative force
("as herewith we forgive our debtors"), doubts that the Greek aorist can naturally bear this, yet
grants the rendering an "appropriately challenging perspective."

In vv.14-15 France holds the implicit conditionality becomes explicit, stated both positively and
negatively: "Only the forgiving will be forgiven." He raises the resulting "uncomfortable
questions" about [[grace|grace]] candidly, noting the same problem at 25:31-46 and 22:1-14, where
"Salvation ... may be undeserved and unexpected, but it is not without conditions." His verdict, by
analogy with the traditional Paul–James antinomy, is that "there is not perhaps a simple
contradiction, but there is certainly a clear difference of perspective on what the forgiveness of
sins involves." He adds that if the forgiveness achieved through Jesus' death (26:28) is not matched
by a forgiving attitude, "it cannot be presumed upon."

France also raises the scope problem sharply — "Should disciples forgive war criminals, serial
murderers, and abusers of children? What does 'forgive' mean in such circumstances?" — and answers
that forgiveness properly applies where there is *personal* offense: those whose evil does not touch
us should be objects of prayerful concern and sympathetic understanding, but "it is properly
speaking not for us to 'forgive' them: that is God's prerogative." Within that scope he insists
there is no limit. See [[sin|sin]].

### "Do not bring us into testing, but rescue us from the Evil One"

France reads the sequence as "a petition for the forgiveness of past sin" followed by "one for
protection from future sin." He adopts "the Evil One" for *tou ponērou*, noting that the parallel
between the two lines is then clear, since *peirasmos* was the devil's role in 4:1-11, where he is
called *ho peirazōn*. **Note:** France's argument for this translation is located in a footnote
(p. 231, n. 14) which the plain-text conversion used for this wiki dropped; his reasoning is not
recoverable, only his conclusion. See [[temptation-of-jesus|the temptation of Jesus]] and
[[satan|Satan]].

He allows that one might see a progression between the two lines — avoid testing, then rescue if it
occurs — but calls this "perhaps to be too pedantic"; both are vivid ways of saying disciples need
God's help against the devil.

France answers the objection that God's "bringing us into *peirasmos*" is incompatible with his
goodness by naming two mistakes in it. First, a negative request does not imply the positive is
otherwise expected — his illustration is a husband saying "Don't ever leave me." Second, *peirasmos*
is not always bad: "it was after all the Holy Spirit who took Jesus into the wilderness 'to be
tested'" (4:1); he reads Jas 1:13 as using *peirazō* in the narrower sense "tempt to do wrong,"
while God's *testing* of his people is a biblical idea (Gen 22:1; Deut 8:2). He concludes that as in
4:1-11 "it is possible to discern in the same circumstances both the devil's 'tempting' and God's
'testing' of his people."

France rejects reading the "testing" as the eschatological tribulation on four grounds: the lack of
a definite article before *peirasmon*; that *peirasmos* is not a recognized term for that idea; that
a community eager for the consummation could not coherently pray to be spared the trial without
which it cannot come; and that 26:41 uses *peirasmos* of immediate danger. His own reading refers
the petition to "the testing experiences which are the normal lot of disciples who try to live
according to the principles of the kingdom of God" in a world that does not share them, with the
persecution of 5:11-12 in mind.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

France treats the doxology ("For thine is the kingdom...") as a **secondary addition**, describing
it as "later added" and grouping it with "the textual accretions following v. 13." He draws a
theological irony from its later status: disciples who have just prayed for the kingdom's coming
immediately declare it already a reality, which he takes as vividly illustrating the already/not-yet
tension.

**Source limitation.** France's detailed text-critical argument for the doxology's secondary status
sits in a footnote (p. 231, n. 15) which the plain-text conversion dropped. Only his conclusion
survives in the body text; the manuscript evidence he adduced is unrecoverable from the source as
ingested. This wiki records his verdict without reconstructing his reasoning.

France also notes MS variation in the tense (aorist vs present) of the verb "we have forgiven" at
v. 12, which he suggests "perhaps reflects ancient uncertainty on this very issue" of whether human
forgiveness is strictly prior.

On the relation of the two gospel forms, France states that "The question which form of the prayer
is 'the original' is of course unanswerable," and canvasses both directions: the general tendency of
liturgical texts to expand favours Luke's shorter form as earlier, while Matthew's wording "has been
judged to be closer to a likely Aramaic original." He attacks what he calls "the instinctive
critical assumption that any piece of Jesus' teaching could have been given only on a single
occasion," which he holds is "here more than usually suspect": a unit this central is the likeliest
of all to have been repeated with varying wording to different audiences. His conclusion is a
refusal: "There is then no basis for judging one or the other version to be 'more authentic'" —
each may be taken as demonstrating Jesus' concept of true prayer, "and the differences are not at a
fundamental level." See [[synoptic-problem|the Synoptic problem]].

He further reports the suggestion that Luke's "When you pray say" implies a set form while Matthew's
"This, then, is how you should pray" implies a pattern, and rejects it as "too artificial a
distinction," holding it "likely that when Jesus taught these words ... he would have been content
for them to be used either way."

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

- **France's frame** is grammatical-historical and lexical, with heavy weight on the semantics of
  *epiousios*, *peirasmos*, *ponēros* and the debt metaphor, and on the prayer's Aramaic and Jewish
  liturgical background.
- **Against a thoroughgoing eschatological reading.** France reports that many interpreters, on the
  analogy of the Qaddish, argue for an eschatological orientation across the whole prayer, and he
  contests this at vv. 11 and 13. His counter-position is that because the kingdom has already been
  announced and is working its way in through Jesus' ministry, "every clause of this prayer has an
  immediate relevance to the present situation and concerns of those who are praying." He adds a
  concessive point: even granting an eschatological frame for vv. 9b-10, "it would not necessarily
  follow that the provision of daily needs, material and spiritual, would be inappropriate to such a
  prayer."
- **Jewish liturgical background.** France reproduces Jeremias's translation of the earliest
  accessible form of the Aramaic Qaddish, "already in regular synagogue use by the time of Jesus,"
  and notes its strong resemblance to the prayer's first three clauses. He identifies exactly two
  differences: "first in the second-person address to God as 'Our Father in heaven' and secondly in
  the continuation of the prayer to include the community's petitions." He concludes that the first
  part is a distillation of familiar eschatological hope which "would have caused no surprise to his
  disciples."

## Canon and Reception

France cites Did. 8:2-3, which he places in the latter part of the first century, as giving the full
wording of the prayer in the expanded form familiar in modern use with the instruction "Pray thus
three times a day" — normally taken, he notes, to envisage liturgical repetition rather than merely
praying in the prayer's spirit. He infers from the early church's tolerance of divergent forms that
it "was more concerned with the content of the prayer than with its exact form," and states that
Matthew presents the prayer in a form close to that most widely used today.

He notes that Jerome's Vulgate coined *supersubstantialis* for *epiousios*, and that the KJV's "vain
repetitions" at 6:7 is an unfortunate rendering which has often been taken to denigrate persistence
in prayer — something France holds the text does not do, since neither liturgical forms (a
formulated prayer immediately follows) nor persistence are prohibited.

France frames the immediate context (6:7-8) as contrasting disciples with "the Gentiles," whose
fault is not ethnic but that of religious outsiders who do not know God as heavenly Father and so
"think they must badger a reluctant Deity into taking notice of them (cf. the expressive modern term
'God-botherer')." He glosses *battalogeō* as "babbling," a noisy flow of sound without meaning, and
*polylogia* as "much speaking" — "an approach to prayer which values quantity (and perhaps volume?)
rather than quality." He raises directly the theological problem — if God knows our needs, why tell
him? — and answers with the tradition that prayer is neither information transfer nor a technique
for getting things, but the expression of a trust relationship.

## Scholarship and Historiography

Positions recorded, not adjudicated:

- **Jeremias** on the Qaddish parallel (reproduced approvingly) and on the performative aorist
  *aphēkamen* (reported, doubted linguistically, granted rhetorical value).
- **Hagner** on the second-person paraphrase of the three God-clauses (reported and criticized for
  obscuring the human dimension) and on the eschatological paraphrase of the bread petition
  (reported and rejected as adding to the Greek).
- **Carson** on first-century daily-wage precarity (endorsed).
- **Unnamed "many interpreters"** on the thoroughgoing eschatological reading of the prayer
  (reported; France contests it at vv. 11 and 13).
- **Unnamed "eschatological interpreters"** on bread as messianic-banquet provision, supported by
  deriving *epiousios* from *epienai* so that the bread rather than the day is "coming" (reported
  and rejected).

**Attribution caveat.** Because all footnote bodies were dropped in the plain-text conversion of
France's commentary, his engagement with secondary literature is only partially visible. Scholars
are named above only where the surviving body text names them; no attributions have been supplied
from outside the source.

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **The meaning of *epiousios*** — a hapax; France canvasses seven renderings and settles on "for
   the coming day," with the sense depending on the hour of utterance.
2. **Whether the prayer is eschatological throughout** — France says no, and gives four arguments
   against the eschatological reading of the *peirasmos* petition.
3. **Whether human forgiveness is a prior condition of divine forgiveness** — France leaves it open
   and says precision is unnecessary, while affirming that "Only the forgiving will be forgiven."
4. **Conditional forgiveness and Pauline grace** — France concedes "a clear difference of
   perspective" rather than resolving it.
5. **"Evil" or "the Evil One" at 6:13** — France chooses the personal sense; his argument is in a
   dropped footnote.
6. **Which form is original** — France declares the question "unanswerable" and refuses to rank the
   two versions for authenticity.
