Daniel is the Bible's foremost apocalyptic text — the source of the most important single passage for NT Christology ("one like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven," 7:13-14), the origin of Jesus's primary self-designation, the locus of the "abomination of desolation" that Jesus applies to his own time (Matt 24:15), and the framework of much of Revelation. It also contains six of the OT's most beloved narrative episodes (the lion's den, the fiery furnace, the writing on the wall) beloved for their simple clarity — while the second half confounds seasoned interpreters with its oblique visions.

The book is written in two languages: chapters 1:1-2:4a and 8-12 in Hebrew; chapters 2:4b-7:28 in Aramaic. The Aramaic section (chs. 2-7) itself is a chiastic unit, with the two halves (chs. 2-3 / chs. 5-6 and ch. 4 / ch. 4) mirroring each other, and ch. 7 answering ch. 2's statue vision with the Son of Man vision.

## Narrative Summary

The Book of Daniel is divided into two distinct genres: court narratives (chapters 1–6) and apocalyptic visions (chapters 7–12). The narrative begins in the sixth century B.C. (605 B.C.) with Daniel and his three friends exiled from Judah to Babylon, where they are trained for royal service. Despite being immersed in a foreign culture under kings like Nebuchadnezzar, they maintain their faith. The stories recount God’s miraculous interventions, such as revealing Nebuchadnezzar’s dream (ch. 2), saving the friends from the fiery furnace (ch. 3), and delivering Daniel from the lions' den (ch. 6). The second half transitions to Daniel’s apocalyptic visions concerning future empires, uniformly conveying the message that "in spite of present appearances, God is in control" of human history.

## Structure and Genre

The book's two halves are radically different in genre:

| Section | Chapters | Genre | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Court narratives | 1-6 | Stories **of** Daniel | Accessible; children's "Sunday school" material |
| Apocalyptic visions | 7-12 | Visions **by** Daniel | Technically demanding; vast interpretive debate |

Despite the genre shift at ch. 7, the message of both halves is the same: **"In spite of present appearances, God is in control."**

Longman's analogy (from series general editor Terry Muck): the court stories are like a loon bobbing on the lake's surface; the apocalyptic visions are what happens on the long underwater dives. Both are necessary. Stories alone could lead to a works-based theology (prosper like Daniel by being virtuous). Visions alone could lead to disembodied mysticism. Together, they produce confident faith: God controls both ordinary life and the cosmic sweep of history.

## Historical Context

Daniel is set entirely in the Babylonian exile. The book begins in 605 BCE (Daniel's deportation under Jehoiakim) and spans to 537 BCE (third year of Cyrus the Persian). The exilic audience had suffered the destruction of Jerusalem, the temple, and the Davidic monarchy — all four "pillars" of official orthodox theology (cf. [[ezekiel|Ezekiel]]'s framework). Babylonian victory could seem to mean Marduk had defeated YHWH. Daniel's book is written to address this theological crisis: divine sovereignty has not been defeated; it is being expressed through events that only look like defeat.

## Date and Composition — The Central Debate

**The critical consensus (second-century date)**: The prophecies of chs. 7-12 are uncannily accurate through the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175-164 BCE), then appear to fail at chs. 11:40-45. This pattern suggests the book was written in the Maccabean period, ca. 167-164 BCE, with the "prophecies" being historical narrative dressed as future prediction (*vaticinium ex eventu*). Historical difficulties in chs. 1-6 (e.g., the puzzling "Darius the Mede") also support a later, less accurate memory of 6th-century events.

**Longman's defense of sixth-century origin**: He argues with genuine intellectual humility, acknowledging that "brilliantly insightful" commentators like Goldingay hold a late date. His key arguments:

1. The alternative (*vaticinium ex eventu*) requires the author to deceive his audience — presenting as genuine prediction what he knows is past history. This conflicts with the book's own theological logic: Daniel makes God's ability to predict the future the proof of his divine superiority over idols (an argument central to Isaiah 40-48 as well).

2. The historical difficulties in chs. 1-6 have harmonizations; they are not decisive.

3. The precision of chs. 7-11 requires one of two explanations: either it was written after the fact, or it is genuine prediction. Either way, the theological content is the same.

Longman cites Joyce Baldwin: "The fact that the standpoint of the writer... cannot be ascertained for certain does not greatly affect the interpretation." He explicitly refuses to make the date question a "litmus test" for evangelical orthodoxy.

## The Apocalyptic Genre — The Hermeneutical Key

The most important interpretive contribution Longman makes: **apocalyptic is a metaphor-rich genre**, and this determines how it must be read.

### What Apocalyptic Is

The term comes from Greek *apokalypsis* = "revelation." Key formal features:
- **Intense symbolic imagery**: hybrid beasts, cosmic thrones, divine figures, unusual creatures
- **Symbolic numbers**: chronological references that cannot be plotted on a literal timeline
- **Heavenly intermediaries**: angelic interpreters explaining the visions
- **Cosmic perspective**: events in heaven corresponding to events on earth
- **Vindication of the oppressed**: God's final defeat of evil powers and establishment of his kingdom

### How Apocalyptic Must Be Read

**Images communicate truth but not precision.** The four beasts of ch. 7 are images for evil kingdoms, not literal monsters. The "Ancient of Days" is not literally an old man on a physical throne. The "one like a son of man" is a divine figure using imagery, not a physical description. To press the details of apocalyptic images is to commit a genre error.

**Numbers are symbolic.** "A time, times, and half a time" (7:25); "seventy weeks" (9:24-27); "1,290 days" (12:11); "1,335 days" (12:12) — these are symbolic formulations, not literal calendar entries. Longman: "A crucial principle to reading apocalyptic literature according to its original intention is to expect images. It is wrong to say that we interpret apocalyptic literally except when it is absurd to do so." This popular evangelical hermeneutic inverts the proper approach: in apocalyptic, the default is symbolic; literalism requires demonstration.

**The purpose is assurance, not calendar**: Apocalyptic literature is not a schedule of end-times events. It is a cosmic vision of divine sovereignty over history designed to sustain faithful endurance in the present. Daniel 7-12 says: the current evil empire is not the final word; God will intervene decisively; the saints will receive the kingdom.

## Key Passages

### Daniel 2: The Statue Dream

Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a statue with a gold head, silver chest, bronze belly, iron legs, iron-and-clay feet — smashed by a stone "cut without hands" that becomes a mountain filling the earth. Daniel interprets: four kingdoms succeeding each other, then God establishing an eternal kingdom.

The four kingdoms are typically identified as Babylon, Media/Persia, Greece, and Rome. Longman argues ch. 7 (where the same kingdoms appear as beasts) intentionally resists specific identification: they represent evil kingdoms in general, an unspecified succession from the Exile to the end of history.

The stone "cut without human hands" represents the divine kingdom that supersedes all human kingdoms — a passage NT writers appropriated for Christ and for the church.

### Daniel 7: The Son of Man — The Book's Christological Climax

The most important single passage in Daniel for NT theology. Four hybrid beast-kingdoms rise from the sea; then Daniel sees:

> "The Ancient of Days took his seat. His clothing was as white as snow; the hair of his head was white like wool. His throne was flaming with fire, and its wheels were all ablaze... A river of fire was flowing, coming out from before him. Thousands upon thousands attended him; ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him. The court was seated, and the books were opened." (7:9-10)

> "In my vision at night I looked, and there before me was one like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven. He approached the Ancient of Days and was led into his presence. He was given authority, glory and sovereign power; all peoples, nations and men of every language worshiped him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that will not pass away, and his kingdom is one that will never be destroyed." (7:13-14)

**The Ancient of Days**: God as universal judge; the **cloud chariot** (cf. Ezek 1's *merkabah*) and the fire echo the *kābôd* theology of Ezekiel and Isaiah.

**"One like a son of man"**: A divine figure riding clouds — in OT theology, the cloud chariot is the exclusive prerogative of YHWH. This figure is divine. He receives eternal dominion, universal worship, and an unending kingdom — the direct reversal of all four beast-kingdoms.

**The identity question**: The interpreting angel identifies him with "the saints of the Most High" (v. 18) — either God's people or (per the Aramaic usage in Dead Sea Scrolls) angelic beings. Longman: the tension between individual and collective is intentional, anticipating NT revelation.

**NT appropriation — the most extensive in any OT passage**:
- **Jesus's self-designation**: "Son of Man" is Jesus's primary self-designation in all four Gospels — approximately 80 occurrences. By using this title, Jesus claims the identity of the Dan 7:13 figure.
- **The trial before the Sanhedrin** (Mark 14:62): "You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven." Jesus quotes Dan 7:13-14 directly. The high priest tears his robes: Jesus has claimed divine prerogatives. This is the direct cause of the blasphemy charge.
- **Revelation**: The throne vision of Rev 4-5; the "coming on the clouds" in Rev 1:7; "one like a son of man" in Rev 1:13; 14:14 — all draw on Daniel 7.
- **Acts 7:56**: Stephen's vision of "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" — echoing Dan 7:13.

### Daniel 9: Prayer, Confession, and the Seventy Weeks

The chapter begins with Daniel reading Jeremiah's 70-year prophecy (Jer 25:11-12; 29:10) and confessing Israel's sins in one of the greatest penitential prayers in Scripture. He confesses on behalf of the people: "We have sinned and done wrong... we have not listened to your servants the prophets."

The "seventy weeks" oracle (9:24-27) then follows: 70 × 7 = 490 "weeks" (years?), divided into 7 + 62 + 1, concerning Jerusalem's future and the "Anointed One." The "abomination of desolation" (9:27) appears at the end.

Longman: treat the 70 × 7 as symbolic; the oracle addresses the completion of God's redemptive purposes, not a precise calendar. The "abomination of desolation" has an initial fulfillment in Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167 BCE) and a typological extension that Jesus applies to his own future (Matt 24:15; Mark 13:14). Apocalyptic imagery is designed for multiple fulfillment across history.

### Daniel 12: Resurrection

The book ends with the only clear OT statement of individual bodily resurrection: "Multitudes who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake: some to everlasting life, others to shame and everlasting contempt" (12:2). This provides the linguistic and conceptual foundation for NT resurrection theology.

## Theology

### 1. Divine Sovereignty — The Central Theme

"In spite of present appearances, God is in control." Every chapter illustrates this from a different angle — from God giving Jehoiakim into Nebuchadnezzar's hand (1:2) through Daniel's success surpassing all rivals through every political crisis to the cosmic defeat of evil in the visions. The book is not primarily about Daniel's heroism; it is a revelation of God's character through his actions in history.

### 2. Faith Under Pressure: Resistance, Assimilation, and Wisdom

Chapters 1-6 illustrate how the faithful can live in a hostile culture. Key patterns:
- Daniel endures substantial assimilation (new name, pagan education, state service in a pagan court)
- He draws specific, carefully chosen lines (the food in ch. 1; prayer in ch. 6; interpretation of pagan dreams in ways that give God the credit)
- He acts with wisdom and tact (1:12-13: a quiet ten-day test rather than a public confrontation)
- He is "shrewd as snakes and innocent as doves" (Matt 10:16): neither withdrawing from culture nor accommodating to it wholesale

Longman: Daniel provides multiple strategies for cultural engagement, not a single blueprint. The book critiques both "resistance only" (culture war coercion) and cultural accommodation.

### 3. The Limits of Human Power

Every powerful human figure in Daniel (Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Darius) ultimately confronts the reality that human power is derived, temporary, and bounded by divine sovereignty. Nebuchadnezzar's madness (ch. 4) and Belshazzar's death on the night of the fall of Babylon (ch. 5) are paradigmatic.
