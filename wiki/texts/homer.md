The **Iliad** and the **Odyssey**, attributed to **Homer** (c. 8th c. BCE), are the foundational epics of
Greek culture and the great literary source for the **[[zeus|Olympian gods]] in action**. Though never
scripture, they functioned as a shared cultural and moral "Bible" — children learned the gods and the
heroic code from Homer.

## The Gods in Homer

Homer's gods are **vividly anthropomorphic**: they feast on Olympus, take sides, quarrel, deceive one
another, and intervene directly in human affairs — [[athena|Athena]] guiding Odysseus, [[apollo|Apollo]]
sending plague, [[aphrodite|Aphrodite]] rescuing her favorites, [[zeus|Zeus]] weighing fates and
restraining the rest. They are immortal and powerful but **not omniscient or morally perfect** — a portrait
the philosophers (Xenophanes, Plato) would later criticize and the allegorists defend.

- **Iliad** — the wrath of Achilles in the Trojan War; the gods as partisan participants; fate (*moira*)
  above even Zeus.
- **Odyssey** — Odysseus' homecoming under Athena's protection and Poseidon's wrath; hospitality (*xenia*)
  as a sacred, Zeus-guarded law.

## Significance

Homer fixed the **personalities and relationships** of the Greek gods for all later antiquity (and the
[[roman-religion|Roman]] inheritance through [[virgil-aeneid|Virgil]]), and posed the enduring problem of
the gods' morality that drove Greek philosophical theology.

## The Trojan War (in detail)

**The cause.** At the wedding of Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis, **Eris (Strife)** — uninvited — threw down a
golden apple marked "**to the fairest**." [[hera|Hera]], [[athena|Athena]], and [[aphrodite|Aphrodite]] each
claimed it, and [[zeus|Zeus]] sent them to the Trojan prince **Paris** to judge (the **Judgment of Paris**).
Aphrodite won by promising him the most beautiful woman alive — **Helen**, already wife of **Menelaus** of
Sparta. Paris carried Helen off to Troy, and Menelaus's brother **Agamemnon** led a vast Greek expedition to
win her back.

**The wrath of Achilles** (the subject of the *Iliad*). The poem covers only a few weeks in the war's tenth
year. When Agamemnon is forced to give up a captive girl, he seizes **Briseis** from the greatest Greek
warrior, **Achilles** — who, enraged, withdraws from battle and asks his mother Thetis to have Zeus turn the
tide against the Greeks. The Trojans, led by noble **Hector**, drive the Greeks back to their ships. Achilles
still refuses, until his beloved companion **Patroclus**, fighting in Achilles' armor, is killed by Hector.
Mad with grief and guilt, Achilles returns, slaughters the Trojans, and kills **Hector**, dragging his body
behind his chariot. The poem ends not with the city's fall but with the aged King **Priam** stealing into the
Greek camp (guided by [[hermes|Hermes]]) to ransom his son's body, and the funeral of Hector.

**The gods at war.** Throughout, the Olympians take sides and fight openly: Hera, Athena, and [[poseidon|
Poseidon]] for the Greeks; Aphrodite, [[apollo|Apollo]], and [[ares|Ares]] for the Trojans — wounded,
deceived, and overruled by Zeus, who holds the [[fate-moirai|scales of fate]] above them all.

**The fall of Troy** (told in the later epic cycle, not the *Iliad*). Achilles is slain by Paris's arrow
(guided by Apollo) to the heel; at last the Greeks feign departure, leaving the hollow **Wooden Horse** full
of warriors. The Trojans drag it inside; by night the Greeks pour out, and Troy is sacked and burned — the
escape of [[aphrodite|Aphrodite's]] son **Aeneas** carrying his household gods begins the thread that leads to
Rome ([[virgil-aeneid|the *Aeneid*]]).

**The homecoming** (the *Odyssey*). The second epic follows **Odysseus's** ten-year struggle to return to
Ithaca — the Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens, the descent to the dead, the wrath of [[poseidon|Poseidon]], and the
protection of [[athena|Athena]] — to reclaim his wife Penelope and his home.
