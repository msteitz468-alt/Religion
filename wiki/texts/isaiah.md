Isaiah is the wiki's first prophetic book — the first of the Latter Prophets — and one of the most theologically comprehensive texts in the entire OT canon. It is quoted more in the NT than all other prophetic books combined (21 direct quotations plus numerous allusions), and along with Psalms and Deuteronomy is one of the three most-cited OT texts in the NT. The Servant Songs of Isaiah 40-55, especially the fourth (52:13-53:12), constitute the most detailed OT prediction of the Messiah's atoning work.

The book addresses three different historical eras: the Assyrian crisis (Isaiah's lifetime, 740-701 BCE, chs. 1-39), the Babylonian exile (chs. 40-55), and the post-exilic return (chs. 56-66). Whether these three sections come from one author (Isaiah of Jerusalem) or two or three is the most contested issue in OT scholarship. Oswalt defends single authorship.

## Structure

| Section | Chapters | Historical Setting | Theological Theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 1-5 | Isaiah's lifetime | Problem: sinful yet called |
| The Pivot | 6 | Isaiah's call | Solution in miniature: Holy One, cleansing, commission |
| Chs. 7-39 | 7-39 | Assyrian crisis (735-701 BCE) | Trust: God vs. the nations |
| The Servant of God | 40-55 | Babylonian exile (550-538 BCE) | Motivation (40-48) and Means (49-55) of servanthood |
| Marks of Servanthood | 56-66 | Post-exilic return (539-500 BCE) | Human inability / Divine ability |

Within chs. 40-66 the structure is:
- **40-48**: God's declaration that he will demonstrate his deity by delivering Israel from Babylon (Cyrus named, 44:28; 45:1); sarcastic dismantling of idolatry (44:9-20)
- **49-55**: The Servant — the means whereby sinful Israel becomes servant Israel; climax at 52:13-53:12; joy in chs. 54-55 is only possible because of ch. 53
- **56-66**: The outworking of servanthood in the post-exilic community; chiastic structure (56-59 = human inability; 60-62 = divine promises; 63-66 = divine ability) ending with the eschatological climax fulfilling 2:1-5

## The Authorship Question

The dominant critical consensus since the 18th century:
- **First Isaiah** (chs. 1-39): Isaiah of Jerusalem, 740-701 BCE
- **Second Isaiah** (chs. 40-55): Anonymous Babylonian exilic prophet, ca. 550 BCE
- **Third Isaiah** (chs. 56-66): Postexilic author(s), ca. 520-500 BCE

The arguments for multiple authorship: (1) dramatic stylistic change at ch. 40 (the second part is more lyrical and exalted); (2) Cyrus of Persia named by name in 44:28 and 45:1, approximately 150 years before his birth; (3) the chapters seem to address the exile and post-exilic conditions as present realities, not future predictions; (4) the prior conviction (among many scholars) that specific predictive prophecy is not possible.

Oswalt's defense of single authorship:

**1. The self-defeating theology argument**: The central theological argument of chs. 40-55 is that YHWH is superior to the Babylonian gods because he alone can predict the future (41:22-23; 44:7-8; 46:10-11). If "Second Isaiah" actually wrote these chapters in 545 BCE — after Cyrus had already risen to power — then the theological proof he stakes his whole argument on is hollow. He would be manufacturing evidence for a theology about YHWH's ability to predict, while knowing that YHWH had not in fact predicted anything. The theology requires the authorship it claims.

**2. No external evidence for a Deutero-Isaiah**: Every extant manuscript presents the 66 chapters as one book. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa, Qumran, at least 100 BCE) is continuous and textually almost identical to the Masoretic Text — showing the book was in stable final form centuries before the common era with no division. No fragment of an independent "Second Isaiah" has ever been found.

**3. Contemporary scholarship admits ideological unity while struggling with origins**: Brueggemann: "Second Isaiah theologically is seen to be organically derived from First Isaiah." Rendtorff: Third Isaiah "depends so completely on I and II that an independent existence is hardly conceivable." Childs (canonical approach): the final form consciously suppressed historical references to strengthen theological unity. Oswalt's response: if the parts are organically related, the thesis of a complex multi-century redactional process becomes harder to sustain, not easier.

**4. The theological necessity argument**: The theology of chs. 1-39 necessarily demands chs. 40-66. If God defeats Assyria (chs. 36-37) but then loses to Babylon (chs. 38-39), the whole argument about God's trustworthiness collapses unless chs. 40-55 provide the answer in advance. The book has an internal logical structure that requires a single governing intelligence.

See [[servant-songs|Servant Songs / Suffering Servant]] for the implications of this debate for ch. 53's Christological status.

## The Holy One of Israel

Oswalt's governing theological appellation: *qĕdôš yiśrāʾēl* ("the Holy One of Israel") appears 29 times in Isaiah (13 in chs. 1-39; 16 in chs. 40-66) vs. only 7 times in the rest of the entire OT. This distribution is one of the strongest arguments for the book's unity — and it shapes every theological claim.

**Holiness = two inseparable dimensions:**

1. **Ontological otherness**: God is of a completely different order from all he has made — "high and lifted up" (6:1; 57:15); not merely superhuman but transcendent; "other than the psycho-socio-physical system of this world" (Oswalt). This is expressed through terms of height, removal, and transcendence throughout the book (6:1; 8:12-13; 40:25; 45:15; 55:8-9; 57:15).

2. **Moral and ethical perfection**: "The LORD of Hosts is exalted in justice, and the Holy God is sanctified in righteousness" (5:16). This is the primary characteristic that sets God apart — not power alone but moral purity. Isaiah's response to the vision is "I am unclean" (6:5) — not "I am finite" or "I am mortal" but *morally* defiled. The people's problem before the Holy One is fundamentally ethical, not ontological.

The implication for idolatry: the idol passages (44:9-20; 41:6-7; 46:1-7) are among the most sarcastic in all literature — who would cut a tree, use half for fuel, and bow down to the other half? The idols are "merely reflections of humanity with all its limitations." They cannot explain the past, predict the future, or do anything genuinely new. They are participants in the world system, not transcendent over it. Thus the worship of idols is not merely sinful — it is *stupid*.

## The Central Theme: Servanthood

Oswalt's most important structural contribution: the entire book is organized around the theme of **servanthood** — God's calling of his people to become the means through which his character and salvation are revealed to all nations (2:1-5: "the nations shall flow to [Zion]...").

**Chapter 6 as the book's hermeneutical key**: Isaiah's call experience is the prototype for the whole book's theology. The progression:
1. Vision of the Holy One (6:1-5) — both terrifying and glorious; Isaiah's response: "I am undone; I am a man of unclean lips"
2. Redeeming grace (6:6-8) — the coal from the altar touches his lips: "Your guilt is taken away, your sin atoned for"
3. Commission (6:8) — only then: "Whom shall I send?" / "Here am I, send me"

The rest of the book is a fleshing out of these three movements:
- Chs. 7-39 = the glory and justice of God (6:1-5)
- Chs. 40-55 = the grace and deliverance of God (6:6-8)
- Chs. 56-66 = the realities of living as God's servant (6:9-13)

## Key Theological Themes

### 1. God's Sovereignty Over History

What is unique to Isaiah among all biblical books: God is not merely the God of Israel but the Lord who uses pagan nations as his deliberate instruments. Assyria is "the rod of my anger" (10:5) — a tool in God's hand for judgment, yet also accountable to God for its own pride (10:12-19). Cyrus is God's *māšîaḥ* (anointed) and shepherd (44:28; 45:1) — the only non-Israelite to receive both covenantal titles in all Scripture. The series of oracles against the nations (chs. 13-23) are not merely denunciations of Israel's enemies; they demonstrate that from Babylon in the east to Tyre in the west, every nation is under God's judgment and serves his purposes.

The implication: "Zion might be his throne, but the earth was his stage." After Isaiah, Israel can never again think of God as merely a tribal deity whose relevance ends at her borders.

### 2. Sin as Pride and Rebellion

Sin for Isaiah = rebellion rooted in human pride — the refusal to accept one's creatureliness, the refusal to admit dependency. "Woe to you who say to wood, 'You are my father,' and to stone, 'You gave me birth'" (44:19-20, paraphrase). The sequence: alienation from God → unfaithfulness → oppression of others → destruction. This connects idolatry, adultery (of the covenant), and social injustice as inseparably linked: "for the prophets, idolatry, adultery, and oppression are always indissolubly linked" (Oswalt on 2:6-17).

The paradox: pretensions to greatness inevitably produce insignificance (2:11-17); surrender of those pretensions to God produces genuine significance (52:13-53:12; 65:13-16).

### 3. The Judgment-Redemption Dialectic

Judgment is never Isaiah's final word. In the structure of every major section, judgment is followed by or interwoven with redemption: chs. 1-5 (judgment) and 2:1-5 (redemption); ch. 6 (commission to judgment) with the reversal implicit in 6:11-13; chs. 28-33 (folly of human alliances) and ch. 35 (the garden of the redeemed); chs. 40-55 (redemption answering the judgment of chs. 1-39). Even the darkest oracles contain the seeds of restoration.

Judgment may become the vehicle for redemption (4:2-6): the Babylonian exile is not the defeat of God but the means by which a prideful people is finally prepared for servanthood.

### 4. Redemption Requires Atonement

God cannot merely declare the effects of sin suspended. "Sin and transgression must be covered, atoned for." This principle reaches its climax in 52:13-53:12. Many interpreters read the promises of chs. 40-48 (free grace, forgiveness, return) as unconditional. Oswalt argues they are not unconditional — there must be a moral and judicial satisfaction before the covenant curses can be reversed. Chapter 53 is precisely that satisfaction. This is why chs. 54-55, not chs. 40-48, are the great triumph songs: by the time of chs. 54-55 the Servant has acted (ch. 53), and only then is the covenant of peace possible (54:10).

### 5. The Messiah

Isaiah's Messiah is both the promised royal figure and the Suffering Servant — and both are in some sense identified with God himself. Key passages:
- **7:14**: "Behold, a virgin/young woman shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" — the sign given to Ahaz
- **9:1-7**: "For to us a child is born... his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace" — the royal child who rules in justice (9:5 in Hebrew)
- **11:1-9**: The branch from the stump of Jesse — the Spirit-anointed king who judges with righteousness; the peaceable kingdom
- **42:1-9; 49:1-13; 50:4-11; 52:13-53:12**: The Servant Songs — see [[servant-songs|Servant Songs]]

The Messiah in Isaiah unifies what might seem separate categories: the glorious royal figure of chs. 9 and 11 and the suffering figure of ch. 53 are aspects of the same person. This is why 52:13 describes the Servant as "high and lifted up" — the exact phrase used of God in 6:1 and 57:15, only used of God elsewhere in the book. The Servant's identity touches the divine.

## The Servant Songs

See [[servant-songs|Servant Songs / Suffering Servant]] for the full treatment. Summary:

The four Servant Songs (42:1-9; 49:1-13; 50:4-11; 52:13-53:12) move from calling (42) to commissioning (49) to obedient suffering (50) to vicarious, atoning death and vindication (52-53). The climax:

> "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned — every one — to his own way; and the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all." (53:5-6)

The "we" of ch. 53 is the prophet and the people he addresses — Israel's confession that the Servant suffered for them. The NT identifies this Servant with Jesus of Nazareth (Acts 8:30-35; Matt 8:17; 1 Pet 2:24; Luke 22:37; Rom 4:25).

## NT Connections

Isaiah is the single most-used OT source in the NT. Key NT appropriations:
- **Matt 1:23**: Isa 7:14 (Immanuel) — the virgin birth
- **Matt 4:15-16**: Isa 9:1-2 (Galilee of the Gentiles, great light)
- **Luke 4:18-19**: Isa 61:1-2 — Jesus' sermon at Nazareth ("Today this scripture is fulfilled")
- **John 12:38-41**: Isa 53:1 and 6:10 — quoted together, with the comment that "Isaiah said this because he saw his [Christ's] glory" — the most explicit NT identification of Jesus with both the Servant and the Holy One
- **Acts 8:30-35**: The Ethiopian eunuch reading Isa 53:7-8 — Philip's first text for explaining the gospel
- **Romans 9:27-29; 10:16; 10:20-21; 11:26**: Multiple Isaiah quotations in Paul's theology of Israel and the Gentiles
- **Romans 8:18-21**: *Mataiotes* (= *hebel*, Ecclesiastes) echoes Isa 40:6-8 (human transience) and the creation subjected to futility, awaiting liberation

## The Divine-Human Marriage Metaphor in Isaiah

Isaiah develops the covenant-as-marriage metaphor more extensively than any other OT book after Hosea/Ezekiel:
- "Your Maker is your husband" (54:5) — the fullest single statement of the metaphor
- God has not divorced his wife Israel (50:1) — the exile is a temporary separation, not abandonment
- The restoration as remarriage and renewal of vows (54:6-8; 62:4-5)

## Key Interpretive Controversies

**1. Single vs. multiple authorship** (see above): The dominant critical division

**2. The identity of the Servant in 52:13-53:12**: Historical individual (various proposals), collective Israel, or messianic prediction? Oswalt: "both/and" — the Servant is identified as Israel in some passages but is also the One who redeems Israel; ultimately individual, identified with divine power, and fulfilled in Jesus

**3. Isaiah and Job — direction of influence**: [[john-hartley|Hartley]] argued Isaiah was influenced *by* Job (Job's suffering and Servant paradigm → Isaiah's Servant Songs → NT Christology). Oswalt's Isaiah presents the Servant Songs as Isaiah's own prophetic revelation. The scholarly question of which direction the influence ran remains unresolved.

**4. Isaiah 7:14 — *ʿalmâ*** ("virgin" or "young woman"): The LXX translates with *parthenos* (virgin), the basis for Matt 1:23's Christological application. The Hebrew *ʿalmâ* means "young woman of marriageable age," not necessarily a virgin (*bĕtûlâ*). Whether the oracle was intended as a prediction of a literal virgin birth or was reapplied by Matthew to a deeper fulfillment is debated within evangelical scholarship.
