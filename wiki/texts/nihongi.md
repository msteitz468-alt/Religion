# The Nihongi (Nihon Shoki)

The **Nihongi** (日本紀) or **Nihon Shoki** (日本書紀, "Chronicles of Japan," **720 CE**) is the second of the
two great **[[shinto|Shinto]]** chronicles, completed eight years after the **[[kojiki|Kojiki]]** under Prince
Toneri. Where the Kojiki is a native-style sacred narrative, the Nihongi is the **official court history**,
written in **literary Chinese (kanbun)** in the continental annalistic style, and far more detailed and
"public." The wiki's source is **W. G. Aston's** translation (1896).

## Character and relation to the Kojiki

The Nihongi covers the same **age of the gods** and imperial line but is distinctive in method:

- it presents the myths in a **court-historiographical** frame aimed at a Chinese-literate, international
  (especially Korean-court) readership, giving Japan a dignified chronicle to match Chinese histories;
- crucially, for the age of the gods it **records multiple variant versions** of each myth side by side
  ("in one writing it is said…"), preserving **divergent traditions** the Kojiki harmonizes away — making it
  invaluable for seeing the **plurality** behind the "standard" myth;
- it continues **much further into historical times** (to 697 CE), with dense political, diplomatic, and
  ritual detail, including the **introduction of Buddhism** to Japan (552/538) and the resulting court
  conflicts.

## Significance

Together the Kojiki and Nihongi form the **mythic-historical foundation** of Shinto and of the Japanese state.
The Nihongi's preservation of **variant myths** is a textual-critical gift (a built-in record of competing
traditions, akin to a critical apparatus), and its account of the **arrival of Buddhism** documents the very
moment that produced the long fusion of [[folk-shinto|shinbutsu-shūgō]] and the need to name native worship
"Shintō." Its imperial chronology was, like the Kojiki's, pressed into the service of
**[[state-shinto|State Shinto]]**.
