# Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew (Origen)

[[origen|Origen]]'s *Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew* was originally 25 books. Books X–XIV survive in Greek; a Latin *Series Commentariorum* covers the Passion narrative. The Schaff/ANF edition includes Books I, II (fragmentary) and Books X–XIV (substantial). Written at Caesarea c. 246–248 CE, it is one of Origen's mature works.

## Books I–II — Fragments

Books I and II survive only as brief excerpts in the Schaff/ANF collection. Both are fragmentary; the surrounding books (III–IX) are lost entirely.

**Book I** (lines 39385–39397) preserves a single passage on the fourfold Gospel canon and its ordering. [[origen|Origen]] records that Matthew was written first, "in the Hebrew tongue and published it for the converts from Judaism" (line 39391); Mark second, "according to the instruction of Peter" who acknowledged him as a son in 1 Peter 5:13 (lines 39393–39396); Luke third, "the Gospel commended by Paul, which he composed for the converts from the Gentiles" (lines 39397–39398); and John last. This is one of the earliest patristic testimonies to the canonical ordering and to Matthean priority.

**Book II** (lines 39398–39442) is a fragment on the unity and harmony of Scripture, commenting on Matt 5:9 ("Blessed are the peacemakers"). Origen argues that the peacemaker par excellence is the expositor who demonstrates that apparent contradictions in Scripture are in fact harmonious: "he becomes a third peacemaker as he demonstrates that that which appears to others to be a conflict in the Scriptures is no conflict, and exhibits their concord and peace, whether of the Old Scriptures with the New, or of the Law with the Prophets, or of the Gospels with the Apostolic Scriptures" (lines 39407–39412). The key analogy is musical: "all the Scripture is the one perfect and harmonised instrument of God, which from different sounds gives forth one saving voice to those willing to learn" (lines 39435–39437). Those who hear discord are like the musically untrained; those instructed in "the music of God" are like David, who knew when to strike the chords of the Law, then the Gospel chords, then the Prophetic (lines 39426–39433). The fragment ends mid-argument. No further content from Books I or II survives in this edition.

## Book X — Parables and Herod

Book X covers Matthew 13:36–14:12, moving through the inner-circle parables (tares, treasure, pearl, dragnet, householder) and the death of [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] under Herod. Origen employs sustained allegory throughout; this book is among the richest surviving specimens of Alexandrian figurative exegesis applied to the Synoptics.

### Parable of the Tares (Matt 13:36–43)

Origen begins at Matt 13:36, where Jesus leaves the crowds and enters "His house." The house itself is a mystical category: disciples who are "more genuine hearers" follow Jesus into the house, whereas the multitudes remain outside (lines 39450–39459). The entry into the house signals that the explanation of the parable is reserved for those capable of more acute hearing: "for those who have the will and the power to hear them more acutely there are concealed things wise and worthy of the Word of God" (lines 39478–39480).

The allegorical key is supplied by Jesus's own exposition (Matt 13:37–43), which Origen extends. The good seed = "children of the kingdom," glossed as "wholesome words about anything" that are sown by God the Word; "whatsoever good things are sown in the human soul, these are the offspring of the kingdom of God" (lines 39488–39491). The tares = "evil opinions" sown by the devil while people sleep (lines 39495–39497). The field = not only the Church but "the whole world" — "in the whole world the Son of man sowed the good seed, but the wicked one tares — that is, evil words — which, springing from wickedness, are children of the evil one" (lines 39498–39501). The harvest/consummation = the moment when "the angels of God who have been appointed for this work may gather up the bad opinions that have grown upon the soul, and overturning them may give them over to fire which is said to burn, that they may be consumed" (lines 39503–39506). The gnashing of teeth = the self-reproach of those who, having been asleep, realize they received the seeds of the evil one (lines 39510–39513).

The shining of the righteous "as one sun" (Matt 13:43) receives a careful eschatological reading. Origen distinguishes between the present differentiation of glory among the saved (citing Dan 12:3 and 1 Cor 15:41–42) and the final state in which "the righteous having become one light of the sun shine in the kingdom of their Father" (lines 39550–39551). The convergence to one light follows only after all stumbling-blocks are removed from Christ's kingdom and the wicked come to self-consciousness; the righteous will then shine for those below them as the sun shines for those on the earth (lines 39545–39554).

### Parables of the Treasure, Pearl, and Householder (Matt 13:44–52)

Origen distinguishes between parables (spoken to the multitudes outside) and similitudes (spoken to the disciples in the house), arguing on the basis of Mark 4:30 that similitude is the higher genus containing parable as a species (lines 39582–39589). The treasure hidden in the field has a double identification: the field = Scripture ("planted with what is manifest in the words of the history, and the law, and the prophets"), and the treasure = "the thoughts concealed and lying under that which is manifest, of wisdom hidden in a mystery, even Christ, in whom are all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden" (Col 2:3; lines 39602–39609). Alternatively, the field = Christ himself and the treasure = "the things said to have been hidden in Christ by Paul" (lines 39610–39612). The man who hides the treasure after finding it is one who judges it "not without danger to reveal to everybody the secret meanings of the Scriptures" (lines 39629–39631) — a characteristic Origenian rationale for esoteric reserve.

For the pearl of great price, Origen inserts an extended natural-historical digression on pearl formation, drawing on lapidary sources (lines 39660–39714). The allegory follows: "the prophets be, so to speak, the mussels which conceive the dew of heaven, and become pregnant with the word of truth from heaven, the goodly pearls which the merchantman seeks" (lines 39725–39728). Christ himself is "the very costly pearl, the Christ of God, the Word which is superior to the precious letters and thoughts in the law and the prophets, on the finding of which also all the rest are easily taken" (lines 39729–39732). The heretical or heterodox words are the "muddy" or "darkened" pearls, those produced in marshes rather than in clear Indian waters (lines 39747–39750). The relation of the Law and Prophets to the Gospel is that of preparatory pearls to the one great pearl: "the perfect apprehension of the law and the prophets is an elementary discipline for the perfect apprehension of the Gospel, and all the meaning in the words and deeds of Christ" (lines 39800–39803).

The householder who brings forth things new and old (Matt 13:52) is read as Jesus himself, "who brings forth out of His treasury, according to the time of the teaching, things new, things spiritual … and old things, things written and engraven on stones" (lines 40107–40112). The scribe "made a disciple to the kingdom of heaven" first in a simple sense (a Jew converted to Jesus), then in a deeper sense (one who, having received elementary scriptural knowledge, "ascends to things spiritual, which are called the kingdom of the heavens," lines 40030–40032). Origen uses this to polemicize against those who "cleave in two the Godhead and cut off the New from the Old" (lines 40097–40099), i.e., Marcionites.

### Parable of the Dragnet (Matt 13:47–50)

Origen uses the dragnet explicitly to refute the Gnostic doctrine of fixed natures. The "kinds" of fish gathered do not correspond to ontologically distinct natures of righteous and wicked: "to such an interpretation all the Scriptures are opposed, which emphasise the freedom of the will" (lines 39827–39829). The reason fishes are good or bad lies not in their souls but in God's creative word; "here we are responsible for our being good kinds and worthy of what are called vessels, or bad and worthy of being cast outside. For it is not the nature in us which is the cause of the evil, but it is the voluntary choice which works evil" (lines 39839–39843). The argument is backed by Ezek 18:20–24 (the righteous man who turns to sin, the sinner who turns to righteousness), which Origen cites at length as evidence that moral change is real and that no fixed nature can explain it (lines 39851–39879).

The net itself is then re-allegorized positively: "the kingdom of heaven is likened unto the variegated texture of a net, with reference to the Old and the New Scripture which is woven of thoughts of all kinds and greatly varied" (lines 39888–39891). Each part of the net corresponds to a part of Scripture — prophetic, legal, Gospel, apostolic — and people are caught in whichever part of Scripture first reaches them (lines 39893–39899). The sea into which the net is cast = "the wave-tossed life of men in every part of the world, and which swims in the bitter affairs of life" (lines 39904–39906). Before Jesus, "the net of the law and the prophets had to be completed by Him who says, Think not that I came to destroy the law and the prophets, I came not to destroy but to fulfil" (lines 39907–39909). The angels who draw up the net do so only when "the fullness of the Gentiles has come into it" (line 39919).

### Herod's Birthday and the Death of John the Baptist (Matt 14:1–12)

Origen opens the discussion of Herod by analyzing the popular confusion between Jesus and John (Matt 14:1–2). He reviews three possibilities: (1) Herod mistakenly thought the beheaded John had literally risen under a new name; (2) some held the false dogma of transmigration of souls (metempsychosis), imagining John reborn as Jesus — but Origen rejects this because the gap between births (only six months) makes it implausible (lines 40428–40430); (3) most likely, Herod thought "the powers which wrought in John had passed over to Jesus" (lines 40431–40433), analogous to the way the spirit of Elijah passed to John (Matt 11:14; lines 40435–40437).

The historical narrative of John's imprisonment is given an allegorical overlay: "as the law and the prophets were until John, after whom the grace of prophecy ceased from among the Jews; so the authority of those who had rule among the people, which included the power to kill those whom they thought worthy of death, existed until John" (lines 40464–40469). Herod's imprisonment of John = "he bound and imprisoned the prophetic word, and prevented him from continuing to abide a herald the truth in freedom" (lines 40484–40486).

The birthday feast and Herodias's daughter receive sustained allegory. Origen observes that no righteous man in Scripture is recorded keeping a birthday, whereas both Pharaoh (Gen 40:20) and Herod do — the latter being worse, for "by the former on his birthday feast a chief baker is killed; but by the former, John, than whom no one greater has risen among those born of women" (lines 40521–40524). "The wife of the king of Trachonitis — which is a kind of evil opinion and wicked teaching — gave birth to a daughter of the same name, whose movements, seemingly harmonious, pleasing Herod, who was fond of matters connected with birthdays, came the cause of there being no longer a prophetic head among the people" (lines 40505–40509). The dancing of Herodias's daughter = "the movements of the people of the Jews, which seem to be according to the law" but which are opposed to "that holy dancing with which those who have not danced will be reproached when they hear the words, We piped unto you, and you did not dance" (lines 40510–40514). The beheading of John = the Jews cutting off the "head of prophecy" because they "disown the crown of all prophecy, Christ Jesus" (lines 40534–40536). Origen adds a practical application: "not every keeping of oaths is seemly, just as the keeping of the oath of Herod was not" (lines 40545–40546).

Jesus's withdrawal to the desert after John's death (Matt 14:13) is read as withdrawal to the Gentiles: "when prophecy was plotted against among the Jews … then Jesus withdraws from the place in which prophecy was attacked and condemned; and He withdraws to the place which had been barren of God among the Gentiles, in order that the Word of God, when the kingdom was taken from the Jews and given to a nation bringing forth the fruits thereof, might be among the Gentiles" (lines 40583–40589). The "boat" in which he travels = the body; the "desert place apart" = the teaching of Christ standing "contrary to the customs and usages which obtained among the Gentiles" (lines 40597–40599). The crowds who followed "by land, not in a boat" = those who followed "not with the body but with the soul only" (lines 40603–40604). Book X ends with a discussion of the spiritual "sicknesses" (greed, vainglory, lust) that Jesus healed, and the principle that the sick must be healed before they can participate in "the loaves of blessing" (lines 40660–40674).

## Book XI — Feeding, Walking on Water, Peter's Confession

Book XI covers Matthew 14:13–17:9. Origen's treatment is densely allegorical throughout, consistently reading narrative details as figures of the soul's progress, the relation between disciples and multitudes, and the transition from the Law to the Gospel age.

### Feeding of the Five Thousand (Matt 14:13–21)

Book XI opens by re-reading the temporal markers of the feeding miracle. "When even had come" = the eschatological moment: "at the consummation of the age in regard to which we may fitly say what is found in the Epistle of John, It is the last hour" (lines 40680–40682). The disciples' complaint — "The place is desert and the time is already past" (Matt 14:15) — receives an allegorical interpretation: "The place is desert" = "the desert condition of the masses in respect of God and the Law and the Word"; "The time is past" = "the fitting season of the law and prophets had passed" after the beheading of John (lines 40683–40690). The disciples' proposal to send the crowds to "villages" (places "more ignoble") = their despair "that, after the letter of the law had been abrogated and prophecies had ceased, … unexpected and new food being found for the multitudes" (lines 40695–40698).

Jesus's command, "Give ye them to eat," is addressed to disciples whom "He has trained … and made … fit to give rational food" (lines 40706–40707). The five loaves = "the sensible words of the Scriptures, corresponding in number on this account to the five senses"; the two fishes = "the word expressed and the word conceived, which are a relish, so to speak, to the sensible things contained in the Scriptures; or, perhaps, to the word which had come to them about the Father and the Son" (lines 40723–40728). The barley loaves noted by John alone (John 6:9) are distinguished from the unspecified loaves of the Synoptics (lines 40738–40742). The loaves multiply only when taken by the Saviour: "when the Saviour took them, and in the first place looked up to heaven, with the rays of His eyes, as it were, drawing down from it power which was to be mingled with the loaves and the fishes" (lines 40745–40748).

The command to sit on the grass (Matt 14:19) is read through Isa 40:6 ("All flesh is grass"): Jesus "commanded them to put the flesh under, and to keep in subjection the mind of the flesh, that so any one might be able to partake of the loaves which Jesus blesses" (lines 40806–40809). The division into companies of a hundred and fifty (Mark 6:39–40; Luke 9:14) symbolizes two sacred orders: the hundred = "the sacred number … consecrated to God, because of the unit," and the fifty = "the number which embraces the remission of sins, in accordance with the mystery of the Jubilee which took place every fifty years, and of the feast at Pentecost" (lines 40818–40822). The twelve baskets of leftovers = the twelve disciples, who correspond to the twelve tribes, each basket being food for its tribe (lines 40822–40828). The "five thousand men, beside children and women" (Matt 14:21) is read allegorically: children = those who are "babes in Christ" (1 Cor 3:1); women = those whom Paul wishes to "present as a pure virgin to Christ" (2 Cor 11:2); men = those who have "put away childish things" (1 Cor 13:11) — "those who ate had reached the stage of sensible things" (lines 40791–40796, 40768).

### Disciples and Multitudes Contrasted; Walking on Water (Matt 14:22–34)

Origen uses the moment when Jesus "constrained the disciples to enter into the boat" (Matt 14:22) as the occasion for an extended meditation on the distinction between disciples and multitudes in the Gospels. The multitudes could not go to the other side "as they were not in the mystic sense Hebrews, which are by interpretation, dwelling on the other side. But this was the work of the disciples of Jesus — I mean to go away to the other side, and to pass beyond things seen and material, as temporal, and to go on to things unseen and eternal" (lines 40907–40911). To be dismissed by Jesus (the multitudes' portion) requires first eating his blessed loaves; to cross to the other side (the disciples' portion) requires yet more.

The boat itself is read as "the conflict of temptations and difficulties into which any one is constrained by the Word, and goes unwillingly, as it were, when the Saviour wishes to train by exercise the disciples" (lines 40944–40947). Jesus deliberately separated the disciples from himself, "wishing to teach them by experience that it was not possible apart from Him to go to the other side" (lines 40938–40940). The disciples reached only the middle of the sea — the midst of temptation — before the contrary wind stopped them. Then "the Word, taking compassion upon them who had done all that was in their power to reach the other side, came to them walking upon the sea, which for Him had no waves or wind that was able to oppose if He so willed; for it is not written, He came to them walking upon the waves, but, upon the waters" (lines 40972–40976). Origen marks the textual detail carefully: the distinction between "waves" and "waters" is significant.

Peter's walking on the water repeats the distinction: "Peter, who at first when Jesus said to him, Come, went down from the boat and walked not upon the waves, but upon the waters to come to Jesus; but when he doubted he saw that the wind was strong, which was not strong to him who laid aside his little faith and his doubting. But, when Jesus went up with Peter into the boat, the wind ceased, as it had no power to energise against the boat when Jesus had gone up into it" (lines 40976–40982). Faith is the operative category: the wind's strength is real only to the one whose faith fails; when faith is full, the wind cannot energize against the disciple.

**Conclusion of Walking on Water; Peter's Little Faith (lines 41001–41049)**

Origen continues the allegory of Matt 14:27–31. The three watches of the night through which the disciples struggled correspond to resistance against a trinitarian opposition of evil: "the first watch against the father of darkness and wickedness, the second watch against his son who opposes and exalts himself against all that is called God… and the third watch against the spirit that is opposed to the Holy Spirit" (lines 41022–41026). Peter's sinking is not the sinking of one without faith but of one with imperfect faith: Origen marks the textual precision — Jesus says not "O you without faith" but "O you of little faith" (lines 41047–41048), and notes the parallel *wherefore did you doubt*, indicating "he had still a measure of faith, but also had a tendency towards that which was opposed to faith" (lines 41048–41049).

**Pharisees on Hand-Washing; the Corban Rule (lines 41087–41270)**

At Matt 15:1–9, the Pharisees challenge the disciples about the tradition of washing hands before meals. Origen observes that the Pharisees bring no charge involving a commandment of God, only "a single tradition of the Jewish elders" (lines 41111–41113), which he reads as testimony to the disciples' actual piety. He explains the Corban mechanism through a Hebrew informant: creditors could declare a debt "Corban" (a gift consecrated to the poor), binding the debtor to pay under the name of God rather than man; similarly, sons told parents that money owed them was Corban and thereby evaded filial support (lines 41178–41197). He draws a present-day application: a church official who diverts property from needy kin to the common fund under pretext of benefiting the poor "might with justice be called a brother of those Pharisees who made void the word of God through their own tradition" (lines 41220–41222). The passage ends with an extended parallel to Judas, "who in appearance championed the cause of the poor… but in reality was a thief, and having the bag took away what was put therein" (lines 41226–41230); anyone who similarly handles the church's funds is assigned "the portion along with Judas" (lines 41232–41233).

**Isaiah and the Traditions; Clean and Unclean (lines 41274–41432)**

Origen explains why Matthew quotes Isa 29:13 ("This people draws near to Me with their mouth") in an abbreviated form different from the LXX (lines 41279–41282). The Isaiah passage predicts that the eyes of Israel's rulers and prophets will be closed, making the prophetic words "as the words of a sealed book" — a prophecy Origen says has been fulfilled since Israel denied the Saviour: "from the time at which they denied our Saviour, it might be said about them by God, But in vain do they worship Me" (lines 41327–41329).

On clean and unclean (Matt 15:10–11), Origen argues that if "not that which enters into the mouth defiles the man, but that which proceeds out of the mouth" (lines 41360–41361), then the Levitical dietary categories are spiritually reinterpreted, not abolished. Mark's note that Jesus "made all meats clean" (Mk 7:19) is the key text (lines 41362–41363). The genuinely defiling foods are those "supplied by covetousness, and are the result of base love of gain, and are taken up from love of pleasure, and from deifying the belly" (lines 41396–41399). He cites Titus 1:15 — "To them that are defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure, since both their minds and their conscience are defiled" — to ground the principle that purity is a function of the soul's orientation, not the food's material nature (lines 41386–41389). The teaching is aimed in part against the Ebionites, who "differ little from the material Jews" in insisting on literal dietary observance (lines 41357–41359).

**The Canaanitish Woman and the Lost Sheep of Israel (lines 41700–41842)**

Origen reads Matt 15:21–28 allegorically. The Canaanitish woman is a figure for those of a lower rational class who nonetheless have great faith. Jesus's declaration that He was sent "not but unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matt 15:24) is interpreted as referring not to ethnic Israel but to pre-eminently rational souls: "a lost race of souls possessed of clear vision" (lines 41741–41742). Because these souls mostly rejected the Word, God chose "the foolish things of the world… that He might put to shame the wise ones of Israel" (lines 41778–41780; cf. 1 Cor 1:27). The woman's acceptance of the "little dog" designation and her insistence on receiving even crumbs models a soul that, though born into a lower intellectual class, advances through humility and faith to the condition of a son: "that which at one time was a dog, loving to eat of the crumbs that fell from the table of its masters, comes into the condition of a son" (lines 41826–41828). Origen is explicit that he rejects metempsychosis as an explanation for such advance: the change is one of rational disposition, not of the soul entering an animal body (lines 41818–41825).

### Sign of Jonah; Adulterous Generation (Matt 16:1–12; lines 42003–42330)

Origen opens Book XII by noting that the Pharisees and Sadducees, who disagree on the resurrection, unite against Jesus — a pattern he explicitly parallels with Herod and Pilate becoming friends to kill Jesus (Luke 23:12; lines 42015–42028). He explains why Jesus calls them "an adulterous generation" (Matt 16:4): they are "adulterous" because, leaving "the word of truth or the law," which is figuratively their husband, they were "debauched by falsehood and the law of sin" (lines 42155–42157). Drawing on Rom 7:2–3, Origen argues that the law is the soul's lawful husband; the soul under the law's influence who turns to the flesh commits adultery (lines 42157–42200).

The sign of Jonah (Matt 16:4) is Jesus's response: he did not refuse them a sign from heaven but gave them one — the resurrection. "If, as Jonah passed three days and three nights in the whale's belly, so the Son of man did in the heart of the earth, and after this rose up from it — whence but from heaven shall we say that the sign of the resurrection of Christ came?" (lines 42122–42125). The sign of Jonah thus prefigured the resurrection.

Origen interprets the "leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (Matt 16:6) as their "truly ancient leaven — that according to the bare letter, and on this account not freed from those things which arise from wickedness" (lines 42237–42239). Jesus offers instead "a new and spiritual lump, offering Himself to those who gave up the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees and had come to Him — the living bread which came down from heaven and gives life to the world" (lines 42241–42244; John 6:33). Origen also uses the disciples' misunderstanding as an occasion to argue that secret thoughts — "the reasonings only which we reason within ourselves" — are subject to divine judgment and censure, since Jesus knew their hearts without being told (lines 42335–42337).

### Peter's Confession; the Rock and the Keys (Matt 16:13–20; lines 42397–42703)

This is the theological centre of Book XII. [[origen|Origen]] gives Peter's confession the richest ecclesiological treatment in the surviving commentary. His central move is to democratize the "rock" and "keys" promises: they belong not to Peter alone but to every believer who makes Peter's confession in the same spirit.

The crowds' opinions about Jesus (John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah, one of the prophets) are explained by the fact that each prophet "took upon themselves certain features that were symbolic of Him" without fulfilling those prophecies in their own persons (lines 42452–42455). Peter, by contrast, received not a human opinion but a divine revelation: he is "not a disciple of flesh and blood… but… one fit to receive the revelation of the Father in heaven" (lines 42425–42427).

On Matt 16:18 ("You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church"), Origen gives his foundational universalizing reading: "if we too have said like Peter, You are the Christ, the Son of the living God, not as if flesh and blood had revealed it unto us, but by light from the Father in heaven having shone in our heart, we become a Peter, and to us there might be said by the Word, You are Peter, etc. For a rock is every disciple of Christ of whom those drank who drank of the spiritual rock which followed them" (lines 42469–42475; citing 1 Cor 10:4). Origen explicitly universalizes the surname: "all bear the surname of rock who are the imitators of Christ, that is, of the spiritual rock which followed those who are being saved… they are called Christians, and from the rock, Peters" (lines 42512–42518).

Against a reading that restricts the promises to Peter alone, Origen poses a direct challenge: "if you suppose that upon that one Peter only the whole church is built by God, what would you say about John the son of thunder or each one of the Apostles? Shall we otherwise dare to say, that against Peter in particular the gates of Hades shall not prevail, but that they shall prevail against the other Apostles and the perfect?" (lines 42482–42488). The same logic extends to the keys: the binding and loosing power is given "to every one who becomes such as that Peter was" (lines 42510–42512).

The "gates of Hades" are interpreted as species of sin and false doctrine: "each of the sins through which there is a way to Hades is a gate of Hades" (lines 42542–42544). Origen lists specific heresies: "Marcion one gate, and Basilides another, and Valentinus another" (lines 42584–42585). The keys of the kingdom are correspondingly multiple — one key per virtue — for "for every virtue of knowledge certain mysteries of wisdom corresponding to the species of the virtue are opened up to him who has lived according to virtue; the Saviour giving to those who are not mastered by the gates of Hades as many keys as there are virtues, which open gates equal in number" (lines 42653–42657).

Origen is equally direct about the limits of episcopal authority: bishops who invoke Matt 16:19 ("I will give unto you the keys") speak "wholesomely if they have the way of life on account of which it was said to that Peter, You are Peter" (lines 42684–42686). One who is "tightly bound with the cords of his sins… to no purpose does he bind and loose" (lines 42689–42690). The passage is one of Origen's sharpest critiques of clerical presumption.

### Peter as Stumbling-Block; Injunction of Silence (Matt 16:20–23; lines 42704–43016)

Following the confession, Jesus commands silence about his messianic identity (Matt 16:20). Origen uses this to distinguish belief from knowledge: "the belief that Jesus is the Christ is inferior to the knowledge of that which is believed" (lines 42737–42739). Peter's knowledge at the moment of blessing was the highest form — knowledge received "by the revelation of the Father" (lines 42756–42757) — a level higher than what the Twelve had previously held.

When Peter rebukes Jesus's Passion prediction and is called "Satan" (Matt 16:22–23), Origen offers two interpretations. One takes the rebuke as demonic: Peter, at the Transfiguration, spoke "under the influence of some spirit… not a Holy Spirit," perhaps the spirit Jesus also called a "stumbling-block" at Matt 16:23 (lines 42563–42570). The other interpretation reads Peter's words as well-intentioned but spiritually uninstructed: he "held it to be a shameful thing and unworthy of Jesus to admit that the Son of the living God… should be killed" (lines 42628–42630). "These contrary qualities, accordingly, were still in Peter truth and falsehood; and from truth he said, You are the Christ, the Son of the living God, but from falsehood he said, May God be propitious to You, Lord, this shall not be unto You" (lines 42619–42623).

### Transfiguration — Moses, Elijah, the Cloud (Matt 17:1–9; lines 43231–43757)

Origen provides one of the most elaborate allegorical readings of the Transfiguration in patristic literature. The "six days" before the ascent are not incidental but carry meaning: since the world was created in six days, the soul who "transcends all the things of the world by beholding no longer the things which are seen, for they are temporal, but already the things which are not seen, because that they are eternal, is represented in the words, After six days Jesus took up with Him certain persons" (lines 43450–43456).

The transfiguration itself is reader-relative: "the Word has different forms, as He appears to each as is expedient for the beholder, and is manifested to no one beyond the capacity of the beholder" (lines 43470–43472). Jesus is "transfigured before them" — before those who ascend, not all. The face shining as the sun means Christ "will shine unto them not simply as the sun, but as demonstrated to be the sun of righteousness" (lines 43500–43501). The white garments are allegorized as "the expressions and letters of the Gospels with which He invested Himself" (lines 43506–43507): when the Gospels' full meaning is grasped, "the garments of Jesus have become white as the light" (lines 43515–43516).

Moses and Elijah are read typologically: "Moses — the law — and Elijah — in the way of synecdoche, not one prophet only, but all the prophets — holding converse with Jesus" (lines 43519–43521). The disciple who sees the transfigured Christ simultaneously sees "the glory of Moses, having understood the spiritual law as a discourse in harmony with Jesus, and the wisdom in the prophets which is hidden in a mystery, … sees Moses and Elijah in glory when he sees them with Jesus" (lines 43524–43528).

The bright cloud (Matt 17:5) is given a trinitarian interpretation: it may be the Father (from whom the voice comes), or the Holy Spirit, or the Saviour himself — or all three: "a bright cloud of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit overshadows the genuine disciples of Jesus; or a cloud overshadows the Gospel and the law and the prophets, which is bright to him who is able to see the light of it in the Gospel, and the law, and the prophets" (lines 43702–43706). The voice's address — "This is My beloved Son in whom I am well-pleased, hear Him" (Matt 17:5) — is read as God speaking to Moses and Elijah, who had longed to see the Son of man (lines 43707–43713).

After the cloud, the disciples see "Jesus only and no other" (Matt 17:8): "Moses, the law, and Elijah, the prophet, became one only with the Gospel of Jesus; and not, as they were formerly three, did they so abide, but the three became one" (lines 43730–43732). The injunction of silence until the resurrection (Matt 17:9) is because the vision of glory, known before the Passion, would have caused the crowds to stumble when they saw Jesus crucified (lines 43752–43757).

### Elijah and John the Baptist — Spirit and Power, Not Soul (Matt 17:10–13; lines 43758–43899)

[[origen|Origen]] opens Book XIII with one of his most precise theological distinctions: the relation between [[elijah|Elijah]] and [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] is not metempsychosis (transmigration of souls) but the sharing of a *spirit and power*.

The disciples ask why the scribes say Elijah must come first (Matt 17:10), confused because Elijah appeared at the Transfiguration apparently *after* rather than before Jesus. Jesus's answer is that John was the Elijah who came first, but the scribes — not recognising him — did to him what they wished (Matt 17:12), foreshadowing what they would do to Jesus (lines 43771–43779).

Origen then raises and refuses the transmigration reading. His argument is three-pronged. First, transmigration conflicts with Scripture's teaching that the world has a consummation and that souls are punished outside the body after death, not recycled through it (lines 43786–43830). Second, if souls transmigrate, there would be no fixed number of sinners at the eschatological moment, contradicting Matt 24:37–39 and Luke 18:8 (lines 43813–43820). Third, and decisively, Gabriel's words to Zechariah settle the matter: "he shall go before his face *in the spirit and power of Elijah*" (Luke 1:17; lines 43874–43876). Origen notes the precise wording: Gabriel did not say "in the soul of Elijah" — "in which case the doctrine of transmigration might have some ground" — but "in the spirit and power of Elijah. For the Scripture well knows the distinction between spirit and soul" (lines 43875–43883; citing 1 Thess 5:23). The spirit of Elijah that rested on Elisha (2 Kgs 2:15) is the precedent: the same prophetic spirit can inhabit successive bearers without any soul transmigrating. "Elijah, therefore, was not called John because of the soul, but because of the spirit and the power, which in no way conflicts with the teaching of the church" (lines 43883–43885).

He then raises the further question of whether the spirit of Elijah is identical with the Spirit of God in Elijah or distinct from it, citing Rom 8:16 ("The Spirit itself bears witness with our spirit that we are the children of God") as evidence that God's Spirit and the human spirit are distinct even when united (lines 43890–43899).

### The Temple Tax; Peter and the Fish (Matt 17:24–27; lines 44282–44381)

Origen interprets the Temple tax pericope (Matt 17:24–27) as an allegory about freedom, sonship, and the nature of Christ. The structure is: there are earthly kings whose sons do not pay tribute, and strangers who do. Jesus and his disciples are sons — free — but pay the tax anyway to avoid causing offense.

The key Christological point is that the coin was not in Jesus's possession: "this coin was not in the house of Jesus, but it was in the sea, and in the mouth of a fish of the sea which, in my judgment, was benefited when it came up and was caught in the net of Peter, who became a fisher of men, in which net was that which is figuratively called a fish, in order also that the coin with the image of Caesar might be taken from it" (lines 44299–44304). Jesus has no Caesar's image because "Jesus, who was the image of the invisible God, had not the image of Caesar, for the prince of this age had nothing in Him" (lines 44307–44309; cf. John 14:31, Col 1:15). He takes the coin from the sea — the world — to pay what the earthly kings demand, without that image ever having been part of himself or his possessions.

A secondary allegory applies the fish to the lover of money healed by a Peter: "you will say that such an one was in the sea, and in the bitter affairs of life, and in the waves of the cares and anxieties of avarice, having the stater in his mouth when he was unbelieving and avaricious, but that he came up from the sea and was caught in the rational net, and being benefited by some Peter who has taught him the truth, no longer has the stater in his mouth, but in place of it those things which contain His image, the oracles of God" (lines 44361–44367). Origen also draws on Num 3:47 to distinguish the secular half-shekel from the "sacred half-shekel" paid for the saints of God; Jesus cannot possess the profane shekel, so the sacred coin is found in the sea (lines 44369–44381).

### Who is Greatest? Conversion to Childhood (Matt 18:1–4; lines 44382–44500)

The disciples' question "Who then is greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" (Matt 18:1) is explained by the immediately preceding context: Peter had been singled out by Jesus to catch the tax fish, giving the impression of special honor, and the disciples wanted to know whether this meant Peter outranked them (lines 44403–44410). The Transfiguration, where three were chosen over nine, added further uncertainty about rank (lines 44414–44426).

Origen's answer begins with a philosophical distinction: greatness admits of degrees — Isaac "grew great and became exceedingly great" (Gen 26:13; lines 44459–44460), implying there is great, exceedingly great, and exceedingly exceedingly great. Rather than naming one person greatest, Jesus shows "what was the quality of him who was greatest in the kingdom of heaven" (lines 44475–44476) by calling a child.

Origen's allegorical reading of the child focuses on the soul's pre-passional state. One who "mortifies the lusts of manhood, putting to death by the spirit the deeds of the body, and always bearing about in the body the putting to death of Jesus… to such a degree that he has the condition of the little child who has not tasted sensual pleasures, and has had no conception of the impulses of manhood, then such an one is converted, and has become as the little children" (lines 44483–44489). The same logic extends to anger, grief, and "all the affections and infirmities and sicknesses of the soul, into which it is not the nature of little children to fall" (lines 44494–44497). Greatness in the kingdom = the degree to which an adult has recovered this pre-passional simplicity. The passage ends at line 44500 mid-argument as Book XIII continues into Matt 18 content beyond this agent's range.

**Book XIII continuation (lines 44501–45215) — Little Ones, Angels, and the Power to Bind**

*(Agent R: lines 44501–45215)*

The continuation of Book XIII extends from the stumbling-block passages through Origen's allegorical reading of the "little child," the theology of guardian angels, the sinning brother, and the authority to bind and loose.

*The little child as the Holy Spirit (§18, ll. 44599–44645).* Origen proposes what he calls "another interpretation different from what is called the simpler": "consider if you can say that the little child, whom Jesus called, was the Holy Spirit who humbled Himself, when He was called by the Saviour, and set in the midst of the reason of the disciples of Jesus; if, indeed, He wishes us, being turned away from everything else, to be turned towards the examples suggested by the Holy Spirit, so that we may so become as the little children" (ll. 44603–44608). He supports this from Isa 8:18 ("Behold, I and the little children which God has given to me"), concluding that the Holy Spirit, "descending from His own perfection to men as a little child, was set by Jesus in the midst of the disciples" (ll. 44615–44616). Therefore "to humble oneself as that little child is to imitate the Holy Spirit, who humbled Himself for the salvation of men" (ll. 44619–44621).

*Parallel passages in Mark and Luke (§19, ll. 44648–44705).* Origen notes that Luke uniquely places the child "by His side" rather than merely "in the midst," and reads this as meaning each believer must personally receive from Jesus the same little child who "lives as immortal" (l. 44681). To receive the child, the Son, and the Father is to become "least of all the disciples of Jesus" yet thereby great (ll. 44689–44694).

*The word "world" (*cosmos*) and the woe of stumbling blocks (§§20–23, ll. 44706–44849).* Origen gives an extended semantic analysis of *cosmos* in Matt 18:7, "Woe unto the world because of occasions of stumbling." He distinguishes several biblical usages and concludes that "world" here means "only the place which is round about the earth" (ll. 44718–44721). Disciples who are "not of the world" cannot suffer the woe; only those who love the world as belonging to it are subject. On the necessity of stumbling blocks, Origen insists they arise not from divine creation but from free will: "free-will begot the stumbling-blocks in some who did not wish to endure toils for virtue" (ll. 44847–44849).

*The hand, foot, and eye allegorized (§§24–25, ll. 44850–44909).* Origen reads the bodily members as figures for members of the church and of the soul, and also as nearest kin: "let us cut off from ourselves as a hand or a foot or an eye, a father or mother who wishes us to do that which is contrary to piety" (ll. 44891–44893).

*The little ones and their angels (§§26–29, ll. 44910–45047).* Origen draws on Matt 18:10 ("their angels always behold the face of My Father") to develop a theology of souls and guardian angels. Souls have varying spiritual "stature"; little ones are guarded by angels, while the great have passed beyond such guardianship and are "governed by the Lord Himself" (ll. 44976–44977). He then raises the striking possibility that a guardian angel, wicked at first, may itself advance in holiness in proportion as the soul it guards advances in faith: "it is possible that the angel, to whom any soul has been entrusted at birth, may be wicked at the first, but afterwards may at some time believe in proportion as the man believes, and may make such advance that he may become one of the angels who always behold the face of the Father in heaven" (ll. 45010–45015). This is one of Origen's clearest hints at the universal mobility of rational beings between states.

*The sinning brother and the binding power (§§30–31, ll. 45077–45215).* On Matt 18:15, Origen refuses both to extend pardon promiscuously to great sins and to apply it mechanically to minor ones; he notes that Jesus left what happens after each stage deliberately open. On Matt 18:18 (binding and loosing), Origen distinguishes the communal authority given to all who thrice admonish a sinning brother from Peter's unique prerogative: "it is no small difference that Peter received the keys not of one heaven but of more, and in order that whatsoever things he binds on the earth may be bound not in one heaven but in them all, as compared with the many who bind on earth and loose on earth, so that these things are bound and loosed not in the heavens, as in the case of Peter, but in one only" (ll. 45208–45213). Book XIII closes with the transition formula at ll. 45215–45216.

## Book XIV — Harmony, Forgiveness, Unmerciful Servant, Divorce (Matt 18:19–19:11)

*(Agent R: lines 45216–46509)*

Book XIV as Origen marks it (opening at l. 45216) covers Matt 18:19 through Matt 19:11. The passages anticipated in the scaffold (Passion prediction Matt 20:17–19, sons of Zebedee 20:20–28, Triumphal Entry 21:1–11, Sadducees 22:23–33) do not appear in this range; those pericopes fall beyond line 46509 and await a further range.

**Symphony/harmony in prayer (§§1–4, ll. 45218–45388, Matt 18:19–20).** Origen opens Book XIV with a sustained meditation on *symphonia* (agreement/concord) as the condition for answered prayer. He traces the term through musical usage (Luke 15:25, "a symphony and dancing") and applies it to apostolic partnerships: Paul and Sosthenes writing 1 Corinthians, Paul and Timothy writing 2 Corinthians, Paul and Silvanus and Timothy writing to the Thessalonians (ll. 45264–45269) — all constitute symphonies on earth. He then offers three further allegorical interpretations of "the two who agree on earth": (a) husband and wife who abstain from marital relations by *symphonia* for prayer (§2, ll. 45320–45337; 1 Cor 7:5); (b) body and spirit in the perfected soul, when spirit has "put to death the deeds of the body" so that both offer a single harmonious prayer (§3, ll. 45338–45369); (c) the Old and New Covenants themselves, with the Holy Spirit as the unifying "third" (§4, ll. 45370–45388). On the phrase "there am I in the midst of them" (Matt 18:20), Origen marks a verbal precision: Jesus said not "there shall I be" but "there am I": "not going to be, not delaying, but at the very moment of the concord being Himself found, and being in the midst of them" (ll. 45385–45388).

**The limit of forgiveness (§5, ll. 45389–45430, Matt 18:21–22).** Peter's question ("how often shall my brother sin against me?") and Jesus' answer of seventy-times-seven receive an eschatological reading. Origen holds that "six" represents worldly toil and "seven" the end of sin in this age (ll. 45408–45413); forgiveness extends to sins committed in this world, but "there is no forgiveness, not even to a brother, who has sinned beyond the seven and seventy times" (ll. 45424–45426) — a sin transcending the earthly order admits no remission.

**The unmerciful servant parable (§§6–13, ll. 45431–45930, Matt 18:23–35).** One of the most sustained allegorical expositions in the Commentary on Matthew. Origen identifies "the kingdom of heaven" likened to a king as Christ himself: "who must we say that it is but the Son of God? For He is the King of the heavens, and as He is absolute Wisdom and absolute Righteousness and absolute Truth, is He not so also absolute Kingdom?" (ll. 45500–45503). The king's reckoning with servants he refers to the final judgment, comparing it to the parables of the Talents (Matt 25:14–30) and the Pounds (Luke 19:12–27). The debtor of "many talents" he tentatively identifies as either the devil, who has "ruined so many who received him," or "the man of sin, the son of perdition" (ll. 45682–45686; 2 Thess 2:3–4). The fellow-servant owed a hundred pence resists identification: "what the truth in these matters is, I declare that no one can interpret unless Jesus, who explained all things to His own disciples privately, takes up His abode in his reason" (ll. 45736–45739). Origen explicitly disputes Gnostic misuse of this parable, noting that those who accuse the Creator of being passionate because of divine wrath language "ought also to accuse this king, because that being angry, he delivered the debtor to the tormentors" (ll. 45915–45918).

**"How Jesus finished his words" (§14, ll. 45930–45977, Matt 19:1).** Origen pauses on the formula "Jesus had finished these words," arguing that only Jesus can be said to have truly "finished" (completed, perfected) his words, since he "came to put an end to things, and to fulfil what was defective in the law" (ll. 45940–45941). This is a hermeneutical reflection distinguishing the Saviour's speech from that of Moses and the prophets.

**The divorce pericope (§§16–25, ll. 45999–46509, Matt 19:3–12).** The longest sustained exegesis in Book XIV. Origen treats the Pharisees' question as a double trap (ll. 46017–46028), defends Jesus' use of Gen 1:27 and 2:24, and notes that the two creation texts refer to different beings — "male and female made He them" (Gen 1:27) refers to those formed "after the image," while "for this cause shall a man leave his own father and mother" (Gen 2:24) refers to those formed from dust (ll. 46045–46059).

The most extended reading is the allegorical "divorce of Israel" (§§17–21). Origen applies Paul's typology of "the two shall be one flesh" (Eph 5:31–32) to Christ and the Church, then to Christ and the former synagogue. Israel was the "former wife" of Christ who "committed fornication, being made an adulteress by the evil one, and along with him plotted against her husband and slew Him, saying, Away with such a fellow from the earth, crucify Him, crucify Him" (ll. 46100–46103). The destruction of Jerusalem is read as the bill of divorcement: "a sign that she has received the bill of divorcement is this, that Jerusalem was destroyed along with what they called the sanctuary" (ll. 46180–46182). After Israel's divorce, Christ took a new wife "of fornication" from the Gentiles (applying Hos 1:2). Origen further allegorizes Deut 24:1–4 as governing the soul's relationship to its guardian angel (§21, ll. 46267–46308): a soul given to luxury may pass from Michael to the angel of luxury, then to the angel of punishment, then to the angel of repentance — each a lesser guardian — and "it is not of good omen to receive, as it were, the marriage of an angel with our own soul" (ll. 46307–46308).

**Laws by concession and celibacy (§§23–25, ll. 46365–46509, Matt 19:10–11).** Origen argues that some Mosaic laws — and some Pauline rulings — were given as concessions to human weakness rather than as ideals (ll. 46366–46397). On the disciples' response "it is not expedient to marry" (Matt 19:10), Origen treats Jesus' reply ("to whom it is given") not as fatalistic limitation but as an invitation to petition: "God therefore will give the good gift, perfect purity in celibacy and chastity, to those who ask Him with the whole soul, and with faith, and in prayers without ceasing" (ll. 46507–46509). Book XIV in this range closes at Matt 19:11.

## Key Themes

**1. Scripture as one harmonized instrument.** The Book II fragment on Matt 5:9 establishes the governing hermeneutical premise: all of Scripture is "the one perfect and harmonised instrument of God." The commentator's task is to demonstrate concord where others see discord, across Law, Prophets, and Gospel.

**2. Sustained anti-Gnostic allegoresis.** Throughout Books X–XIV, Origen's allegory is explicitly deployed against two targets: Gnostics who posit fixed natures (refuted by the dragnet and by Ezek 18's account of moral change) and Marcionites who sever Old from New (refuted by the householder bringing "things new and old"). Origen's allegory is simultaneously a hermeneutical method and a weapon in the anti-Gnostic polemic.

**3. The democratization of Peter's keys.** Book XII's treatment of Matt 16:18–19 is one of Origen's most ecclesiologically significant passages: Peter's "rock" is any disciple who confesses Christ; the "keys" are multiple virtues given to all who bind and loose sin. This reading directly counters Roman episcopal claims to Petrine uniqueness, and Origen explicitly critiques bishops who misread the passage as a personal privilege.

**4. Soul pre-existence as exegetical resource.** The Elijah/Baptist identification (Book XIII) is handled through the "spirit and power" of Elijah rather than metempsychosis, but Origen's broader framework of soul pre-existence structures his readings of the guardian angel material (Book XIV, §26–29): the assigned angel may itself advance or recede in holiness in proportion as its human charge advances — a striking consequence of applying pre-existence logic to angelology.

**5. Marriage, divorce, and celibacy as eschatological categories.** Books XIII–XIV treat the divorce pericope (Matt 19:3–12) and the celibacy saying (19:12) as simultaneously ethical norms (remarriage after divorce = adultery in most cases) and allegories of Israel as divorced wife / Gentile Church as new wife. The celibacy "gift" is re-eschatologized as a response to the approaching consummation.
