# On Christian Doctrine (*De Doctrina Christiana*)

Augustine's four-book manual of biblical interpretation and Christian teaching — the
foundational text of Western Christian hermeneutics. Authored by [[augustine|Augustine of
Hippo]]. Books I–III (to the quotation of the leaven parable in III.25) were written **397
CE**; Augustine left the work unfinished, returning to complete Book III and add Book IV in
**426/427 CE**, as he records in his *Retractations* (II.4). Translated here by J. F. Shaw
(NPNF series).

The work divides into two halves on a distinction Augustine borrows and adapts: the *modus
inveniendi* (the way of **discovering** what Scripture means, Books I–III) and the *modus
proferendi* (the way of **expressing**/teaching it, Book IV). The first half is a theory of
signs; the second is a Christianized rhetoric.

## Structure and Argument

- **Book I** — *Things* (*res*). The famous distinction between **things and signs**, and
  within things between what is to be **enjoyed** (*frui*) and what is to be **used** (*uti*):
  only the Triune God is to be enjoyed for his own sake; all else is to be used in reference
  to him. Love of God and neighbor (*caritas*) is the end and fullness of all Scripture; the
  whole point of interpretation is to build up this double love. See [[use-and-enjoyment]],
  [[charity-and-cupidity]].
- **Book II** — *Signs* (*signa*), specifically **unknown signs**. Natural vs. conventional
  signs; words as the chief conventional signs. Remedies for ignorance: the biblical
  languages (Hebrew, Greek), comparison of translations, the canon of Scripture (Augustine's
  list of the **forty-four** Old Testament books), and the selective use of pagan learning —
  the "**spoils of Egypt**" principle.
- **Book III** — **ambiguous signs**. Rules for resolving punctuation/pronunciation
  ambiguities; the master rule for distinguishing literal from **figurative** expression
  (anything that, read literally, contradicts purity of life or soundness of doctrine must be
  taken figuratively); and an exposition of the **seven rules of [[tichonius|Tichonius]] the
  Donatist**. See [[figurative-interpretation]], [[tichonius-seven-rules]].
- **Book IV** — *Expression*. A **Christian rhetoric**: not a treatise on rhetorical rules
  (those are learned elsewhere) but on how the Christian teacher should deploy eloquence in
  service of wisdom. The biblical authors as models of eloquence; the three styles (subdued,
  temperate, grand) keyed to teaching, delighting, and moving; eloquence subordinated to
  truth and prayer. See [[christian-eloquence]].

## Hermeneutical Significance

*De Doctrina Christiana* is the charter document of the Latin exegetical tradition. It
establishes: (1) that interpretation aims at **charity** — the "rule of love" as the
criterion of valid reading; (2) the legitimacy of **figurative/allegorical** reading
disciplined by the [[rule-of-faith]]; (3) the conscripting of classical learning and
rhetoric into the service of Scripture; and (4) the integration of the four senses that later
crystallizes as the [[fourfold-sense|Quadriga]]. It is the principal patristic source behind
medieval [[allegorical-reading|allegory]] and the *artes praedicandi* (preaching manuals).

## Augustine's Canon of Scripture (Book II)

In Book II (ch. 8), Augustine states that "the authority of the Old Testament is contained
within the limits of these **forty-four books**" and enumerates them. The list unambiguously
includes the **deuterocanonical** books — **Tobias (Tobit), Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom of
Solomon, and Ecclesiasticus (Sirach)** — alongside the Hebrew canon (the twelve minor prophets
counted individually to reach forty-four). He notes that Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus are ascribed
to Solomon only "from a certain resemblance of style," judging Jesus son of Sirach the likelier
author, yet keeps both as "authoritative" (a judgment he partly revisits in the *Retractations*
regarding Wisdom). His **criterion of canonicity** is the judgment of the greater number of
catholic churches, weighting those that are apostolic sees most heavily (II.8, §§12–13).

This forty-four-book Old Testament is essentially the canon ratified by the North African
councils of Augustine's circle (Hippo 393, Carthage 397) and is the principal patristic
warrant for the later Catholic deuterocanon — a point of divergence from the Hebrew/Protestant
canon and from Jerome's *hebraica veritas* reservations. The New Testament list (II.8) is the
familiar twenty-seven books.

## Canon and Reception

The work is **authoritative** across the major Christian traditions (Catholic, Orthodox,
Protestant) as patristic theology and the classic statement of Christian hermeneutics, but it
is not itself scripture. Notably, Augustine commends the seven hermeneutical rules of
**Tichonius**, a [[donatism|Donatist]] — i.e. a schismatic by Catholic reckoning — while
explicitly rejecting Tichonius's ecclesiology: a striking case of received method across a
sectarian boundary.
