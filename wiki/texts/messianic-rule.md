# The Messianic Rule (1QSa)
**Sigla**: 1Q28a (1QSa); also called the Rule of the Congregation
**Full title** (per the opening line): "The Rule for all the congregation of Israel in the last days"

The **Messianic Rule** (1QSa) is a short document (two columns) appended to the **[[community-rule|Community Rule (1QS)]]** in the Qumran Cave 1 manuscript. It describes the organization and governance of the ideal eschatological congregation — the community as it will be constituted "in the last days" — and provides the most important evidence for the **Two Messiahs** doctrine and the precedence of the priestly Messiah over the Davidic king.

## Canon and Reception

Purely sectarian; no reception outside the Qumran community. Its importance is entirely doctrinal: it provides the clearest account of Qumran messianic expectation, and its depiction of the **Messianic Banquet** is the primary comparative text for NT Last Supper traditions and eschatological meal imagery.

## Structure

**Column I — Life Stages and Admission**:
The congregation organized by **life stages**:
- Ages 0–10 years: education in the Book of Hagi (the regulations)
- Ages 10–20: knowledge of Torah; admitted to communal organization
- Ages 20–25: instructed in the "two spirits" and the community laws
- Age 25: eligible for communal census and service
- Age 30: full participation in "the judgements" and councils

Specific exclusions from the congregation: those with physical blemishes "so that no one might enter upon a holy task or approach the [divine] presence" — the same language as the Temple regulations for the levitical priesthood (Lev 21:17-21). The congregation mirrors the Temple's standards.

**Column II — The Messianic Banquet**:
The climax of the document and its most theologically significant section. 1QSa II:11-22 describes the eschatological **assembly at the end of days** (= the messianic banquet):

> If [God] should beget the Messiah among them, the priest shall come [at the head of all the congregation of Israel] and all [his brethren the priests...] shall sit before him, each according to his rank. And then [the Messiah of Israel shall enter], and before him shall sit the chiefs of the [thousands of Israel]...

The sequence is **priest first, then the Davidic Messiah**: the **Messiah of Aaron** (priestly) precedes and blesses the food; the **Messiah of Israel** (Davidic/royal) enters second. This fixes the hierarchy: the priestly Messiah has precedence over the royal Messiah at the eschatological feast.

The banquet procedure:
1. The **priest** blesses the bread and wine first
2. The **Messiah of Israel** extends his hand to the bread
3. "Each member of the congregation shall pronounce a blessing, [each] in order of his rank"
4. This procedure governs "every [session] where at least ten persons [are assembled]"

The "at least ten" clause is significant: the same procedure (priest leads, all bless in order) applies to any communal meal of ten or more. The eschatological banquet is the template for every community meal.

## Two Messiahs Doctrine

1QSa is the primary narrative illustration of the [[two-messiahs|Two Messiahs]] doctrine stated abstractly in 1QS IX:10-11 ("until the coming of the Prophet and the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel"). Here we see:

- **Messiah of Aaron** = the priestly figure who presides at the meal, blesses first, leads the congregation
- **Messiah of Israel** = the Davidic figure who enters and participates, but **after** the priest

The priestly Messiah's precedence reflects the Qumran community's Zadokite priestly identity: even the eschatological age will be a priestly order where the (legitimate, restored) Aaronic priest outranks the Davidic king.

## NT Comparison: The Last Supper and Messianic Meals

The messianic banquet of 1QSa is one of several Qumran texts that parallel NT Last Supper traditions:
- The priest's blessing of bread and wine at an assembly of at least ten
- The anticipatory/eschatological function of the meal ("in the last days")
- The Lucan "I will not eat it until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God" (Luke 22:16, 18)

The comparison does not establish direct literary dependence but shows that "eschatological meal inaugurating the messianic age" was a live Jewish concept in the 1st century BCE/CE. Whether Jesus' Last Supper drew on or paralleled this tradition is debated; see [[two-messiahs]] for the comparative discussion.
