# The Pearl of Great Price

The **Pearl of Great Price** is the fourth Latter-day Saint "standard work" — a short anthology of texts produced by [[joseph-smith|Joseph Smith]]. It is **canon for the [[latter-day-saints|LDS Church]]** but, unlike the [[book-of-mormon|Book of Mormon]] and [[doctrine-and-covenants|D&C]], was **not** canonized by the [[community-of-christ|Community of Christ]] — so its `canon_scope` places that body in `rejected_by`, a fine-grained example of intra-movement canon divergence.

## Canon and Reception

| Standing | Community |
|----------|-----------|
| **Canonical** | LDS Church (canonized 1880) |
| **Not used / rejected** | Community of Christ; historic Christian canons |

Small in bulk, doctrinally weighty: the **Book of Abraham** and **Moses** underwrite cosmology (council of gods, premortal intelligences, plurality of worlds, Zion of Enoch) that most sharply distinguish LDS teaching from creedal Christianity. **Joseph Smith—History** is the canonical First Vision narrative.

## Contents

### 1. The Book of Moses (chs. 1–8)

A revelatory expansion of [[genesis|Genesis 1–6]], framed as God's word to Moses on a high mountain (part of the Joseph Smith Translation material). It opens (Moses 1) with a face-to-face theophany in which God shows Moses "worlds without number" and states his whole purpose — "this is my work and my glory—to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man" (1:39); Satan then confronts Moses demanding worship and is driven off. Moses 2–3 retell the creation, carried out through the Only Begotten, and add a **spiritual creation** preceding the physical one, recasting the forbidden tree as a matter of agency ("thou mayest choose for thyself… but… I forbid it").

The heavenly **council** follows in Moses 4: Satan offers to redeem all humanity by force in exchange for God's honor, the Beloved Son answers "thy will be done," and Satan is cast down for seeking to destroy "the agency of man" — after which comes the Fall. In Moses 5, Adam and Eve offer sacrifice as a similitude of the Only Begotten and rejoice in the Fall's consequences, while Cain becomes "**Master Mahan**" and founds the secret combinations that will recur across LDS scripture. Moses 6–7 center on **Enoch**: called to preach a fully Christian gospel (belief, repentance, baptism in the name of Jesus Christ, the Holy Ghost) said to have been taught from Adam onward, Enoch builds the city of **Zion**, "of one heart and one mind… no poor among them," which is taken up into heaven. In the book's most striking scene **God weeps** over human violence, and Enoch is shown the flood, the future New Jerusalem, and the cry "ZION IS FLED." Moses 8 closes the thread by tying it to Noah, ordained and preaching baptism-or-flood.

### 2. The Book of Abraham (chs. 1–5)

Presented as a translation of Egyptian papyri purchased in Kirtland (1835); this public-domain text **does not include the facsimiles** (only the in-text references at Abr 1:12, 14). Abraham 1 opens in Chaldea, where Abraham narrowly escapes being sacrificed by idolatrous priests when **Jehovah** delivers him; it traces a priesthood lineage back to Adam alongside the story of Ham, Egyptus, and a Pharaoh whose royal line is said to hold authority but **not "the right of the priesthood"** — a passage historically entangled with the later LDS priesthood restriction (recorded here as reception, not flattened). Abraham 2 gives the covenant proper: Abraham's seed will carry the priesthood and gospel to all nations.

Abraham 3 supplies the cosmology that most sharply distinguishes the book. A hierarchy of stars and planets rises toward **Kolob**, the body nearest God's throne (one Kolob day equalling a thousand earth years); above the created order stand eternal, uncreated **intelligences** ranked in nobility, among them Abraham, chosen before his birth. A premortal council stages the earth as a proving ground of "first" and "second estates," and God asks "Whom shall I send?" — the first to answer is sent, while the second grows angry and "kept not his first estate." Abraham 4–5 then retell the creation as the work of "**the Gods**" who organize and prepare the earth and place the spirit in man — the plural language underwriting later LDS teaching on the plurality of Gods.

**Major controversy:** the relation of the published Book of Abraham to the surviving Joseph Smith papyri (Egyptological readings as ordinary funerary texts vs. missing-source, catalyst, or revelatory models). See [[book-of-abraham-translation]].

### 3. Joseph Smith—Matthew

Joseph Smith's revision of [[matthew|Matthew]] 24 (Olivet Discourse): a two-horizon structure (the destruction of Jerusalem / the last days), with "the end of the world" glossed as the **destruction of the wicked**, the elect gathered according to covenant, and the gospel going to all nations before the end.

### 4. Joseph Smith—History (extract)

A first-person account (the 1838 version, canonical in LDS use) of how the Church began. Amid the revival excitement of the "burned-over district," the teenage Joseph Smith, prompted by James 1:5 to ask God for wisdom, receives the **First Vision** in the spring of 1820: a pillar of light and **two Personages**, one introducing the other — "This is My Beloved Son. Hear Him!" — who tell him to join **none** of the existing churches, whose creeds are "an abomination." Three years later (21 September 1823) the angel **Moroni** appears repeatedly, revealing gold plates recording former inhabitants of the continent, buried in a nearby hill, and instructing Joseph annually for four years before he obtains them (22 September 1827).

The narrative recounts the **Charles Anthon** "sealed book" episode involving Martin Harris and the arrival of Oliver Cowdery as scribe (1829), and closes on 15 May 1829 with **John the Baptist** restoring the **Aaronic Priesthood** (the keys of the ministering of angels, of repentance, and of baptism by immersion), with the higher Melchizedek keys promised through Peter, James, and John. Only this 1838 version of the First Vision (with two Personages) appears in the text; the other historical accounts of the vision lie outside this source.

### 5. The Articles of Faith (13)

Joseph Smith's 1842 summary (Wentworth letter tradition), ending "— Joseph Smith":

1. Godhead: Eternal Father, Son, Holy Ghost.
2. Men punished for own sins, **not for Adam's transgression**.
3. Atonement + obedience to laws and ordinances.
4. First principles: faith, repentance, baptism by immersion, laying on of hands for the Holy Ghost.
5. Authority by prophecy and laying on of hands.
6. Primitive church organization (apostles, prophets, etc.).
7. Spiritual gifts.
8. **Bible** word of God **as far as it is translated correctly**; also **Book of Mormon** word of God.
9. **Continuing revelation** — past, present, and future.
10. Gathering of Israel; Ten Tribes; **Zion (New Jerusalem) on the American continent**; millennial reign; renewed earth.
11. Religious liberty.
12. Civic loyalty.
13. Pauline moral admonition (honest, true, chaste… seek after virtuous/lovely things).

## Major Themes

1. **Work and glory** — immortality and eternal life of humankind (Moses 1:39).
2. **Premortal existence and agency** — intelligences; council; first/second estate; Satan as anti-agency.
3. **Plurality of worlds / Gods language** — worlds without number; "the Gods" organize (Abr 4).
4. **Zion** — Enoch's city; New Jerusalem on the American continent (AoF 10; Moses 7).
5. **Restoration narrative** — First Vision; priesthood restoration; corrected/expanded Genesis.
6. **Conditional biblical authority** — AoF 8; "plain and precious" logic shared with the Book of Mormon.

## Hermeneutical Method

- **Restored text claim** — Moses as recovery of words taken from Moses' book.
- **Translation / revelation hybrid** — Abraham presented as translation of papyri; critical debate over that claim is reception.
- **JST midrash** — Genesis and Matthew expanded with Christian gospel language (baptism in Jesus' name from Adam; explicit Christology).
- **Autobiographical scripture** — JS-H as history that functions as canon.
- **Creed-like summary** — Articles of Faith as portable doctrine.
