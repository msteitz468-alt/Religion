# Messianic Apocalypse (4Q521)
**Sigla**: 4Q521

A short but extraordinarily significant eschatological text from Cave 4 that combines **messianic expectation**, **divine saving acts**, and **explicit resurrection language** in a single composition. It provides the closest pre-Christian Jewish parallel to the Gospel tradition's account of Jesus's response to John the Baptist's question ("Are you the one who is to come?").

## Canon and Reception
Purely sectarian; significant comparative value for New Testament studies. The text is not cited in any known rabbinic or Christian source, and its content was unknown before the Qumran discovery.

## Content

### Fragment 2, Column II (The Core Passage)
The most important section:

> "**The heavens and the earth will listen to His Messiah**, and none therein will stray from the commandments of the holy ones. Seekers of the Lord, strengthen yourselves in His service! All you hopeful in heart, will you not find the Lord in this? For the Lord will consider the pious (*hasidim*) and call the righteous by name. Over the poor His spirit will hover and will renew the faithful by His power. He will glorify the pious on the throne of the eternal Kingdom. **He will release the captives, make the blind see, raise up the downtrodden.** Forever I will cleave to the hopeful and in His mercy… **And the Lord will accomplish glorious things which have never been as He… For He will heal the wounded, and revive the dead and bring good news to the poor** (Isaiah 61:1), and He will satisfy the destitute, and guide the uprooted, and enrich the hungry… **God is He who will raise the dead of His people**."

### Key Features

**Singular Messiah**: The text uses singular "His Messiah" (*meshiaho*) — not the dual Messiah of Aaron and Israel but a single figure whose presence commands heaven and earth. This is the most explicit single-Messiah language in Qumran.

**Resurrection**: The phrase "He will revive the dead" (*yaḥyeh ha-metim*) is explicit resurrection language. "God is He who will raise the dead of His people" reinforces this. This is significant: Qumran has often been said to lack resurrection belief (the scrolls emphasize spiritual immortality and angelification more than bodily resurrection), but 4Q521 is a clear exception.

**Isaiah 61:1**: "Bring good news to the poor" — the same Isaiah passage Jesus quotes in Luke 4:18-19 as his programmatic statement ("The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me…"). At Qumran, Isaiah 61:1 also provides language for the Heavenly Prince Melchizedek (11Q13: "proclaim liberty to the captives"). The passage functions as a standard eschatological liberation text across multiple Qumran documents.

**Hasidim**: The "pious ones" (*hasidim*) receive special notice — they will be "glorified on the throne of the eternal Kingdom." The *hasidim* terminology connects to the early Maccabaean-era pious movement from which the Essenes may have emerged.

## Relationship to the Gospels
Matthew 11:2-6 / Luke 7:18-23: John the Baptist sends from prison asking Jesus, "Are you the one who is to come?" Jesus responds:
> "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and **the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them**."

The parallel with 4Q521 is exact in structure — a list of eschatological saving acts culminating in resurrection and good news to the poor, all presented as evidence of the messianic era's arrival. Scholars debate whether:
1. Jesus is drawing on a common eschatological checklist (that 4Q521 also reflects)
2. 4Q521 represents the expected messianic program against which Jesus is implicitly claiming fulfillment
3. The Gospel tradition has shaped its response formula after the 4Q521 type

Vermes does not adjudicate this but notes the parallel as a significant bridge between Qumran messianism and Gospel tradition.

## Theological Significance
4Q521 demonstrates that by the 1st century BCE, **Jewish messianism could include**:
- A single Messiah figure of commanding cosmic authority
- Explicit bodily resurrection as part of the messianic program
- A combination of Isaiah 61:1 eschatological liberation with healing, sight restoration, and raising of the dead
- Divine saving acts accomplished at or through the Messiah's arrival

This substantially complicates older scholarly pictures of Qumran messianism as exclusively focused on priestly and political restoration. The Messianic Apocalypse shows that at least one strand of Qumran eschatology included resurrection hope and a figure whose arrival triggers cosmic transformation.
