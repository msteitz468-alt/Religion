# Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen)
**Sigla**: 1QapGen (also 1Q20)

An **Aramaic retelling** of selected Genesis narratives — one of the seven original Dead Sea Scrolls found in Cave 1 in 1947 and among the most extensively preserved. The Apocryphon expands Genesis in a narrative, often first-person style, with additional dialogue, psychological depth, and legendary elaborations not found in the biblical text. Extant portions cover Genesis 5–15, with columns 0–II (Enoch/Lamech narrative) and XII–XXII (Abraham narrative) being the most legible.

## Canon and Reception
Sectarian / para-sectarian; Aramaic rather than Hebrew suggests possibly broader appeal than the purely Hebrew sectarian texts. Not canonical for any tradition; unknown before 1947. Provides important evidence for Jewish Aramaic haggadic traditions of the Second Temple period.

## Extant Contents

### Columns I–V: The Birth of Noah (Lamech Narrative)
Lamech fears that his newborn son Noah was fathered by the Watchers (fallen angels), not by himself. He confronts his wife Bitenosh, who swears Noah's miraculous appearance (glowing, angelic features) is the result of divine blessing, not heavenly intercourse. Lamech consults his father Methuselah, who goes to Enoch for clarification. Enoch confirms Noah's human paternity but acknowledges his special destined role. (This narrative overlaps with the Book of Noah material [4Q534-6] and parallels the Book of Enoch [1 Enoch 106–107].)

### Columns XII–XXII: The Abraham Narrative
The most extensively preserved and theologically rich section.

**The Beauty of Sarah (Cols. XIX–XX)**: When Pharaoh's courtiers see Sarah in Egypt, they report her extraordinary beauty to Pharaoh's court. The text includes an extended **ekphrasis** (beauty description):
> "Her face is very beautiful; how fine are her hair, how lovely her eyes, how desirable her nose and all the whiteness of her face; how fair and pretty is her breast… her hands are so beautiful… She is more beautiful than all the maidens and brides who enter under the wedding canopy."

This is one of the most elaborate beauty descriptions in ancient Jewish literature. Sarah's beauty is compared favorably to all other women; even the Pharaoh's courtiers are overwhelmed. The passage expands Genesis 12:14 ("the Egyptians saw that the woman was very beautiful") into an extended literary set piece.

**Abraham's Prayer Against Pharaoh (Cols. XX)**: When Pharaoh takes Sarah, God afflicts him with a "scourging evil spirit." Abraham prays — an intercessory prayer not found in Genesis — and "God laid chastisements upon Pharaoh Zoan." Lot, asked by Pharaoh's court for help, says he cannot intervene because Sarah is Abram's wife. God's afflictions are explicitly connected to Abraham's prayer.

**Melchizedek as "Priest of the Most High God" (Col. XXII)**:
After the battle of Kedorlaomer, the text follows Genesis 14:18-20 closely: Melchizedek (here rendered by name) brings out bread and wine and blesses Abram. The Apocryphon identifies him explicitly as "Melchizedek, king of Salem, who was **Priest of the Most High God**." This is a straightforward rendering of the Genesis text but confirms that the Priest of El Elyon identification was standard in Qumran interpretation of Genesis 14 — providing context for the more developed Melchizedek angelology of 11Q13.

**Abraham's Land Tour (Col. XXI)**: God tells Abram to walk through the entire land of Canaan and see it — an expansion of Genesis 13:14-17. Abram tours from the Euphrates to Egypt and all directions, giving the land tour cosmic scope.

**The Battle of Kedorlaomer (Cols. XXI–XXII)**: The battle narrative expands Genesis 14, giving names and details to the allied forces and emphasizing Abram's military prowess with his 318 trained men.

**Covenant of the Pieces (Col. XXII end)**: The Apocryphon covers the covenant of Genesis 15 briefly.

## Literary Features
The Genesis Apocryphon employs:
- **First-person narration**: Several sections are narrated in the first person by Lamech, Bitenosh, or Abraham — creating a pseudo-autobiographical intimacy not found in Genesis
- **Expansive dialogue**: Conversations not recorded in Genesis are invented with psychological and theological detail
- **Haggadic elaboration**: Traditions also preserved in rabbinic Midrash, Jubilees, and Philo appear in early Aramaic form
- **Aramaic literary quality**: The Aramaic is polished and literary; the ekphrasis of Sarah's beauty is a formal literary exercise

## Significance
The Genesis Apocryphon provides evidence for:
1. **Aramaic haggadic traditions** about the patriarchs circulating in Second Temple Judaism
2. **Early Melchizedek interpretation** — his identification as priest of El Elyon is standard, but the Apocryphon does not yet develop the angelic Melchizedek of 11Q13
3. **Narrative expansion techniques** used in contemporary Jewish interpretation (techniques also found in Jubilees, Philo, and later Midrash)
4. **Sarah's beauty tradition** — attested here in its earliest surviving literary form
