# The Guide for the Perplexed (Moreh Nevukhim / Dalalat al-Ha'irin)

The **Guide for the Perplexed** (Judeo-Arabic *Dalalat al-Ha'irin*; Hebrew *Moreh Nevukhim*)
is the philosophical-theological masterwork of **[[maimonides|Moses Maimonides (Rambam)]]**
(1135–1204), composed in Judeo-Arabic c. 1190. It is addressed not to the masses but to the
single reader "perplexed" (*ha'ir*) by the apparent conflict between the literal sense of
Scripture and the conclusions of Aristotelian philosophy (*[[falsafa|falsafa]]*). Maimonides'
project is to show that, rightly interpreted, Torah and demonstrated philosophy do not conflict —
chiefly by reading the Torah's anthropomorphic and anthropopathic language as deliberate
parable (*mashal*).

Edition ingested: the English translation from the Judeo-Arabic by **M. Friedländer**
(2nd revised edition, one volume, 1904; repr. Dover).

## Structure: Three Parts

The Guide is built in three Parts, prefaced by Maimonides' celebrated **Introduction** on the
parable (*mashal*) and the deliberate concealment of truths from the vulgar, and on the seven
causes of contradiction in a text.

- **Part I** (Introduction + 76 chapters) — a "lexicon" of equivocal (homonymous) scriptural
  terms applied to God (*tselem*, "place," "sitting," "seeing," etc.), establishing divine
  incorporeality and **negative theology** (the doctrine of attributes), and closing with a
  critique of the **[[kalam|Mutakallimun]]** (Islamic dialectical theologians) and their proofs.
- **Part II** — opens with twenty-five Aristotelian **propositions** (plus a 26th, the eternity
  of the world, which Maimonides withholds), then the proofs of God's existence, unity, and
  incorporeality; the spheres and separate Intelligences; the great treatment of **creation
  versus the eternity of the world**; and the theory of **prophecy**.
- **Part III** — the esoteric exposition of Ezekiel's chariot (*[[maaseh-merkabah|Maaseh
  Merkabah]]*); matter and form and the problem of **evil**; **divine providence** and
  omniscience; the book of **[[job|Job]]** as a treatise on providence; and the long concluding
  account of the **reasons for the commandments** (*[[reasons-for-the-commandments|ta'amei
  ha-mitzvot]]*), ending with human perfection and true knowledge of God.

## Hermeneutical Framing

The Guide's master key is the doctrine of the **parable (*mashal*)**: Scripture speaks in the
"language of the sons of man," veiling philosophical truth in figurative husks whose gold lies
within. This is Jewish **[[allegorical-reading|allegoresis]]** (the *sod*/esoteric register of
PaRDeS) put to systematic philosophical use, paralleling the Islamic *[[zahir-batin|zahir/batin]]*
distinction Maimonides inherited from [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]] and [[avicenna|Avicenna]]. He warns
that the Account of Creation (*Maaseh Bereshit* = physics) and the Account of the Chariot
(*Maaseh Merkabah* = metaphysics) may be taught only by hints, to one student at a time.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- **Divine attributes** (I:50–60) — God can be described only by **negation** or by attributes of
  action; positive essential attributes would compromise his absolute unity. See
  [[negative-theology]].
- **Creation vs. eternity** (II:13–25) — Maimonides rejects the demonstrability of *both*
  creation *ex nihilo* and Aristotle's eternal world, then argues creation is the more probable
  and is required by the Law. See [[eternity-of-the-world]], [[creation-ex-nihilo]].
- **Prophecy** (II:32–48) — prophecy as the natural overflow (*fayd*) of the Active Intellect upon
  a perfected imagination and intellect, which God may miraculously withhold. See [[prophecy]].
- **Providence and evil** (III:8–24) — evil as privation; providence as proportional to
  intellectual perfection; the book of [[job|Job]] read as a debate over providence.
- **Reasons for the commandments** (III:25–49) — every commandment has a rational purpose, many
  aimed at uprooting [[sabians|Sabian]] idolatry. See [[reasons-for-the-commandments]].

## Canon and Reception

The Guide is not scripture but the central **philosophical classic of rationalist
[[rabbinic-judaism|rabbinic Judaism]]**. Its reception split the medieval Jewish world: the
**Maimonidean controversy** (13th c.) saw anti-rationalists in Provence and Spain seek to ban
its study for the young, culminating in denunciations to the Dominicans and the public burning
of Maimonidean writings (Montpellier, c. 1232). It was defended by rationalists and translated
from Arabic into Hebrew (Ibn Tibbon; Al-Harizi) and Latin, deeply influencing
Aquinas and Scholasticism. See [[maimonides|Maimonides]] for the controversy.
