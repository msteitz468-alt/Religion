The **Sahih al-Bukhari** is the most authoritative **hadith** collection in [[sunni-islam|Sunni
Islam]] — for Sunnis, the most reliable book after the [[quran|Qur'an]] itself. Compiled by **Muhammad
ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari** (810–870) from a reported 600,000 traditions, of which he judged ~7,275
(with repetition) authentic (*sahih*), it is the first and foremost of the Sunni *Kutub al-Sittah*
(Six Books).

## Why this page is a canon-split exemplar

The Qur'an's [[quran|`canon_scope`]] is identical for every Muslim. The **hadith canon is where the
sects divide**, and this page makes that structural: Sahih al-Bukhari is `canonical_for:
[sunni-islam]` but `rejected_by: [shia-islam]`. Twelver [[shia-islam|Shia]] reject the Sunni
collections because their chains of transmission (*isnad*) run through Companions the Shia hold to
have wronged [[islam|Ali]]; the Shia receive instead their own **Four Books** (al-Kafi, etc.). One
text, opposite canonical standing in two sects of one tradition — exactly what the `canon_scope`
field exists to record.

## Content and Method

The collection is organized topically into *kitabs* (books) — faith, knowledge, prayer, alms,
pilgrimage, jihad, marriage, transactions, and the famous opening on the *beginning of revelation*.
Each report pairs an *isnad* (chain of transmitters back to the Prophet) with a *matn* (the text of
the saying or deed). Al-Bukhari's reputation rests on the **rigor of his isnad criticism** — his
demand that every link in the chain be a known, reliable transmitter with attested contact with the
next — which became a model for the science of hadith (*ilm al-rijal*).

## Structure (Complete 9-Volume Collection)

The 9-volume collection contains 93 *kitabs* (books):

**Volume 1** (Books 1–12): Revelation · Belief · Knowledge · Ablutions · Bathing · Menstrual Periods ·
Tayammum · Prayers · Sutra of the Musalla · Times of Prayer · Call to Prayer · Characteristics of Prayer

**Volume 2** (Books 13–26): Friday Prayer · Fear Prayer · Two Festivals · Witr Prayer · Rain Prayer ·
Eclipses · Prostration in Recital · Shortening Prayers · Night Prayer · Actions while Praying · Funerals ·
Zakat · Zakat al-Fitr · Hajj

**Volume 3** (Books 27–50): Minor Pilgrimage · Pilgrims Prevented · Penalty of Hunting · Virtues of
Madinah · Fasting · Taraweeh · I'tikaf · Sales and Trade · As-Salam · Hiring · Debt Transfer · Agency ·
Agriculture · Water Distribution · Loans · Lost Property · Oppressions · Partnership · Mortgaging ·
Manumission · Gifts · Witnesses · Peacemaking · Conditions

**Volume 4** (Books 51–56): Wills · Jihad · Khumus · Beginning of Creation · Prophets · Virtues of the Prophet

**Volume 5** (Books 57–59): Companions · Ansar · Military Expeditions (al-Maghazi)

**Volume 6** (Books 60–61): Prophetic Commentary on the Qur'an (Tafsir) · Virtues of the Qur'an

**Volume 7** (Books 62–72): Marriage · Divorce · Supporting the Family · Food · Aqiqa · Hunting · Adha · Drinks · Patients · Medicine · Dress

**Volume 8** (Books 73–82): Good Manners · Asking Permission · Invocations · Ar-Riqaq · Divine Will · Oaths · Expiation · Inheritance · Hudood · Punishment of Disbelievers

**Volume 9** (Books 83–93): Blood Money · Apostasy · Compulsion · Tricks · Dreams · Afflictions · Judgments · Wishes · Truthful Persons · Qur'an and Sunnah · Tawheed

## Key Theological Themes (Volumes 1–3)

### Revelation and Prophethood (Vol. 1, Book 1)

Book 1 opens the collection with the first hadith of Islam: Umar ibn al-Khattab transmits the
Prophet's teaching on *niyyah* (intention) — "The reward of deeds depends upon intentions." This
positions intention (*niyyah*) as the hermeneutical key to Islamic practice from the outset.

The same book contains Aisha's account of the first revelation (*wahy*): the angel Jibril squeezing
Muhammad in the cave of Hira and commanding him to recite, leading to Muhammad returning trembling
to Khadija and the reassurance from Waraqa ibn Nawfal. Key theological details: (1) revelation was
sometimes like a ringing bell and sometimes like a man speaking; (2) the revelation ceased (*fatra*)
for a period; (3) Waraqa identifies the being as the "same one who kept the secrets" (*al-namus*)
sent to Moses — explicitly linking Islamic prophecy to the Mosaic tradition.

### Iman and the Articles of Belief (Vol. 1, Book 2)

Book 2 (*Kitab al-Iman*) defines faith structurally. Key hadiths:
- **The Jibril hadith** (parallel to Sahih Muslim): the angel appears to the Prophet in human form
  and tests him on *al-Islam* (five practices), *al-Iman* (belief in Allah, angels, books, messengers,
  the Last Day, and *qadar*/divine decree), and *al-Ihsan* (worshipping Allah as if you see Him). This
  three-tier structure — Islam/Iman/Ihsan — is Sunni orthodoxy's formal definition of the religion.
- Faith is described as comprising over sixty branches (*shu'ab*), the highest being the Shahada and
  the lowest being removing harm from the road.
- "None of you will have faith till he wishes for his brother what he likes for himself" — a Golden
  Rule formulation central to Islamic ethics.
- Faith can increase and decrease (*yazid wa yanqus*) — a significant Sunni doctrinal position
  against Murji'ite claims that faith is static.

### Knowledge and Hadith Epistemology (Vol. 1, Book 3)

Book 3 (*Kitab al-Ilm*) establishes the epistemological framework for the hadith enterprise itself:
- "Seeking knowledge is an obligation upon every Muslim."
- Al-Bukhari inserts the Prophet's instruction to "convey (my teachings) even if it were a single
  verse" — the mandate for hadith transmission.
- False transmission is warned against severely: attributing to the Prophet what he did not say is
  hellfire-worthy.
- The chapter on women's right to religious education appears here, citing Aisha as a teacher.

### Ritual Purity (Tahara) — Books 4–7

Books 4–7 (*wudu*, bathing, menstruation, *tayammum*) establish the conditions for valid prayer.
Key theological principle: ritual purity (*tahara*) is the prerequisite for *salat*; the five daily
prayers depend on this. Al-Bukhari records numerous hadiths on which actions break *wudu*, what
requires full *ghusl* (bathing), and the *tayammum* (dry ablution with dust) as a dispensation for
travellers and the sick. These hadiths are the primary source for Sunni ritual law (*fiqh*) on purity.

### Prayer (Salat) — Books 8–12

The longest single-topic section in Volume 1. Key themes:
- **Niyyah in prayer**: the prayer must be preceded by conscious intention.
- **Qibla**: the direction of prayer facing Mecca — hadiths on the initial direction toward Jerusalem
  and the *tahwil al-qibla* (change of direction) to Mecca are documented.
- **Sutra** (screen): the Prophet's practice of placing a screen in front to prevent interruption.
- **Times of prayer**: the five prayers are anchored to natural phenomena (sunrise, midday, afternoon,
  sunset, night), with hadith specifying the exact conditions.
- **Adhan** (call to prayer): its institution by divine dream rather than by qiyas (analogy), with
  the role of Bilal ibn Rabah (the Ethiopian freed slave) as the first *muadhdhin*.
- **Prayer characteristics**: precise bodily postures, recitations, number of *raka'at*, the salam
  closing — all documented via hadith.

### Funerals and Eschatology — including *'Adhab al-Qabr* (Vol. 2, Book 23)

Book 23 is theologically rich: hadiths on the preparation of the body, funeral prayer, burial rites,
and — crucially — accounts of what happens in the grave. The **[[azab-al-qabr|'adhab al-qabr]]** (punishment of the
grave) is attested here: the dead are questioned by two angels (*Munkar and Nakir* implied though
not always named), and the Prophet is attested weeping at graves and seeking refuge from grave
punishment. The Prophet's teaching on cemetery visitation ("Visit graves, for they remind you of
death") appears here.

### Zakat — Social Justice Dimension (Vol. 2, Book 24)

Book 24 articulates Zakat as more than poor-tax: it is the structural mechanism of Sunni economic
ethics. Key hadiths:
- Zakat is collected from the wealthy and given directly to the poor of the same community.
- Specific *nisab* (minimum thresholds) and rates for different kinds of wealth are given.
- Abu Bakr's decision to fight those who withheld Zakat after the Prophet's death (also in Sahih
  Muslim) is affirmed — establishing Zakat refusal as equivalent to apostasy.
- The *sadaqa* (voluntary charity) hadiths in adjacent chapters expand the principle beyond
  obligatory Zakat to all giving.

### Hajj — Pilgrimage Law and Theology (Vol. 2, Book 26)

The longest book in Volume 2, covering the full ritual of Hajj with many hadiths from Aisha and Ibn
Abbas. Key content:
- *Ihram* (consecration state), *tawaf* (circumambulation), *sa'y* (walking between Safa and Marwa),
  *wuquf* (standing at Arafat), *rami al-jamarat* (stoning), *sacrifice*, and *tawaf al-wada* (farewell).
- The Prophet's own *Hajjat al-Wada* (Farewell Pilgrimage) is extensively documented.
- Theological: the Ka'ba as the first house of worship, the Black Stone (*al-Hajar al-Aswad*), and
  the role of Ibrahim (Abraham) as the founder of the rite.

### Commerce and Social Ethics (Vol. 3, Books 34–50)

The commercial law books (Sales, Loans, Agriculture, etc.) apply the framework of *halal/haram* to
economic life. Recurring principles:
- Prohibition of *riba* (interest/usury) — hadiths on the prohibition appear in several books.
- Requirement of honesty in transactions.
- Rules for slaves and manumission (*Book 46*) — the collection records detailed regulations and
  the Prophet's encouragement of freeing slaves as expiation.
- Gifts and bequests to non-Muslims, the binding nature of conditions in contracts.

## Key Theological Themes (Volumes 7–9)

*(Content developed from Pass 3 ingest — see Coverage Ledger below.)*

### Marriage Law and Social Ethics — Book 62

Book 62 (*Kitab al-Nikah*) establishes Islamic marriage as a normative *sunna*: "He who does not follow my tradition in religion, is not from me" (No. 1). Key rulings: mahr (bride-gift) is required but flexible in form; mut'a (temporary marriage) was abrogated at Khaybar per Ali's narration (No. 50), though Ibn Abbas preserved a minority permissive view (No. 51) — the Sunni/Shia split point. Night-turn rotation system among the Prophet's nine wives documented. See [[nikah|Nikah (Islamic Marriage)]].

### Divorce Law — Book 63

Book 63 (*Kitab al-Talaq*) establishes the *tahlil* (legalization) requirement after triple divorce, wife-initiated *khul'* with mahr return (the Thabit case), and the 'idda periods. Intra-Companion dispute: Aisha vs. Fatima bint Qais on divorced women's residence rights. See [[talaq|Talaq (Divorce)]].

### Food, Medicine, and Ethics (Books 65–76)

Books 67–71 document halal/haram food rulings, the Islamic medical tradition (honey as medicine, black seed "heals all diseases except death," cupping, the quarantine principle), and the prohibition of all intoxicants — with Umar's sermon (Book 69, No. 487) defining *khamr* functionally as "that which disturbs the mind."

Book 73 (*Kitab al-Adab* / Good Manners) is the longest single book in volumes 7–8, covering filial duty, neighbor rights, anger management ("Do not become angry" — the *la taghdab* hadith), truthfulness vs. lying, prohibition of backbiting (*ghiba*) and slander, and the "you will be with those whom you love" consolation.

### Piety and Eschatology — Book 76 (Ar-Riqaq)

Book 76 (*Kitab al-Riqaq* — Softening of Hearts) is the eschatological heart of the collection. Key content: the Great Intercession chain (Adam → Noah → Abraham → Moses → Jesus → Muhammad, No. 570); description of the Sirat bridge with hooks "like thorns of As-Sa'dan"; the Lake-Fount with warnings about apostates driven away; the vision of Allah in Paradise (ru'yat Allah) "as you see the full moon" (No. 577). The *waliyy* hadith (No. 509) — "I become his hearing, his sight, his hand, his leg" — is the foundational text of Sufi spirituality. See [[shafaa|Shafa'a (Intercession)]].

### Divine Decree — Book 77 (Qadar)

Book 77 contains the 40-day womb hadith (when the angel inscribes the child's destiny), the *fitra* hadith, and the Adam-Moses predestination debate (No. 611 — "Adam overcame Moses"). See [[tawhid|Tawhid]] for the theological implications.

### Legal Punishments — Book 81

Book 81 (*Hudood*) documents the two-tier adultery punishment: 100 lashes plus exile for the unmarried; stoning (*rajm*) for the married. The Ma'iz case (No. 813) is the primary named stoning narrative. Theft threshold: quarter-dinar (Aisha's narration) vs. three dirhams (Ibn Umar) — a minor contradiction preserved.

### The Rajm Verse — Book 82

Umar's sermon (Book 82, No. 817) declares: "Allah sent Muhammad with the Truth and revealed… the Verse of the Rajm (stoning) … I am afraid that after a long time has passed, somebody will say 'We do not find the Verses of the Rajm in Allah's Book.'" This hadith records a Caliph explicitly stating that a revealed verse is absent from the written *mushaf* — one of the most politically sensitive passages in the entire collection. See [[qiraat-variant-readings|Qira'at Controversy]].

### Apostasy — Book 84

"Whoever changed his Islamic religion, then kill him" (No. 57, Ibn Abbas from the Prophet). The death penalty for apostasy is stated twice. Mu'adh bin Jabal insists on execution: "This is the judgment of Allah and His Apostle" (No. 58).

### Dreams — Book 87

The prophetic status of dreams is documented extensively: good dreams are "1/46 of prophecy" (Nos. 112, 116–118, 144). Samura's long eschatological dream receives full prophetic interpretation (the Quran-neglector, habitual liar, adulterers, usury-eater, Abraham with children of the *fitra*, Malik the gatekeeper of Hell). "Whoever sees me (the Prophet) in a dream truly sees me, for Satan cannot imitate my form" (various narrations).

### End-Times Signs — Book 88 (Fitan)

The eleven signs of the Last Hour enumerated (No. 237): Smoke, Dajjal, the Beast, rising of the sun from the West (closing repentance), Jesus's descent, Gog and Magog, three landslides, and a driving fire. The Dajjal is described as one-eyed with "Kafir" written between his eyes. Medina is protected from both plague and the Dajjal. Umar is "the door" whose breaking releases tribulation (No. 216). Notably, **the Mahdi does not appear in Bukhari's Fitan book** — a fact relevant to debates about the Mahdi hadiths' authenticity. See [[fitan|Fitan]].

### Tawhid — Book 93 (Closing the Collection)

Book 93 (*Kitab al-Tawhid*) is the theological crown. Major content: the 99 Names hadith (No. 489); divine attribute hadiths — Hand, Shin, Foot, Laughter affirmed without *ta'wil*; the full-moon *ru'yat Allah* (Nos. 529–532); Allah as Time (*al-Dahr*, No. 583); shirk as the greatest sin (Nos. 611, 623). The collection closes (No. 652) with: "*Subhan Allah wa-bi hamdihi* and *Subhan Allah Al-Azim*" — two words light on the tongue but heavy in the divine balance. See [[tawhid|Tawhid]].

## Major Companion-Narrators in Volumes 1–3

The collection is transmitted through chains (*isnad*) that pass through named Companions. The most
frequent narrators in Volumes 1–3:
- **Aisha bint Abi Bakr** — primary source for private life of the Prophet, first revelation
  narrative, prayer details, ritual purity. One of the most prolific hadith transmitters overall.
- **[[abu-hurairah|Abu Hurairah]]** (Abd al-Rahman ibn Sakhr) — the single most prolific transmitter; wide-ranging
  theological and ethical hadiths.
- **Ibn Abbas** (Abd Allah ibn Abbas) — Qur'anic interpretation hadiths; prominent in Hajj sections.
- **Umar ibn al-Khattab** — transmits the famous opening *niyyah* hadith; present in legal rulings.
- **Anas ibn Malik** — the Prophet's personal servant; transmits details of daily practice.
- **Ibn Umar** (Abd Allah ibn Umar) — transmits the Five Pillars hadith and many legal rulings.

## Significance

As the principal record of the *sunna* (the Prophet's normative practice), Sahih al-Bukhari is a
primary source of Sunni law (*fiqh*) and theology, second only to the Qur'an. Its hermeneutics are
those of *[[tafsir|tafsir bi'l-ma'thur]]* — the Qur'an interpreted through the Prophet's transmitted
words and deeds. The collection also functions as a theological source: the *Kitab al-Iman* (Book 2)
and the *Kitab al-Tawhid* (Book 93, Volume 9) bracket the collection theologically and are among
the most cited sections in Sunni kalam.
