# The Book of Mormon

The **Book of Mormon** is the founding scripture of the [[latter-day-saints|Latter-day Saint movement]], published by **[[joseph-smith|Joseph Smith]]** in 1830 and later subtitled "Another Testament of Jesus Christ." It is the wiki's **headline `canon_scope` case** — one book with three different standings across communities, plus a narrative claim that it *continues* the biblical story of Israel.

## Canon and Reception

| Standing | Community |
|----------|-----------|
| **Canonical** | [[latter-day-saints|LDS Church]] — one of the four "standard works," co-equal with the Bible, [[doctrine-and-covenants|D&C]], and [[pearl-of-great-price|Pearl of Great Price]] |
| **Deuterocanonical / graded** | [[community-of-christ|Community of Christ]] (and some other Restoration branches) — scripture but often secondary/optional for belief |
| **Rejected** | Historic [[catholicism|Catholic]], [[eastern-orthodoxy|Orthodox]], and [[protestantism|Protestant]] canons |

**Reception fact (not a canon bucket):** the book *presents itself* as a continuation of the biblical narrative — Israelite migrants to the Americas and a post-resurrection ministry of Christ among them. That claim is recorded here as self-presentation, not adjudicated.

**Epistemology of acceptance.** The book ends with the **promise of Moroni** (Moroni 10:3–5): readers are to ask God with real intent whether the book is true; the Holy Ghost will manifest its truth. This private spiritual confirmation is the dominant LDS conversion method and is itself a major locus of dispute with historical-critical criteria.

## Composition Claim and Text Frame

- **Title page (Mormon / Moroni):** an abridgment of Nephite and Lamanite records by the prophet-historian **[[mormon-historian|Mormon]]**, sealed by his son **[[moroni|Moroni]]**, to come forth "by way of the Gentile"; also an abridgment from the **Book of Ether** (Jaredites, associated with the tower / confounding of language). Purpose: show the remnant of Israel the Lord's covenants, and **convince Jew and Gentile that Jesus is the Christ, the Eternal God**, manifesting himself to all nations. Fault disclaimer: mistakes of men, not of God. Translator line: Joseph Smith, Jun.
- **Three Witnesses** (Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, Martin Harris): angelic exhibition of the plates; voice of God on the translation.
- **Eight Witnesses** (Whitmers, Page, Smith family): physical handling of plates "of the appearance of gold."
- **Small plates vs. large plates:** Nephi keeps a ministry record (small plates) and a political/war record (large plates). **Words of Mormon** is Mormon's editorial bridge: he inserts the small plates for a "wise purpose" he does not fully understand — a meta-narrative that later LDS reading connects to the lost 116 pages of early translation.

## Structure and Narrative Arc

The book gathers fifteen named books (some only a page long) — **1–2 Nephi, Jacob, Enos, Jarom, Omni, Words of Mormon, Mosiah, Alma, Helaman, 3–4 Nephi, Mormon, Ether, Moroni** — but they tell one continuous, roughly thousand-year story of a single family and the two rival nations that descend from it. The spine of that story is simple: a family leaves Jerusalem for a "promised land" in the Americas, splits into a believing people and a rebellious one, and the two war and reconcile across the centuries until the entire believing nation is finally destroyed — with the whole record building toward, and then remembering, the day the resurrected Christ appeared among them. The four movements below follow that arc.

### 1. Exodus and the founding split (1 Nephi – Words of Mormon)

Around 600 BCE, in the first year of King **Zedekiah** of Judah, a Jerusalem prophet named **[[lehi|Lehi]]** is warned of the city's coming destruction and leads his family into the wilderness toward the Red Sea. His youngest capable son, **[[nephi|Nephi]]**, is the faithful one; his eldest sons, Laman and Lemuel, resent being uprooted and murmur constantly — a family rift that will harden into the book's central division. The sons are sent back twice: once to obtain the **brass plates** (the law and the prophets, including Jeremiah, plus their Josephite genealogy) from Laban, whom Nephi kills at the Spirit's command to secure the record; and once to bring back wives. Guided by a faith-operated compass, the **Liahona**, they cross Arabia to a coastal place they call **Bountiful**, build a ship, and sail to the promised land.

Two visionary set-pieces frame everything that follows. Lehi's and Nephi's **tree-of-life vision** (1 Ne 8, 11–15) previews the coming of the Messiah and the distant future of Lehi's descendants, and introduces the claim that "plain and precious" truths would be taken out of the Bible and later restored through Nephite scripture. After Lehi dies, the fracture becomes permanent: those who follow Nephi become the **[[nephites|Nephites]]**, those who follow Laman the **[[lamanites|Lamanites]]** (2 Ne 5), and the enmity between them drives the rest of the narrative. Before the record narrows, Nephi and his brother **[[jacob-bom|Jacob]]** lay down the doctrinal foundation — the "opposition in all things" and the fortunate Fall ("Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they might have joy," 2 Ne 2:25); a prophecy that a latter-day seer named Joseph will bring the book forth (2 Ne 3); a long block of **[[isaiah|Isaiah]]** (2 Ne 12–24) "likened" to their own people; the plain "doctrine of Christ" (2 Ne 31); and Zenos's allegory of the tame and wild olive trees for Israel's scattering and gathering (Jacob 5). The small plates then dwindle through a chain of brief keepers (Enos, Jarom, Omni), during which the Nephites discover and absorb the people of **Zarahemla** (Mulekites, another group of Jerusalem refugees). **Words of Mormon** is the later editor's seam stitching this early record to the main history that follows.

### 2. Kings, conversions, and the long war (Mosiah – Alma)

The center of the book unfolds around the city of Zarahemla. The righteous **[[king-benjamin|King Benjamin]]** binds his people by covenant to take upon them the name of Christ (Mosiah 2–5). The narrative then follows a splinter colony that had gone back to resettle the old land of Nephi, where it falls under the wicked king Noah; there the prophet **[[abinadi|Abinadi]]** preaches Christ from Isaiah 53 and is burned alive, but his words secretly convert one of Noah's priests, Alma, who founds a covenant church in hiding at the waters of Mormon and leads his followers back to Zarahemla. In the next generation the persecutor **[[alma-the-younger|Alma the Younger]]** and the sons of Mosiah are struck down and dramatically converted (Mosiah 27); the monarchy is dissolved in favor of elected judges, and the converted princes go as missionaries to the Lamanites — winning converts (the **Anti-Nephi-Lehies**) who bury their weapons and vow never to shed blood again.

As chief judge and high priest, Alma the Younger anchors the book's doctrinal peak. He preaches spiritual rebirth (Alma 5) and answers a series of challengers and crises: the anti-Christ **Korihor**, who demands empirical proof; the apostate Zoramites, to whom Alma teaches faith as a seed to be experimentally planted (Alma 32); his companion Amulek's sermon on the **infinite atonement** (Alma 34); and his private counsel to his sons, especially the wayward Corianton, where the book works out justice and mercy, the state of the soul after death, and the "plan of happiness" — God himself, Alma says, would "cease to be God" if mercy could rob justice (Alma 39–42). The book's back half (Alma 43–63) turns to a grinding cycle of Lamanite wars, dominated by the general **Captain Moroni**, who rallies the Nephites under the **title of liberty**, and by **Helaman's** two thousand young "stripling warriors" — with internal treason and dissent (the Pahoran correspondence) as dangerous as the external enemy.

### 3. Prophecy, catastrophe, and the appearance of Christ (Helaman – 3 Nephi)

Prosperity breeds the recurring "pride cycle," and Nephite society decays from within as the **Gadianton robbers** — secret oath-bound bands whose evil the text traces back to Cain and to the earlier Jaredites — infiltrate and seize the government. Prophets multiply against the decline: Nephi and Lehi (Helaman's sons) are converted in prison amid fire and a still voice (Hel 5), and the outsider prophet **Samuel the Lamanite** proclaims from the city wall the exact signs that will mark Christ's birth and death, together with the coming ruin of the Nephites (Hel 13–15).

Those signs arrive, and **3 Nephi** brings the whole book to its climax. A day, a night, and a day pass without darkness at Christ's birth; at his death, three days of storm, earthquake, and darkness annihilate the wicked cities, and the voice of Christ is heard mourning them. Then the resurrected **Jesus Christ descends** to the survivors gathered at the temple in Bountiful (3 Ne 11): the Father introduces him, the multitude come forward one by one to feel the wounds in his hands and side, and he institutes baptism and declares "this is my doctrine." He delivers a version of the **Sermon on the Mount** (3 Ne 12–14, paralleling Matthew 5–7 with distinctive wording), identifies these people as the "other sheep" of John 10, promises a gathering of Israel and a New Jerusalem on "this land," institutes the sacrament, blesses the children as angels descend, and chooses twelve disciples — three of whom, like John, are promised they will never taste death. This visitation is the theological reason the entire record exists.

### 4. Decline, the Jaredite mirror, and Moroni's seal (4 Nephi – Moroni)

The visit inaugurates nearly two centuries of utopian peace: the people hold all things in common and the old tribal labels ("-ites") vanish (4 Nephi). But pride, class division, false churches, and the Gadianton bands return, and the society slides toward its end. The prophet-general **[[mormon-historian|Mormon]]** — the same figure who abridged the whole preceding record — narrates the final wars in the first person, culminating in the annihilation of the Nephite nation at the hill Cumorah, where he numbers the twenty-four survivors, among them his son **[[moroni|Moroni]]**. Mormon is killed, and Moroni is left alone (Mormon 8) to finish the book.

Moroni first appends the **Book of Ether**, the record of a far older people, the **[[jaredites|Jaredites]]**, who had come to the same land from the tower of Babel. Their story is a deliberate mirror of the Nephites': it opens with the **brother of Jared's** overwhelming vision of the premortal Christ, who shows himself as a full spirit body and declares "I am Jesus Christ… the Father and the Son" (Ether 3), and it ends — like the Nephite story — in mutual extermination driven by the same secret combinations, on a hill (Ramah) the text identifies with Cumorah. Moroni then closes the whole book with a short pastoral appendix: instructions on ordination and the sacrament prayers, a discourse on faith, hope, and charity, a sharp condemnation of infant baptism, an account of the gifts of the Spirit, and finally the **promise of Moroni** (Moroni 10) — that a reader who asks God with real intent will receive a spiritual confirmation of the book's truth — before he seals up the plates and takes his leave.

## Major Themes

1. **Christ from the beginning** — Pre-Christian Nephite prophecy names the Messiah, and the narrative climaxes in his American ministry; "Jesus is the Christ, the Eternal God" (title page).
2. **Covenant Israel and the American promised land** — choice land; prosper/cut-off formula; gathering; Gentiles as both scourge and nursing parents; New Jerusalem on this continent.
3. **Plan of salvation** — Fall, probation, free agency, infinite atonement, resurrection, justice/mercy.
4. **Pride cycle and secret combinations** — prosperity → pride → destruction; Gadianton oaths as satanic social order.
5. **Records and witnesses** — dual plates; sealed book; three/eight witnesses; Moroni's epistemological promise.
6. **Isaiah and "likening"** — extensive KJV-adjacent Isaiah with Nephite application (1 Ne 19:23; 2 Ne 11, 25).

## Hermeneutical Method (native to the text)

- **Plainness** vs. Jewish "manner of prophesying" (2 Ne 25).
- **Likening** scriptures to one's own people (1 Ne 19:23).
- **Typology** — law of Moses and all things typify Christ (2 Ne 11:4); Liahona and brass plates as "small means."
- **Prophetic present** — Christ spoken of as if already come (Jarom; Abinadi).
- **Experimental faith** (Alma 32) and **spiritual confirmation** (Moroni 10) as truth procedures.
- **Editorial self-consciousness** — Mormon and Moroni comment on abridgment limits ("hundredth part").

## Historicity and Controversy

Mainstream archaeology, linguistics, and genetics do not support the book's claims about pre-Columbian Israelite civilizations in the Americas as narrated. Believing readers accept the book on the strength of spiritual witness, prophetic authority, and (for some) limited-geography or other harmonizing models. Critical readers treat it as a 19th-century religious composition engaging the KJV, American frontier theology, and questions of Indian origins. **Recorded without adjudication** — see [[book-of-mormon-historicity]] and [[mormon-christian-identity]].
