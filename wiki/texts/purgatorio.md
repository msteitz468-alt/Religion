# Purgatorio (*Divine Comedy*, Cantica 2)

The second cantica of [[divine-comedy|the *Commedia*]] (33 cantos): the ascent of
the character [[dante-alighieri|Dante]], still guided by [[virgil|Virgil]] (joined
later by [[statius|Statius]]), up the mountain-island of [[purgatory|Purgatory]] in
the southern hemisphere, from the shore to the Garden of Eden at its summit, where
[[virgil|Virgil]] departs and [[beatrice|Beatrice]] takes over. The journey runs
from Easter Sunday dawn to noon of the Wednesday after. Read here in Robin
Kirkpatrick's translation with his per-canto notes.

## The Mountain's Structure

- **Ante-Purgatory** (the shore and lower slopes, Cantos 1–9): souls who delayed
  repentance must wait before entering. Guarded by **Cato of Utica** — a pagan and
  a suicide, whose role Kirkpatrick flags as "surprising" (Dante reads his death
  for liberty as self-sacrifice, *De Monarchia* 2:5; in *Convivio* 4:28 Cato is
  "compared allegorically to God"). Souls cannot climb at night, which "knots the
  will in powerlessness" (7:57).
- **The Gate** (Canto 9): three steps (recognition of sin / contrition /
  satisfaction), an angel-warder with two keys (gold = the Church's power to
  forgive, silver = discernment, both from Peter), who inscribes **seven Ps**
  (*peccata*, the seven capital vices) on Dante's brow — one erased on each
  terrace.
- **The Seven Terraces**, purging the [[sin|capital vices]] in order, each with
  carved or voiced exemplars of the opposing virtue (the "whip") and of the vice
  punished (the "bridle"), and each ending with an angel pronouncing a Beatitude:

| Terrace | Vice | Purgation |
|---------|------|-----------|
| 1 | Pride | Bent double under heavy stones; floor carved with the humbled-proud |
| 2 | Envy | Eyes sewn shut with iron wire, in hair shirts |
| 3 | Wrath | Walking blind in acrid smoke, chanting *Agnus Dei* |
| 4 | Sloth | Running ceaselessly with zeal |
| 5 | Avarice/Prodigality | Lying face-down, bound, "my soul cleaves to the ground" |
| 6 | Gluttony | Emaciated, starving past fragrant unreachable trees |
| 7 | Lust | Walking within a wall of fire |

- **The Earthly Paradise** (the Garden of Eden atop the mountain, Cantos 28–33):
  the forest, [[matelda|Matelda]], the mystic procession, the rivers Lethe and
  Eunoë.

## Selected Cantos and Figures

- **Cantos 1–8 (Ante-Purgatory).** Cato; the angel-boat singing Psalm 114 (*In
  exitu Israel*); **Casella** singing Dante's own canzone until Cato rebukes the
  delay; **Manfred**, excommunicate yet saved — "while hope shows any hint of
  green" no curse of the Church damns absolutely (3:133–35); the late-repentant
  dead **Buonconte da Montefeltro** (son of [[guido-da-montefeltro|Guido]]), saved
  by "one little tear" and Mary's name even as a devil claims his body — the exact
  inverse of his father's fate; **La Pia**; the troubadour **Sordello**, who
  embraces Virgil at the mere word "Mantua," prompting Dante's great invective
  against a leaderless Italy (Canto 6); the Valley of the Princes.
- **Cantos 10–12 (Pride).** Carved exemplars of humility (Annunciation, David
  dancing before the Ark, Trajan and the widow); **Oderisi da Gubbio** on the
  vanity of artistic fame — "the roar of earthly fame is just a breath of wind"
  (11:100); **Provenzan Salvani**, fast-tracked for one act of public
  self-abasement. "Worms born to become angelic butterflies" (10:124–25).
- **Cantos 13–14 (Envy).** **Sapia of Siena**, who rejoiced at her own city's
  defeat; **Guido del Duca** on the degraded Arno valley.
- **Cantos 15–17 (Wrath).** **Marco Lombardo**'s central discourse on **free will**
  — the stars incline but do not compel: "you have a mind / that planets cannot
  rule" (16:79–81) — and the **"two suns"**: Rome once had Church and Empire as
  co-equal guides; their merger ("the sword is joined now to the shepherd's crook")
  is the root of corruption (16:106–11; cf. *De Monarchia*). The terraces are
  framed by Virgil's account of **love as the root of all virtue and vice**
  (Cantos 17–18): natural love cannot err, but rational love can be misdirected,
  excessive, or deficient.
- **Cantos 19–20 (Avarice).** **Pope Adrian V**, who forbids Dante to kneel ("I am…
  co-servant of one single Power"); **Hugh Capet**'s diatribe against his Capetian
  descendants, including Charles of Valois (whose intervention caused Dante's
  exile) and the outrage at Anagni against Boniface VIII — "Christ recaptured in
  his vicar's form" (20:87).
- **Cantos 21–22 (Statius).** An earthquake and *Gloria* mark a soul's completed
  purgation: **[[statius|Statius]]**, freed after centuries, joins the journey.
  Dante invents for him a secret Christian conversion catalysed by [[virgil|Virgil]]'s
  Fourth *Eclogue* (read as a prophecy of Christ): "I was, through you, a poet and
  was Christian, too" (22:73) — yet he hid his faith and so spent 400 years on the
  terrace of sloth. The *Aeneid* was "a mum to me, to me my nurse in poetry."
- **Cantos 23–24 (Gluttony).** **Forese Donati**, Dante's old friend, fast-tracked
  by his widow Nella's prayers; **Bonagiunta da Lucca** and the definition of the
  *dolce stil novo*: "I am just one who, when / Love breathes in me, takes note and
  then goes on / showing the meaning that's ordained within" (24:52–54).
- **Canto 25 (the soul).** **Statius**'s dense discourse on **embryology and the
  soul**: the body is *generated* but the rational soul is *created* directly by
  God (against Averroes, who made the intellect a separable communal pool); after
  death the soul radiates an "aerial body" or shade — which is why the gluttons can
  still appear emaciated.
- **Cantos 26–27 (Lust).** Two bands of penitents (heterosexual and homosexual)
  exchanging a brief kiss as they pass in the fire; **Guido Guinizelli**, "the sire
  of me and all those… who ever wrote about love in sweet… rhyme," who defers to
  the troubadour **Arnaut Daniel** — who speaks eight lines of Provençal (the only
  non-Italian speech in the poem) before hiding "in the fire that sharpens them."
  Dante must pass *through* the wall of fire; Virgil overcomes his terror only by
  naming **Beatrice**, then speaks his last words: "Lord of yourself, I crown and
  mitre you" (27:142).
- **Cantos 28–29 (Earthly Paradise).** **[[matelda|Matelda]]** gathering flowers
  across the river Lethe; her explanation of Eden's meteorology and the two rivers;
  the **mystic procession** — seven candlesticks (gifts of the Spirit), 24 elders
  (OT books), four winged beasts (Evangelists), the **griffin** (Christ in two
  natures) drawing a **chariot** (the Church), and the dancing theological and
  cardinal virtues.
- **Cantos 30–31 (Beatrice).** "*Veni, sponsa, de Libano*"; **Beatrice** appears
  (green, white, flame-red) as **Virgil silently vanishes** — Dante's grief
  framed by an unspoken quotation of Virgil's own Dido ("I recognize the signs of
  ancient flame," 30:48). Beatrice speaks Dante's name (the poem's only
  self-naming), rebukes his straying after her death toward "images of failing
  good," and exacts his confession; **Matelda** draws him through **Lethe**
  (oblivion of sin). The griffin's two natures shine alternately in Beatrice's eyes.
- **Cantos 32–33.** The allegorical **masque of the Church's afflictions**
  (persecutions, heresy, the Donation of Constantine, Islam, Carolingian
  endowments, the seven-headed monster, the Whore and the Giant — read as the
  papacy's humiliation and removal to Avignon). Beatrice's deliberately insoluble
  **DXV prophecy** ("FIVE HUNDRED TEN AND FIVE… a messenger of God," 33:43) of a
  coming deliverer (linked to the Inferno's *veltro*), and her attack on the limits
  of "your school" of philosophy. Dante drinks from **Eunoë** (restored memory of
  the good) and is "pure and prepared to rise towards the stars."

## Theological Notes

- **Purgatory as hope and process.** Unlike [[hell|Hell]], Purgatory is temporary,
  hopeful, and subject to time, day/night, and the senses. Its souls *will* their
  own purgation; pain is willingly embraced (see [[purgatory|Purgatory]]).
- **Suffrages for the dead.** Repeatedly, the prayers of the living "in a state of
  grace" shorten a soul's time (Manfred, Belacqua, Forese, Provenzan); intercession
  is bilateral (the proud's Lord's Prayer is offered *for the living*).
- **Reason yielding to revelation.** Virgil (natural reason) guides as far as the
  Earthly Paradise but no further; [[beatrice|Beatrice]] (revelation/grace)
  supersedes him. [[matelda|Matelda]] images the natural happiness humanity would
  have kept without the Fall.
- **Free will.** Marco Lombardo (Canto 16) and Virgil (Cantos 17–18) make
  Purgatorio the poem's fullest treatment of [[freedom|free will]] and moral
  responsibility.
