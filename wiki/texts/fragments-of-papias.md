# Fragments of Papias

## Textual Overview

The **Fragments of Papias** preserve virtually everything that survives of the writings and traditions associated with **Papias of Hierapolis**, bishop of Hierapolis in Asia Minor (modern Pamukkale, Turkey). His major work — *Expositions of the Sayings of the Lord* (*Kyriakōn logiōn exēgēsis*), in five books — is lost entirely except for fragments quoted in later writers, primarily Eusebius of Caesarea (*Church History*) and Irenaeus.

**Format of survival**: Unlike the other Apostolic Fathers texts, Papias' work does not survive as a document. Instead, the Holmes edition compiles:
1. Fragments preserved in Eusebius (*Church History* 3.36; 3.39)
2. Fragments preserved in Irenaeus (*Against Heresies*)
3. Fragments from Philip of Side (5th century) and other later writers
4. "Traditions of the Elders" — a section from Lightfoot's edition presenting anonymous elder-traditions in Irenaeus that may have been mediated through Papias
5. Additional fragments (nos. 21–28 in Holmes' 3rd edition) not in earlier collections

The Holmes edition provides 28 fragments; the total is the most complete collection available.

**Papias' relationship to [[polycarp|Polycarp]]**: Both are attested as "hearers of John" and contemporaries. Eusebius identifies them as "the most ancient" witnesses of the Asian church (Frag. 2). Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna; Papias was bishop of Hierapolis. Irenaeus knew both Papias and Polycarp in his youth.

## Historical Context

**Date**: Eusebius places Papias in the reign of Trajan (98–117 CE), which accords with his being a companion of Polycarp. If he was a hearer of John the Elder, he was probably active from ca. 80–130 CE. His *Expositions* were probably written ca. 100–125 CE.

**John the Elder vs. John the Apostle**: The most famous and contested issue in Papias scholarship. In the preface to his *Expositions* (Fragment 3 = Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.3–4), Papias names two persons called John:
1. "Andrew, or Peter, or Philip, or Thomas, or James, or John or Matthew, or any other of the Lord's disciples" — past tense; the Apostle John
2. "Aristion and the elder John, the Lord's disciples, **were saying**" — present tense; still alive in Papias' time

Eusebius uses this passage to argue that **two different Johns** lived in Ephesus — the Apostle (one of the Twelve) and "John the Elder" (a disciple but not an apostle) — and concludes that the *Revelation* was written by the Elder, not the Apostle. This allows Eusebius (following Dionysius of Alexandria) to separate the John who wrote the Gospel and Letters from the John who wrote the apocalyptic book — the latter being a millenarian, which Eusebius found embarrassing.

**Eusebius' assessment of Papias**: "A man of very little intelligence (*sphodra smikros tōn noun*)" — Eusebius is scornful of Papias' millennialism, which he blames for misleading "even Irenaeus and many others." This polemical dismissal should be noted; Eusebius quotes Papias extensively even while denigrating him.

## The Key Traditions

### Fragment 3.15 — On Mark

> "And the elder said this: 'Mark, having become Peter's interpreter (*hermēneutēs*), wrote down accurately (*akribōs*) everything he remembered, though not in order (*ou mentoi taxei*), of the things either said or done by Christ. For he neither heard the Lord nor followed him, but afterward, as I said, followed Peter, who used to give his teachings as the need arose, not as though he were making a systematic compilation of the Lord's sayings. So then, Mark did nothing wrong in writing down some things as he remembered them, for he made it his concern not to omit anything he heard or make any false statement in them.'"

This passage is **the most discussed text in NT scholarship** concerning the origins of the Gospel of Mark. Key claims:
1. **Mark = Peter's interpreter**: Mark translates or mediates Peter's teaching (Aramaic-to-Greek? oral-to-written?)
2. **Accurate but not in order**: Mark records faithfully what he remembers, but the arrangement is not according to chronological or logical order
3. **Peter taught as needed**: Peter's teaching was occasional, responsive to audience needs — not a systematic account
4. **Mark neither heard nor followed Jesus**: Mark was not an eyewitness; his authority is indirect, through Peter

The tradition has been read as:
- A defense of Mark against criticisms of its arrangement and incompleteness
- Evidence that the Second Gospel was associated with Peter's preaching in Rome
- A witness that by ca. 120 CE, "Mark" was identified as the compiler of a gospel and "Peter" was his source

### Fragment 3.16 — On Matthew

> "So Matthew composed the oracles (*logia*) in the Hebrew language (*Hebraïdi dialektōi*), and each person interpreted (*hermēneusen*) them as best he could."

This fragment is equally debated:
- **Logia**: Does this mean the canonical *Gospel of Matthew*? Or an earlier collection of Jesus' sayings ("Q"? an *Ur*-Matthew?)? Or OT testimonies about Jesus?
- **Hebrew language**: Was there a Hebrew/Aramaic Matthew? The canonical Matthew shows no clear signs of being a translation from Hebrew/Aramaic. Many scholars conclude Papias does not refer to our canonical Matthew but to an earlier source
- **"Each person interpreted them as best he could"**: This may explain diversity in the reception of Matthew's tradition, or it may reflect Papias' awareness that the "Hebrew" source required translation and adaptation

### Fragment 3.11–12 — The Millennium

> "Among other things [Papias] says there will be a millennium after the resurrection of the dead, when the kingdom of Christ will be set up in material form on this earth... And he also passes along other accounts of the words of the Lord belonging to Aristion, who has been mentioned above..."

Papias describes a materialist millennium — after the resurrection, Christ's kingdom will be established on earth, with extraordinary agricultural fertility (a vine producing 10,000 branches, each branch 10,000 clusters, etc.). Eusebius attributes this to Papias having "misunderstood" the apostolic accounts, "not realizing that the things recorded in figurative language were spoken by them mystically."

Irenaeus adopted Papias' millennial teaching (Irenaeus explicitly says Papias was "a hearer of John and companion of Polycarp"), which is partly why Eusebius disparages Papias — discrediting the source helps discredit the tradition.

**Relevance**: Papias' millennialism is directly connected to the authorship of Revelation. If Papias received his millennialism from John the Elder (not the Apostle), then Revelation — the text that inspired Papias' millennialism — may have been written by the Elder, not the Apostle. Eusebius exploits this connection.

### Fragment 5 — John and James Killed by Jews?

From Philip of Side (5th century), a less reliable source:

> "Papias says in his second book that John the Theologian and James his brother were killed by Jews."

If authentic, this would mean John the Apostle was martyred (by Jews), contradicting the dominant tradition that John lived to an extreme old age in Ephesus and died peacefully. The fragment is from a secondary source and may misrepresent Papias. It is, however, consistent with Mark 10:38–39 ("Can you drink the cup I drink?"), which may predict the martyrdom of both James and John.

### Fragments 4 and 23 — The Woman Taken in Adultery

Papias is associated (via Eusebius' statement in Frag. 3.17 and Fragment 23) with a tradition about "a woman accused of many sins before the Lord" — related to, but not identical with, the *pericope adulterae* of John 7:53–8:11 (a late addition to John's Gospel). Holmes discusses at length which form of the story Papias knew; the evidence is mixed.

## Papias' Hermeneutical Principles

The preface to Papias' *Expositions* (Fragment 3.3–4) is one of the most important statements about early Christian oral tradition and its relationship to written texts:

> "I will not hesitate to set down for you along with my interpretations everything I carefully learned from the elders and carefully remembered, guaranteeing their truth. For unlike most people I did not enjoy those who have a great deal to say, but those who teach the truth. Nor did I enjoy those who recall someone else's commandments, but those who remember the commandments given by the Lord to the faith and proceeding from the truth itself. And if by chance someone who had been a follower of the elders should come my way, I inquired about the words of the elders — what Andrew or Peter said, or Philip or Thomas or James or John or Matthew or any other of the Lord's disciples, and whatever Aristion and the elder John, the Lord's disciples, were saying. For I did not think that information from books would profit me as much as information from a living and abiding voice." (Frag. 3.3–4)

**The "living voice" principle**: Papias preferred oral tradition from those who had known the elders to written books — even the Gospels. This is remarkable given that by ca. 120 CE, all four canonical Gospels were probably in circulation. The preference for "living voice" over "books" does not mean Papias rejected written texts, but that he valued direct oral connection to the eyewitness generation above all.

This principle is directly relevant to the [[nt-canon-formation|NT canon formation]] question: Papias represents a stage when written texts were authoritative but the oral tradition was still valued above them.

## Hermeneutical Framework

Papias is neither an allegorist nor a systematizer. He is a collector of traditions in the mode of an **oral historian** — interested in what the elders said, what the disciples reported, and what the "living voice" transmitted. His hermeneutical commitment is to the oral chain of custody from eyewitnesses to elders to informants to himself.

His reading of the millennium as literal and material is consistent with a strongly **literal-historical** approach — though Eusebius categorizes this as obtuse rather than principled. The contrast with Alexandrian allegorism ([[clement-of-alexandria|Clement]], [[origen|Origen]]) is stark: Papias reads the promised abundance of the messianic age literally; the Alexandrians read it as spiritual allegory.

## Canon and Reception

Papias's *Expositions* were **patristic testimony, never canon**; they survive only as fragments
quoted by later writers (chiefly [[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] and Eusebius). Their enduring importance is
evidential — the traditions that **Mark wrote as Peter's interpreter** and that **Matthew composed
"the logia"** are foundational for later [[nt-canon-formation|gospel-origins]] discussion — rather
than canonical. Recorded as `authoritative_for` proto-orthodoxy.
