# Haggai

The tenth book of the [[book-of-the-twelve|Book of the Twelve]] (in both the Hebrew
and Greek orders), and the second-shortest book in the Old Testament (38 verses, two
chapters). Haggai records four dated oracles delivered by the prophet Haggai over
fifteen weeks in 520 BCE, urging the discouraged post-exilic community in Jerusalem to
resume rebuilding the temple.

The wiki's coverage rests on [[pieter-verhoef]], *The Books of Haggai and Malachi*
(NICOT, 1987). Interpretive claims below are attributed to Verhoef.

## Textual Overview

Haggai is a third-person *report* on the prophet's utterances and their effect,
rather than a first-person collection of oracles — a feature central to the
authorship debate (below). Verhoef's pericope structure:

| Section | Reference | Theme | Julian date |
|---|---|---|---|
| A.1 | 1:1–11 | The command to rebuild the temple | 29 Aug 520 (1:1) |
| A.2 | 1:12–15a | The people's favorable response | 21 Sept 520 (1:15) |
| B.1 | 1:15b–2:9 | The promised glory of the new temple | 17 Oct 520 (2:1) |
| B.2 | 2:10–19 | Blessings for a defiled people | 18 Dec 520 (2:10) |
| B.3 | 2:20–23 | [[zerubbabel|Zerubbabel]], the Lord's chosen signet ring | 18 Dec 520 (2:20) |

The four dates are synchronized to Babylonian records and new-moon tables. Verhoef
holds the temple was completed as a *result* of the prophecies, in spring 515 BCE
(Ezra 6:15), not during Haggai's ministry.

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

Verhoef organizes Haggai's "theology" around three themes — **God, the temple, and
the anointed one of the future.**

- **"Consider your ways"** (1:5, 7; 2:15, 18). Haggai's characteristic phrase (*śîm
  lēḇ ʿal-darḵêḵem*, "set your heart upon your ways"), in the double sense of "what
  you have been doing and what it is leading to." The people plead the "time has not
  yet come," but their misplaced priorities — paneled houses while God's house lies a
  ruin — betray failure to "seek first his kingdom" (Verhoef's recurring cross-ref to
  Matt. 6:33).
- **Covenant curse and the drought** (1:6, 9–11). See [[covenant]] and
  [[doctrine-of-retribution]]. Verhoef reads the futility of their labor as a
  covenant sanction (Lev. 26; Deut. 28), not mere misfortune — turning on the
  wordplay between the temple's *ḥārēḇ* ("ruin") and the *ḥōreḇ* ("drought") God
  sends: "the one is the cause and explanation of the other."
- **"I am with you"** (1:13; 2:4). The assurance of God's guiding, accompanying
  presence, tied to his Spirit remaining "among you" (2:5) — trust that must replace
  the people's fear before they can work.
- **The glory of the new temple** (2:1–9). See [[eschatological-temple]] and
  [[divine-glory-kabod]]. Against those who despaired that the new temple was as
  "nothing" beside Solomon's, God promises to "shake" the nations and fill "this
  house" with glory. Verhoef reads the shaking within the **holy-war / Day of the
  Lord** framework and interprets "the precious things of all nations" (2:7) as
  spoils/treasures, *not* (as the Vulgate–Luther "desire of all nations" tradition
  held) the Messiah — a christological reading he says "is now generally abandoned."
- **The priestly torah on defilement** (2:10–14). Haggai asks the priests for a
  ruling: sanctity does not transfer by contact, but defilement does "like a
  contagious disease." A defiled people — defiled by neglect of the ruined temple —
  defiles everything it touches. Verhoef rejects the anti-Samaritan reading of "this
  people" (Rothstein).
- **From curse to blessing** (2:15–19). "From this day on I will bless you" — the
  climax; the parenthetical vv. 16–17 recall past covenant curses (blight, mildew,
  hail).
- **Zerubbabel the signet ring** (2:20–23). See [[zerubbabel]] and
  [[davidic-covenant]]. God will overthrow the thrones of the nations and make
  Zerubbabel "like a signet ring," reversing Jehoiachin's rejection (Jer. 22:24) — the
  renewed election of the Davidic line, read by Verhoef as messianic and fulfilled
  "centrally in the first and finally in the second advent of Christ."

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

Verhoef judges the text of Haggai "well preserved"; most emendations proposed in
BHK/BHS he regards as unnecessary, ascribing the book's "defectiveness" to stylistic
clumsiness (Harrison) rather than corruption. The **Murabbaʿat** fragments support the
MT, differing in only two minor points among 289 extant words. The four main versions
(LXX, Vulgate, Peshitta, Targum) essentially agree with the MT; the LXX renumbers
parts of ch. 2 and inclines to "verbal expansion" but does not support the proposed
transposition of 2:15–19.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

- **Grammatical-historical, redemptive-historical (Verhoef).** Sustained Hebrew
  philology and text-criticism, defending the unity and integrity of the MT against
  source-critical dissection; a "telescoping" reading of prophecy fulfilled in stages
  culminating in Christ.
- **Structural analysis.** Verhoef uses sentence-level structural analysis as an
  "antidote to dismembering a literary unit" — his chief argument against the
  source-splitting of Rothstein, Eissfeldt, and Elliger.
- **Dialogue style** akin to [[malachi|Malachi]]'s; frequent chiasm (1:4, 9, 10;
  2:23) and wordplay.

## Canon and Reception

Haggai's canonicity is undisputed; it stands tenth among the Twelve and is named,
with [[zechariah|Zechariah]], in Ezra 5:1; 6:14 as a spur to the temple's rebuilding.
Ben Sira mentions Zerubbabel (Sir. 49:11–12) in connection with this period.

`canon_scope`: canonical for [[judaism|rabbinic Judaism]] and all major branches of
[[christianity|Christianity]].

## Scholarship and Historiography — as positions

- **Authorship.** That the four oracles were *delivered* by Haggai is, in Verhoef's
  words, "incontestable" (precise dates; the *b'yaḏ*, "through," accreditation). Whether
  Haggai composed the *written book* is generally denied — because of its
  report-character and consistent third-person references. Verhoef surveys and rejects
  several redactional theories: Rudolph's *Flugblatt* (a disciples' apology asserting
  Haggai's priority over Zechariah), Ackroyd's late compiler, Klostermann's lost
  temple-chronicle, and Beuken's "Chronistic milieu." He leans toward Eissfeldt and
  Koole (Haggai plausibly responsible, choosing the third person for objectivity and
  dictating to disciples), while conceding "some doubt" about the final written form.
- **The prophet.** Verhoef holds Haggai was **not** a priest (2:12–14 shows he had to
  *obtain* a ruling *from* the priests) and **not** a cultic prophet; he is
  "deliberately and emphatically called 'the prophet.'" Theories of his origin
  (elderly returnee who saw Solomon's temple; Judean farmer left in the land; priestly
  family) are all left open.
- **Unity.** Verhoef defends the integrity of 1:13, 2:10–19, and 2:20–23 against
  Böhme, André, Eissfeldt, Steck, and especially **Rothstein**, whose anti-Samaritan
  reading of "this people" (2:14) and transposition of 2:15–19 he calls "completely
  unsupported by any textual evidence."
- **Historical background.** The setting is the restoration under Persian rule: Cyrus'
  539 BCE capture of Babylon and edict of return (Ezra 1; corroborated by the Cyrus
  Cylinder); the Sheshbazzar–Zerubbabel foundation problem; a Judahite population
  "scarcely above 20,000" by 522; the accession struggles of [[persians|Darius I]];
  and the memorandum found at Ecbatana forbidding Tattenai to obstruct the work
  (Ezra 5–6). Whether Judah was already a separate sub-province (Grosheide, Verhoef's
  lean) or only became one under Nehemiah (Alt) turns on the meaning of *peḥâ*
  ("governor") applied to Zerubbabel.

## Key interpretive cruxes

- **2:7 "the precious things (*ḥemdaṯ*) of all nations"** — treasures/spoils, or the
  Messiah ("the desire of all nations")? Verhoef, on the ground of v. 8 (silver and
  gold), reads treasures within a holy-war framework and regards the older
  christological reading (Vulgate, Luther, AV) as abandoned.
- **2:9 "the glory of this latter house"** — the comparison is of the *glory*
  (*kāḇôḏ*), not "this house"; the temple is one continuous house across its history.
  Verhoef reads the promise as fulfilled in stages (Herod's temple / the second
  advent / Christ and the church).
- **The "was Haggai mistaken?" debate** on Zerubbabel — see [[zerubbabel]].
- **2:18 "the foundation of the Lord's temple was laid"** vs. the earlier
  foundation-laying of Ezra 3 — Verhoef declines to deny Ezra 3's historicity, taking
  Haggai's statement as marking the "actual and official commencement" of the work.
