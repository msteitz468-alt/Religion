# Protrepticus (Exhortation to the Greeks)

The first work of [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]]'s trilogy
(Protrepticus → [[paedagogus|Paedagogus]] → [[stromata|Stromata]]). A *protreptic* —
the classical genre of exhortation to philosophy — turned to urge educated pagans to
abandon Greek religion for the [[logos-christology|Logos]]. The Delphi edition prints two
complete parallel English translations, William Wilson's (Ante-Nicene Fathers) and
G. W. Butterworth's (Loeb); twelve chapters.

## Structure and Argument

The work opens by contrasting the legendary pagan minstrels (Amphion, Arion, Orpheus,
Eunomus) with a divine singer, and announces its governing image: the **"New Song"** — the
pre-existent [[logos-christology|Word]] who is also Christ. "Behold the might of the new
song! It has made men out of stones, men out of beasts" (l. 200). The Song is not new but
eternal: "do not suppose the song of salvation to be new... For 'before the morning star it
was;' and 'in the beginning was the Word'" (l. 240). The Logos "composed the universe into
melodious order, and tuned the discord of the elements to harmonious arrangement" (l. 203).

The twelve chapters move: (I) the divine Word vs. the mysteries; (II) the obscene fables and
mysteries of the gods; (III) the cruelty of pagan sacrifice; (IV) the absurdity of images;
(V–VII) the philosophers and poets who, "by divine inspiration," sometimes touched the truth;
(VIII) the true doctrine in the Hebrew prophets; (IX–X) the sin of despising God's call and
the answer to the "ancestral custom" objection; (XI–XII) the benefits of Christ's advent and
the closing exhortation.

## Major Themes

### The anthropology of religion — the seven origins of the gods
Clement's most-cited contribution: a euhemerist/anthropological account of how "men
manufactured gods" in stages (Ch. II) — star-worship (deriving θεός from θεῖν, "to run"),
deification of agricultural produce (Demeter = grain, Dionysus = the vine), of retributions
(the Erinyes), of human passions (altars to Fear, Love, Insult), the twelve gods of Hesiod
and Homer, and finally deified human benefactors (the Dioscuri, Heracles, Asclepius). The
gods are dead men (euhemerism): "Seek your Jupiter. Ransack not heaven, but earth. The
Cretan, in whose country he was buried, will show him to you" (l. 866); temples are really
tombs (l. 1046).

### Polemic against the mystery cults
Claiming insider knowledge, Clement "publishes" the secret tokens of the Eleusinian and
Cybele/Attis mysteries (ll. 415, 437, 526) and recounts the Orphic Dionysus-Zagreus myth (the
Titans' toys: "dice, ball, hoop, apples, top, looking-glass," l. 469) and the obscene Baubo
episode, quoting Orpheus as his authority. The mysteries are "murders and funerals" (l. 484).
Yet he co-opts the very vocabulary for Christianity: "O truly sacred mysteries!... The Lord is
the hierophant" (l. 2548) — Christian baptism as the true initiation. See [[mystery-cults|mystery cults]].

### Idolatry and the critique of art
Idols began as unworked wood and stone (the Scythians' sabres, the Arabs' stones) and degenerated
as art advanced: "when art flourished, error increased" (l. 1084). Like Plato, Clement holds
figurative art to be deception; sculptors make gods in the image of their human lovers (Praxiteles'
Aphrodite modelled on his mistress Cratine, l. 1252). Serapis is exposed as a manufactured composite
idol (l. 1119).

### Philosophy and poetry as preparation for the Gospel
Greek thought contains "scintillations of the divine word" (l. 1705); "a certain divine effluence
has been instilled" into thoughtful men (l. 1568). Plato is most praised — "We do not, if you have
no objection, wholly disown Plato" (l. 1562) — and quoted (Timaeus 28c on the difficulty of finding
"the Father and Maker of this universe"). The poets (Aratus, Hesiod, the Sibyl, a monotheistic
"palinode" ascribed to Orpheus) are read as inadvertent witnesses to one God. But Greek insight is
derivative: "for the laws that are consistent with truth, and your sentiments respecting God, you are
indebted to the Hebrews" (l. 1608) — the **"theft of the Greeks"** motif. See
[[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy|harmony of religion and philosophy]].

### Deification — "that man may become God"
The work's theological climax: "the Word of God became man, that thou mayest learn from man how
man may become God" (Wilson, l. 312; Butterworth, "how it is even possible for man to become a
god," l. 2821). Anchored in the image-of-God anthropology ("the image of God is His Word... and the
image of the Word is the true man, the mind which is in man," l. 2142) and Ps 82:6 ("I said, ye are
gods"). See [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]].

## Hermeneutical Cruxes

- The **Odysseus/Sirens allegory** (Ch. XII): pagan custom is the Sirens' deadly song; "bound to
  the wood of the cross, thou shalt be freed from destruction" (l. 2517).
- The serpent of Eden read allegorically as "pleasure crawling on its belly" (l. 2362).
- The inverted Bacchae: the idolater is invited to drop thyrsus and ivy and be initiated into the
  Word's true mysteries.

## Figures and Groups

Recurring foils: **Orpheus** (deceiver, then "palinode" monotheist), the Greek gods (treated
euhemeristically), and a long roll of philosophers (Plato most praised; Epicurus condemned as carrying
"impiety to its full length"). The "atheist" rationalists (Euhemerus, Diagoras, Theodorus) are praised
for at least suspecting the error — "their suspicion is no insignificant seed" (l. 575).
[[moses|Moses]] is "the hierophant of the truth"; the Hebrews are the true source of theology. See
[[clement-of-alexandria|Clement (commentator)]] for the underlying method.

## Canon and Reception

Not scripture in any tradition, but a foundational text of Greek Christian apologetics, valued in
Eastern Orthodoxy and Catholicism as patristic witness. Its anthropology of religion and its
appropriation of mystery-cult and philosophical language shaped later Alexandrian theology
([[origen|Origen]]).
