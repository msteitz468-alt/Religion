Acts of the Apostles is the second volume of the [[luke-acts-unity|Luke-Acts]] two-volume work, picking up where the Gospel of Luke ends (the ascension) and narrating the expansion of the early church from Jerusalem to Rome. Its author is identified by unanimous ancient tradition as Luke, physician and traveling companion of Paul.

## Structure and Genre

Acts moves geographically outward from Jerusalem to Rome, following a mission program that echoes Jesus' words in 1:8: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth."

- **Jerusalem phase** (1–7): Pentecost, early community, Stephen's martyrdom
- **Judea and Samaria** (8–12): Philip, Paul's conversion, Cornelius
- **Paul's first mission** (13–14): Cyprus and Galatia
- **Jerusalem Council** (15): Gentile inclusion settled
- **Paul's second mission** (16–18): Macedonia, Athens, Corinth
- **Paul's third mission** (19–20): Ephesus
- **Paul's arrest and trials** (21–26): Jerusalem, Caesarea
- **Journey to Rome** (27–28): Shipwreck, Malta, Rome

Bock classifies Acts as a "historical monograph" in the ancient sense rather than a classic Greco-Roman "acts" of a single hero (such as Alexander or Augustus). While the title "Acts of the Apostles" highlights the role of witnesses sent from God, the central character is not Peter, Paul, or the apostolic band but **God himself** — his sovereign plan (βουλή τοῦ θεοῦ), his activity, and his orchestration of events. The Spirit is the enabling power sent by the risen Jesus, but the ultimate director is God. The work is selective: not every community or apostle is treated in detail; the narrative highlights themes of continuity with Israel, Gentile inclusion, the ongoing lordship of Jesus, and faithful witness amid opposition. Luke was innovative in creating this two-volume account of how God brought about a new era and community.

The "we passages" (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16) underscore the eyewitness dimension of the historiography. Speeches (roughly one-third of the book) are treated by Bock as authentic summaries that fit their narrative settings and speakers' distinctives, in keeping with ancient historiographical practice (Thucydides, Polybius), not wholesale Lukan invention.

## Four Dominant Issues

Bock identifies four interlocking burdens that drive the narrative of Luke-Acts as a whole:

1. How salvation tied to the God of Israel can include Gentiles without requiring circumcision or full Torah observance (resolved especially in Acts 2–11 and the Jerusalem Council of ch. 15).
2. How the movement can claim to fulfill God's promises when so many in Israel remain unresponsive (the Gospel focuses more here; Acts shows both rejection by many Jews and the continuing faithfulness of a remnant).
3. Jesus' continuing role as the exalted Lord who distributes the Spirit, receives the church's prayers, and will return as judge (Peter's Pentecost sermon in Acts 2 is paradigmatic).
4. The story of faithful witnesses (Peter, John, Stephen, Philip, Paul, Barnabas, and others) who carry the message to the ends of the earth despite opposition. These figures serve as models for the ongoing mission.

The book is therefore both historical and theological: it explains the roots of the new community while demonstrating that what began in Jerusalem is the legitimate, Spirit-empowered continuation of God's purposes for Israel and the nations.

## Authorship and Date

External evidence for Lukan authorship is unanimous by AD 200 (Muratorian Fragment, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius). [[darrell-bock|Bock]] argues that the unanimity is statistically improbable if invented, given the number of Pauline companions who could have been named instead. He prefers a date in the **late 60s CE** (before the fall of Jerusalem, whose approaching destruction Acts seems to sense without yet describing), while acknowledging the 80–100 CE range supported by many critical scholars.

## Key Theological Themes

**God's sovereign plan** (*boulē tou theou*): Luke presents the entire narrative as the unfolding of God's announced purpose, rooted in OT prophecy and executed through the Spirit. The phrase "it was necessary" (*edei*) recurs as a theological marker.

**The Holy Spirit as the driving agent**: The Spirit initiates, guides, and empowers every major development in Acts. Chrysostom's label for Acts as "the Gospel of the Holy Spirit" captures this emphasis.

**Jesus as the exalted Lord**: Lukan Christology is "high" — Jesus is at God's right hand (2:33), actively distributing the Spirit, and will return as judge. His exaltation is the pivot of Peter's Pentecost sermon (2:14–36).

**Gentile inclusion and the new community**: Acts narrates how the predominantly Jewish Jesus-movement becomes a universal community. The Jerusalem Council (ch. 15) is the structural climax: circumcision is not required for Gentiles; the mission to the nations is definitively authorized.

**Continuity with Israel**: Luke presents the Jesus-movement not as a break from Judaism but as its fulfillment. The speeches consistently appeal to Abraham, Moses, and the Prophets. Paul worships in synagogues and the Temple.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**The speeches**: Approximately one-third of Acts consists of speeches (Peter's Pentecost sermon, Paul's Areopagus address, etc.). The critical question is whether these are Luke's literary compositions or summaries of historical tradition. Bock defends them as authentic summaries: their brevity, theological distinctiveness across speakers, and tight fit into narrative settings argue against wholesale Lukan invention. Ancient historians (Thucydides, Polybius) treated speech-composition as a legitimate form of representation, not fabrication.

**Pentecost and tongues (Acts 2)**: The outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost with the sign of speaking in tongues (*glossolalia*) raises questions of theological continuity. Is this a once-for-all inaugurating event or a pattern for all believers? Bock does not engage the cessationist/continuationist debate directly but treats tongues as the Spirit's "revelatory, declarative role" manifesting the new messianic age.

**The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) and Galatians 2**: The critical historical question is whether the Acts 15 council corresponds to the Jerusalem visit Paul describes in Galatians 2. Bock holds a minority position: Acts 11 corresponds to Gal 2, not Acts 15. This would mean Galatians was written before the Council, significantly reshuffling early Pauline chronology. See [[acts-paul-tensions|Acts and Paul's Letters]].

**The "we passages"** (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16): These first-person passages imply Luke's personal presence on certain journeys, supporting the eyewitness dimension of his historical account.

**Paul in Acts vs. Paul's letters**: Acts' portrait of Paul — his temple participation (21:26), Timothy's circumcision (16:3), and harmonious relationship with Jerusalem — appears in tension with Paul's own assertions in Galatians of independence and principled opposition to circumcision. See [[acts-paul-tensions|Acts and Paul's Letters]].

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

Acts is studied through several modern frameworks:
- **Historical-critical**: Questioning speeches' authenticity (Dibelius, Haenchen), late dating, theological tendentiousness
- **Narrative/literary criticism**: Luke-Acts as unified two-volume narrative (Tannehill, Johnson)
- **Social-scientific criticism**: Community formation, patron-client relations, honor-shame dynamics
- **Evangelical historical approach** (Bock, Bruce, Marshall): Luke as careful ancient historian; historicity defensible
