# The Letter to Philemon

Paul's shortest letter, a personal appeal on behalf of Onesimus, a runaway slave who has become a Christian. Written from imprisonment (likely Rome), it is a masterpiece of persuasive rhetoric, pastoral sensitivity, and theological depth on reconciliation, brotherhood in Christ, and the transformation of social relationships by the gospel. McKnight (NICNT, 2017) highlights its enduring message for modern slaveries and racism.

## Authorship and Setting

Pauline authorship is undisputed (with Timothy as co-sender). Paul is imprisoned (vv. 1, 9, 10, 13, 23); traditional view is Rome (c. AD 60–62), though Ephesus or Caesarea are possible. Onesimus, a slave of Philemon (a wealthy Christian in Colossae, host of a house church), has run away, encountered Paul, become a believer ("my child," v. 10), and is now returning with this letter.

The letter is carried by Onesimus himself, along with Tychicus (Col 4:7–9 links it to Colossians).

## Structure (per McKnight)

McKnight structures the letter around three appeals for Onesimus, building rhetorically:

**I. Opening (vv. 1–3)**  
Prescript: Paul (prisoner of Christ) and Timothy to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the house church. Grace and peace.

**II. Thanksgiving and Prayer (vv. 4–7)**  
Paul thanks God for Philemon's love, faith, and the "refreshing" (παράκλησις) he has brought to the saints. Prays that his partnership (κοινωνία) in the faith may become effective in "the knowledge of every good thing that is in us for Christ."

**III. The Body: Three Appeals for Onesimus (vv. 8–22)**  
A. First Appeal in the Presentation of Onesimus (vv. 8–11): Paul could command but appeals "for love's sake." Onesimus ("useful") was once useless but is now useful to both Paul and Philemon. Paul sends him back "as my own heart."

B. Second Appeal in the Presentation of Onesimus (vv. 12–16): Receive him as more than a slave — as a beloved brother, "especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord." Providence: perhaps he was separated for a while so that Philemon might have him back forever.

C. Third Appeal in their Partnership (vv. 17–21): If Philemon considers Paul a partner (κοινωνός), receive Onesimus as Paul himself. Paul offers to repay any debt ("charge it to my account") — a promissory note. Appeals to Philemon's obedience and love. "I know that you will do even more than I say."

D. Final Appeal in a Presence (v. 22): Prepare a guest room; Paul hopes to be granted to them through their prayers.

**IV. Conclusion (vv. 23–25)**  
Greetings from fellow prisoners and co-workers (Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke). Grace.

## Narrative Context: Roman Slavery and Mediation

The core narrative of the letter involves Paul acting as a mediator for a runaway slave, **[[onesimus|Onesimus]]**, whom he is sending back to his master, **[[philemon-figure|Philemon]]**. McKnight extensively analyzes the social realities of slavery in the Roman Empire to contextualize this mediation. Roman slavery was ubiquitous, brutal, and formed the economic backbone of the empire. Slaves were legally property (res) without rights. A runaway slave (fugitivus) faced severe punishment, including crucifixion, branding, or being forced into the arena.

By sending Onesimus back, Paul navigates a dangerous social and legal minefield. He does not attack the institution of slavery abstractly; instead, he subverts it relationally. By mediating the dispute and commanding Philemon to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (v. 16), Paul introduces a kinship dynamic that fundamentally undermines the master-slave hierarchy.

## Major Themes and Narrative

McKnight emphasizes the letter's rhetorical power as a "presentation" of Onesimus: Paul presents the slave as a brother, using every social and theological lever (love, partnership, usefulness, providence, his own "heart," repayment, obedience, eschatological hope).

**Reconciliation and Brotherhood in Christ**: The gospel transforms the master-slave relationship into one of brothers "in the Lord." Onesimus is no longer property but family. This is not abolitionist legislation but a personal, relational transformation that undermines the institution from within.

**Providence and Usefulness**: What was "separation" (perhaps theft or flight) God used for good (cf. Gen 50:20). Onesimus ("useful") lives up to his name in Christ.

**Apostolic Authority and Love**: Paul could command (as apostle and "father" of Onesimus) but appeals "for love's sake." The letter models how authority in the church operates through love and persuasion rather than coercion.

**Modern Application (McKnight)**: The letter speaks directly to modern slaveries (human trafficking, racial injustice, economic exploitation). "What would Paul say to the church about slaveries of all sorts today?" The answer is not mere manumission but full recognition of the slave as brother/sister in Christ, with all the social and economic implications.

**Connection to Colossians**: Sent with Tychicus (Col 4:7–9); Onesimus is "one of you." The letter is "public" in the house church, pressuring Philemon toward the right response.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- **Onesimus' offense**: Runaway? Thief? The letter implies a debt (v. 18) but focuses on reconciliation, not the crime.
- **Manumission?** Paul does not explicitly demand legal freedom, but the language ("no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother") and the public reading make continued slavery untenable. McKnight sees it as effectively emancipatory in the Christian community.
- **"Charge it to my account" (v. 18)**: Legal language of a promissory note. Paul assumes financial responsibility, modeling substitutionary love.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

McKnight employs a "known-to-unknown" methodology (similar to his Colossians work): close reading of social realities (patronage, slavery, honor), rhetorical analysis, and theological synthesis. Strong Second Temple and Greco-Roman background (e.g., slave laws, letter-writing conventions). Pastoral application to contemporary slaveries.

See also controversies around slavery in the Bible and the letter's role in abolitionist arguments (e.g., in 19th-century America).
