1 and 2 Kings complete the narrative arc of Israel’s monarchy from Solomon’s succession struggle through the fall of both kingdoms—Israel to Assyria (722/721 BCE) and Judah to Babylon (586 BCE). The book ends with a flicker of hope: Jehoiachin eating at the table of the king of Babylon (2 Kgs 25:27–30). Jewish tradition ascribed the work to Jeremiah (*b. B. Bat.* 15a); modern scholarship often treats it as the culmination of a [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic History]].

## Textual Overview

In the Hebrew canon Kings belongs to the **Former Prophets** (*Neviʾim rishonim*) with Joshua, Judges, and Samuel. In Christian Bibles it is among the Historical Books. The traditional division into “First” and “Second” Kings is secondary: the break falls mid-Ahaziah (1 Kgs 22:51–2 Kgs 1:18). The LXX titles Samuel–Kings as “1–4 Reigns,” underscoring continuity with Samuel.

The narrative is ordered by **regnal formulae** (accession relative to another king, length of reign, capital, evaluation of religious policy, death/burial, successor, source notice; for Judean kings also mother’s name and age). A full set appears at 1 Kgs 22:41–43, 45, 50.

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

### Structure

| Section | Chapters | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Transition David → Solomon | 1 Kgs 1–2 | Succession struggle; David’s charge |
| Solomon’s reign | 1 Kgs 3–11 | Wisdom; temple; apostasy; deferred division |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kgs 12 – 2 Kgs 17 | Jeroboam’s sin; Elijah/Elisha; fall of Samaria |
| Final years of Judah | 2 Kgs 18–25 | Hezekiah; Manasseh; Josiah; exile; Jehoiachin |

### Solomon (1 Kgs 1–11)

David charges Solomon to keep the **law of Moses** and act with **wisdom** (2:1–4). For much of the reign wisdom functions positively (discerning heart, 3:9; temple, 5–8; renown, 9–10). At both ends it operates self-interestedly: eliminating rivals (ch. 2) and accumulating wealth, horses, and foreign wives that lead to high places (9–11). Apostasy brings the announcement that the kingdom will be torn—not in Solomon’s lifetime; one tribe remains for David’s “lamp” in Jerusalem; division is “not forever” (11:11–39).

**Solomon’s prayer (1 Kgs 8:22–53)** is, for [[iain-provan|Provan]], the theological heart of the book: the temple hosts the **Name** without containing God; the major petition anticipates exile and restoration; “grace always underpins law.”

### Divided kingdom (1 Kgs 12 – 2 Kgs 17)

Rehoboam’s hard line over forced labor triggers secession under Jeroboam. Jeroboam’s golden calves at Bethel and Dan echo Exodus 32; non-Levitical priests and a rival calendar make **“the sin of Jeroboam”** the refrain for every northern king. Prophets (Ahijah, man of God from Judah, Jehu son of Hanani) structure history by word and fulfillment.

**Elijah and Elisha** (1 Kgs 17 – 2 Kgs 13) form the longest continuous prophetic block. Provan reads them as **agents of salvation within judgment**: Carmel; Horeb’s *qôl demāmâh daqqâh*; Naboth; Micaiah; double-portion succession; Naaman; Jehu’s incomplete purge; Joash’s survival of Athaliah (“David” survives “Jezebel”).

**2 Kgs 17** is the book’s most extended theological indictment of the north (first-commandment failure; catalog that also implicates Judah; Jeroboam’s great sin). Mixed resettlement “worship” (17:24–41) is ironic non-worship.

### Final Judah (2 Kgs 18–25)

**Hezekiah** removes high places and trusts YHWH against Sennacherib; even the best king is saved by grace for YHWH’s and David’s sake. **Manasseh** is the worst king; judgment becomes irrevocable. **Josiah** is the best reformer—yet comes “too late”; Huldah’s word stands; he dies at Megiddo. Three deportations dismantle Solomonic glory. **Jehoiachin** at Babylon’s table is Provan’s preferred Joash-like hope that grace may still triumph over law (2 Sam 7:15–16).

## Narrative Summary

### The succession and Solomon's purge (1 Kgs 1–2)

[[david|David]] is old and cannot keep warm; Abishag the Shunammite is brought to lie in his bosom (1:1-4). Adonijah, the eldest surviving son, proclaims himself king at En-rogel with the backing of [[joab|Joab]] and Abiathar the priest, but without [[nathan|Nathan]], Zadok, Benaiah, or the mighty men (1:5-10). Nathan and [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] approach David in concert; David swears that [[solomon|Solomon]] will reign, and Solomon is anointed at Gihon while Adonijah's feast breaks up in panic (1:11-53). David's deathbed charge combines the demand to keep the law of Moses with instructions to deal with Joab and Shimei — do not let Joab's gray head go down to Sheol in peace (2:1-9).

Solomon then consolidates by killing. Adonijah asks for Abishag as wife; Solomon reads it as a claim on the throne and has Benaiah strike him down (2:13-25). Abiathar is exiled to Anathoth (2:26-27). Joab flees to the tent of YHWH and grasps the horns of the altar; Benaiah kills him there and he is buried in the wilderness (2:28-34). Shimei is confined to [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] on pain of death, leaves after three years to retrieve runaway slaves from Gath, and is executed (2:36-46).

### Solomon: wisdom, temple, apostasy (1 Kgs 3–11)

Solomon marries Pharaoh's daughter, and at Gibeon asks in a dream for a listening heart rather than long life or riches (3:1-15). The judgment of the two prostitutes and the living child follows immediately (3:16-28). The reign is described in prosperity and administration: twelve district officers, daily provisions, proverbs and songs, and peace from Dan to Beersheba (4:1-34). With cedar and labor from Hiram of Tyre and a corvée levy, Solomon builds the temple over seven years (5:1–6:38) and his own palace over thirteen (7:1-12), furnished by Hiram the bronzeworker with the two pillars, the molten sea on twelve oxen, and the stands (7:13-51). The ark is installed in the inner sanctuary, the cloud fills the house, and Solomon prays at length that YHWH hear from heaven — including the petition for a people carried captive to the land of their enemies who turn and pray toward the city and the house (8:1-53), followed by an immense dedication sacrifice (8:62-66).

Solomon fortifies [[hazor|Hazor]], [[megiddo|Megiddo]], and [[gezer|Gezer]] (9:15), receives the queen of Sheba, who is left breathless (10:1-13), and accumulates gold, chariots, and horses from Egypt (10:14-29). In old age his seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines turn his heart to Ashtoreth, Milcom, and Chemosh, and he builds high places east of Jerusalem (11:1-8). YHWH announces the tearing of the kingdom from his son, and adversaries rise: Hadad the Edomite, Rezon of [[damascus|Damascus]], and [[jeroboam|Jeroboam]] son of Nebat, to whom the prophet Ahijah gives ten pieces of a torn cloak before he flees to Egypt (11:9-40).

### The schism and the sin of Jeroboam (1 Kgs 12–16)

At [[shechem|Shechem]] [[rehoboam|Rehoboam]] rejects the elders' counsel for the young men's — "my little finger is thicker than my father's loins" — and the northern tribes secede with the cry "What portion have we in David?" (12:1-19). Adoram the taskmaster is stoned to death. Jeroboam sets golden calves at [[bethel|Bethel]] and [[dan|Dan]], installs non-Levitical priests, and moves the feast to the eighth month (12:25-33); an unnamed man of God from Judah condemns the Bethel altar and is later killed by a lion for eating in the north against his own oracle (13:1-32). Ahijah, now blind, tells Jeroboam's disguised wife that the dynasty will be swept away like dung (14:1-18).

The narrative then alternates between the two lines by regnal formula. Judah passes from Rehoboam — whose treasury Shishak of Egypt strips (14:25-28) — through Abijam to Asa, who removes his mother Maacah for her Asherah image (15:9-15). The north churns through coups: Nadab is killed by Baasha, who wipes out Jeroboam's house; Elah is killed by Zimri, who burns himself in the palace after seven days; Omri prevails and buys the hill of [[samaria|Samaria]] for two talents of silver (15:25–16:28). Omri's son [[ahab|Ahab]] marries [[jezebel|Jezebel]] of Sidon and erects an altar to Baal in Samaria (16:29-33).

### Elijah and Elisha (1 Kgs 17 – 2 Kgs 13)

[[elijah|Elijah]] the Tishbite announces drought, is fed by ravens at the Wadi Cherith, multiplies the widow of Zarephath's flour and oil, and revives her son (17:1-24). After three years he meets Ahab and stages the contest on Mount Carmel: the prophets of Baal cut themselves and cry from morning to noon, Elijah mocks them, fire falls on his drenched altar, and he has the four hundred fifty prophets of Baal seized and slaughtered at the Wadi Kishon (18:1-40). Rain comes; Jezebel's death threat sends Elijah to Horeb, where after wind, earthquake, and fire he hears the low whisper and is sent to anoint Hazael, [[jehu|Jehu]], and [[elisha|Elisha]] (19:1-21). Ahab twice defeats Ben-hadad of the [[arameans|Arameans]] and is condemned for sparing him (ch. 20). Jezebel arranges Naboth's judicial murder — a fast, two worthless witnesses charging him with cursing God and king, and stoning outside the city — so Ahab can take the vineyard, and Elijah meets him there with the sentence that dogs will lick his blood and eat Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel (21:1-24). Micaiah son of Imlah alone predicts disaster at Ramoth-gilead against four hundred prophets; Ahab is killed by a random arrow between the scale armor and the joints, and dogs lick his blood at the pool of Samaria (22:1-38).

2 Kings opens with Ahaziah falling through a lattice and sending to Baal-zebub of Ekron; Elijah calls down fire on two companies of fifty (1:1-16). Elijah parts the Jordan with his rolled mantle and is taken up in a whirlwind with chariots of fire; Elisha, having asked for a double portion, takes the mantle and crosses back (2:1-14). Elisha's cycle follows: the boys mauled by two she-bears at Bethel (2:23-25), the widow's oil, the Shunammite's son restored, poison in the pot, twenty loaves for a hundred men (chs. 4), the cleansing of the Aramean commander Naaman in the Jordan and Gehazi's leprosy (ch. 5), the floating axe head, and the blinded Aramean raiders led into Samaria and fed (ch. 6). During Ben-hadad's siege of Samaria a woman appeals to the king because she and a neighbor had agreed to eat their sons — hers was boiled and eaten, and the other has hidden her own (6:24-31); the king tears his clothes and swears to behead Elisha, but four lepers discover the abandoned Aramean camp and the famine breaks overnight (6:32–7:20). Elisha's oracle sets Hazael on the throne of Damascus by smothering Ben-hadad with a wet cloth (8:7-15), and a young prophet anoints Jehu at Ramoth-gilead (9:1-13).

### Jehu, Athaliah, and the fall of Samaria (2 Kgs 9–17)

Jehu drives furiously to Jezreel, shoots Joram between the shoulders and dumps him on Naboth's plot, and has Ahaziah of Judah shot as well (9:14-28). Jezebel paints her eyes and calls from the window; her eunuchs throw her down, horses trample her, and dogs leave only her skull, feet, and palms (9:30-37). Jehu has the seventy sons of Ahab beheaded in Samaria and their heads piled in two heaps at the gate (10:1-11), kills forty-two kinsmen of Ahaziah at the pit of Beth-eked (10:12-14), and lures the Baal worshippers into a great assembly in the temple of Baal where eighty men cut them down; the temple is demolished and made a latrine (10:18-27). He nonetheless keeps the calves of Bethel and Dan.

In Judah, [[athaliah|Athaliah]], mother of Ahaziah, destroys the whole royal seed, but the infant Joash is hidden in the temple for six years by Jehosheba and crowned by the priest Jehoiada, and Athaliah is killed at the horses' entrance (11:1-20). Joash repairs the temple, then buys off Hazael with its treasures and is murdered by his servants (ch. 12). The north declines under Jehoahaz and Jehoash, and Elisha dies; a corpse thrown into his tomb touches his bones and revives (13:20-21). Jeroboam II restores the northern borders (14:23-29). Then the [[assyrians|Assyrians]] come: Menahem pays Pul a thousand talents (15:19-20), Tiglath-Pileser takes Gilead and Galilee and deports the population (15:29), Ahaz of Judah strips the temple to buy Assyrian help against the Syro-Ephraimite coalition and copies an altar he saw in Damascus (16:1-18) — and, like [[manasseh|Manasseh]] after him, burns his own son as an offering (16:3; 21:6). Hoshea's revolt brings Shalmaneser; Samaria falls after a three-year siege and Israel is deported to Halah, the Habor, and the cities of the Medes (17:1-6). The chapter that follows is the narrator's indictment: Israel went after worthless things, made calves and an Asherah, passed sons and daughters through fire, and Judah did the same (17:7-23); the resettled peoples are taught to fear YHWH while serving their own gods (17:24-41).

### The last of Judah (2 Kgs 18–25)

[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] removes the high places and breaks up the bronze serpent (18:1-6). [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] takes [[lachish|Lachish]] and the fortified towns; the Rabshakeh taunts Jerusalem in Judahite in the hearing of the wall (18:17-35); [[isaiah-prophet|Isaiah]] promises deliverance, and the angel of YHWH strikes 185,000 in the Assyrian camp, after which Sennacherib is killed by his sons at [[nineveh|Nineveh]] (19:35-37). Hezekiah recovers from illness with the shadow retreating ten steps and shows the whole treasury to Babylonian envoys — for which Isaiah announces that everything will be carried to [[babylon|Babylon]] (ch. 20). Manasseh reigns fifty-five years, rebuilds the high places, sets an Asherah in the temple, burns his son, practices divination, and fills Jerusalem with innocent blood; the judgment is declared irrevocable (21:1-16).

[[josiah|Josiah]]'s temple repairs uncover a book of the law; Huldah's oracle confirms the coming disaster but promises Josiah will be gathered to his grave in peace (22:1-20). The reform is violent and thorough: vessels for Baal burned in the Kidron, the Asherah ground to dust and scattered on graves, the houses of the male cult prostitutes torn down, Topheth in the valley of Ben-hinnom defiled so no one could burn a son or daughter, the Bethel altar broken and bones from nearby tombs burned on it, and the priests of the northern high places slaughtered on their altars (23:1-20), followed by a Passover unlike any since the judges. Josiah dies at [[megiddo|Megiddo]] when Pharaoh Neco confronts him (23:29-30).

Judah collapses in a generation. Neco deposes Jehoahaz; Jehoiakim shifts allegiance to [[nebuchadnezzar|Nebuchadnezzar]] and rebels; [[babylonians|Babylonian]] and allied raiders harass the land (23:31–24:7). Jehoiachin surrenders after three months and is deported with the officials, the craftsmen and smiths, and the temple's gold vessels (24:8-16). [[zedekiah|Zedekiah]], installed as vassal, rebels; Jerusalem is besieged eighteen months until the famine breaks the walls, the army flees by night, and Zedekiah is captured near Jericho. His sons are slaughtered before his eyes, then his eyes are put out and he is bound in bronze fetters and taken to Babylon (25:1-7). Nebuzaradan burns the temple, the palace, and every great house, breaks down the walls, cuts up the bronze pillars and the sea for their metal, and Nebuchadnezzar has the chief priests and sixty men of the land executed at Riblah (25:8-21). Gedaliah is appointed governor at Mizpah and assassinated by Ishmael of the royal line, and the remnant flees to Egypt (25:22-26). The book closes in the thirty-seventh year of exile: Evil-merodach releases Jehoiachin from prison, gives him a seat above the other kings, and a regular allowance for the rest of his life (25:27-30).

The parallel account in [[1-2-chronicles|2 Chronicles]] covers the same reigns with different emphases — omitting the northern kingdom's regnal series and the Elijah–Elisha cycle almost entirely, adding Manasseh's Babylonian captivity and repentance (2 Chr 33:10-13), and ending with Cyrus's decree rather than Jehoiachin's ration. Both are recorded here without harmonization.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

Hebrew Masoretic text; verse-number differences between MT and NIV are noted by Provan (e.g. 1 Kgs 4:21–34 = MT 5:1–14). Large shared blocks: 2 Kgs 18:17–20:19 // Isa 36–39 (partial); 2 Kgs 24:18–25:30 // Jer 52. Ancient ascription to Jeremiah (*b. B. Bat.* 15a). Critical models posit composite growth and Deuteronomistic editing; Provan’s working assumption is that the final “authors” crafted a cumulative story that “works” without requiring reconstruction to cohere.

## Canon and Reception

Canonical for rabbinic Judaism and the major Christian communions (`canon_scope` above). Reception history includes: Jeremiah ascription; DH hypothesis (Noth and successors); chronicler’s parallel retelling ([[1-2-chronicles|1–2 Chronicles]]); heavy NT uptake of Elijah/Elisha, Solomon, and Davidic hope.

## Scholarship and Historiography

**Positions (not adjudicated):**

1. **Composite / multi-editor DtrH** (Noth et al.): Kings as edited corpus with source and redaction layers; tensions as conflicting voices.
2. **Final-form narrative unity** (Provan and related literary readers): apparent incoherence often reflects artistry, irony, or theological complexity; trouble may lie with readers’ models.
3. **Historiographical debate**: “biblical Israel” vs “historical Israel” separation (e.g. Davies-style minimalism) vs affirmations of referential intent with selective, theologically shaped portraiture (Provan: the portrait is authoritative for the church, but referentiality cannot be dodged).
4. **Dating of final form**: not before mid-6th century (Jehoiachin release); some push later.

## Key interpretive cruxes

- **Conditional vs unconditional Davidic promise** (1 Kgs 2:4; 8:25; 9:4–5 vs 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kgs 8:19; ending 25:27–30)
- **Wisdom inseparable from covenant fidelity** (Solomon arc)
- **Sin of Jeroboam** as northern identity-marker vs “sincere Yahwist calves” readings
- **Elijah/Elisha** as salvation-within-judgment vs pure judgment prophets
- **2 Kgs 17** catalog and Judah’s implication; irony of resettlement worship
- **Manasseh’s irrevocable judgment** vs Josiah’s reform
- **Jehoiachin epilogue**: cynicism vs hope
- **DH label**: useful for Deut–Kings links; Provan warns against abstract “Deuteronomistic theology” flattening final-form subtlety
