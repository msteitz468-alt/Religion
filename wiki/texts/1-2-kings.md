1 and 2 Kings complete the Deuteronomistic History (Deuteronomy → Joshua → Judges → Samuel → Kings) and narrate Israel's monarchy from Solomon's accession through the fall of both kingdoms — Israel to Assyria (722/721 BCE) and Judah to Babylon (586 BCE). The book ends with a flicker of hope: Jehoiachin eating at the table of the king of Babylon (2 Kgs 25:27-30), suggesting that the Davidic promise has not been extinguished by accumulated sin.

Jewish tradition ascribed the book to Jeremiah. Modern scholarship has identified it as the culmination of the Deuteronomistic History — a coherent theological narrative shaped by Deuteronomic vocabulary and criteria. Provan (NIBC, 1995) defends reading it as a unified literary whole rather than dissecting it into hypothetical sources and redactional layers. For Provan, Kings is one book (not two), a single narrative with historiographical intent that is also didactic — it tells a story about the past in order to teach its readers about God and the ways of God. It constantly presupposes the prior story (Pentateuch, Joshua–Judges, Samuel) and invites reflection in light of the prophetic writings and the full Christian canon. The series aim is to make the ancient text's power and meaning transparent without precritical paraphrase, anticritical defense, or a "desert of criticism" that brackets belief.

## The Story

### Solomon's Reign (1 Kgs 1-11)

The narrative organizes around two ideas David gives Solomon: keep the **law of Moses** and act with **wisdom** (2:1-4). For much of his reign, Solomon's wisdom functions positively — the request for a discerning heart (3:9), the building of the temple (chs. 5-8), international renown (chs. 9-10). But the wisdom at both ends of his reign operates selfishly: eliminating rivals (ch. 2) and eventually leading him into idolatry through his foreign wives (ch. 11). Solomon's apostasy brings the announcement that the kingdom will be divided — but not in his day; for David's sake the punishment is deferred (11:11-13).

**Solomon's prayer at the temple dedication (1 Kgs 8)** is the theological heart of the book — Provan calls it the most important single speech in Kings. Solomon acknowledges that YHWH cannot be confined to any house (8:27), anticipates future sin and exile, and grounds his appeal in the Abrahamic and Davidic covenants. The prayer looks forward to restoration after repentance, even from exile (8:46-53).

### The Divided Kingdom (1 Kgs 12 – 2 Kgs 17)

When Rehoboam refuses to reduce Solomon's forced labor (ch. 12), the northern tribes secede under Jeroboam ben Nebat. Jeroboam immediately establishes golden calves at Bethel and Dan ("Here are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt," 12:28 — a deliberate echo of the Exodus apostasy) and appoints non-Levitical priests. **"The sin of Jeroboam"** becomes the refrain condemning every subsequent northern king. No northern king ever turns away from this fundamental apostasy; every king does "evil in the eyes of YHWH."

Judah's story is more varied. Some kings are condemned (Rehoboam, Jehoram, Ahaziah); others receive qualified commendation (Asa, Jehoshaphat, Amaziah, Uzziah); and occasional relative goodness appears between long stretches of decline.

**The Elijah and Elisha narratives** (1 Kgs 17 – 2 Kgs 13) are the longest narrative section in Kings. These prophets function as agents of salvation within Israel's judgment:
- Elijah on Carmel (1 Kgs 18): the great confrontation with the prophets of Baal; fire from heaven; the undeniable demonstration that YHWH alone is God
- Elijah at Horeb (1 Kgs 19): exhausted prophet; the still small voice; recommissioning
- Naboth's vineyard (1 Kgs 21): Ahab's judicial murder; Elijah's denunciation; divine retribution
- Elisha's miracles (2 Kgs 2-9): widow's oil; Shunammite's son; Naaman's cleansing; floating axhead — a concentrated display of YHWH's power operating through his prophet

The final judgment on northern Israel (2 Kgs 17:7-23) is the book's most extended theological interpretation: a systematic indictment explaining *why* Israel fell to Assyria — serving other gods, following the customs of the nations, rejecting the covenant, hardening their hearts against all the prophets' warnings.

### Final Years of Judah (2 Kgs 18-25)

**Hezekiah** (2 Kgs 18-20) is one of the two great kings: he "did what was right in the eyes of YHWH, just as his ancestor David had done" (18:3); he removed the high places (unique among Judean kings); he trusted YHWH against Sennacherib's overwhelming forces. The miraculous deliverance of Jerusalem from Sennacherib (19:35-37) is presented as the culminating proof that God can be trusted.

**Manasseh** (2 Kgs 21) is the counterpoint: the worst king ever, who undid Hezekiah's reforms and whose sin is so extreme that God announces Judah's judgment — a judgment that will fall "even though Josiah comes" (21:10-15). Sin has accumulated beyond repair.

**Josiah** (2 Kgs 22-23) is the other great king: finding the book of the law (Deuteronomy), implementing sweeping reforms, celebrating Passover as never before since the judges. Yet his reforms cannot avert the announced judgment. He dies at Megiddo trying to stop Pharaoh Necho (23:29) — a tragic end that raises profound questions about divine providence.

The final chapters (24-25) record three deportations. Jerusalem falls; the temple is destroyed; the Davidic line is broken. The book ends in near-total darkness — except that Jehoiachin is released from prison and eats at the king's table "for the rest of his life" (25:29-30). This glimmer of Davidic survival is the book's last word.

## Theological Themes

### 1. YHWH is God Alone

The incomparability of YHWH — over against Baal, Asherah, Molech, Chemosh, Marduk — is the book's foundational premise. When Elijah defeats the 450 prophets of Baal on Carmel, the people cry "The LORD, he is God! The LORD, he is God!" (18:39). The book constantly exposes the futility and danger of trusting anything else: national alliances, military strength, foreign gods. History is not a contest between national deities; it is YHWH directing all things according to his purposes.

### 2. Exclusive Worship as the Criterion of Evaluation

Every king is judged by one standard: faithfulness to YHWH as revealed through the Mosaic law, especially Deuteronomy. The proper place of worship (Jerusalem), the proper form (no idols, no high places), and the proper object (YHWH alone) are the criteria. True wisdom cannot be separated from obedience to this covenantal standard (Solomon's failure demonstrates this precisely).

### 3. YHWH as Righteous Judge

Sin has consequences — but not mechanically. YHWH is compassionate, slow to bring final judgment, repeatedly delaying on account of his promises and his mercy (2 Kgs 13:23; 14:27). Kings shows that individuals and nations can "accumulate" sin to the breaking point; but it also shows that sincere humility can delay or mitigate judgment (1 Kgs 21:27-29; 2 Kgs 22:15-20).

### 4. The Promise: Conditional and Unconditional Tension

The Davidic promise (2 Sam 7) runs through Kings in paradoxical tension. David's descendants survive catastrophes that destroy other dynasties — because YHWH has committed himself to David. Yet accumulated disobedience ultimately brings Judah to exile as thoroughly as Israel. The tension is never resolved within the book; Jehoiachin's survival at the end keeps open the possibility that the unconditional promise will triumph. As Provan notes: "Has David told Solomon everything he knows?"

## The Deuteronomistic Framework

Kings is the culmination of what scholars call the **Deuteronomistic History** — the narrative sequence of Deuteronomy–Joshua–Judges–Samuel–Kings. The same theological grammar runs through all five books:

Provan's contribution emphasizes that the book functions as narrative literature first: it is story with a past referent, yet shaped to instruct. The Deuteronomic criteria (exclusive worship at Jerusalem, obedience to Torah, prophetic warning before judgment) are not imposed externally but emerge from the story's own logic. The Elijah and Elisha cycles (the longest continuous narrative block) function as "agents of salvation within judgment," demonstrating YHWH's power and mercy even as the northern kingdom spirals toward the explicit theological indictment of 2 Kgs 17.

## Provan's Hermeneutical Stance and Canonical Excursuses

Provan treats the book as a literary and theological unity best read in its final form within the larger OT and Christian canon. He includes dedicated excursuses on Solomon, Elijah, and Elisha "in Canonical Context," tracing how these figures are taken up in later Scripture (e.g., Elijah traditions in Malachi and the Synoptics; Elisha's miracles as backdrop for Jesus' compassion and power in Luke 4). Wisdom and apostasy in Solomon's reign are read as a single arc: the gift of wisdom (1 Kgs 3) is real, yet true wisdom is inseparable from covenant fidelity (the foreign wives and high places in ch. 11 bring the division announcement). The Davidic promise ("Has David told Solomon everything he knows?") remains in unresolved tension with accumulated sin until the book's final flicker of hope with Jehoiachin.
- YHWH's covenant with Israel was mediated through Moses
- Obedience to Torah = divine blessing and security in the land
- Disobedience = covenant curses, culminating in exile (Deut 28)
- Prophets are sent to warn before judgment falls (2 Kgs 17:13)

Kings is the story of Israel exhausting its chances and the covenant curses coming to their full extent.

## Canonical and NT Connections

Provan reads Kings typologically within the full biblical canon:
- **Elijah/John the Baptist**: Malachi 3:1; 4:5-6 identify a returning Elijah figure; Mark 1:2; Matt 11:14 identify John the Baptist with this figure
- **Elijah/Elisha miracles → Jesus's miracles**: Luke 4:25-27 explicitly connects Elijah's widow of Zarephath and Elisha's cleansing of Naaman to Jesus's ministry
- **Solomon's temple prayer → Hebrews, Revelation**: The prayer's vision of universal access to YHWH through repentance and prayer informs NT soteriology
- **The "greater David"**: The unfulfilled Davidic promise provides the messianic framework that the NT claims Jesus fulfills
