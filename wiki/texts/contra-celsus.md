# Contra Celsus (*Kata Kelsou* — Against Celsus)

[[origen|Origen of Alexandria]]'s *Contra Celsus* (c. 248 CE) — written near the end of his life at Caesarea — is the most substantial and intellectually sophisticated Christian apologetic of antiquity. It responds point-by-point to **Celsus**'s *True Doctrine* (*Alethes Logos*, c. 178 CE), the most serious pagan philosophical critique of Christianity to survive, which Origen quotes extensively (making Celsus's work partially recoverable only through Origen). The work covers the full range of objections to Christianity: historical, philosophical, ethical, and political.

## Book I — Jewish Origins, Jesus's Birth, Miracles

### Preface and Apologetic Frame (Chapters 1–13, lines 11120–11668)

Origen opens with a deliberate hesitation: Jesus himself remained silent before false witnesses, and "His whole life and conduct among the Jews were a better refutation than any answer" (lines 11125–11126). He questions whether writing an apology may actually "weaken that defence of Christianity which rests on facts" (lines 11171–11173). He proceeds nonetheless, motivated by concern for weak believers who might be shaken — not strong ones, whose faith no argument can unsettle.

Origen establishes at the outset that Celsus's work, titled *True Discourse*, is unworthy of the name. Celsus is identified as probably an Epicurean; Origen notes there were "two individuals of the name of Celsus, both of whom were Epicureans; the earlier of the two having lived in the time of Nero, but this one in that of Adrian, and later" (lines 11474–11476). Yet Celsus disguises his Epicureanism in this work, pretending to affirm providence — "because he thought that he could assail Christianity with better effect by not professing the opinions of Epicurus" (lines 11459–11461).

**Celsus's charge against Christian irrationalism (Chapters 9–13)**. Celsus compares Christian preachers to *Metragyrtae*, "soothsayers, and Mithrae, and Sabbadians" who exploit credulous audiences, and says Christians repeat: "Do not examine, but believe! and, Your faith will save you!" and "The wisdom of this life is bad, but that foolishness is a good thing!" (lines 11489–11491). Origen's response is threefold. First, all philosophical schools require initial commitment before one learns their reasons — no one auditions every philosophical school before choosing Stoicism or Platonism. Second, for the multitude who cannot devote themselves to leisured philosophical inquiry, simple faith produces moral transformation, which itself constitutes evidence of divine assistance: "let us inquire... whether it were better for them to believe without a reason, and (so) to have become reformed and improved in their habits, through the belief that men are chastised for sins, and honoured for good works or not" (lines 11506–11510). Third, Paul's words about "the wisdom of this world" are carefully qualified — "not wisdom universally, but the wisdom of *this world*" (lines 11638–11643); Origen distinguishes false worldly philosophy from genuine wisdom.

### The Jewish Interlocutor and the Panthera Story (Chapters 28–39, lines 12124–12530)

**Celsus's literary device**. Origen identifies a crucial feature of Celsus's strategy: "in imitation of a rhetorician training a pupil, he introduces a Jew, who enters into a personal discussion with Jesus, and speaks in a very childish manner, altogether unworthy of the grey hairs of a philosopher" (lines 12124–12127). Origen will demonstrate that Celsus's Jewish persona is internally inconsistent — a Jew who accepts OT prophecy and visions cannot coherently raise many of the objections Celsus puts in his mouth.

**The Panthera / illegitimate birth accusation**. Speaking through his Jewish character, Celsus launches the most detailed surviving pagan attack on the circumstances of Jesus's birth. The Jew "accuses Him of having invented his birth from a virgin, and upbraids Him with being born in a certain Jewish village, of a poor woman of the country, who gained her subsistence by spinning, and who was turned out of doors by her husband, a carpenter by trade, because she was convicted of adultery; that after being driven away by her husband, and wandering about for a time, she disgracefully gave birth to Jesus, an illegitimate child, who having hired himself out as a servant in Egypt on account of his poverty, and having there acquired some miraculous powers, on which the Egyptians greatly pride themselves, returned to his own country, highly elated on account of them, and by means of these proclaimed himself a God" (lines 12132–12142). Origen returns to this at Chapter 32, naming the soldier: "she bore a child to a certain soldier named Panthera" (lines 12279–12280). Origen's interpretive counter is pointed — the invention inadvertently concedes what it meant to deny: "their preserving the fact that it was not by Joseph that the Virgin conceived Jesus, rendered the falsehood very palpable" (lines 12290–12292). By inventing Panthera to explain the fatherless birth, Celsus's Jew implicitly acknowledges that Joseph was not the biological father — exactly what the Christian account claims.

**Origen's defense of the virgin birth: Isaiah and the magi**. Origen argues Celsus deliberately omitted the Isaiah prophecy, even though "he makes numerous quotations from the Gospel according to Matthew, as of the star that appeared at the birth of Christ" (lines 12356–12358). The omitted prophecy is the sign to Ahaz: "Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call His name Immanuel, which is, being interpreted, God with us" (lines 12352–12354). Origen defends the LXX rendering (*parthenos*) against the Jewish alternative (*neanis*, "young woman"): the Hebrew word *olmah* is used in Deuteronomy precisely in contexts implying virginity (lines 12362–12371). He presses the logic of the sign: "What kind of sign, then, would that have been — a young woman who was not a virgin giving birth to a child?" (lines 12381–12382).

On the magi and star, Origen treats these as historical fulfillment. He also deploys a philosophical argument: if souls are sent into bodies "according to [their] deserts and former actions" (invoking "the opinion of Pythagoras, and Plato, and Empedocles, whom Celsus frequently names," lines 12307–12308), then "this soul also, which conferred more benefit by its residence in the flesh than that of many men... stood in need of a body not only superior to others, but invested with all excellent qualities" (lines 12310–12313). The adulterous birth Celsus posits would have been an absurdly inappropriate vehicle. Origen also cites the Greek tradition that Plato was born of Amphictione and Apollo (lines 12473–12481) — Greeks themselves tell stories of exceptional men with divine paternity.

### The Sorcery / Magic Charge and Origen's Response (Chapters 6 and 38–39, lines 11393–11421, 12489–12517)

**Celsus's charge**. Celsus makes two related accusations: that Christians operate "by the names of certain demons, and by the use of incantations" (lines 11393–11396), and that Jesus himself "having been brought up as an illegitimate child, and having served for hire in Egypt, and then coming to the knowledge of certain miraculous powers, returned from thence to his own country, and by means of those powers proclaimed himself a god" (lines 12499–12501). Celsus constructs a dilemma: Jesus excluded from the kingdom those who did miracles in his name, yet did the same things himself — so "He is a wicked man; but if He is not guilty of wickedness in doing such things, neither are they who do the same as He" (lines 11415–11417).

**Origen's response: miracle vs. magic**. Origen insists that "it is not by incantations that Christians seem to prevail (over evil spirits), but by the name of Jesus, accompanied by the announcement of the narratives which relate to Him" (lines 11398–11401). The decisive criterion is moral-theological: "I do not understand how a magician should exert himself to teach a doctrine which persuades us always to act as if God were to judge every man for his deeds" (lines 12502–12504). No sorcerer would build a movement whose disciples preached moral accountability and died for it. "If they indeed wrought miracles, then how can it be believed that magicians exposed themselves to such hazards to introduce a doctrine which forbade the practice of magic?" (lines 12514–12516). Christians use "not... spells or incantations, but the simple name of Jesus, and certain other words in which they repose faith, according to the holy Scriptures" (lines 11420–11422).

### Christianity and the Uneducated (Chapters 9 and 27, lines 11478–11530, 12100–12122)

**Celsus's social charge**. Celsus argues Christianity targets the unlearned. He "thinks that this philanthropic doctrine, which reaches to every soul under the sun, is vulgar, and on account of its vulgarity and its want of reasoning power, obtained a hold only over the ignorant" (lines 12115–12117). Yet Origen notes Celsus "acknowledges that there were among them some persons of moderate intelligence, and gentle disposition, and possessed of understanding, and capable of comprehending allegories" (lines 12119–12122) — a concession that undercuts his own charge.

**Origen's response: the universal physician**. Origen reframes the social breadth of Christianity not as a defect but as evidence of divine origin: even a doctor who cures many bodies does so "not without divine permission, since no good happens to men without the help of God" (lines 11521–11522). How much more must divine assistance be inferred when "He has healed the souls of many, and has turned them (to virtue), and improved their nature, and attached them to God who is over all things" (lines 11525–11527)? The moral transformation of former sinners — "certain among them, from a desire of exceeding chastity, and a wish to worship God with greater purity, abstain even from the permitted indulgences of (lawful) love" (lines 12096–12098) — is itself a greater miracle than physical healing.

### Key Philosophical Vocabulary and Apologetic Strategy

**"Manifestation of the Spirit and of power"**. Origen acknowledges that the Gospel's form of proof differs from Greek dialectics: "the Gospel has a demonstration of its own, more divine than any established by Grecian dialectics. And this diviner method is called by the apostle the *manifestation of the Spirit and of power*: of the Spirit, on account of the prophecies... and of power, because of the signs and wonders" (lines 11320–11327). This *pneumatic* mode of demonstration is available to those prepared by faith; it is not irrational, but supra-dialectical.

**Philosophy of names (Chapter 25)**. Origen develops a sophisticated argument against Celsus's claim that Zeus and Sabaoth are interchangeable names for the same deity. Drawing on debates between Aristotle (names by convention), the Stoics (names by nature), and Epicurus, Origen argues that divine names — "Sabaoth, and Adonai, and the other names treated with so much reverence among the Hebrews" — "belong to a secret theology which refers to the Framer of all things" and retain power that translations lose (lines 11977–11981). This is why Christians "struggle even to death to avoid calling God by the name of Zeus" (lines 12032–12033). The argument draws on a widespread ancient view that incantations lose efficacy when translated.

**Josephus as external witness**. Origen cites Josephus's *Antiquities* (Book 18) as independent evidence for John the Baptist: "Josephus bears witness to John as having been a Baptist, and as promising purification to those who underwent the rite" (lines 12751–12752). He further notes that Josephus — "although not believing in Jesus as the Christ" — attributes the fall of Jerusalem to the death of James the Just, "a brother of Jesus (called Christ)" (lines 12760–12761), which Origen reads as an inadvertent near-admission of Jesus's significance.

**The Celsus-ventriloquized Jew as a structural problem**. Origen's most consistent apologetic move is to turn Celsus's choice of persona against him: a Jew who accepts the prophets must accept OT visions (Ezekiel's opened heavens, Isaiah's throne-room vision) on grounds epistemically no stronger than those offered for the baptismal dove. "How can we tell whether [Isaiah] really saw [the seraphim] or not? Now, O Jew, you have believed these visions to be true, and to have been not only shown to the prophet by a diviner Spirit" (lines 12641–12644). The Jewish interlocutor is self-undermining as a critic of Christian miracle-claims — he already accepts miracles of the same structural type.

## Book II — Resurrection and the Apostles

Book II is formally addressed to Jewish converts to Christianity who have been challenged by Celsus's fictional Jewish spokesman. Much of the book uses a rhetorical tu quoque: every objection levelled at Jesus can be retorted against Moses. The major theological confrontations are over resurrection, foreknowledge, the Judas and Peter episodes, and the nature of Christ's post-resurrection body.

### Jewish Converts and the Law

[[origen|Origen]] opens by contesting Celsus's claim that Jewish-Christian converts had abandoned the law of their fathers. He argues that many converts — the Ebionites chief among them — continued to observe Mosaic law; "Ebion signifies poor among the Jews, and those Jews who have received Jesus as Christ are called by the name of Ebionites" [lines 13736–13737]. He notes that Peter himself still observed Jewish dietary customs until the vision in Acts 10 directed him otherwise: the Spirit instructed Peter in "the true and spiritual meaning of meats, by saying, What God has cleansed, call not common" [lines 13830–13831]. Origen also cites Paul's becoming "as a Jew to the Jews" (1 Cor 9) and his Temple offering in Acts as evidence that continuity with the law was not abandoned but spiritualized.

### Foreknowledge, Judas, and the Peter Denial

Celsus's most pointed charge in the early chapters of Book II is that Jesus foreknew his betrayal and denial — and that this foreknowledge incriminates him rather than vindicating him, since a divine being should have prevented such outcomes. Origen's response pivots on the logic of prophecy: "Celsus imagines that an event, predicted through foreknowledge, comes to pass because it was predicted; but we do not grant this, maintaining that he who foretold it was not the cause of its happening, because he foretold it would happen; but the future event itself, which would have taken place though not predicted, afforded the occasion to him, who was endowed with foreknowledge, of foretelling its occurrence" [lines 14522–14527]. Origen illustrates this by applying the classical "idle argument" (*argos logos*) — a logician's sophism — to show that foreknowledge does not compel the foreseen event.

On Judas, Origen refuses to accept the Celsus caricature of a total apostate. He notes Judas's kiss of identification retained "some element of respect for his Master" [line 14165], and that his subsequent repentance and return of the thirty pieces of silver demonstrate that "the instructions of Jesus had been able to produce some feeling of repentance in his mind" [lines 14181–14182]. Origen draws on Psalm 108 (LXX) as prophetic foretelling of Judas's role, and uses the philosophical parallel of Aristotle departing from Plato to argue that a teacher's greatness is not disproved by a disciple's betrayal.

On Peter's denial, Origen turns the charge into evidence for the Gospels' truthfulness: "If they had not been lovers of truth, but, as Celsus supposes, inventors of fictions, they would not have represented Peter as denying, nor His disciples as being offended. For although these events actually happened, who could have proved that they turned out in that manner?" [lines 14363–14366]. Peter's later course — weeping bitterly, then facing martyrdom — is the counter-evidence Celsus ignores: "Peter, after his denial, perceiving to what a depth of wickedness he had fallen, went out and wept bitterly; while the others, although stricken with dismay on account of what had happened to Jesus (for they still continued to admire Him), had, by His glorious appearance, their belief more firmly established than before that He was the Son of God" [lines 15060–15065].

### The Resurrection Appearances: Celsus's Attack

The central attack in Book II is on the resurrection itself. Celsus's Jew formulates the objection with explicit contempt: "That while alive he was of no assistance to himself, but that when dead he rose again, and showed the marks of his punishment, and how his hands were pierced with nails: who beheld this? A half-frantic woman, as you state, and some other one, perhaps, of those who were engaged in the same system of delusion, who had either dreamed so, owing to a peculiar state of mind, or under the influence of a wandering imagination had formed to himself an appearance according to his own wishes" [lines 15570–15575]. Celsus thus dismisses the witnesses as hysterical, deluded, or conscious fraudsters.

[[origen|Origen]] responds on multiple fronts. First, he corrects Celsus's factual claim that only one woman saw the risen Jesus, citing Matthew's account of two Marys at the tomb [lines 16061–16067]. Second, he appeals to [[paul-apostle|Paul]]'s witness-list in 1 Corinthians 15, quoting it in full: "He was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve: after that He was seen of above five hundred brethren at once, of whom the greater part remain unto the present time... And last of all He was seen of me also, as of one born out of due time" [lines 15804–15808].

Third, Origen tackles the Epicurean hallucination explanation: Celsus "adopts this opinion as an Epicurean" that the disciples experienced visions produced by "a perverted imagination" [lines 15710–15713]. Origen concedes the theoretical possibility of post-mortem apparitions but turns Plato against Celsus: "even Plato says in his treatise on the Soul that shadowy phantoms of persons already dead have appeared to some around their sepulchres" [lines 15720–15722]. If the Platonist tradition accepts soul-phantoms, Celsus cannot dismiss the disciples' testimony as simply impossible.

The decisive proof Origen offers is historical, not philosophical: the transformation of the disciples from fearful deniers into willing martyrs. "A clear and unmistakeable proof of the fact I hold to be the undertaking of His disciples, who devoted themselves to the teaching of a doctrine which was attended with danger to human life — a doctrine which they would not have taught with such courage had they invented the resurrection of Jesus from the dead; and who also, at the same time, not only prepared others to despise death, but were themselves the first to manifest their disregard for its terrors" [lines 15637–15643].

### The Nature of the Resurrection Body

Origen's most technically precise statement in this book concerns the resurrection body. Responding to the Thomas episode (Jn 20:27), he presents a graduated ontology: "after His resurrection, He existed in a body intermediate, as it were, between the grossness of that which He had before His sufferings, and the appearance of a soul uncovered by such a body" [lines 15768–15771]. This intermediate body explains both why [[jesus-christ|Jesus]] could appear through closed doors and why Thomas could touch the wounds — real but transformed.

Origen extends this into a theory of graduated visibility. The post-resurrection appearances followed the same logic as the Transfiguration: only those capable of receiving Christ's glory could see him fully. "He did not show Himself to all after His resurrection from the dead" [line 15864] because such an appearance would have blinded those incapable of receiving it. On immortality, Origen offers a careful distinction: "it is not the dead man who is immortal, but He who rose from the dead... Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more: death has no more dominion over Him" [lines 14425–14431] — mortal before the resurrection, immortal after.

### Consistency of OT and NT

Celsus's Jew implies inconsistency between Hebrew prophecy and the figure of Jesus: the prophets foretold a mighty king, not a suffering criminal, and Jesus's "Woe unto you" pronouncements display an inability to persuade rather than divine authority. Origen's counter-strategy is systematic: "Celsus could not discover any objections to be urged against the Gospel which might not be retorted on him as liable to be brought also against the law and the prophets" [lines 16207–16209]. The "Woe unto you" is matched by Isaiah's "Woe unto them that join house to house," and Ezekiel's revilings of Israel; if the Jew defends those passages, he cannot condemn the parallel in Jesus.

Origen also turns the resurrection argument back on the Jewish interlocutor: for a Jew to deny bodily resurrection is self-refuting, since "a Jew would not have uttered these words, who believed what is recorded in the third and fourth books of Kings regarding little children, of whom the one was raised up by Elijah, and the other by Elisha" [lines 15648–15651]. Celsus's ventriloquized Jew has become inconsistent with Jewish scripture itself.

### Philosophical Arguments

Origen deploys Platonic categories at several points. Against the Epicurean hallucination theory, he invokes the Platonic doctrine of soul-phantoms to show that post-mortem appearance is philosophically defensible even on the opponents' terms. He compares Jesus's willingness to face death — despite foreknowing it — to Socrates: "Socrates knew that he would die after drinking the hemlock, and it was in his power, if he had allowed himself to be persuaded by Crito, by escaping from prison, to avoid these calamities; but nevertheless he decided, as it appeared to him consistent with right reason, that it was better for him to die as became a philosopher" [lines 14441–14444]. On the Logos doctrine, Origen clarifies that Jesus's body and soul were not themselves God; rather, "it was the Logos God, and Son of the God of all things, who spoke in Jesus these words, I am the way, and the truth, and the life" [lines 14052–14053], while the Logos simultaneously transcended the individual body of Jesus — present "where two or three are gathered together in My name" and "even to the end of the world" [lines 14086–14087].

## Book III — Nature of Faith and Christianity's Social Character

Book III opens with [[origen|Origen]] addressing Celsus's framing of the Jewish-Christian dispute over messianic prophecy as a trivial squabble — "a fight about the shadow of an ass" (line 16411). Origen insists that the debate over whether Jesus is the predicted Messiah is anything but trivial: it concerns prophecies of birthplace, virginal conception, miracles, worldwide apostolic reach, death, and resurrection foretold long before his advent (lines 16426–16436). The book then moves through Celsus's charges about the origins of Judaism (his claim that the Hebrews were Egyptian rebels) and Christianity (allegedly another rebellion), which Origen rebuts on linguistic and historical grounds, noting that Hebrew names and language disprove Egyptian ancestry (lines 16562–16600).

**Celsus's Accusation: Christianity Targets the Uneducated**

The most sustained polemic of Book III concerns Celsus's charge that Christians deliberately recruit the ignorant and repel the wise. Celsus quotes what he presents as the Christian teacher's own invitation: "Let no one come to us who has been instructed, or who is wise or prudent (for such qualifications are deemed evil by us); but if there be any ignorant, or unintelligent, or uninstructed, or foolish persons, let them come with confidence" (lines 17649–17652). Celsus concludes that Christians "desire and are able to gain over only the silly, and the mean, and the stupid, with women and children" (lines 17654–17655).

Origen flatly denies that this represents Christian teaching and argues from the Jewish scriptures themselves that wisdom has always been prized: "To such a degree does the Gospel desire that there should be wise men among believers, that for the sake of exercising the understanding of its hearers, it has spoken certain truths in enigmas, others in what are called dark sayings, others in parables, and others in problems" (lines 17703–17707). He marshals Solomon's legendary wisdom, the prophet Hosea's closing query ("Who is wise, and he will understand these things?"), and Daniel's superiority over Babylonian sages as scriptural evidence that the tradition positively prizes the intellect (lines 17675–17715).

Paul's ranking of spiritual gifts is invoked as further proof: "Paul also, in the catalogue of charismata bestowed by God, placed first the word of wisdom, and second, as being inferior to it, the word of knowledge, but third, and lower down, faith" (lines 17725–17727). Origen stresses that Paul's dismissal of "the wisdom of the world" targets not wisdom as such but the materialist philosophy that treats the visible cosmos as ultimate reality, while true wisdom is "those opinions which raise the soul from things here to the blessedness which is with God" (lines 17757–17762).

**Origen's Defense of Educated Christianity and Faith vs. Credulity**

Origen distinguishes sharply between genuine faith and mere credulity. When Celsus says that "faith, having taken possession of our minds, makes us yield the assent which we give to the doctrine of Jesus," Origen concedes the point but reframes it: "Observe, however, whether that faith does not of itself exhibit what is worthy of praise, seeing we entrust ourselves to the God who is over all, acknowledging our gratitude to Him who has led us to such a faith, and declaring that He could not have attempted or accomplished such a result without the divine assistance" (lines 17498–17504). Faith for Origen is not irrational credulity but a rational trust grounded in confidence in the Gospel writers' integrity: "souls unacquainted with those artifices which are taught by the cunning sophistry of the Greeks…would not have been able thus to invent occurrences which are fitted of themselves to conduct to faith, and to a life in keeping with faith" (lines 17510–17514).

He acknowledges that Christian teaching is calibrated to the hearer's capacity — simpler minds receive "milk," advanced believers receive "strong meat" — but insists that this is an esoteric-to-exoteric graduation, not a permanent ceiling: "we do everything in our power to secure that our meetings should be composed of wise men, and those things among us which are especially excellent and divine we then venture to bring forward publicly in our discussions when we have an abundance of intelligent hearers, while we conceal and pass by in silence the truths of deeper import when we see that our audience is composed of simpler minds" (lines 17902–17909). The graduated teaching of Jesus — parables for the crowd, private explanation for the disciples — is cited as the model: "For, privately, to His own disciples did Jesus open up all things, esteeming above the multitudes those who desired to know His wisdom" (lines 17721–17722).

To Celsus's charge that "no wise man believes the Gospel, being driven away by the multitudes who adhere to it," Origen replies that this logic would equally disqualify obedience to Solon or Lycurgus, since the many ignorant also live under those laws (lines 18473–18479). The presence of simple converts does not debase the doctrine, just as a physician sent to cure a city is not discredited because most of its sick inhabitants are ordinary people.

**Pagan Mystery Religions Compared to Christian Initiation**

Celsus compares the Christian community to Egyptian temple religion — splendid exterior, animal gods within — and contrasts the dignity of Eleusinian mystery proclamations ("Every one who has clean hands, and a prudent tongue"; "He who is pure from all pollution, and whose soul is conscious of no evil") with what he caricatures as the Christian invitation: open to thieves, housebreakers, poisoners, and robbers of the dead (lines 18083–18093).

Origen accepts the comparison of Christian initiation to mystery rites but inverts Celsus's evaluation. The Christian teacher, he argues, operates on a two-stage model that pagan mysteries collapse into one. First, sinners are invited to healing: "it is not the same thing to invite those who are sick in soul to be cured, and those who are in health to the knowledge and study of divine things" (lines 18095–18097). After moral transformation and purification — a process more rigorous than pagan vetting — initiates are admitted to deeper mysteries: "when those who have been turned towards virtue have made progress, and have shown that they have been purified by the word, and have led as far as they can a better life, then and not before do we invite them to participation in our mysteries. For we speak wisdom among them that are perfect" (lines 18104–18108).

He then formulates a Christian counter-proclamation rivaling Celsus's mystery invitations: "Whoever has clean hands, and therefore lifts up holy hands to God…let him come to us; and whoever has a wise tongue through meditating on the law of the Lord day and night…let him have no reluctance in coming to the strong and rational sustenance which is adapted to those who are athletes in piety and every virtue" (lines 18112–18120). The key apologetic move is that pagan mystery initiation admits only the already pure; Christianity is the process by which the impure are made pure and then admitted to wisdom. Celsus, Origen says, "does not know the difference between inviting the wicked to be healed, and initiating those already purified into the sacred mysteries" (lines 18133–18135).

**Internal Diversity of the Christian Community**

Celsus attacks Christian sectarian divisions as proof of Christianity's instability: at first believers "held the same opinions; but when they grew to be a great multitude, they were divided and separated, each wishing to have his own individual party" (lines 16649–16651). He also observes that heresies have multiplied to the point where Christians "confute one another, still having, so to speak, one name in common" (lines 16696–16698).

Origen's response deploys a philosophical analogy: heresies arise wherever a discipline touches on things of great importance. Medicine generates medical sects; philosophy generates philosophical schools; Judaism generated interpretive disputes over Moses and the prophets (lines 16702–16716). Christian heresies arose not from faction but from "the earnest desire of many literary men to become acquainted with the doctrines of Christianity," who "taking in different acceptations those discourses which were believed by all to be divine, there arose heresies" (lines 16719–16724). He cites Paul approvingly: "For there must be heresies among you, that they who are approved may be made manifest among you" (lines 16736–16737). The wisest Christian is one who has carefully studied the heresies and selected the best, just as the best physician is one who has examined competing medical systems (lines 16742–16743).

On the unity that does exist, Origen contrasts Christian assemblies with civic assemblies city by city: "the Church of God, e.g., which is at Athens, is a meek and stable body, as being one which desires to please God, who is over all things; whereas the assembly of the Athenians is given to sedition, and is not at all to be compared to the Church of God in that city" (lines 17212–17216). The same contrast applies at Corinth and Alexandria. Even the Church's inferior members, Origen claims, surpass many civic officials in moral quality (lines 17206–17210).

**Apologetic Strategy and Philosophical Framework**

Throughout Book III, [[origen|Origen]] employs a consistent comparative method: whatever Celsus credits to pagan heroes, oracles, or philosophers, Origen tests by the criterion of moral benefit to humanity. Aristeas of Proconnesus, Abaris the Hyperborean, Cleomedes of Astypalæa — all the pagan wonder-workers Celsus adduces — are judged deficient because their marvels served no clear providential purpose and produced no reformation of morals. By contrast, Jesus's miracles are validated by the Churches they produced and the moral transformation they effected: "Place yourself, then, as a neutral party, between what is related of Aristeas and what is recorded of Jesus, and see whether, from the result, and from the benefits which have accrued from the reformation of morals, and to the worship of the God who is over all things, it is not allowable to conclude that we must believe the events recorded of Jesus not to have happened without the divine intervention" (lines 17124–17130).

The treatment of human changeability is philosophically notable. Against Celsus's Stoic-tinged pessimism that nature is fixed and sinners cannot be reformed, Origen argues that wickedness is acquired through "education, and perverse example, and surrounding influences" rather than being innate (lines 18357–18359), and that the divine word can transform even deeply habituated sinners — pointing to Phaedo (rescued from a brothel) and Polemon (a profligate who became Xenocrates's successor) as pagan examples of radical moral conversion (lines 18307–18325). Book III closes with Origen comparing the Christian end — "communion with God in Christ — that is, the word, and the wisdom, and all virtue" — favorably against Epicurean, Platonic, and Pythagorean accounts of the soul's destiny, challenging Celsus to show which alternative promises anything superior (lines 18712–18722).

## Book IV — Old Testament, Providence, Creation

Book IV opens with [[origen|Origen]] invoking Jer 1:10 ("I have put My words in your mouth as fire… to root out and to pull down… and to build and to plant," lines 18743–18747) to frame the entire book as a counter-demolition and counter-construction against Celsus’s *True Doctrine*. The book is the longest sustained engagement with the Old Testament in *Contra Celsus*, covering prophecy, Genesis narrative, divine anthropomorphism, special human status, cosmology, and the problem of evil.

### 1. OT Anthropomorphisms — Celsus’s Attack and Origen’s Allegorical Defense

Celsus presses the critique that the OT represents God in crudely human terms: descending, changing, becoming angry, threatening sinners. At lines 19403–19406, Origen quotes Celsus directly: *“God is good, and beautiful, and blessed, and that in the best and most beautiful degree. But if he come down among men, he must undergo a change, and a change from good to evil, from virtue to vice, from happiness to misery, and from best to worst.”* Origen responds that "the holy Scriptures represent God as unchangeable, both by such words as *You are the same,* and *I change not*” (line 19152), and that the Stoic god, being corporeal, "at one time has his whole essence composed of the guiding principle when the conflagration of the world takes place; and at another… he again becomes partly material" — making Stoicism less capable of a truly immutable deity than Christianity (lines 19156–19162).

The descent of the Word is defended not as spatial movement but as pedagogical accommodation: "the term is used in a figurative sense. For God comes down from His own height and greatness when He arranges the affairs of men" (lines 19087–19089). This *synkatabasis* leaves God’s substance unaltered: "continuing unchangeable in His essence, He condescends to human affairs by the economy of His providence" (lines 19149–19150).

The deeper [[allegorical-reading|allegorical]] defense of OT anthropomorphisms comes in Chapters 71–72. Celsus ridicules scriptural passages about God’s anger and threats as projecting passion onto the divine. Origen responds by calling this the "anthropopathic" mode: "the word of God appears to have dealt with the history, making the capacity of the hearers, and the benefit which they were to receive, the standard of the appropriateness of its announcements" (lines 21127–21129). In Deuteronomy, Scripture itself acknowledges this accommodation: *“The Lord your God bare with your manners, as a man would bear with the manners of his son”* (lines 21132–21133). Then in Chapter 72: *“We speak, indeed, of the wrath of God. We do not, however, assert that it indicates any passion on His part, but that it is something which is assumed in order to discipline by stern means those sinners who have committed many and grievous sins”* (lines 21145–21148). Divine anger is a pedagogical instrument — Origen likens God’s threats to a physician’s warning: *“I will have to use the knife, and apply cauteries, if you do not obey my prescriptions”* (lines 21184–21186). Nor is “sleep” to be taken literally: "if sleep must mean something else, and not what the first acceptation of the word conveys, why should not wrath also be understood in a similar way?" (lines 21179–21181).

### 2. Allegorical Defense of Genesis Narratives

Celsus launches a sustained assault on Genesis, calling its stories *“most incredible and insipid… most impiously representing God as weak at the very beginning of things, and unable to convince even a single human being whom He Himself had formed”* (lines 19923–19925). Origen identifies the error as treating as literal what was written to convey "secret and mystical doctrine" (line 20173).

Key attacks and responses:

- **Man formed by God’s hands**: Celsus charges the text with picturing a bodily deity. Origen notes Genesis does not say "hands" at all — that phrase comes from Job and the Psalms — and that those "who do not understand these and similar expressions in the sacred Scriptures, imagine that we attribute to the God who is over all things a form such as that of man" (lines 19957–19960). The inbreathing of life is misread by Celsus as "inflating skins" — Origen says the expression means "God communicated to man of His incorruptible Spirit" (lines 19975–19977).

- **Woman from the rib / expulsion from paradise**: Origen concedes "the more modest among Jews and Christians are ashamed of these things, and endeavour to give them somehow an allegorical signification" (lines 19988–19989, quoting Celsus), but counters that Hesiod’s Pandora myth is equally absurd literally and is not scorned by Celsus: *“Are the Greeks alone at liberty to convey a philosophic meaning in a secret covering?”* (lines 20065–20066). The expulsion is interpreted as the soul losing its wings: *“a certain secret and mystical doctrine (far transcending that of Plato) of the soul losing its wings, and being borne downwards to earth, until it can lay hold of some stable resting-place”* (lines 20172–20173).

- **The serpent**: Origen connects the garden of Eden with Plato’s *Symposium* — the myth of Eros born in Zeus’s garden — and suggests Plato may have encountered Jewish philosophical tradition in Egypt (lines 20128–20141), leaving "the garden of Zeus" as a parallel to paradise and Penia’s plot as a parallel to the serpent.

- **"Adam" as universal man**: *“Adam signifies man; and that in those parts of the narrative which appear to refer to Adam as an individual, Moses is discoursing upon the nature of man in general. For in Adam (as the Scripture says) all die”* (lines 20159–20164). This transforms the narrative from a primitive etiological tale into a universal anthropological claim.

- **Allegorical reading as native to Scripture**: Origen cites Paul’s allegorical reading of Hagar and Sarah (Gal 4), the rock in the wilderness as Christ (1 Cor 10), and Asaph’s Psalm 78 as proof that "the fathers and authors of the doctrines themselves give them an allegorical signification" (lines 20455–20457). He also invokes Numenius the Pythagorean — "a surpassingly excellent expounder of Plato" — who "quotes from the writings of Moses and the prophets, and applies to the passages in question a not improbable allegorical meaning" (lines 20531–20536).

### 3. Divine Providence and the Problem of Evil

**On divine knowledge and moral improvement** (Chs. 3–4): Celsus asks why God does not simply make men good by divine power. Origen argues this would abolish free will: *“Grant it to be so… Where, then, is our free will? and what credit is there in assenting to the truth? Or how is the rejection of what is false praiseworthy?”* (lines 18844–18847). Foundational principle: "if you take away the spontaneity of virtue, you destroy its essence" (lines 18853–18855).

**On the fixed quantity of evils** (Chs. 62–64): Celsus states — paraphrasing Plato’s *Theaetetus* — *“There neither were formerly, nor are there now, nor will there be again, more or fewer evils in the world… For the nature of all things is one and the same, and the generation of evils is always the same”* (lines 20859–20862). Origen rejects this as inconsistent with Celsus’s own admission of providence, and cites Chrysippus’s historical evidence that moral evil visibly waxes and wanes (lines 20892–20909). The fixed-quantity thesis also implicitly posits a "Providence which presides, as it were, over evils (the number of which is fixed), to prevent their being either increased or diminished!" — an absurd conclusion (lines 20886–20890).

**On the origin of evil** (Chs. 65–66): Celsus says evils "do not proceed from God, but cleave to matter, and have their abode among mortal things" (lines 20982–20984). Origen agrees evil does not come from God, citing Jeremiah (*“out of the mouth of the Most High proceeds not evil and good”*, line 20986), but rejects matter as its cause: *“it is the mind of each individual which is the cause of the evil which arises in him”* (lines 20988–20989). The deeper origin requires revealed knowledge of the devil and "those who are termed his angels" (lines 20966–20974). See also [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation]].

**On eternal cycles and free will** (Chs. 67–68): Celsus invokes Stoic eternal recurrence. Origen follows the implication to its limit: *“it is clear that, of necessity, Socrates will always be a philosopher, and be condemned for introducing strange gods… And in the same way, according to the appointed cycles, Phalaris must always play the tyrant”* (lines 21005–21010). And: "Moses must again come forth from Egypt with the Jewish people, and Jesus again come to dwell in human life… Nay, Christians too will be the same in the appointed cycles; and Celsus will again write this treatise of his, which he has done innumerable times before" (lines 21018–21023).

**Providence as active management of history** (Chs. 69–70): *“it is not as a man who has imperfectly designed some piece of workmanship… that God administers correction to the world… but in order to prevent the tide of evil from rising to a greater height”* (lines 21056–21059). God governs "entire ages of time, as if they were, so to speak, so many individual years, performing during each one of them what is requisite with a reasonable regard to the care of the world" (lines 21076–21078).

### 4. Celsus’s Critique of Special Human Status

**Worms, ants, frogs** (Chs. 23–30): Celsus compares Jews and Christians to *“a flight of bats or to a swarm of ants issuing out of their nest, or to frogs holding council in a marsh, or to worms crawling together in the corner of a dunghill”* (lines 19419–19422). Origen replies: *“Reason, having its beginning in the reason of God, cannot allow of the rational animal being considered wholly alien from Deity”* (lines 19490–19492). **Image and likeness** (Ch. 30): Origen distinguishes man made *after the image* of God, but "not then also after His likeness" — the likeness being an eschatological goal, not a present possession (lines 19654–19658).

**Human superiority over irrational animals** (Chs. 74–99): Celsus argues creation was made no more for humans than for lions, eagles, or dolphins, and praises the intelligence and piety of animals. Origen allies with the Stoics: "Rational beings, then, as being the principal ones, occupy the place, as it were, of children in the womb, while irrational and soulless beings hold that of the envelope which is created along with the child" (lines 21234–21237). Any apparent animal wisdom — bird augury, elephant oath-keeping — is attributed to demonic influence: *“wicked demons, and, so to speak, of the race of Titans or Giants… possessing some power of distinguishing future events… secretly enter the bodies of the more rapacious and savage and wicked of animals, and stir them up”* (lines 21860–21867). This is why Moses classified divination-animals as unclean: "you will find that not only in the law, but also in the prophets, these animals are employed as examples of all that is most wicked" (lines 21892–21895). The image of God in humans is decisive: *“the image of the Supreme God is his reason”* (line 21608).

### 5. Creation, World-Ages, and Cosmology

**Antiquity of Moses** (Chs. 11–12, 21, 36): Celsus argues Jewish and Christian accounts plagiarized Greek sources. Origen counters: *“Moses… is shown to be much older than the time of the Trojan war”* (lines 19932–19933). The Greeks misunderstood Mosaic accounts and invented their cycles — not the reverse.

**Body, soul, matter, and creation** (Chs. 52–61): Celsus’s "paraphrase from the *Timaeus*" (line 20611) asserts: God made nothing mortal; the soul is God’s work; and "there is no difference between the body of a bat, or of a worm, or of a frog, and that of a man; for the matter is the same, and their corruptible part alike" (lines 20563–20565). Origen argues this leads to absurdity: if matter is undifferentiated, then the sun, moon, and stars are equally base as a bat’s body (lines 20693–20696). Against Aristotle’s fifth substance (ether), both Platonists and Stoics have already protested (lines 20704–20707). Origen’s resolution: matter is capable of "receiving those qualities which the Creator pleases to bestow" (lines 20732–20734), and at the resurrection, bodies "sown in corruption" are "raised in incorruption" (lines 20728–20731), citing 1 Cor 15.

**World-ages and purification**: God "administers entire ages of time, as if they were, so to speak, so many individual years, performing during each one of them what is requisite with a reasonable regard to the care of the world" (lines 21076–21078). Purificatory floods and conflagrations express providential timing, not improvised repair.

### 6. Neoplatonic and Stoic Vocabulary

- **Stoic theology**: Criticized as corporeal — the Stoics "were unable distinctly to comprehend the natural idea of God, as of a being altogether incorruptible and simple, and uncompounded and indivisible" (lines 21160–21162). Yet Stoic priority of rational over irrational nature is recruited in defense of human dignity (lines 21231–21233).
- **Platonic vocabulary**: Origen draws on the *Timaeus* (creation, soul, matter), the *Theaetetus* (evils), the *Symposium* (Eros-myth as parallel to Genesis paradise and serpent), and the *Republic* (expelling mythmakers). He uses Platonic "soul losing its wings" language to interpret the expulsion from Eden (line 20173). Numenius the Pythagorean is cited as an independent witness to the allegorical reading of Moses (lines 20531–20536).
- **Logos**: Appears at line 21656, where the instinctive behaviors of animals are attributed to "a natural constitutional tendency implanted by the *Logos*" — bridging Stoic *logos spermatikos* and Origen’s Logos-Christology.
- **Evil as will, not matter**: Against the Platonic-Stoic position that evil "cleaves to matter," Origen’s counter — "the mind of each individual which is the cause of the evil which arises in him" (lines 20988–20989) — locates evil in will rather than metaphysical substrate, anticipating Neoplatonic privation theory while grounding it in the personal history of demonic fall.

## Book V — The Soul, Oracles, Prophecy

Book V opens with [[origen|Origen]] dedicating the book to Ambrosius and invoking the need for divine assistance: "may God grant that we approach not our subject with our understanding and reason empty and devoid of divine inspiration, that the faith of those whom we wish to aid may not depend upon human wisdom" (lines 22133–22134).

**Celsus's Comparative Religion Argument**

Celsus's central comparative argument in Book V comes in two waves. First, he challenges the Jewish and Christian claim to privileged divine communication, arguing that pagan oracles — Apollo, Asclepius, Jupiter Ammon — perform the same mediating function as the God of Israel and his angels. Origen quotes Celsus: "O Jews and Christians, no God or son of a God either came or will come down (to earth). But if you mean that certain angels did so, then what do you call them? Are they gods, or some other race of beings? Some other race of beings (doubtless), and in all probability demons" (lines 22146–22150). Origen turns the argument back on Celsus: if no God descends to earth, then the Pythian Apollo and Asclepius are equally undermined. Celsus cannot reject Christian divine descent without also rejecting the pagan oracular tradition he implicitly respects.

Second, Celsus mounts a thoroughgoing argument for the equality of all national religious customs, quoting Herodotus to show that each people regards its own traditions as best — Ethiopians near Meroe worship only Jupiter and Bacchus; Arabians only Bacchus and Urania; Egyptians Osiris and Isis — concluding: "I think it makes no difference whether you call the highest being Zeus, or Zen, or Adonai, or Sabaoth, or Ammoun like the Egyptians, or Pappæus like the Scythians" (lines 23494–23496). Jewish circumcision is merely an Egyptian and Colchian custom; the oracle of Jupiter Ammon is no worse an ambassador than the angels of the Jews: "Ammon in divine things would not make a worse ambassador than the angels of the Jews, so that there is nothing wrong in each nation observing its established method of worship" (lines 23245–23247).

Celsus bolsters this with an argument from superintending spirits: the various quarters of the earth were allotted "from the beginning to different superintending spirits, and were distributed among certain governing powers, and in this manner the administration of the world is carried on. And whatever is done among each nation in this way would be rightly done, wherever it was agreeable to the wishes (of the superintending powers)" (lines 22909–22914). Jewish and Christian universalism thus becomes an act of impiety against the original divine order of nations.

**Origen's Defense of Biblical Prophecy and Monotheist Specificity**

Origen attacks the comparative-religion argument on multiple fronts. He argues that pagan oracles do not "reform the morals of their worshippers" and instead "tear away the souls of those who give heed to them from the one only visible and true God, under a pretence of paying honour to a multitude of deities" (lines 22193–22198). The Jewish prophets received the spirit of God precisely because of "their high degree of purity," and knowledge of the future was "sought… in the souls of those who, owing to their high degree of purity, received the spirit of the Supreme God" (lines 23549–23550). Origen distinguishes sharply between divination — called a practice of "wicked demons" that "bewitches men to no purpose" (lines 23546–23547) — and genuine prophecy.

On the names of God, Origen develops a counter-argument drawing on a theory of language he explicitly contrasts with Aristotle's conventional view. Languages are not arbitrary human inventions; "the languages which are prevalent among men do not derive their origin from men" (lines 23607–23608). Divine names carry intrinsic power tied to their original tongue, which is why "Sabaoth" and "Adonai" in their original Hebrew pronunciation produce effects translations cannot: "if we retain the original pronunciation, we shall, as those who are skilled in such matters maintain, produce some effect. And the same observation holds good of Adonai. If, then, neither Sabaoth nor Adonai, when rendered into what appears to be their meaning in the Greek tongue, can accomplish anything, how much less would be the result among those who regard it as a matter of indifference whether the highest being be called Jupiter, or Zen, or Adonai, or Sabaoth!" (lines 23648–23658). "Jupiter" belongs not to God but to a "demon, unfriendly to men and to the true God" (lines 23667–23668).

Against Celsus's superintending-spirits framework, Origen develops an allegorical reading of the Tower of Babel and the Table of Nations (Deuteronomy 32). The dispersion of peoples was a punishment: those who "wandered away from the east were delivered over, on account of their sins, to a reprobate mind, and to vile affections" (lines 23169–23171), allotted to sterner or gentler governing angels according to the degree of their transgression. Israel alone remained under a ruler "far more powerful than the others" who did not receive his charges for punishment but for formation (lines 23155–23161). The Christian mission enacts a further gathering: Christ "has rescued us from the present evil world, and from the princes of the world that come to nought" (lines 23179–23181), and the "law of nature" — identified with the law of God — supersedes the written laws of cities when the two conflict (lines 23352–23363).

Origen also defends the superiority of Jewish moral formation: the Jews possessed instruction "almost from their birth" in "the immortality of the soul, and in the existence of courts of justice under the earth, and in the rewards provided for those who have lived righteous lives" (lines 23537–23540) — a moral depth no pagan legal tradition achieved.

**Celsus's Attack on the Christian Afterlife Concept**

Celsus's sharpest polemic in Book V targets bodily resurrection. He frames the Christian hope as philosophically absurd and morally undignified: "It is folly on their part to suppose that when God, as if He were a cook, introduces the fire (which is to consume the world), all the rest of the human race will be burnt up, while they alone will remain… For what sort of human soul is that which would still long for a body that had been subject to corruption?" (lines 22537–22543). He invokes Heraclitus — "dead bodies are, as Heraclitus observes, more worthless than dung" — and adds that "God neither can nor will declare, contrary to all reason, that the flesh, which is full of those things which it is not even honourable to mention, is to exist for ever" (lines 22558–22562). He concedes one point only: "For the soul, indeed, He might be able to provide an everlasting life" (line 22557) — granting soul-immortality while categorically rejecting bodily resurrection. He also notes that some Christians themselves "pronounce it to be exceedingly vile, and loathsome, and impossible" (lines 22545–22546), using internal Christian disagreement as further evidence against the doctrine.

**Origen's Defense of the Resurrection and the Soul**

Origen's response refuses both Celsus's literal caricature and the gnosticizing Christians who reject resurrection entirely. His key move is the seed analogy from 1 Corinthians 15: "that which you sow, you sow not that body that shall be, but bare grain, it may chance of wheat, or of some other grain; but God gives it a body as it has pleased Him, and to every seed his own body" (lines 22683–22687). The resurrection body is continuous with but transformed from the earthly body: "It is sown in corruption, it is raised in incorruption; it is sown in dishonour, it is raised in glory; it is sown in weakness, it is raised in power; it is sown a natural body, it is raised a spiritual body" (lines 22701–22703). Origen insists that "neither we, then, nor the holy Scriptures, assert that with the same bodies, without a change to a higher condition, shall those who were long dead arise from the earth" (lines 22675–22677), correcting Celsus's straw-man reading.

On the soul's immortality, Origen agrees with Celsus that God can provide "an eternal life for the soul" and that He "possesses not only the power, but the will" (lines 22875–22876). But the soul, although it "may require a body, for the sake of moving from place to place" (lines 22726–22727), aspires to transformed spiritual embodiment, not a return to corrupt flesh: the hope "is not the hope of worms" (line 22724). The eschatological fire is interpreted not as crude destruction but as purification — it "burns indeed, but does not consume, those who are without a material body, which needs to be consumed by that fire" (lines 22574–22575) — after Malachi and Ezekiel rather than the cook's fire Celsus mocks.

Against Celsus's claim that God cannot do what is contrary to nature, Origen draws a careful distinction: God cannot do what is "contrary to reason or contrary to Himself" (lines 22895–22896), but raising humanity above its natural level is not contrary to nature — it is God "raising man above the level of human nature, and causing him to pass into a better and more divine condition" (lines 22861–22863). The Stoic doctrine of eternal recurrence (*ekpyrosis*) is offered as a counter-example: the Stoics maintain "that Socrates will again be born of the same parents, and suffer the same treatment, being accused by Anytus and Melitus" (lines 22796–22799), which is no less strange than resurrection and escapes Celsus's ridicule only through inconsistency.

**Notable Philosophical Arguments: Stoic and Platonic Vocabulary**

Book V deploys extensive Stoic and Platonic vocabulary throughout. The "law of nature" (*nomos physeos*) framework anchors Origen's rebuttal of cultural relativism: there are "two laws presented to us, the one being the law of nature, of which God would be the legislator, and the other being the written law of cities" (lines 23352–23354), and when the two conflict the law of God takes precedence. Platonic cosmology in the Stoic reception is engaged: Origen notes the Stoics call the Cosmos "the First God," the followers of Plato "the Second, and some of them the Third" (lines 22306–22307), and he presses this to an *ad absurdum* — it would make flies, gnats, worms, and serpents divine as parts of the cosmos (lines 22320–22322). Pythagorean transmigration is explicitly contrasted with Christian resurrection: "we do not hold… the doctrine of the transmigration from one body into another" (lines 23071–23072), and Pythagorean abstinence from meat is distinguished from Christian fasting by its grounding in metempsychosis (lines 23764–23765) rather than moral discipline.

Origen's Christology in Book V is stated with unusual philosophical directness: "although we may call Him a second God, let men know that by the term second God we mean nothing else than a virtue capable of including all other virtues, and a reason capable of containing all reason whatsoever" (lines 23454–23457). The Johannine prologue — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" — is glossed as establishing that "God cannot do anything which is contrary to reason, or contrary to Himself" (lines 22893–22896), positioning the Logos as both the rational principle of the cosmos and the ground of God's moral consistency — a comparison with Platonic and Stoic philosophy that Book VI pursues directly.

Book V closes with Origen noting that Celsus's concluding move — comparing Christian philosophical claims unfavorably with Greek philosophy — will be answered in the next book.

## Book VI — Plato vs. Moses; Divine Nature

Book VI opens with [[origen|Origen]]'s reframing of the central comparison Celsus poses throughout: Celsus had placed Platonic philosophical theology above the Mosaic Scriptures, arguing that "these things are stated much better among the Greeks (than in the Scriptures), and in a manner which is free from all exaggerations and promises on the part of God, or the Son of God" (lines 24270–24272). Origen does not deny Platonic insight but redirects the comparison toward efficacy: Plato's exalted language about the Good reached few; the "simple and practical" language of Scripture reached the many. "Few have been benefited (if they have indeed been benefited at all) by the beautiful and polished style of Plato, and those who have written like him; while, on the contrary, many have received advantage from those who wrote and taught in a simple and practical manner" (lines 24296–24299). The criterion of universality — converting the ignorant alongside the learned — distinguishes the apostolic mission from philosophical schools that confine "their views of the public good within very strait and narrow limits" (line 24285).

**Plato's theology vs. Moses on light and the Good.** Celsus quotes from Plato's Epistles: "The chief good can by no means be described in words, but is produced by long habit, and bursts forth suddenly as a light in the soul, as from a fire which had leapt forth" (lines 24330–24332). Origen accepts this as genuine insight — "it is God who revealed to men these as well as all other noble expressions" (lines 24333–24334) — but turns the compliment into a charge: those who have entertained correct ideas of God while worshipping at Artemis's shrine and offering a cock to Asclepius have "held the truth in unrighteousness" (line 24340, citing Romans 1). The Platonic philosophers "became foolish in their imaginations, and their foolish heart was involved in darkness and ignorance as to the (true) worship of God" (lines 24370–24371). Origen then argues that Moses antedates Plato and already knew what Plato later expressed: "our wise men — Moses, the most ancient of them all, and the prophets who followed him — knowing that the chief good could by no means be described in words, were the first who wrote that, as God manifests Himself to the deserving … He appeared to Abraham, or to Isaac, or to Jacob" (lines 24383–24389). Biblical light-language is traced back to prophets older than Plato: John's "That which was in the Logos was life, and the life was the light of men" (lines 24398–24401), David's "The Lord is my light and my salvation" (lines 24406–24407). Plato's doctrine of illumination did not produce worship worthy of God; "the simple language of the holy Scriptures has led to their honest readers being filled with a divine spirit" (lines 24423–24424).

**Platonic vs. biblical esoteric reserve.** Celsus quotes Plato's Seventh Letter to show that Plato could not adequately convey the highest things in writing. Origen matches and raises: Ezekiel swallowed his scroll; Paul "heard unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter" (line 24445); Jesus "conversed with His disciples in private … concerning the Gospel of God; but the words which He uttered have not been preserved" because they could not be conveyed to the multitude (lines 24447–24451). The esoteric reserve of Scripture exceeds that of Plato — the prophets "saw better than Plato (by means of the intelligence which they received by the grace of God)" (lines 24452–24454).

**The Logos doctrine: Platonic parallel and Origen's distinction.** Celsus quotes Plato's schema from the Seventh Letter — name, word, image, knowledge, the knowable true — and maps it onto Christian categories. Origen accepts the comparison selectively: John the Baptist corresponds to the "name" (voice crying in the wilderness); Jesus to the "word" (The Word became flesh). But Origen distinguishes: "Plato terms the third image; but we … would say with greater precision, that the mark of the wounds which is made in the soul by the word is the Christ which is in each one of us" (lines 24552–24555). The [[logos-theology|Logos theology]] of John 1 is aligned with but not reduced to the Platonic schema. In ch. 65, Origen refines the claim against Celsus's assertion that God is "not to be reached by word": "if he means the word that is in us … I, too, admit that God is not to be reached by word. If, however, we attend to the passage, In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God, we are of opinion that God is to be reached by this Word" (lines 26432–26434). God-as-Word and God-reached-by-the-Word are carefully distinguished, with the Son positioned as the medium of the Father's knowability.

**The super-celestial place: Plato, Scripture, and Philo.** When Celsus claims certain Christians misunderstood Plato in boasting of a "super-celestial God," Origen counters that the Phaedrus's supra-celestial place — "the essence, which is both colourless and formless, and which cannot be touched, which really exists, is the pilot of the soul, and is beheld by the understanding alone" (lines 24915–24917) — was itself derived from Hebrew sources, not the reverse: "I do not, indeed, deny that Plato learned from certain Hebrews the words quoted from the Phædrus, or even, as some have recorded, that he quoted them from a perusal of our prophetic writings" (lines 24910–24912). Jacob's ladder "obscurely pointing, by this matter of the ladder, either to the same truths which Plato had in view, or to something greater than these" (lines 24965–24966) addresses the same ascent-of-souls theme Celsus treated via Mithraic planetary symbolism. On the ladder's significance, Origen recommends that [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] "has composed a treatise which deserves the thoughtful and intelligent investigation of all lovers of truth" (lines 24967–24968).

**The good and the divine in Greek philosophy.** Celsus cites Heraclitus and Plato to show that the distinction between human and divine wisdom is a Hellenistic commonplace; Origen agrees but presses the priority of the prophets: "This opinion … is truly an ancient one, its antiquity not being referred back, as Celsus thinks, merely to Heraclitus and Plato. For before these individuals lived, the prophets distinguished between the two kinds of wisdom" (lines 24693–24695). Human wisdom is "the wisdom of the world, which is foolishness with God"; divine wisdom "comes, through the grace of God who bestows it, to those who have evinced their capacity for receiving it" (lines 24680–24683). Origen deploys Middle Platonic vocabulary approvingly: the Platonic triad "All things are around the King of all, and all things exist for his sake, and he is the cause of all good things. With things of the second rank he is second, and with those of the third rank he is third" (lines 24873–24876, from Plato's Second Epistle) is compared favorably to the seraphim-and-cherubim imagery of Isaiah and Ezekiel. Origen suggests Scripture's hierarchical theology of God and the divine Logos maps naturally onto this Platonic first-second-third schema.

**God's incorporeality and the critique of anthropomorphism.** Celsus charges that God "has neither mouth nor voice" and that Christian Scripture naively anthropomorphizes the divine. Origen responds that biblical language about God's mouth, voice, hands, and movements must be read figuratively: "What is called the voice of God is said to be seen as God's voice by the people … the word saw being taken, agreeably to the custom of Scripture, in a spiritual sense" (lines 26336–26338). God does not "partake of form or colour. Nor does He even partake of motion, because He stands firm, and His nature is permanent" (lines 26395–26396). The image-of-God language in Genesis refers not to the body but to "our inner man, which is also renewed … when a man becomes perfect" (lines 26384–26386). On substance, Origen raises and then suspends the question: "that which is permanent and immaterial be substance properly so called, so that it would be found that God is beyond substance, communicating of His substance, by means of office and power, to those to whom He communicates Himself by His Word" (lines 26410–26413) — language echoing Middle Platonic epekeina tes ousias. The Son is tentatively described as "substance of substances, and idea of ideas, and the principle of all things, while above all there is His Father and God" (lines 26419–26420). Paul's formulation from Romans 11:36 — "of Him, and through Him, and to Him are all things" — is glossed as indicating that God is "the beginning of the substance of all things," "the bond of their subsistence," and "their final end" (lines 26424–26426), a triadic schema Origen treats as integrating Platonic causal language with scriptural theology.

## Book VII — Second Coming and Christian Ethics

Book VII opens with [[origen|Origen]] addressing Celsus's sustained assault on Jewish and Christian prophecy before pivoting to eschatology, ethics, and the Christian refusal to participate in civic religious life.

### False Prophets and the Defense of Hebrew Prophecy (Chs. 1–11; lines 26967–27305)

Celsus attacks Christian reliance on prophecy by arguing that itinerant prophets in Phoenicia and Palestine were common and fraudulent. He quotes their typical speech directly: *"I am God; I am the Son of God; or, I am the Divine Spirit; I have come because the world is perishing, and you, O men, are perishing for your iniquities. But I wish to save you, and you shall see me returning again with heavenly power. Blessed is he who now does me homage. On all the rest I will send down eternal fire, both on cities and on countries."* (lines 27226–27232). Celsus adds that such speakers, when pressed, confess their words were meaningless: *"strange, fanatical, and quite unintelligible words, of which no rational person can find the meaning; for so dark are they as to have no meaning at all; but they give occasion to every fool or impostor to apply them to suit his own purposes"* (lines 27265–27268).

Origen responds by contrasting these wandering figures with the Hebrew prophets, who were selected by divine providence for their moral excellence: *"They were selected by Divine Providence to receive the Divine Spirit, and to be the depositaries of His holy oracles, on the ground of their leading a life of almost unapproachable excellence, intrepid, noble, unmoved by danger or death"* (lines 27153–27157). Unlike the Pythian priestess, who prophesied in ecstatic loss of self-control and was entered by the spirit through shameful bodily means, the Hebrew prophets received the Holy Spirit in clarity of mind (Chs. 3–4; lines 27028–27056). Origen also denies Celsus's claim to have personally heard such false prophets: had they existed, their prophecies would have been recorded (lines 27293–27305).

### Eschatology: Resurrection and the Pure Land (Chs. 28–35; lines 27784–27994)

Celsus ridicules the Christian doctrine of the resurrection, treating it as borrowed from Plato's vision of the pure land in the *Phaedo* and as intellectually contemptible when held by simple people: *"Celsus ridicules this doctrine because he does not understand it, and because he has learned it from ignorant persons, who were unable to support it on any reasonable grounds"* (lines 27911–27913). Celsus also accuses Christians of expecting to see and touch God with bodily organs after death.

Origen's defense operates on two registers simultaneously — literal and spiritual. On the literal plane, he insists resurrection is not metempsychosis. The soul successively takes on and exchanges bodies suited to the level of existence it inhabits: *"it at one time puts off one body which was necessary before, but which is no longer adequate in its changed state, and it exchanges it for a second; and at another time it assumes another in addition to the former, which is needed as a better covering, suited to the purer ethereal regions of heaven"* (lines 27920–27924). The corruptible puts on incorruption and the mortal puts on immortality as garments — these are not identical to the incorruptible and the immortal themselves but coverings transforming those who wear them (lines 27931–27940).

On the spiritual register, Origen reframes the question of seeing God after death. The resurrection is not needed to see God bodily; God has no body and occupies no material place. It is the mind, made in the image of the Creator, that is capable of knowing God: *"For that which sees God is not the eye of the body; it is the mind which is made in the image of the Creator, and which God has in His providence rendered capable of that knowledge. To see God belongs to the pure heart"* (lines 27955–27958). The eyes, ears, and hands mentioned in Scripture are a *"diviner sense"* distinct from bodily organs (lines 27976–27990). The Beatitudes — *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God* — provide the central eschatological promise (line 27960).

Celsus's claim that Christians borrowed the idea of the better land from Plato's *Phaedo* is met with the chronological priority argument: Moses was older than the Greek literature, and the Promised Land — *"a good land and a large, a land flowing with milk and honey"* — refers not to earthly Judea (which shares in Adam's curse) but to the heavenly Jerusalem, the pure land in the pure region of heaven (lines 27806–27831). The apostle's words in Hebrews — *"You have come unto Mount Zion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem"* — confirm this (lines 27835–27837). Plato, Origen argues, derived his cosmological imagery from the prophets, not the reverse (lines 27863–27875).

### Moses vs. Jesus: Law, Ethics, and Allegory (Chs. 18–26; lines 27484–27762)

Celsus poses a major challenge to Christian coherence: if the same God gave Moses commands to accumulate wealth, take dominion over nations, and destroy enemies, but Jesus taught poverty, non-retaliation, and dependence on providence, *"Whether is it Moses or Jesus who teaches falsely? Did the Father, when he sent Jesus, forget the commands which he had given to Moses? Or did he change his mind, condemn his own laws, and send forth a messenger with counter instructions?"* (lines 27496–27499).

Origen's extended response (Chs. 18–25) is his most sustained exercise in allegorical exegesis within the work. The law has *"a twofold sense — the one literal, the other spiritual"* (lines 27568–27569). Promises of riches in the law refer not to worldly wealth but to *"those riches which enlighten the eyes, and which enrich a man in all utterance and in all knowledge"* (lines 27593–27595). Commands to slay enemies mean the destruction of one's own vices: *"by the land he means the flesh whose lusts are at enmity with God; and by the city of Jehovah he designates his own soul"* (lines 27623–27625). Even Psalm 137's imprecation against Babylon's infants is read as the destruction of nascent sinful thoughts: *"the little ones of Babylon (which signifies confusion) are those troublesome sinful thoughts which arise in the soul"* (lines 27638–27639). There is therefore no contradiction between the Mosaic code and the teaching of Jesus.

### Christian Ethics and the Stoic Comparison (Chs. 58–65; lines 28716–28930)

Celsus argues that the Christian command to turn the other cheek is *"an ancient saying, which had been admirably expressed long before, and which they have only reported in a coarser way"* — crediting it to Plato's *Crito* (lines 28719–28721). He implies Christianity is a degraded version of Greek philosophical ethics.

Origen's answer distinguishes source from effect. Even if the same moral principle appears in Plato and in Jesus, the mode of transmission matters. Plato's formulation is inaccessible to common people; Jesus's plain speech reaches the multitudes. Origen uses the analogy of food: the same nutritious dish adapted to aristocratic palates feeds only a few, while the same dish prepared for common tastes nourishes the many. The divine Word accommodated itself *"to the capacities of the simple multitudes"* (lines 28809–28810). The Christian version of the precept is not corrupted but universalized.

On the comparison of Christian abstinence from idolatry with that of the Scythians, Persians, or nomadic Libyans (Chs. 62–64), Origen offers a rigorous analysis of motive. Stoics abstain from adultery out of regard for social rationality; Epicureans abstain because adultery brings more pain than pleasure; the ordinary person abstains from fear of law. Likewise, Persians avoid images without knowing the true God — and still worship the sun. Christians avoid images and temples from an explicit command: *"Christians and Jews have regard to this command, You shall fear the Lord your God, and serve Him alone"* (lines 28920–28921), and are *"ready to suffer death when it is necessary, rather than debase by any such impiety the conception which they have of the Most High God"* (lines 28928–28930).

### Demons, Providence, and Christian Withdrawal from Civic Religion (Chs. 66–70; lines 28954–29108)

Celsus's final challenge in Book VII concerns cosmic governance. He argues that all things — including the work of angels, demons, and heroes — are *"regulated by the law of the Most High God"* and that these powers have been assigned their departments by God; therefore it is just to serve them alongside God (lines 29011–29016). The Christian refusal to honor demons through temple sacrifice constitutes, in Celsus's view, a withdrawal from the properly ordered cosmos.

Origen denies the premise. The claim that *all* things in the universe are ordered by God's will would entail that sins and demonic wickedness are also God's will — a conclusion he rejects as morally incoherent: *"if we take the word ordered in its proper signification, and say that all the results of sin were ordered, then it is evident that all things are ordered according to God's will, and that all, therefore, who do evil do not offend against His government"* (lines 29031–29033). Demons are not subordinate servants assigned their departments; they *"were not demons originally, but they became so in departing from the true way"* (lines 29058–29059). Temple worship is not civil deference to a rationally ordered hierarchy — it is service to fallen beings. Christians drive demons out by prayer and adjuration rather than sacrificing to them (lines 29000–29003).

Book VII closes at the edge of the question about serving many masters, deferring the full resolution to Book VIII.

## Book VIII — Civic Duty, Emperor Cult, Peroration

Book VIII opens with [[origen|Origen]]'s invocation of Paul's ambassadorial language — "We are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us" (line 29117) — before engaging Celsus's final cluster of objections: the Christian refusal to worship demons, the civic-participation charge, the emperor-cult question, and the political consequences of a hypothetically Christian Rome.

### Celsus's Civic-Parasite Charge

Celsus presses the social objection at multiple points. In Chapter 2 he frames the Christian refusal to serve multiple masters as "the language of sedition" by "those who separate themselves and stand aloof from all human society" (line 29135). The charge hardens in Chapter 55, where Celsus delivers an ultimatum: "If they refuse to render due service to the gods, and to respect those who are set over this service, let them not come to manhood, or marry wives, or have children, or indeed take any share in the affairs of life; but let them depart hence with all speed, and leave no posterity behind them, that such a race may become extinct from the face of the earth" (lines 30652–30657). The alternative, he says, is civic participation — including sacrifice to the presiding gods. In Chapter 68 he invokes Homer's monarchy maxim and warns that if all people behaved as Christians do, "the affairs of the earth would fall into the hands of the wildest and most lawless barbarians; and then there would no longer remain among men any of the glory of your religion or of the true wisdom" (lines 31040–31043). Chapter 73 makes the demand explicit: Celsus "urges us to help the king with all our might, and to labour with him in the maintenance of justice, to fight for him; and if he requires it, to fight under him, or lead an army along with him" (lines 31207–31210). Chapter 75 repeats the demand for civic office: "Celsus also urges us to take office in the government of the country, if that is required for the maintenance of the laws and the support of religion" (lines 31253–31255).

### Origen's Defense: Spiritual Warfare and Prayer as Civic Service

[[origen|Origen]]'s reply to the military and civic objections is systematic. Christians do serve the empire, but by a higher means. Chapter 73 states: "we do, when occasion requires, give help to kings, and that, so to say, a divine help, putting on the whole armour of God... the more any one excels in piety, the more effective help does he render to kings, even more than is given by soldiers, who go forth to fight and slay as many of the enemy as they can" (lines 31210–31217). The analogy to pagan temple priests is offered: since the Romans exempt their own priests from military service "that they may with hands unstained and free from human blood offer the appointed sacrifices to your gods," Christians who pray "for those who are fighting in a righteous cause" deserve the same exemption (lines 31220–31227). The conclusion is pointed: "And as we by our prayers vanquish all demons who stir up war, and lead to the violation of oaths, and disturb the peace, we in this way are much more helpful to the kings than those who go into the field to fight for them. And we do take our part in public affairs, when along with righteous prayers we join self-denying exercises and meditations" (lines 31229–31234). Chapter 74 reframes this as patriotism: "Christians are benefactors of their country more than others. For they train up citizens, and inculcate piety to the Supreme Being" (lines 31244–31246).

On civic office, Origen counters not with refusal but with a parallel polity: "we recognise in each state the existence of another national organization, founded by the Word of God, and we exhort those who are mighty in word and of blameless life to rule over Churches" (lines 31255–31258). Christians decline secular office "not for the purpose of escaping public duties... but that they may reserve themselves for a diviner and more necessary service in the Church of God— for the salvation of men" (lines 31267–31270).

Origen also directly refuses Celsus's extinction ultimatum: "there appears to us to be no good reason for our leaving this world, except when piety and virtue require it... But God has allowed us to marry, because all are not fit for the higher, that is, the perfectly pure life; and God would have us to bring up all our children" (lines 30668–30674).

### The Emperor-Cult and Sacrifice Question

Celsus throughout Book VIII presses Christians on their refusal to sacrifice to the gods of the state. Chapter 17 records his accusation that Christians "shrink from raising altars, statues, and temples," calling this "the badge or distinctive mark of a secret and forbidden society" (lines 29523–29525). Origen replies by spiritualizing temple, altar, and statue: "we regard the spirit of every good man as an altar from which arises an incense which is truly and spiritually sweet-smelling, namely, the prayers ascending from a pure conscience" (lines 29527–29529); the virtues cultivated by the divine word are the true statues; the human body is the true temple. The Pauline text "You also, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house" (line 29601) is adduced as scriptural warrant.

On the emperor-oath specifically, Chapter 65 addresses refusal to swear by the king's fortune: "we will never swear by the fortune of the king, nor by ought else that is considered equivalent to God" (lines 30961–30962). If the king's "fortune" means the luck of uncertain events, swearing by it is swearing by a fiction; if it means the king's demon, then "we must die sooner than swear by a wicked and treacherous demon" (line 30971). Chapter 66 similarly refuses to celebrate "a joyful triumphal song in praise of Minerva," though Origen will gladly praise the sun as "the glorious workmanship of God, which obeys His laws" (lines 30989–30991). The refusal is not anti-civic contempt but principled monotheism: "we sing hymns to the Most High alone, and His Only-begotten, who is the Word and God... the sun, the moon, the stars, and all the host of heaven... form a divine chorus, and unite with the just among men in celebrating the praises of the Most High God" (lines 31017–31021).

Chapter 21 sharpens the exchange on public feasts. Celsus argues that a good, non-jealous God should not object to Christians joining public sacrificial banquets. Origen replies that participation could only be acceptable "if it were proved that the public feasts had nothing wrong in them, and were grounded upon true views of the character of God" (lines 29655–29657) — a condition idol-feasts cannot meet. The Pauline logic from 1 Corinthians is applied: sacrifices are made to demons, and one cannot be both "a partaker of the table of the Lord and of the table of demons" (lines 29737–29738).

### Origen's Vision of a Christian Empire

Book VIII contains [[origen|Origen]]'s most explicit speculations about a Christianized political order. Chapter 68 replies to Celsus's Homeric monarchy maxim with a counter-vision: "And would that all were to follow my example in rejecting the maxim of Homer, maintaining the divine origin of the kingdom, and observing the precept to honour the king!... For if, in the words of Celsus, they do as I do, then it is evident that even the barbarians, when they yield obedience to the word of God, will become most obedient to the law, and most humane; and every form of worship will be destroyed except the religion of Christ, which will alone prevail. And indeed it will one day triumph, as its principles take possession of the minds of men more and more every day" (lines 31059–31070).

Chapter 69 addresses Celsus's direct question of whether God would fight for Rome if the Romans converted. Origen answers that united Christian prayer would be more powerful than armies, citing the Mosaic precedent. Chapter 70 develops this into a vision of Christian civic ordering: "if all the Romans... embrace the Christian faith, they will... overcome their enemies; or rather, they will not war at all, being guarded by that divine power... For men of God are assuredly the salt of the earth: they preserve the order of the world; and society is held together as long as the salt is uncorrupted" (lines 31107–31112). The vision is theocratic in character — not Christians filling Roman magistracies, but Christian prayer and virtue sustaining the fabric of civilized order from below.

Chapter 72 extends this to universal scope, responding to Celsus's claim that global agreement was impossible: "our belief is, that the Word shall prevail over the entire rational creation, and change every soul into His own perfection; in which state every one, by the mere exercise of his power, will choose what he desires, and obtain what he chooses" (lines 31161–31164). A quotation from Zephaniah (lines 31176–31191) provides prophetic warrant for the eventual gathering of all peoples under one consent to serve God.

### Closing Peroration: Reason, Faith, and Providence

The treatise closes at Chapter 76, addressed to Ambrose, the patron who commissioned the work: "In eight books we have embraced all that we considered it proper to say in reply to that book of Celsus which he entitles *A True Discourse*. And now it remains for the readers of his discourse and of my reply to judge which of the two breathes most of the Spirit of the true God, of piety towards Him, and of that truth which leads men by sound doctrines to the noblest life" (lines 31282–31287).

The peroration rests on the conviction argued throughout Book VIII and the whole work: that reason and faith are not antagonists. The Word (*Logos*) who governs all rational creatures is the same Word incarnate in Jesus, and the Christian life — prayer, virtue, spiritual warfare against demons — is simultaneously the most rational and the most beneficial life possible for both the individual and the empire. Origen closes with a practical note: if Celsus wrote a promised sequel, Origen asks Ambrose to send it so it may be answered, promising to "either overthrow the false teaching that may be in it, or, laying aside all jealousy, we may testify our approval of whatever truth it may contain" (lines 31295–31297) — a characteristic gesture of confident intellectual engagement rather than dismissal.

The *Letter to Gregory* (lines 31298–31415) is appended in the source text but belongs to a separate work addressed to Gregory Thaumaturgus, not to *Contra Celsus*.

## Key Theological Positions

Five apologetic strategies recur across all eight books, constituting Origen's systematic response to Celsus:

**1. The moral-transformation criterion.** Origen's most distinctive argument: the test of a genuine miracle or true teaching is the transformation it produces. Celsus charges that Jesus was a sorcerer; Origen replies that sorcerers do not teach righteousness, endure martyrdom, or reform the morally debased. This criterion recurs in Books I, III, and VIII as the primary evidence for Christianity's divine origin.

**2. The prophetic-fulfillment argument.** Origen defends the specificity of Hebrew prophecy against pagan divination and against Celsus's comparative religion dismissal. The argument from fulfilled prophecy (Books I, V, VII) includes chronological claims (Daniel's weeks, the cessation of Jewish kingship), geographical specificity (Bethlehem), and the transformation of the world — none of which, Origen argues, can be attributed to the natural cycles of oracular ambiguity.

**3. The allegorical defense of anthropomorphism.** When Celsus attacks OT anthropomorphisms (God walking, resting, repenting, being angry), Origen consistently deploys allegorical reading: these are not naive literalism but pedagogical accommodations (*synkatabasis*) or deliberate figures. The same strategy is applied to objectionable narrative (Eden, the Flood, the conquest). Origen's allegorical method in *Contra Celsus* is the apologetic form of the same hermeneutic systematized in *De Principiis* IV.

**4. Plato as partial ally, partial rival.** Origen freely appropriates Middle Platonic language — divine simplicity, incorporeality, the Logos as mediating principle — while consistently arguing that Plato's wisdom was (a) derived from Moses (chronological priority) and (b) ineffective (reaching only the educated few), in contrast to the Gospel's universal transforming power. Books IV and VI most extensively deploy this strategy.

**5. The civic-parasite refutation.** Book VIII responds to Celsus's charge that Christians are ungrateful empire-dwellers by constructing a theology of spiritual warfare: Christian prayer is more effective than military service; Christian moral transformation is better for the empire than ritual compliance. This anticipates the Constantinian synthesis while maintaining Origen's apolitical stance.

## The Celsus Problem

Celsus's *True Doctrine* survives almost entirely through Origen's refutations — Origen quotes it at length before answering. This makes *Contra Celsus* simultaneously an apologetic and the primary source for the most sophisticated pagan critique of Christianity. Modern scholars have reconstructed Celsus's argument from Origen's quotations (Andresen, Hoffmann).

Celsus was probably an Epicurean or Middle Platonist (scholars differ). His objections combine:
- **Historical criticism**: the virgin birth, the resurrection, the reliability of the Gospels
- **Philosophical criticism**: anthropomorphism; the particularity of divine revelation; the irrationality of the Incarnation
- **Social criticism**: Christianity's appeal to the uneducated; its internal divisions; its anti-civic character

## Canon and Reception

**Not scripture in any tradition.** Major apologetic text for patristic Christianity; quoted by later apologists. The work was not widely read in the Middle Ages (Greek survival only); Erasmus and later humanists revived interest. Modern critics of religion from Enlightenment through the present have found Celsus's recovered objections still compelling.
