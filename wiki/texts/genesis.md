Genesis is the first book of the Hebrew Bible (Torah/Old Testament), covering the creation of the world, the primeval history of humanity, and the stories of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph. Its name derives from the Greek *génesis* (origin), translating the Hebrew *berēʾšît* (in the beginning), the book's first word.

## Structure

Genesis is organized by the **[[toledot|*toledot* formula]]** ("these are the generations/history of X"), which appears ten times and divides the book into narrative and genealogical units. The broad structure:

- **Primeval History** (1:1–11:32): Creation, Eden, Cain and Abel, the Flood, Table of Nations, Babel
- **Abraham Cycle, Part I** (12:1–17:27): Call of Abram, Lot, covenant (Gen 15 and 17), Hagar and Ishmael
- **Abraham Cycle, Part II / Isaac** (18:1–26:35): Sodom, Akedah, Isaac narratives
- **Jacob Cycle** (25:19–36:43): Jacob's birth, Laban, Peniel, Dinah
- **Joseph Narrative** (37:1–50:26): Joseph in Egypt, famine, reconciliation

Geographically: chs. 1–11 are set in Babylonia; chs. 12–36 in Canaan; chs. 37–50 in Egypt — representing the three poles of Israel's ancient world.

[[victor-hamilton|Victor P. Hamilton]] (NICOT) provides a detailed analysis of the *tôlĕdôt* formula. He notes that five instances introduce narrative (creation 2:4a, flood 6:9a, Abraham 11:27a, Isaac 25:19a, Jacob 37:2a) and should be rendered "this is the story of X," while the other five head genealogies. Hamilton defends 2:4a as a superscript introducing what follows (2:4bff.), not a subscript to 1:1–2:3, on grammatical, versional (LXX), and contextual grounds (the formula elsewhere points forward to progeny or story). He discusses the formula as colophons or titles, engaging the Wiseman-Harrison "tablet theory" (eleven tablets with colophons at ends) but prefers the standard view of the formulae as introductory rubrics structuring the book. The first *tôlĕdôt* (heavens and earth) uniquely lacks a personal name and pairs with the creation account to present humanity's dual origins: divine image (1:1–2:3) and earthly creatureliness (2:4ff.). The ten *tôlĕdôt* thus provide the book's "lines of demarcation," with primeval history (chs. 1–11) showing degeneration from creation through Fall, Cain, Flood, and Babel, answered by the election of the patriarchs (chs. 12–50). Hamilton emphasizes the geographical poles (Babylonia–Canaan–Egypt) and the movement from universal failure to the particular family through whom blessing will come to the nations.

## Major Themes

**From degeneration to regeneration**: The primeval history (Gen 1–11) traces a movement from creation (Gen 1–2) through degeneration (Gen 3–11) — Fall, Cain, Flood, Babel — to the election of Abraham (Gen 12) as God's answer to universal human failure. Each of the four generations of patriarchs demonstrates God's faithfulness operating through morally compromised figures. Hamilton underscores that the *tôlĕdôt* structure highlights this arc: the first five *tôlĕdôt* cover the primeval "story" of cosmic and human origins leading to crisis, resolved in the patriarchal "story" of promise and preservation.

**Covenant and promise**: God's promises to Abraham — land, descendants, blessing to all nations (Gen 12:1-3) — structure the entire book. These promises are twice covenanted formally (Gen 15: vision ritual; Gen 17: circumcision as sign) and are unconditional in character despite human failure. See [[covenant|Covenant]]. Hamilton details the Abrahamic covenant's dual ratification and its implications for circumcision as sign rather than condition.

**Divine sovereignty through younger-son reversal**: Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh — the repeated pattern of the younger superseding the firstborn by divine choice signals that election is gratuitous, not based on natural order.

**Providence through human agency**: The Joseph narrative (Gen 37–50) culminates in the explicit statement: "You intended evil against me, but God intended it for good" (Gen 50:20) — the book's clearest formulation of how divine sovereignty and human will coexist without dissolving each other. Hamilton notes the Joseph story as the climax of the patriarchal *tôlĕdôt*, resolving the family tensions introduced in prior cycles through hidden divine providence.

Additional narrative emphases from Hamilton: the book as "origin" (*génesis*) not only of the cosmos and humanity but of Israel as the chosen medium of world blessing; the absence of theogony (God has no *rē’šîṯ*); and the interplay of vertical genealogies (tracing one line) and horizontal/segmented ones (multiple descendants) within the *tôlĕdôt* framework.

## Interpretive Cruxes

**Genesis 1:1 — absolute beginning or temporal clause?**: The Hebrew *berēʾšît* can be read as an absolute statement ("In the beginning, God created") or as a temporal clause ("When God began to create..."). The absolute reading implies creation from nothing (*creatio ex nihilo*); the temporal reading implies pre-existing matter. [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] defends the absolute reading on grammatical and theological grounds. See [[creation-ex-nihilo|Creation ex Nihilo]].

**The two creation accounts (Gen 1:1–2:3 and Gen 2:4–3:24)**: Source criticism assigns Gen 1 to P and Gen 2–3 to J on the basis of different divine names (*Elohim* vs. *YHWH Elohim*), style, and order of events. Hamilton reads them as complementary — Gen 1 focusing on humanity's divine endowment, Gen 2–3 on humanity's creaturely limitations. See [[genesis-creation-accounts|Genesis: Two Creation Accounts]].

**The *imago dei* (Gen 1:26–27)**: What does it mean to be created in God's "image and likeness"? Options: a specific faculty (reason, conscience), a functional role (dominion over creation), or a holistic description of personhood. See [[imago-dei|Imago Dei]].

**"Let us make" (Gen 1:26)**: The cohortative plural has been read as: divine council (polytheistic residue), plural of majesty, Trinitarian foreshadowing, or rhetorical emphasis for the creation of humanity.

**The "sons of God" (Gen 6:1–4)**: The *bĕnê-hāʾelōhîm* who take human wives are read as: (a) divine/angelic beings, (b) kings or tyrants ("sons of the mighty"), (c) the line of Seth intermarrying with the line of Cain. See [[sons-of-god|Sons of God (Gen 6:1-4)]].

**Flood: universal or local?**: The text's language implies a universal deluge, but geological and geographical questions press toward a local flood interpretation. See [[flood-universality|The Flood's Scope]].

**Genesis 15 vs. Genesis 17 — one covenant or two?**: Gen 15 uses a vision and animal-splitting ritual; Gen 17 introduces circumcision and a name-change. Source criticism assigns these to different hands (J/pre-J vs. P). Theologically, whether they represent one covenant in two stages or two covenants is significant for understanding circumcision's role.

**The Akedah (Gen 22)**: The near-sacrifice of Isaac raises questions of divine ethics, the abolition of child sacrifice, the nature of faith-testing, and the typological relationship to NT atonement theology. See [[akedah-interpretation|The Akedah: Interpretive Controversies]].

## The Kabbalistic (Zoharic) Reading of Genesis

The [[zohar|Zohar]] reads Genesis at the **Sod** (mystical) level of [[pardes|PaRDeS]], treating the creation and patriarchal narratives not (only) as cosmology and history but as a coded account of the inner life of God — the self-unfolding of the ten [[sefirot|sefirot]] from [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]]. This is a fundamentally different mode from the grammatical-historical reading of [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] and the source-critical reading of the Documentary Hypothesis: it neither contests nor confirms the plain sense but claims to read *beneath* it. Key Zoharic re-readings of Volume I's portion (Gen 1:1–11:32):

- **Genesis 1:1 — theogony, not cosmogony**: The Zohar re-parses *bereshit bara Elohim* so that *Elohim* is the **object**, not the subject, of the verb: "With *Beginning* (*reshit* = the sefirah Ḥokhmah, Wisdom), [Ein Sof] created *Elohim* (= the sefirah Binah)." The verse no longer narrates God creating the world; it narrates the **emergence of the knowable God (the sefirot) from the unknowable Infinite**. The first act is the bursting forth of a single point (Ḥokhmah) from the concealed aura (*avir*) of Ein Sof. This stands in sharp contrast to the [[creation-ex-nihilo|*creatio ex nihilo*]] reading Hamilton defends: the Zohar describes **emanation**, not creation from nothing — though it preserves a "nothing" (*Ayin*) as the supernal source. See [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]], [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]].
- **Tohu, bohu, darkness, and wind (Gen 1:2)** are read as sefirotic or pre-sefirotic potencies — stages in the differentiation of the divine — rather than (or as well as) a description of primordial chaos.
- **"Let there be light" (Gen 1:3)** — the **primordial light** (*or ha-ganuz*, the hidden light) by which Adam saw "from one end of the world to the other," later concealed for the righteous in the world to come; associated with Ḥesed and the radiance of the upper sefirot.
- **The second day and the firmament (Gen 1:6)** — the Zohar locates here the cosmic **conflict between the left side (Gevurah) and the right (Ḥesed)**, mediated by the central pillar (Tif'eret) on the third day; from the unresolved blaze of the left, Gehinnom and the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] emerge. This reading underlies the Zohar's interpretation of the [[korah|Korah]] rebellion.
- **Eden and the Fall (Gen 2–3) as cosmic divorce**: [[adam|Adam]]'s sin is read as the **separation of the Tree of Knowledge ([[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]/Malkhut) from the Tree of Life (Tif'eret)** — a sundering of the divine union that brought death, empowered the Other Side, and drove the Shekhinah toward exile. The serpent is the instrument of the Sitra Aḥra. See [[adam|Adam]].
- **The Flood (Gen 6–9) as cosmic-sexual catastrophe**: the generation's sexual corruption disrupted the union of the upper and lower waters; [[noah|Noah]] is read with marked ambivalence (righteous "in his generation," yet failing to intercede). See [[noah|Noah]].

*Patriarchal Genesis (Zohar Vol II — Lekh Lekha through Va-Yetse, Gen ~12–32):*
- **[[circumcision|Circumcision]] (Gen 17)** as the covenant of [[tsaddiq|Yesod]]: "before [[abraham|Abraham]] was circumcised, one rung spoke with him; once circumcised, all the rungs rested upon him" — the bodily sign that opens the person to the full sefirotic influx.
- **[[melchizedek|Melchizedek]]** (Gen 14) as the joining of "two worlds" (Malkhut and the supernal world); the **Akedah** (Gen 22) as "water vs. fire" ([[abraham|Ḥesed]] vs. [[isaac|Isaac]]/Gevurah), harmonized in [[jacob|Jacob]]/Tif'eret — see [[akedah-interpretation|The Akedah]]; **[[lot|Sodom]]** as executed Din.
- **The four matriarchs and the divine feminine**: [[sarah|Sarah]] with the indwelling Shekhinah; [[rachel|Rachel]] = the lower/revealed world (Malkhut/[[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]) and [[leah|Leah]] = the upper/concealed world (Binah); **Jacob's ladder** as the sefirotic axis.

*End-of-Genesis material (Zohar Vol III — Va-Yishlaḥ through Va-Yeḥi):*
- **The patriarchs as sefirot**: [[jacob|Jacob]] = **Tif'eret** (the central pillar, "select of the patriarchs"); his wrestling at the Jabbok (Gen 32) is combat with **Samael, the Prince of [[esau|Esau]]**, champion of the [[sitra-ahra|Other Side]]; the twelve tribes are the array of the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]].
- **[[joseph|Joseph]] = Yesod, "the Righteous One, foundation of the world"** (Prov 10:25): he guards the sign of the covenant against Potiphar's wife. See [[tsaddiq|Tsaddiq]].
- **Judah and [[tamar|Tamar]]** (Gen 38): the transgressive-but-righteous channeling of the messianic seed; Tamar paired with [[ruth|Ruth]] as the "two women" from whom David and the Messiah descend; Er and Onan's wasted seed feeds the Sitra Aḥra.
- **"[[jacob|Jacob]] did not die"** (Gen 49:33): death of the perfected righteous as being "gathered to the moon" (Shekhinah); the developed Kabbalistic eschatology of the soul. See [[zoharic-afterlife|The Soul and the Afterlife]].

For the broader interpretive system, see [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] and [[zohar|The Zohar]]. Note that **Nahmanides (Ramban)**, already listed among Genesis's commentators, embeds kabbalistic allusions ("by way of truth") in his Torah commentary, forming a bridge between the peshat tradition and the Zoharic Sod.

### The Midrash ha-Ne’lam Layer (Pritzker Vol. X, Nathan Wolski)

The [[zohar|Zohar]]’s earliest literary stratum, the *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* (“Concealed Midrash”), is presented in Pritzker Volume X (translated and annotated by [[nathan-wolski|Nathan Wolski]]). This layer, widely recognized as standing at the very origin of the Zoharic corpus, offers a more classically midrashic and “concealed” voice than the full theosophic/theurgic running commentary of Vols. I–III and later. It is primarily focused on Genesis (creation, patriarchs) and emphasizes hidden meanings, the soul, divine justice, and the inner spiritual drama.

Key characteristics and samples (per Wolski’s edition):
- **Concealed wisdom for the righteous**: “The blessed Holy One removed wisdom above—it is His Name—to convey to His righteous servants, and two remained in the world; they are its existence.” Divine wisdom (often the divine name or Hokhmah) is largely withdrawn, accessible primarily to the righteous as the sustaining power of existence.
- **Body-soul analogy**: “Like the conduct of the body with the soul, so the blessed Holy One deals with the generation.” God’s providential relationship to humanity mirrors the soul’s direction of the body — a central image for understanding hidden divine governance, reward, and the generation’s spiritual state.
- **Accountability and the afterlife**: “It would be better for us had we never come into the world, and not have to give an account to the Bookkeeper!” (Rabbi Yehudah, who then recants). The “Bookkeeper” evokes the soul’s post-mortem reckoning and divine justice — a recurring Midrash ha-Ne’lam concern with the fate of the righteous versus the wicked.
- **Creation and the word**: Discussions of creation “by a single utterance” (Ps 33:6), linking the hidden divine word to cosmic structure and the role of the righteous.

This layer complements the main Zoharic reading of Genesis (Vols. I–III) by providing an earlier, more midrashically oriented and soul-centered lens on the same narratives. It resonates with (and in some cases anticipates) later developments such as gilgul (Vol. V) and the visionary soul material in Vol. IX (Rav Metivta). Its presence underscores that the Zohar is not a monolithic theosophic system but a developing corpus with distinct strata — the “concealed midrash” at the root giving way to the mature sefirotic theurgy of the main commentary.

See [[zohar|The Zohar]] (Vol. X), [[nathan-wolski|Nathan Wolski]], and the “Kabbalistic (Zoharic) Reading of Genesis” section above for the later stratum.

## Composition and Source Criticism

The [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]] (Wellhausen) identifies four sources: J (Yahwist, c. 850 BCE), E (Elohist, c. 850 BCE), P (Priestly, c. 550–450 BCE), and D (Deuteronomist, confined to Deuteronomy). In Genesis, only J, E, and P are present. P predominates in genealogies and the covenant chapters; J in narrative. E first appears in Genesis at ch. 20.

Conservative evangelical scholarship (e.g., [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]], [[peter-craigie|Craigie]]) engages the DH while arguing that compositeness does not imply errancy, and that the canonical text's theological coherence is the primary object of interpretation.

## Ancient Near Eastern Parallels

Genesis 1–11 draws on a world of shared Ancient Near Eastern literary tradition. Key parallels:
- **Enuma Elish** (Babylonian): cosmological combat myth; contrasts with Genesis' God who creates by word, not combat
- **Atrahasis Epic**: five-part structure (creation, divine problem, flood, resolution) paralleling Gen 1–11's overall shape
- **Gilgamesh Epic** (Tablet XI): flood narrative with closest verbal parallels to Gen 6–9
- **Ugaritic El tradition**: divine name and epithets for the God of the patriarchs

See [[genesis-ancient-near-east|Genesis and the Ancient Near East]].

## Manuscript Tradition

The Masoretic Text (MT) is the standard Hebrew tradition. The Septuagint (LXX) and Samaritan Pentateuch diverge significantly in the genealogical numbers of Gen 5 and 11. The Genesis Apocryphon (Dead Sea Scrolls) provides an Aramaic retelling. Targums Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan provide Aramaic translations with interpretive expansions.
