The **Daodejing** ("Classic of the Way and Virtue") is the foundational text of [[daoism|Daoism]] and,
after the Bible, among the most translated books in the world — 81 short, gnomic, often paradoxical
chapters traditionally attributed to **[[laozi|Laozi]]**. By one count it has been translated over
two thousand times into ninety-four languages, with more than five hundred English versions alone.

## The Opening and the Whole

Its first line sets the tone of the entire tradition: **"Any course can be taken as the right course
to take, / but no course like that can be the course taken always."** (Ziporyn) The text praises the
**[[dao|Dao]]** as the nameless source; commends **[[wu-wei|wu-wei]]** (non-action / effortless
action), softness over hardness, emptiness, the "uncarved block" (*pu*), and the lowly valley and
water as images of power; and applies all of this to **sage rulership** — govern by not-governing.
The 81 chapters divide loosely: 1–37 address *dao* proper; 38–81 address *de* (virtuosity/power).

## Textual History and Manuscripts

The Daodejing is not a single-author text with a stable origin. Modern scholarship recognizes several
major manuscript families:

- **Guodian bamboo slips** (c. 4th c. BCE) — the earliest known materials; contain less than half of
  the received text in scrambled order; raise fundamental questions about whether the text was a
  composite from the start.
- **Mawangdui silk manuscripts A and B** (c. 2nd c. BCE) — contain the full text but in reversed
  order (chs. 38–81 first, then chs. 1–37). Also use *heng* for *chang* (constancy) throughout,
  except in three chapters where *chang* appears.
- **Beijing University bamboo slips** (c. 2nd c. BCE, donated 2009) — another full-text version.
- **Heshang Gong edition** (c. 2nd c. CE) — associated with the religious/longevity-cultivation reading.
- **Received / "Wang Bi" version** — the standard text, established in conjunction with Wang Bi's
  commentary (226–249 CE), though recent scholarship suggests Wang Bi was working from a slightly
  different version.

The traditional story — that Laozi (6th c. BCE), an archivist of the Zhou court, wrote the text at a
border gate as he left China for the west — is not supported by the historical record. No figure
named Laozi is attested before the *Zhuangzi* and *Shiji*. Ziporyn proposes that the Daodejing is
better understood as **"a best-of selection of exemplars of a defunct ancient genre"** — an
anthology assembled over time from a shared tradition, with a recognizable but diffuse editorial
sensibility rather than a single author's voice.

### Kirkland's Three-Editions Analysis and the Huan Yüan Hypothesis

[[russell-kirkland|Kirkland]] (*Taoism: The Enduring Tradition*, 2004) develops the composite-text
argument further, distinguishing three strata:

1. **Guodian stratum** (~300 BCE): the earliest recoverable layer; focused on biospiritual cultivation
   with minimal political content; lacks most of the famous "mother," "valley spirit," and "feminine"
   passages. This stratum is closely related to the [[nei-yeh|Nei-yeh]] tradition.

2. **Full-text stratum (Ma-wang-tui / received)**: the text we know, with the politically-oriented
   passages and the feminine imagery. Kirkland hypothesizes this redaction was done at or near the
   **Chi-hsia academy** in the state of Ch'i (~300–240 BCE) by a scholar named **Huan Yüan**. The
   academic setting explains the text's dual nature: older biospiritual wisdom combined with new
   socio-political instruction for a scholarly/aristocratic audience concerned with governance.

3. **Received / Wang Pi text** (early CE): the standard version as shaped by Wang Pi's 3rd-c.
   commentary, which pushed the text further toward philosophical metaphysics (*wu*/non-being).

**Implication**: The "Lao-tzu" as historical author is a Han invention. The famous feminine imagery
(the "valley spirit," the "mother," the idealization of "feminine" behaviors for rulers) was added
or emphasized in the full-text redaction for political-rhetorical purposes. It was NOT a central
feature of the earlier biospiritual cultivation tradition, and it never became a key feature of
the actual organized Taoist community in later centuries.

## Key Terms and Ziporyn's Translation Choices

[[brook-ziporyn|Ziporyn's]] 2022 translation (Liveright) makes several distinctive choices that
illuminate the text's logic:

- ***Dao* → "the course"**: Captures both the ordinary sense (a purposive path to a goal) and the
  ironic reversal the text performs on it — the Dao as *non*-purposive process. "Way" is more
  natural but misses the deliberate flip.
- ***De* → "virtuosity"**: Avoids the moral connotation of "virtue"; foregrounds skill, efficacy,
  and spontaneous power — the ability one has by *being* what one is, not by striving.
- ***Chang/heng* → "constancy"**: The sustainable, ever-present background that persists through
  the rise and fall of foregrounded values.
- ***Ziran* → "of themselves, all on their own"**: Spontaneity-as-self-so; what happens when nothing
  interferes.

## The A/B Structure (Ziporyn's Interpretive Key)

Ziporyn identifies the Daodejing's defining rhetorical move as **ironic inversion of value polarity**.
Conventionally valued items (A: being, having, strength, hardness, fame, knowledge, action, fullness,
the male, the high) are systematically subverted by valorizing their disvalued opposites (B: nonbeing,
lacking, weakness, softness, namelessness, ignorance, nondoing, emptiness, the female, the low). This
"A/B structure" is the consistent through-tone of the text across its diverse literary styles:

> "Reversal, opposition: the activity of the course. / Weakness, fragility: the functioning of the
> course." (Ch. 40)

The pattern appears everywhere: the usefulness of empty space (Ch. 11), water overcoming rock (Ch. 78),
the highest virtue looking like a valley (Ch. 41), knowing by not-traveling (Ch. 47).

## One Text, Two Transformative Readings

The Daodejing is the wiki's paradigm case of a single classic read in two wholly different registers
— the **commentary-as-transformation** framework ([[claude-md-hermeneutics|CLAUDE.md]]):

- **[[wang-bi|Wang Bi]]** (226–249) reads it **philosophically** (*xuanxue*): the Dao as **wu**
  (non-being / nothingness), the metaphysical root of all determinate being. The commentary trains
  the reader in metaphysical insight — seeing being grounded in non-being.
- **[[heshang-gong|Heshang Gong]]** reads it for **longevity cultivation**: its images decode the
  adept's inner body and *qi*, a practical manual for health and immortality.

See [[wang-bi-vs-heshang-gong|the controversy]]. Ziporyn's received text follows the Wang Bi version
while explicitly noting his divergences from it at each variant.

## Key Chapter Themes

- **Ch. 1**: The unnamed/unnameable source; two modes of attention (desirelessness → mystery;
  desire → the ten thousand things); the "gateway into manifold mysteries."
- **Ch. 2**: The mutual generation of opposites; the sage's "work of nondoing, teaching of
  noninstruction"; achievements complete, no credit claimed.
- **Ch. 11**: The usefulness of emptiness — hub, vessel, room; "profit lies in the having, function
  lies in the having of nothing."
- **Ch. 16**: Return to the root; the cycle of growth and return as "real constancy."
- **Ch. 25**: The Dao as what precedes heaven and earth; the four greats (course, sky, earth,
  ruler); the course conforms to *ziran* — "conforming to nothing at all."
- **Ch. 40**: The Dao's two motions — reversal and weakness.
- **Ch. 42**: Cosmogony — "the course generates continuities; continuities generate polarities;
  polarities generate triplicities; triplicities generate the ten thousand things." Yin-yang in
  every creature.
- **Ch. 67**: The three treasures — loving care (*ci*), frugality (*jian*), not-venturing-first.
- **Ch. 78**: "Nothing in the world is softer and weaker than water — yet in attacking the hard and
  rigid none can outdo it."
- **Ch. 81**: "True words are not beautiful. Beautiful words are not true."

## The Political Dimension

Chs. 17, 57, 58, 75, 80 spell out a consistent anti-interventionist politics: the best ruler is
barely known to exist; laws and prohibitions multiply thieves; small states with low populations are
the ideal. This is not anarchism exactly — the sage-ruler *does* govern — but governance consists in
removing obstacles to the people's self-ordering (*ziran*).
