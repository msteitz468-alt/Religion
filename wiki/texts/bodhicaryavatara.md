# Bodhicaryāvatāra

The **Bodhicaryāvatāra** ("Introduction to / Undertaking of the Bodhisattva's Conduct") by
**[[shantideva|Śāntideva]]** (8th c. CE) is the most beloved poetic and practical guide to the
**[[bodhisattva|bodhisattva]] path** in [[mahayana|Mahayana]] [[buddhism|Buddhism]]. A *śāstra*
(treatise) in roughly 913 verses across **ten chapters**, it combines lyrical devotion, a manual of
ethical and meditative training, and — in its ninth chapter — a rigorous statement of
**[[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]]** philosophy. It is especially central to **Tibetan** Buddhism, where it
is among the most studied of all texts; the **Dalai Lama** calls it the source of his own practice
and "the essence of all of Mahayana Buddhism."

## Structure (the Ten Chapters)

1. **Praise of the Awakening Mind** — extols **[[bodhicitta|bodhicitta]]** (the "Awakening Mind," the
   resolve to attain buddhahood for all beings); distinguishes the *Mind resolved on Awakening* from
   the *Mind proceeding toward Awakening* (1.15–16).
2. **Confession of Faults** — the *pūjā* / sevenfold worship: offering, praise, going for refuge, and
   the confession and purification of wrongdoing before the buddhas and bodhisattvas.
3. **Adopting the Awakening Mind** — the formal generation of *bodhicitta* and the bodhisattva vow.
4. **Vigilance regarding the Awakening Mind** — the seriousness of the vow once taken; the rarity and
   preciousness of the "leisure and endowment" of a human life.
5. **The Guarding of Awareness** — guarding the mind through mindfulness (*smṛti*) and introspective
   awareness (*samprajanya*); the famous image that to cover the earth in leather is impossible, but
   to cover one's own feet (i.e. guard one's own mind) suffices.
6. **The Perfection of Forbearance (*kṣānti*)** — the great treatment of **patience and anger**: anger
   destroys merit; the "enemy" is the indispensable teacher of patience and so an object of gratitude;
   a determinist argument against blame.
7. **The Perfection of Vigour (*vīrya*)** — energy/effort against spiritual sloth and despondency.
8. **The Perfection of Meditative Absorption (*dhyāna*)** — solitude and meditation, culminating in
   the two transformative meditations: the **equalizing of self and other** (*parātmasamatā*) and the
   **[[exchange-of-self-and-other|exchanging of self and other]]** (*parātmaparivartana*) — the seed
   of the Tibetan *lojong* (mind-training) and *tonglen* (giving-and-taking) traditions.
9. **The Perfection of Understanding (*prajñā*)** — the philosophical core: a Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka
   exposition of **[[sunyata|emptiness]]**, the **[[two-truths|two truths]]**, and the refutation of
   the self, of really-existent dharmas, and of rival schools (the Hindu realists, the Sāṃkhya self,
   and the Yogācāra "mind-only" view).
10. **Dedication (*pariṇāmanā*)** — the transfer of all merit to the welfare of beings, ending with
    the celebrated vow: *"As long as space abides and as long as the world abides, so long may I abide,
    destroying the sufferings of the world."*

## Major Themes

- **The supremacy of *bodhicitta***: the Awakening Mind is the alchemy that transmutes the base body
  into the priceless jewel of buddhahood; the whole poem is its praise and cultivation.
- **The unity of wisdom and compassion**: the bodhisattva seeks full buddhahood as the state "most
  completely suitable for benefiting others"; "the very being of the bodhisattva is the welfare of
  others."
- **The six perfections (*pāramitās*)** structure chapters 4–9 (forbearance, vigour, meditation,
  understanding being treated at length; giving and morality woven through).

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- **The argument for altruism (chapter 8)**: since there is "no enduring entity to which the term *I*
  corresponds" (8.101–2; cf. [[anatta|nonself]]), the partiality that ranks *my* pain above others'
  has "no grounds in reality." Just as a hand helps the foot though they are different, so a person
  should serve others though they are different bodies (8.91 ff.). "All who suffer in the world do so
  because of their desire for their own happiness; all who are happy do so because of their desire for
  the happiness of others" (8.129).
- **Chapter 9 and emptiness**: Śāntideva states the two truths and argues that **all things, including
  emptiness itself, lack inherent existence** — "if we think of ultimate reality as an Absolute… we
  need to let go of that grasping as well" (the [[sunyata#emptiness-of-emptiness|emptiness of
  emptiness]]). This places the text squarely in the [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjunian]]
  [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] lineage and makes it the standard Tibetan textbook for studying the
  Prāsaṅgika view.
- **Two recensions**: a shorter Dūnhuáng recension and the longer "canonical" (Vaidya) recension
  differ in length and chapter division — a text-critical crux the translators flag.

## Hermeneutics

A devotional-meditative *and* philosophical text: chapters 1–8 train the heart and conduct, chapter 9
trains the view. It operates on the **[[two-truths|two truths]]** and the
**[[neyartha-nitartha|definitive]]** emptiness teaching, read through the [[bodhisattva|bodhisattva]]
ideal and *upāya*.
