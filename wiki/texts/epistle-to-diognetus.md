# Epistle to Diognetus

## Textual Overview

*The Epistle to Diognetus* (*Diogn.*) is, in J. B. Lightfoot's famous description, "the noblest of early Christian writings." It is unique among the Apostolic Fathers in being addressed not to fellow believers but to an **outsider** — a Greco-Roman intellectual named Diognetus (probably a fictional figure or a composite questioner) who wants to understand the Christian religion. It is thus a Christian apology in epistolary form, not a pastoral letter.

**The text is anonymous**: The author, date, and even the identity of the addressee are unknown. Hippolytus of Rome, Theophilus of Antioch, Polycarp of Smyrna, and Pantaenus of Alexandria have all been proposed as authors, without sufficient evidence for any of them. The document's style and concerns anticipate later Alexandrian writers (Clement, Origen) but cannot be securely assigned.

**Date**: Most scholars propose 150–225 CE; some favor an earlier date (before 150). The document must predate the earliest witnesses to its existence, which are not numerous.

**Text — the most fragile in the Apostolic Fathers**: The *Epistle to Diognetus* survived into the modern period in a **single manuscript** — Codex Argentoratensis Graecus ix, a 13th-or-14th-century manuscript held in Strasbourg. In 1870, during the Siege of Strasbourg in the Franco-Prussian War, the library where the manuscript was held was shelled and burned. **The manuscript was destroyed.** The text survives today only because competent scholars had made printed editions and handwritten copies of the manuscript before its destruction. The text we read is reconstructed from those pre-fire copies.

**Two-document problem**: There is a clear break in the text between sections 10 and 11 (the copyist of the original Strasbourg manuscript noted the gap). Sections 11–12 are almost certainly a **different document** — probably a homily or sermon — accidentally bound with *Diognetus* when the medieval manuscript was assembled. The differences in vocabulary, tone, and subject matter are marked: sections 1–10 are an elegant apology; sections 11–12 are a more homiletic meditation on the Logos and Christian wisdom.

## Structure and Content: Sections 1–10

The author addresses three questions that Diognetus has raised (1.1):
1. What God do Christians believe in and how do they worship?
2. What is the nature of their love for one another?
3. Why did this new way of life appear "now and not before"?

### Sections 2–4: What Christians Are *Not*

**Section 2 (Folly of pagan idolatry)**: Christians do not worship the Greco-Roman gods because those gods are material objects — stone, bronze, wood, iron — made by craftsmen, subject to theft and decay. "Is not one of them stone, like that upon which we walk, and another bronze, no better than the utensils that have been forged for our use?" The argument is the standard Jewish and Christian anti-idol polemic, but made with rhetorical elegance.

**Sections 3–4 (Jewish worship also inadequate)**: Jews rightly worship one God, "but insofar as they offer this worship to him in the same way as those already described, they are altogether mistaken." Specifically: (a) animal sacrifice — God created everything and needs nothing from us; offering sacrifices to the One who provides all things is folly; (b) food distinctions and sabbath observance — treating some of God's creation as unclean and watching stars to regulate time is "superstition"; (c) circumcision — a "mutilation of the flesh."

This passage (chs. 3–4) is the most polemically anti-Jewish section of the Apostolic Fathers outside of [[epistle-of-barnabas|Barnabas]]. It does not accuse Jews of idolatry (as Barnabas does) but of a misunderstanding of God's nature.

### Section 5: The Famous "Third Race" Passage

The most celebrated passage in the document. The author describes Christians' social existence:

> "Christians are not distinguished from the rest of humanity by country, language, or custom. For nowhere do they live in cities of their own, nor do they speak some unusual dialect, nor do they practice an eccentric way of life... While they live in both Greek and barbarian cities, as each one's lot was cast, and follow the local customs in dress and food and other aspects of life, at the same time they demonstrate the remarkable and admittedly unusual character of their own citizenship." (5.1–4)

The key paradoxes:
- "They live in their own countries, but only as nonresidents; they participate in everything as citizens, and endure everything as foreigners. Every foreign country is their fatherland, and every fatherland is foreign." (5.5)
- "They marry like everyone else, and have children, but they do not expose their offspring." (5.6)
- "They are in the flesh, but they do not live according to the flesh. They live on earth, but their citizenship is in heaven." (5.8–9)
- "They love everyone, and by everyone they are persecuted." (5.11)
- "They are poor, yet they make many rich; they are in need of everything, yet they abound in everything." (5.13)

This is the "third race" or *tertium genus* idea — Christians are neither Greeks nor Jews but a new humanity defined not by ethnicity or territory but by a mode of being in the world.

### Section 6: The Soul-Body Analogy

The most philosophically elegant passage:

> "In a word, what the soul is to the body, Christians are to the world. The soul is dispersed through all the members of the body, and Christians throughout the cities of the world... The soul dwells in the body, but is not of the body; likewise Christians dwell in the world, but are not of the world." (6.1–3)

The analogy is extended through six parallel points: as the soul is invisible, as the flesh hates the soul, as the soul is imprisoned in the body — so Christians are unseen, hated, and detained. Yet as the soul holds the body together, Christians hold the world together.

### Sections 7–8: The Incarnation — God Sent His Son

The author answers the third question (why now?): God sent his Son not as "an angel or ruler or one who manages earthly matters" but as "the Designer and Creator of the universe himself." And he sent him in gentleness, not tyranny:

> "He sent him as a king might send his own son; he sent him as God; he sent him as a human to humans. When he sent him, he did so as one who saves by persuasion, not compulsion — for compulsion is no attribute of God." (7.4)

The Incarnation is thus the decisive revelation: before Christ, humanity lived in sin, not because God was indifferent but because he was "patient" — building up a case, allowing human lawlessness to accumulate so that salvation's true cost and value would be apparent. "He himself took on the burden of our lawlessness, he himself gave up his own Son as a ransom for us." (9.2)

### Sections 9–10: Divine Initiative and Human Incapacity

Humanity cannot save itself; God's righteousness must come from outside. The scheme of salvation is entirely divine initiative — God's love preceded and prompted human response. The community of the saved receives and shares in the divine life.

## Sections 11–12: The Appended Homily

Almost certainly a different document. The content is a meditation on the Logos as the "Word" who was "from the beginning... always new," on the church as the "Paradise" planted by God, and on the Christian teacher as a dispenser of the Logos. The tone is homiletic; the style more elaborate. Several scholars have proposed Hippolytus, Polycarp's successor presbyter, or Pantaenus as the author — without decisive evidence.

## Major Themes

**Christian identity as "third race"**: Neither Greek nor Jew; defined not by ethnicity, territory, or observance, but by a distinctive mode of being in the world — present everywhere, belonging nowhere, citizens of heaven.

**Apologetic against both paganism and Judaism**: Unusually, *Diognetus* criticizes both pagan idolatry and Jewish observance as inadequate approaches to God. The author positions Christianity as transcending both traditions.

**The Incarnation as persuasion**: A highly distinctive Christology — God does not coerce. The Son was sent "as one who saves by persuasion, not compulsion." This emphasis on divine gentleness and the non-coercive character of the Gospel is unusual among apologetic texts.

**The "soul in the body" cosmology**: Christians function as the spiritual animating principle of secular society, invisible but essential.

**Apophatic theology**: God cannot be seen, named, or comprehended by any creaturely power. He "revealed himself through faith, which is the only means by which one is permitted to see God" (8.6). This anticipates the Alexandrian negative theology of Clement and Origen.

## Hermeneutical Framework

*Diognetus* operates in the mode of **Greek apologetic rhetoric**, addressing a cultured pagan audience. It draws on:
- **Platonizing categories**: Soul-body distinction; the invisible God; reason and persuasion vs. coercion
- **Jewish apologetic tradition**: The anti-idol polemic (compare Wis. Sol. 13–15; Philo)
- **Distinctively Christian material**: The Incarnation, the cross, and salvation by divine initiative

It does not cite the OT directly and makes no use of allegorical exegesis. It addresses its Greco-Roman audience on Greco-Roman terms.

## The Fragment of Quadratus

The Holmes edition includes with *Diognetus* the sole surviving fragment of **Quadratus of Athens** (or of Asia Minor), one of the earliest Christian apologists. Eusebius (*Church History* 4.3.1–2) reports that Quadratus composed an apology during the reign of Hadrian (117–138 CE). Only one paragraph survives:

> "The works of our Savior were always present, for they were real: those who were healed and those who were raised from the dead, who were seen not only when he was healing them or raising them, but were also always present. And they remained alive not only while the Lord was alive, but also for some time after his departure, so that some of them even survived to our own times."

**Significance**: This fragment — probably ca. 120–130 CE — explicitly claims that some of those healed or raised by Jesus were **still alive** when Quadratus wrote. This places the ministry of Jesus within living memory ca. 120–130 CE, consistent with a date of Jesus' ministry in the late 20s–early 30s CE.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Secs. 11–12 as a separate document**: The evidence for the break is strong (textual lacuna noted by the copyist; difference in style, vocabulary, and subject matter). But some scholars still read the whole as a unity (though a minority view).

**The anti-Jewish sections (chs. 3–4)**: Do these reflect actual polemical engagement with Jewish communities, or are "Jews" a literary foil (as in Barnabas) for internal Christian identity-construction? The author seems to address a pagan audience, not Jews — the anti-Jewish content may be part of defining Christianity as "neither of these two."

**Christology in sec. 7**: The sending of the Son described as a king sending his son — does the analogy imply subordinationism (a lesser sent by a greater) or is it deliberately chosen to emphasize the *dignity* of the gift? The context clearly intends the latter.

**The silence of antiquity**: No ancient writer before the manuscript's re-discovery is known to have cited *Diognetus*. This is extraordinary — if it is indeed "the noblest of early Christian writings," why was it not more widely copied or referenced? The most plausible explanation: it was only locally preserved, probably in one institutional library, and was thus unknown to the wider church.

## Canon and Reception

An apologetic treatise rather than a canon candidate, Diognetus was **never proposed for
scriptural status**; its value is as a 2nd–3rd century [[proto-orthodox-christianity|proto-orthodox]]
witness (hence `authoritative_for`, not `canonical_for`). Its transmission was famously fragile — the
sole surviving manuscript was **destroyed in the 1870 Strasbourg fire** — so its reception rests
entirely on early modern copies.
