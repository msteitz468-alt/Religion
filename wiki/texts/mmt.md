# Miqsat Ma'ase Ha-Torah (MMT)
**Hebrew**: מקצת מעשי התורה — "Some Works of the Law" (lit. "A Part of the Deeds of the Torah")
**Sigla**: 4Q394–399 (4QMMTa–f)

A **halakhic letter** (legal document in epistolary form) addressed apparently to the Jerusalem leadership, presenting the Qumran community's distinctive legal rulings and calling for reform. MMT is among the oldest of the Qumran sectarian compositions and provides the clearest evidence of the community's **origins in a halakhic dispute** with the Jerusalem Temple establishment.

## Canon and Reception
Purely sectarian; no standing in any other tradition. Its scholarly significance is immense: it names legal disputes that illuminate the origins of the Essene/Pharisee/Sadducee distinctions, and its phrase "works of the Law" (*ma'asay ha-Torah*) is the same phrase Paul uses polemically in Galatians and Romans.

## Structure

### Calendar (Section A)
MMT opens with a **calendar section** presenting the Qumran solar calendar (364-day year), in which festivals fall on fixed days of the week. This is not merely a preference but a matter of priestly legitimacy: priests serving at the wrong times on the lunar calendar are illegitimate, and the offerings they make are invalid. The calendar dispute is foundational to the sect's separation from the Temple.

### Legal Rulings (Section B)
The bulk of MMT: approximately **20+ specific halakhic disputes** covering Temple purity, offerings, priestly marriage, and community boundaries. Examples:
- **Grain offerings**: Gentile grain may not be brought to the altar
- **Priestly garments**: Must be of specific quality and purity
- **Dogs in Jerusalem**: Dogs may not enter Jerusalem because they might eat sacrificial bones
- **Fruit of the fourth year**: Must be eaten in Jerusalem, not in the towns
- **Niece marriage**: Forbidden (also in the Damascus Document; permitted in rabbinic law)
- **Impurity of the blind and deaf**: Blind and deaf may not enter the Temple precincts
- **Red heifer rite**: Specific rulings about the performer's status
- **Corpse impurity**: Extended periods and stringent conditions

Several of MMT's positions align with **Sadducean** halakha as reported in the Talmud — leading some scholars to propose the community's origins in a proto-Sadducaean priestly group. Vermes cautiously resists this: he considers the positions priestly halakha shared with later Sadducees, not evidence of Sadducaean identity.

### Exhortation (Section C)
A closing exhortation calling on the addressee to study "the Book of Moses and the Books of the Prophets and of David" — the earliest Qumran reference to a **tripartite canon** (Torah, Prophets, Writings [= David's Psalms]). The exhortation promises blessing for obedience and judgment for disobedience, invoking the Deuteronomic blessing/curse pattern.

The exhortation contains the significant phrase: "It shall be reckoned to you as righteousness (*tsedaqah*) when you do what is right and good before Him" — drawn from Deuteronomy 6:25 but applied to halakhic compliance. The reckoning-of-righteousness language (*ḥashabu lo li-tsedaqah*) is the same language used of Abraham's faith in Genesis 15:6 and cited by Paul in Romans 4.

## "Works of the Law"
The MMT phrase *ma'asay ha-Torah* ("works of the Law") is precisely the phrase Paul uses polemically in Galatians 2:16 ("a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ"). Before MMT's publication, "works of the law" was sometimes thought to be Paul's invention or a vague reference. MMT demonstrates it was a specific technical halakhic phrase used by the Qumran community to describe its distinctive legal rulings — the specific acts required by the community's interpretation of Torah.

This does not resolve the debate about Paul's meaning, but it contextualizes it: Paul is apparently arguing against a halakhic framework in which specific community-defining legal observances confer righteous standing before God.

## Hermeneutical Framework

MMT is the clearest surviving specimen of **sectarian halakhic reasoning** — legal derivation from Torah applied to disputed cult practice. Vermes: "MMT is particularly important as a source of ancient legal debate... it is unique among the Dead Sea Scrolls and foreshadows the halakhic process developed and practised by later rabbis." He aligns several of its rulings with Sadducee positions preserved in the Mishnah, placing MMT inside the same disputation genre later systematized by the rabbis.

**Legal derivation by analogy.** The characteristic move is to quote a Torah verse and map it onto contemporary institutional geography. On Leviticus 7's slaughter law: "[And concern]ing that which is written, [If a man slaughters in the camp or slaughters] outside the camp an ox or a lamb or a goat... And we consider the Sanctuary [as the tent of meeting and Je]rusale[m] as... the camp." The wilderness camp of Leviticus is *read as* Jerusalem — an interpretive identification that generates the practical ruling.

**Explicit canon reference.** MMT names a tripartite scriptural corpus as the authority for reading history: "you should understand {the Book of Moses} and the Book[s of the Pr]ophets and Davi[d and all the events] of every age."

**Contemporizing covenant theology.** Deuteronomy 30's blessing-and-curse structure is quoted and then historicized and applied forward: the blessings "came... in the days of Solomon," the curses "in the days of Jeroboam... until Jerusalem and Zedekiah king of Judah were exiled," and then — "we recognize that some of the blessings and curses which are written in the B[ook of Mo]ses have come. And this is at the end of days." The community reads itself as standing at the covenant cycle's final turn. This is contemporizing exegesis, though not the formal lemma-plus-interpretation structure of [[pesher|pesher]].

**Paradigmatic use of biblical figures.** Scriptural history also functions exhortatively: "Remember David, that he was a man of piety, and that he was also saved from many troubles and pardoned."

## Significance
1. **Origins of the sect**: MMT reflects the community in a formative phase, still addressing the Jerusalem establishment as potential reformers, not yet as a permanent separatist group
2. **Tripartite canon**: The earliest Qumran reference to Torah + Prophets + "David" as three divisions of authoritative scripture
3. "Works of the Law" background for Pauline controversy
4. Alignment with Sadducaean halakha — raises questions about the relationship between Qumran and the Sadducees
