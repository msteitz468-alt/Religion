## Overview

The term *agama* (Sanskrit: "coming," signifying doctrine transmitted through a lineage of authoritative teachers) is the nearest Jain equivalent to "scripture." Each [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] preaches, and his disciples interpret the utterances — "the Worthy One (*araha*) enunciates the meaning, then the disciples form the sacred text (*sutta*), and then the sacred text proceeds for the good of the doctrine" (*AvNiry* 92; line 643–644). For [[svetambara|Svetambaras]], Mahavira's utterances were in [[ardhamagadhi|Ardhamagadhi]], a sacred Prakrit understood as the language of heaven, automatically transformed into all listeners' languages (*Aup* p. 178).

The scriptural tradition as a whole is called the "twelve-limbed basket of the disciples" (*duvalasangaganipidaga*), though its twelfth limb (*Drstivada*) is accepted as lost by all Jains. The Svetambara canon represents what survived through three councils of recitation (*vacana*).

The Jain view of scripture is not to be equated with western notions of a fixed written canon. *Agama* can denote both the formal corpus and "any text of a degree of antiquity which is accepted as having been written by an illustrious teacher" (lines 708–710). "Practical canons" — graded textual curricula used by ascetics and laity — span from ancient Ardhamagadhi texts through medieval Sanskrit and Prakrit works and constitute the real working scripture of most Jains (lines 734–754).

## Canon and Reception

The Svetambara canon was fixed in manuscript form at the **Council of Valabhi** (first half of the 5th century CE) under Devarddhi Ganin, to prevent complete loss through oral attrition. Two earlier recitations (at Pataliputra, and simultaneously at Mathura and Valabhi) had established imperfect oral versions; the Mathura version of Skandila became the authoritative text, with Nagarjuna's Valabhi version cited only as a variant (lines 1077–1108).

**The Digambaras reject the authority of the Svetambara canon** entirely, on the grounds that (a) the authentic agama was already entirely lost before the Council of Valabhi, and (b) the editorial process at Valabhi was a human confection not representing authentic transmission. Digambara enumeration of the *anga* texts is identical to the Svetambara list, but this represents "an idea rather than a reality" — the Digambaras hold they no longer survive (lines 1505–1513). See [[digambara]].

**The standard forty-five-text list** is strictly that of the **Svetambara Murtipujaka Sangha** ("Image-Worshipping Assembly"). The aniconic [[sthanakvasi]] and Terapanthi Svetambara sub-sects accept only a **thirty-two-text list**, omitting thirteen texts including the controversial *Mahanisitha* (fifth Chedasutra, written in Maharastri rather than Ardhamagadhi, containing references to goddesses and magic spells) and all ten *prakirnaka* (mixed) texts (lines 1330–1354).

The forty-five-text list was communicated to the German scholar Weber by Bühler in the nineteenth century and has prevailed in western scholarship as the normative enumeration, though Weber himself recognized other enumerations existed. The list can only be dated back to the thirteenth century or so; even the *anga/upanga* division is not attested before the twelfth century (lines 1370–1379).

The Svetambara scriptures have been compared to "an old city in which some buildings have remained exactly the same from the earliest period, some have had extensions built on to them, others have been erected on old foundations, while many have disappeared completely and are known only by name" (lines 1150–1155).

## The Forty-Five Texts

### The Twelve *Angas* (Limbs)

1. **Acara** ("Behaviour"): doctrinal statements about the soul and action, a biography of [[mahavira]], and monastic discipline. The ancient first book was shown by Schubring to have been "in origin a kind of mosaic, often consisting of mere fragments of verses and disjointed bits of prose." Its seventh chapter is last heard of in a hagiography being used as a magic spell (lines 1135–1145). See [[acaranga-sutra]].
2. **Sutrakrta** ("Relating to Heretical Views"): doctrinal material including accounts of opponents' views, discussions of karma, Mahavira, ascetic recommendations, the evils of women, and hells.
3. **Sthana** ("Possibilities"): encyclopaedic lists of doctrine, practice, mythology, and cosmology grouped in clusters of one to ten.
4. **Samavaya** ("Combinations"): similar to *Sthana*; contains descriptions of the contents of the *angas*.
5. **Vyakhyaprajnapti** ("Exposition of Explanations"): the largest text of the canon, containing material on the entire range of Jain doctrine and ascetic practice. The fourth Svetambara *upanga*, the *Prajinapana*, was at some point incorporated into this text, and there are strong similarities between it and the Digambara *Satkhandagama* (lines 1488–1489).
6. **Jnatadharmakathah** ("Stories of Knowledge and Righteousness"): moral narratives; its eighth chapter gives the story of Malli, claimed by Svetambaras to have been a female fordmaker.
7. **Upasakadasah** ("Ten Chapters on Lay Attenders"): stories of advanced laymen and their fortitude against demonic attack.
8. **Antakrddasah** ("Ten Chapters on Endmakers"): stories of those who ended rebirth.
9. **Anuttaraupapatikadasah** ("Ten Chapters about the Arisers in the Highest Heavens"): stories of those reborn as gods.
10. **Prasnavyakaranani** ("Questions and Explanations"): doctrinal discussions. The contents of this text, "which must have been finally redacted at Valabhi, are completely different from their description elsewhere in the scriptures" (lines 1147–1149).
11. **Vipakasruta** ("The Scripture about Ripening"): stories of karma's results.
12. **Drstivada** ("Disputation about Views"): accepted as lost from an early date. This text was supposed to have contained the [[purvas|fourteen Purvas]], the most ancient and authoritative layer of the scriptural tradition, now entirely gone. It seems originally to have contained "large-scale expositions of views (*drsti*) which the Jains considered heretical, and the danger inherent in such a work, at least for the weak monks living in the corrupt age, may have led to its eventual suppression and disappearance" (lines 993–998).

### The Twelve *Upangas* (Subsidiary Limbs)

1. **Aupapatika** ("Spontaneously Arising"): descriptions of gods and hellbeings born spontaneously; elaborates Mahavira's preaching and the mechanism of liberation.
2. **Rajaprasniya** ("Relating to the Questions of King Prasenajit"): discussion between King Prasenajit and the monk Kesin (a follower of Parsva) on the soul-body relationship.
3. **Jivajivabhigama** ("Understanding of the Animate and Inanimate"): ontological matters.
4. **Prajnapana** ("Enunciation"): systematic account of doctrinal matters; at some point incorporated into the fifth *anga*.
5–7. **Three cosmographical works** (*Suryaprajnapti*, *Jambidvipaprajnapti*, *Candraprajnapti*): the first (sun) and third (moon) are virtually identical; the *Jambidvipaprajnapti* includes biographical material about Rsabha and his son Bharata.
8–12. **Five short narrative texts** dealing with the results of actions.

### The Seven *Chedasutras* (on Ascetic Regulation)

1. **Dasasrutaskandha** (Prakrit: *Ayaradasao*): contains as its eighth chapter the **Kalpasuta** (sometimes called *Paryusanakalpa*). See [[kalpasutra]].
2. **Kalpasuta**: a short text on monastic law (distinct from the Kalpasuta above; same name, different text).
3. **Vyavahara**: rules for monks and nuns.
4. **Nisitha**: atonements for breaches of ascetic behaviour; said to have originated as part of the first *anga*.
5. **Mahanisitha**: disparate material including ascetic rules and narratives; written in Maharastri rather than Ardhamagadhi, contains references to goddesses and magic spells; **controversial** — rejected by Sthanakvasis and Terapanthis, accepted by the Murtipujaka Sangha.
6. **Pancakalpa**: the basic text is now regarded as lost and "may never have existed" (line 1284).
7. **Jitakalpa**: prescribes atonements for breaches of conduct; written by Jinabhadra in the seventh century CE.

### The Four *Mulasutras* (Root/Base Scriptures)

Probably so called because they must be mastered at the outset of an ascetic's career:

1. **Uttaradhyayana** ("Later Chapters"): thirty-six chapters, nearly all in verse (some very old), on legend, practice, and doctrine. Points to nakedness as distinguishing Mahavira's monks from Parsva's (*UttS* 23).
2. **Dasavaikali ka** ("Ten Evening Treatises"): concise treatment of correct ascetic behaviour; ascribed to the monk Sayyambhava as a summary of scripture for his son.
3. **Avasyaka** ("Obligatory"): formulae for the six obligatory ritual actions; the *niryukti* mnemonic verse commentaries on this text have "assumed a quasi-canonical status" (lines 1303–1305).
4. **Pindaniryukti and Oghaniryukti**: technical verse texts on begging for alms and care of the alms bowl; generally taken together.

### The Ten *Prakirnakas* (Mixed Texts)

Ten mixed texts dealing with monastic ritual, the religious death, physiology, praise of the fordmakers, astrology, and other topics. There is "no firm canonical list" of prakirnakas; the oldest citation in the *Nandi* numbers them at 14,000 in Mahavira's *tirtha* (NSH pp. 73–4). Twenty such texts currently exist in Jain tradition, and the ongoing "Jaina Agama" series has decided to publish all twenty to avoid the enumeration problem (lines 1416–1435).

### Two Hermeneutical Texts

- **Nandi** ("Auspicious"?): situates the scriptures in an epistemological context; describes the internal structure of scriptural literature and the classification into *arigapravista* (included among the *angas*) and *angabahira* (exterior to the *angas*) texts.
- **Anuyogadvarani** ("Doors of Disquisition"): epistemological framework for scriptural study; reverses the *Nandi*'s classification on the *avasyaka/kalika* question.

## The Purvas (Lost Ancient Texts)

A body of fourteen "ancient" (*parva*) texts, the [[purvas]], were said to be the basis of the scriptural literature and were originally located in the twelfth *anga* (*Drstivada*). Both Svetambaras and Digambaras agree they disappeared — knowledge of them died out one thousand years after Mahavira's death (*Bh* 20.8). The monk Sthtulabhadra was sent to learn them from Bhadrabahu in Nepal but, after mastering ten, used his knowledge for magical purposes and was refused the full import of the last four. The Digambaras further claim that the vast *Satkhandagama* is based on what Dharasena remembered of only one of these Purvas (lines 930–1008).

## Ardhamagadhi as Scriptural Language

Ardhamagadhi, the language of the Svetambara canon, literally means "Half Magadhi" and "is peculiar to the Svetambara scriptures" (lines 1020–1024). Svetambara tradition holds it was the dialect of Mahavira's preaching and the language of heaven. Dundas notes that "in its surviving and apparently somewhat scribally normalised form [it] was certainly not the vernacular in which Mahavira himself preached" but "evolved from some underlying dialect, presumably a variety of Magadhi, into a specifically Jain scriptural dialect" (lines 1030–1035). Its maintenance against translation into Sanskrit was vigorously defended: the logician Siddhasena Divakara was expelled from the community for twelve years for proposing a Sanskrit translation (lines 1052–1062).

## Scripture as Sacred Object and Spectacle

The Svetambara scriptures function not only as intellectual texts but as sacred objects and communal spectacles:

- The **Kalpasuta** is recited at the festival of Paryusan in a "breakneck gabble" rendering the text "completely incomprehensible even to those who know Prakrit," while illuminated manuscript pages are displayed to the audience. See [[kalpasutra]] for full treatment.
- Manuscripts are treated with profound reverence; the oldest Jain manuscript libraries (Patan, Cambay, Broach, Jaisalmer) are the oldest manuscript libraries in India (lines 1628–1709).
- The **Digambara** festival of *Sruta Pancami* ("Scripture Fifth") involves taking manuscripts out, worshipping them, and recopying them.
