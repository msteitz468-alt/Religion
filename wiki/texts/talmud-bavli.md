The **Babylonian Talmud** (*Talmud Bavli*) is the central, authoritative text of Rabbinic Judaism (see [[talmudic-period]]). It is a vast, dialectical compilation comprising two main components: the [[mishna|Mishna]] (the foundational legal code compiled c. 200 CE) and the [[gemara|Babylonian Gemara]] (the expansive analytical commentary on the Mishna developed by the Amoraim from c. 200–500 CE). 

Following its final redaction and sealing by the Savoraim (c. 500–600 CE), the Bavli became the definitive guide to Jewish halakha (law), theology, and ethical life. Its authority eventually eclipsed that of the Palestinian Talmud (*Talmud Yerushalmi*) across the Jewish diaspora due to its broader scope, more rigorous dialectic, and the influence of the Babylonian Geonim.

## Historical Context, Narrative, and Redaction

The development of the Talmud spans several centuries, tracing the transmission of the Oral Law from antiquity through distinct classes of scholars. According to the traditional narrative presented by Unterman, this chain of transmission began with Moses, passed through the Prophets, and reached the **Men of the Great Synod** (including **[[ezra|Ezra the Scribe]]**), who codified the earliest prayers and blessings. From there, the tradition passed to the **Zugoth** (Pairs), such as Simon ben Shetah, and eventually to the great rival houses of **[[hillel|Hillel]]** and **[[shamai|Shamai]]**.

The narrative of this transmission is rich with *Agadaic* (Aggadic) biographical traditions that illustrate the values of the sages:
- **[[hillel|Hillel]]**'s legendary devotion to Torah, demonstrated when he nearly froze to death on a snowy roof just to listen to the academy's lectures.
- **[[shamai|Shamai]]**'s contrasting strictness and rigidity, which formed the dialectical foil to Hillel's leniency.
- **[[akiba|Rabbi Akiba]]**'s rise from an illiterate shepherd to the greatest sage of his generation, culminating in his tragic martyrdom during the Bar Kokhba revolt.

The historical redaction phases are categorized by the classes of scholars:
- **Tannaim (c. 10–220 CE):** The sages whose views are recorded in the Mishna, Tosefta, and Halakhic Midrashim. Their work culminated with **[[judah-hanasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi]]** redacting the Mishna.
- **Amoraim (c. 220–500 CE):** The scholars of the Babylonian academies (Sura, Pumbedita, Nehardea), notably led by foundational figures like Abba Areca (Rav). They analyzed the Mishna, reconciling contradictions, identifying scriptural sources, and deriving new rulings.
- **Savoraim (c. 500–600 CE):** The later editors who finalized the text, added standard terminology, standardized the dialectical flow, and gave the Gemara its current literary form.
- **Geonim (c. 600–1000 CE):** The heads of the Babylonian academies who cemented the Bavli's absolute authority across the diaspora.

The traditional view holds that the primary redaction of the Babylonian Gemara was overseen by Rav Ashi and Ravina (c. 4th-5th century), though modern scholarship recognizes significant editorial work continuing into the 6th century.

## Structure: Orders and Tractates

The Talmud is organized into **6 Orders** (*Sedarim*, abbreviated *Shas* - *Shisha Sedarim*) and **63 Tractates** (*Masekhtot*). The Babylonian Gemara covers 37 of these 63 tractates, focusing primarily on areas of law that remained practically applicable in the diaspora or related to universal civil/ritual law.

## Page Layout: Tzurat HaDaf

The standard pagination used globally today was established by Daniel Bomberg's Venice edition (1520–1523) and finalized in the 19th-century Vilna Edition (the *Vilna Shas*). A Talmud citation is given by Tractate and folio (e.g., *Berakhot* 2a).

The classic page layout comprises:
- **Center:** The text of the Mishna and Gemara.
- **Inner Margin:** The commentary of [[rashi|Rashi]] (11th century), offering clear, localized explanations of the text.
- **Outer Margin:** The commentary of the [[tosafot|Tosafot]] (12th–14th centuries), a dialectical, cross-referencing analysis largely by Rashi's descendants.
- **Margins:** Cross-reference tools such as *Ein Mishpat Ner Mitzvah* (linking Talmudic rulings to the codes of [[maimonides|Maimonides]] and [[joseph-caro|Caro]]), *Mesoret HaShas* (internal Talmudic cross-references), and glosses from later sages like Rabbeinu Chananel or the Maharshal.

## Methodology: Shakla V'Tarya

The Talmud is not a static legal code but a recording of dynamic, intergenerational debates. Its methodology is called *Shakla V'Tarya* (give and take). It uses [[talmudic-hermeneutics|Talmudic Hermeneutics]] and the [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot]] to analyze texts. 

A typical Talmudic discussion moves through:
1. **Meimra / Baraita:** A foundational statement by an Amora or a Tanna.
2. **Kushya:** A difficulty raised against the statement (e.g., a contradictory source or logical flaw).
3. **Havah Amina:** An initial assumption or hypothesis.
4. **Teirutz:** A resolution or refutation.
5. **Maskana:** The final legal conclusion.

This dialectical rigor later evolved into the highly complex analytical method known as [[pilpul|Pilpul]] in European Yeshivas.

## Halacha and Aggadah

The Talmud contains two distinct but intertwined streams of thought:
- **[[halacha|Halacha]]:** The strictly legal, normative material (civil, criminal, ritual).
- **[[aggadah|Aggadah]]:** Non-legal material, including folklore, theology, exegesis, medical advice, and biographical anecdotes about the sages.

While Halacha binds Jewish practice, the authority of Aggadah is debated. Some traditions view Aggadic narratives as literal and historically authoritative, while rationalist thinkers (like Maimonides) often treated them as allegories concealing deep philosophical or moral truths.

## The Sasanian Iranian Context

The Bavli is not only a legal-literary universe but a **document of Sasanian Iran**. Produced by the
Amoraim of the Babylonian academies (Sura, Pumbedita, Maḥoza, Nehardea) in a Mesopotamia that was a
breadbasket and administrative center of the **Persian (Sasanian) Empire**, it took shape amid
**[[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrians]]**, Eastern Christians, Manichaeans, and Mandaeans. [[shai-secunda|Shai
Secunda]]'s [[secunda-the-iranian-talmud|*The Iranian Talmud*]] (building on **Yaakov Elman**) argues —
in the comparative subfield **[[talmudo-iranica|Talmudo-Iranica]]** — that the Bavli must be read against
this world, just as the [[talmud-yerushalmi|Yerushalmi]] and Midrash are read against Greco-Roman
Palestine. Symptoms of the Iranian milieu include:

- **Acculturation as a rabbinic fault line** — Rav Naḥman of Maḥoza (near the capital Ctesiphon) is coded
  as upper-class Sasanian (Iranian loanwords *atrunga*/*anbaga*, citrons and unmixed wine, his daughter
  **Dēnag**), against the "resister" Rav Yehuda of provincial Pumbedita.
- **Law shaped by Sasanian categories** — e.g. Rav Naḥman's "returnable gift" of the four species
  parallels **temporary ownership** in the Sasanian law book *Mādayān ī Hazār Dādestān*.
- **Iranian loanwords, calques, and topoi** in Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, sometimes displacing entrenched
  Hebrew terms.
- **Discourses of the [[magi|magi]]** — the Bavli both vilifies Zoroastrian priests (sorcerers,
  "mumblers," "destroying angels") and, in a concurrent set of texts, mirrors them against the rabbis
  themselves (see [[magi|The Magi]]).
- **Shared imagery** — e.g. the spies' hellish punishment at b. Soṭa 35a reshaped on the template of the
  Middle Persian *Ardā Wirāz nāmag* (see
  [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|the influence page]]).

## Key Commentators

- [[rashi|Rashi]] (1040–1105) — The indispensable primer for reading the text.
- **The Tosafists** — Dialectical analysts who synthesized the entire Talmudic corpus.
- [[maimonides|Maimonides]] (1135–1204) — Abstracted the Talmud into a systematic halakhic code (*Mishneh Torah*).
- [[joseph-caro|Joseph Caro]] (1488–1575) — Compiled the *Shulchan Aruch*, the definitive code based heavily on Talmudic rulings.
