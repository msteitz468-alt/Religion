# Temptation of Jesus (Matthew 4:1-11)

## Textual Overview

Matt 4:1-11 narrates Jesus' forty days in the wilderness and a three-part exchange with the
devil, each of Jesus' replies quoting Deuteronomy. [[rt-france|R. T. France]] (*The Gospel of
Matthew*, NICNT, 2007) titles the section "The testing of the Son of God," and endorses B.
Gerhardsson's monograph title *The Testing of God's Son* as summing up the pericope's thrust
"admirably," calling the study illuminating.

The pericope closes France's long preamble (1:1–4:11) and immediately precedes the withdrawal to
[[galilee|Galilee]] at 4:12 and the proclamation at 4:17 which, on France's structural reading,
begins the main story.

On the narrative's origin France is careful and hedged: the three-point dialogue's details, "if
they are not purely imaginary, can only have come from Jesus' own subsequent recalling of the
event."

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

**Testing rather than tempting.** France's controlling lexical point is that *peirazō* covers
both. The pericope, he argues, is "primarily concerned with this divine 'testing' rather than the
Satanic 'tempting' which was its means," grounding this in Deut 8:2, where God tests Israel to
find what was in their heart. On [[satan|the devil]] he correspondingly rejects the reading — later
made familiar by monastic tradition — that the desert is the devil's native domain, noting that
12:43-45 makes waterless places the last place a demon wants to be: "the devil is present not
because this is his domain, but because he has a vital role in the testing."

**Filial trust, not messianic strategy.** France insists the conditional "if you are the Son of
God" does not cast doubt on the filial relationship but explores its implications — how might
God's Son exploit the relationship to his own advantage. "The devil is trying to drive a wedge
between the newly declared Son and his Father."

**The three tests.**

1. *Stones to loaves.* France hears an echo of Israel's wilderness hunger met by manna picked up
   from the ground like stones (Exod 16:14-16): "history shows that there is no need for God's son
   to be hungry in the wilderness." He reads the reply from Deut 8:3 as teaching priority, not
   that God's word fills the stomach: Jesus understood his hunger as God's will and therefore not
   to be evaded by self-indulgent use of his power — "to do that would be to call in question
   God's priorities, and to set himself at odds with his Father's plan." France stresses there is
   no hint here of miraculous provision for others or of impressing crowds: "It is Jesus' filial
   trust that is under examination, not his messianic agenda."
2. *The temple pinnacle.* France judges it "not very important" to identify the *pterygion*; the
   word is not used elsewhere of a building feature, and "the context makes it clear only that it
   is a high part of the temple from which a fall might be expected to be fatal" — the sanctuary
   (about 50 m) or the portico over the Kidron valley. The devil deploys Jesus' own formula "It is
   written," quoting Ps 91:11-12 in an abbreviated LXX form and arguing a fortiori from those who
   shelter in the Most High to God's special Son: "if Jesus can quote Scripture, so can the
   devil." France's exegetical objection is that the psalm promises protection amid hazards but
   "does not suggest that they should take the initiative in courting such dangers"; the devil
   proposes creating a situation obliging God to act. He quotes Schweizer that thereby "man may
   become lord of God, and compel him to act through the power of his faith," and Keener that it
   would be "to act as if God is there to serve his Son, rather than the reverse." Jesus' reply
   sets Scripture against Scripture — his "again" indicating a countertext — "not because he
   disputes the validity of God's promise in Ps 91:11-12 but because he rejects the devil's use of
   it."
3. *The kingdoms of the world.* France notes that the mention of the kingdoms' "glory" confirms
   that "what the devil is offering is not just a sphere for service, but paramount status" as
   king of kings, contrasting with the single [[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]] Jesus will
   proclaim. The omission of "if you are the Son of God" this time signals the change of tone,
   "since the clause would be blatantly at odds with a proposal to abandon allegiance to God."
   Universal dominion is itself an OT hope (Pss 2:8; 72:8-11; Dan 7:13-14; Zech 9:10), but the
   route via prostration is unacceptable: "it is an offer of the right end by the wrong means" —
   and France questions whether even the end is right when expressed as paramount glory rather
   than self-sacrificing servanthood.

**The dismissal and the angels.** France notes *hypage* is usually positive in Matthew but here,
and at 16:23, carries a sharp tone with the vocative "Satan" ("Enemy") — literally appropriate
here, wounding when applied to [[peter-apostle|Peter]]: "Jesus is not just terminating the
interview: he is sending his adversary packing." He reads v. 11 as the [[angels|angels]]
providing "the sustenance he refused in v. 4 to commandeer for himself," fulfilling the
protective role of the devil's own Ps 91:11 quotation, and compares [[elijah|Elijah]] (1 Kgs
19:4-8), noting that the food there came at the beginning of the forty days, not the end. France
observes that Matthew, unlike Luke, "does not say… that his withdrawal was temporary," though the
narrative contains many further demonic encounters without another confrontation with the chief
demon (cf. 12:28-29); see [[exorcism|exorcism]].

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

The ingested source supplies no textual-critical apparatus for this pericope; the conversion of
the commentary used for this ingest dropped all footnote bodies, where such discussion would
normally sit. This section is therefore left thin rather than filled from elsewhere.

France does treat the form of the OT citations. The three replies are from Deut 8:3; 6:16; 6:13.
On the last he notes two divergences from the LXX — "worship" for "fear," and the added "only" —
"the first clarifying in light of the devil's demand, the second brings into the quotation what
immediately follows in Deut 6:14," the prohibition of other gods. The devil's citation of Ps
91:11-12 is, he notes, in an abbreviated LXX form. On Deut 6:16 France identifies the Massah
reference as a Hebrew wordplay Greek cannot capture, pointing to Exod 17:1-7, where Israel's
demand for water amounted to the cynical challenge "Is the LORD among us or not?" — "that same
cynical challenge would be implied if Jesus were to force God into a physical rescue."

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

France's reading is governed by [[typology|typology]] of a specific and controlled kind. He names
the three [[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]] quotations "the most significant key" to the story, noting
that "all come from Deut 6–8," Moses' recollection of the forty wilderness years as an educative
testing, with Israel as God's son (Exod 4:22; Jer 31:9; Hos 11:1-4). His central formulation is
that another Son of God is in the wilderness, forty days rather than forty years, facing the same
tests — and that "he has learned the lessons which Israel had so imperfectly grasped." France
contrasts Israel's flawed occupation of the land with the new Son who will not fail, so that the
new exodus alluded to in ch. 2 will succeed, quoting: "Where Israel of old stumbled and fell,
Christ the new Israel stood firm." He thinks it probably significant that the Deuteronomy passage
begins with the Shema (Deut 6:4-5), "since it is precisely that total commitment to God that this
wilderness experience is designed to test." His summary verdict: the pericope is "an elaborate
typological presentation of Jesus as himself the true Israel, the 'Son of God'" (see
[[israelites|Israelites]]).

On the forty days France allows possible allusions to [[moses|Moses]] on Sinai or to
[[elijah|Elijah]] in the wilderness — the latter suggestive since angels fed Elijah, cf. v. 11 —
but judges Israel's forty years "more obviously" the background.

He also traces Matthean links forward to the passion: the crowd's "if you are the Son of God" at
27:40, the refused legions of angels at 26:53 (cf. 4:6), and "Away with you, Satan" redeployed
against Peter at 16:23 — "some features of Matthew's wording link these two episodes at the
beginning and end of his story." The third temptation he links to 28:18: authority comes not from
kowtowing to Satan but from obedient suffering, and it will be authority in heaven as well as
earth, "an authority which the devil was not able to offer."

France adds a discipline about application: Matthew gives no means of knowing whether the fast was
self-imposed or forced by scarcity, and Jesus' fasting is not presented as a model for followers —
"this is an experience unique to the Son of God at the outset of his mission." This limits the
devotional-imitative reading of the pericope; see [[discipleship|discipleship]].

## Canon and Reception

Matt 4:1-11 is canonical within the Gospel of Matthew for the Christian traditions holding the
New Testament canon, and no communal dispute over its standing is recorded in the ingested
source. France's engagement with reception is limited but pointed: he rejects the later monastic
reading of the wilderness as the devil's domain, and rejects the popular modern reading of the
episode as a debate over messianic strategy (below). Beyond these, the ingest supplies no
patristic, liturgical, or devotional reception history, and none is supplied here.

On the figure of the devil France notes that the same character appears as devil, tempter, Satan,
later Beelzebul (12:24) and "the evil one" (13:19); the figure is rare in the OT (1 Chr 21:1; Job
1–2; Zech 3:1-2) but by the first century "had developed (under a variety of names: Belial,
Beliar, Mastema, Azazel, but most commonly Satan) into a standard feature of Jewish belief" which
the church shared. He identifies a tension in Jewish references between total hostility to God and
operation within God's ultimate sovereignty — "a tension which Matthew here reflects" by
subsuming the devil's tempting under God's testing.

## Scholarship and Historiography

- **The order of the temptations.** France weighs both: Luke's order climaxes with the devil's own
  scriptural citation and with [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]; Matthew's escalates geographically and
  ends with the devil dropping his disguise and a decisive dismissal anticipating 28:18. He
  reports that "the majority of recent interpreters think that Matthew's order… is more likely to
  be original," and cites Schweizer for the phrase describing the third temptation as the point
  where the devil "drops his disguise." See [[synoptic-problem|synoptic problem]].
- **Was the devil's offer a bluff?** France answers his own question: NT language ("ruler of this
  world," and Satan's own "kingship" at 12:26) means he is understood to have real power in the
  present age, though always under God's ultimate victory — "he can offer power and glory, but not
  ultimate fulfillment, still less an authority in accordance with the will of God."
- **Physical or visionary transportation?** France argues the transportations were visionary, not
  physical, since no actual mountain could show all the world's kingdoms at once, comparing
  Ezekiel's visionary visit to Jerusalem from Babylon (Ezek 8:1-3; 11:24).
- **Named scholars.** The digested material records France engaging Gerhardsson (endorsed),
  Schweizer, and Keener. The dropped footnote bodies mean no fuller bibliography can be recovered
  from this ingest.

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **Testing or tempting?** France subsumes the Satanic tempting under divine testing; this
   governs everything else in his reading.
2. **Filial trust versus messianic strategy — France's sharpest rejection.** He firmly rejects the
   popular reading that Jesus' messianic strategy is under scrutiny: the loaves are for Jesus' own
   hunger, there are no spectators for the temple leap, and the third temptation appeals to
   personal ambition with no messianic agenda mentioned. "This understanding of the story leaves
   little room for the popular notion that what is under scrutiny here is the nature of Jesus'
   messianic agenda." Recorded as France's position against a widely held alternative; the wiki
   does not adjudicate.
3. **The source of the dialogue.** France's hedged formulation — "if they are not purely
   imaginary, can only have come from Jesus' own subsequent recalling of the event" — leaves the
   historical question formally open.
4. **The identity of the *pterygion*.** France declines to settle it and judges it unimportant.
5. **The order of the three temptations.** Matthean versus Lucan priority; France reports the
   majority view rather than arguing the point at length.
