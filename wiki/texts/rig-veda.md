The **Rig Veda** is the oldest and most important of the four Vedas, and one of the **oldest religious texts
still in use** — a collection of **1,028 hymns (*suktas*)**, in ~10,600 verses across **ten books
(*mandalas*)**, addressed to the gods of [[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]]. Composed in archaic Vedic Sanskrit
and transmitted with astonishing oral precision for some three thousand years, it is **shruti** ("that which
is heard," eternal revelation) and remains canonical scripture in [[hinduism|Hinduism]].

## Content

Most hymns are **invocations and praises** sung at the **fire sacrifice**, asking the gods for cattle, sons,
victory, wealth, and long life. The gods most hymned are the storm-king **[[indra|Indra]]** (a quarter of the
hymns), the fire-god **[[agni|Agni]]**, the sacred drink **[[soma|Soma]]** (all of Mandala 9), and the
cosmic sovereign **[[varuna|Varuna]]**, with Mitra, Surya, Ushas, the Ashvins, the Maruts, and many more.

## The Great Cosmic Hymns

The late tenth mandala contains some of the most profound religious poetry of antiquity:

- The **Purusha Sukta** (10.90) — creation by the **sacrifice of the cosmic Man (Purusha)**, from whose
  dismembered body the world, the gods, and the **four social classes (varnas)** arise — the foundational
  text for the caste system.
- The **Nasadiya Sukta** (10.129), the "**Hymn of Creation**" — a breathtaking meditation that asks how
  anything came to be from the primal "neither being nor non-being," and ends in **radical doubt**: "Whence
  this creation arose… perhaps it formed itself, or perhaps not — the one who looks down on it from the
  highest heaven, only he knows — **or perhaps even he does not know.**"

## Significance

The Rig Veda is the **fountainhead of the entire Indian religious tradition** — its gods, its sacrifice, its
sacred Sanskrit, and (in the late hymns) the cosmogonic questioning that flowers in the [[upanishads|
Upanishads]] and [[hinduism|Vedanta]]. Its Indo-European pantheon also links it to the Greek, Roman, and
Iranian gods (see [[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]] and [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]).

## Doniger's Reading (Modern Critical — *The Hindus*, Ch. 5)

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 5, "Humans, Animals, and Gods in the Rig
Veda, 1500–1000 BCE") offers a historical-sociological reading that foregrounds **what the hymns reveal about
Vedic social life**, rather than just their theology. Key moves:

**Oral transmission as sacred refusal.** The Vedas were shruti — "that which is heard" — and the *refusal to
write* was itself religiously charged. The sacred word must remain spoken, embodied, performed; it does not
reduce to inscription.

**Three alliances.** Doniger maps three recurring structural tensions across Vedic religion: (1) Vedic **gods
and humans** against the **antigods** (*Asuras*); (2) human **ascetics** and their accumulated tapas (heat/
power) threatening the gods themselves; (3) the **bhakti** (devotional) relation, in which devoted humans
appeal directly to a personal god. Each alliance pattern recurs across the textual corpus.

**Cattle and horses as social cipher.** The Rig Veda's world is pastoral and martial: Doniger calls the
Vedic peoples "**Indians as cowboys**," whose primary social competition was **cattle raiding** — the word
*gavisthi* ("battle, war") literally means "cow-seeking." The horse is a prestige marker of royal/Kshatriya
power (horses had to be imported; they are not native to the subcontinent). The cow is wealth and purity — the
Brahmin's animal. Doniger's "follow the horse, follow the cow" method is here in action as social history.

**The Puruṣa-sūkta as social document.** Doniger reads RV 10.90 not as pure cosmological myth but as a
**social rationalization**: the hymn was composed at a moment when the four-class system (brahmin, warrior,
peasant, servant) was already a lived fact, and the sacrificial origin story was brought in to *naturalize*
it. See [[varnas|Varnas]].

**Women in the Rig Veda.** The picture is mixed. On one side: the hymn of **Apala** (RV 8.91) — a woman
with a skin disease who presses soma for Indra and receives healing in return, in a relationship Doniger
reads as eroticized, Indra here responsive to a woman's direct approach. Magical spells against **rival
wives** show women with agency in domestic competition. On the other: widows' status is ambiguous (the widow
lies beside the corpse in a hymn, but is then "called back" to the living — sacrificial participation fading
at the edges). Some late hymns hint at reincarnation through imagery of death and return.

**Henotheism / kathenotheism confirmed.** [[henotheism|Doniger confirms Max Müller's observation]]: each god
is addressed as supreme in turn — not because the worshipper believes in only one god, but because each
approached deity receives, *in that moment*, the whole force of devotion. Serial, situational monotheism
rather than true polytheism.
