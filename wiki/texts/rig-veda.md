The **Rig Veda** is the oldest and most important of the four Vedas, and one of the **oldest religious texts
still in use** — a collection of **1,028 hymns (*suktas*)**, in ~10,600 verses across **ten books
(*mandalas*)**, addressed to the gods of [[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]]. Composed in archaic Vedic Sanskrit
and transmitted with astonishing oral precision for some three thousand years, it is **shruti** ("that which
is heard," eternal revelation) and remains canonical scripture in [[hinduism|Hinduism]].

## Content

Most hymns are **invocations and praises** sung at the **fire sacrifice**, asking the gods for cattle, sons,
victory, wealth, and long life. The gods most hymned are the storm-king **[[indra|Indra]]** (a quarter of the
hymns), the fire-god **[[agni|Agni]]**, the sacred drink **[[soma|Soma]]** (all of Mandala 9), and the
cosmic sovereign **[[varuna|Varuna]]**, with Mitra, Surya, Ushas, the Ashvins, the Maruts, and many more.

## The Great Cosmic Hymns

The late tenth mandala contains some of the most profound religious poetry of antiquity:

- The **Purusha Sukta** (10.90) — creation by the **sacrifice of the cosmic Man (Purusha)**, from whose
  dismembered body the world, the gods, and the **four social classes (varnas)** arise — the foundational
  text for the caste system.
- The **Nasadiya Sukta** (10.129), the "**Hymn of Creation**" — a breathtaking meditation that asks how
  anything came to be from the primal "neither being nor non-being," and ends in **radical doubt**: "Whence
  this creation arose… perhaps it formed itself, or perhaps not — the one who looks down on it from the
  highest heaven, only he knows — **or perhaps even he does not know.**"

## Significance

The Rig Veda is the **fountainhead of the entire Indian religious tradition** — its gods, its sacrifice, its
sacred Sanskrit, and (in the late hymns) the cosmogonic questioning that flowers in the [[upanishads|
Upanishads]] and [[hinduism|Vedanta]]. Its Indo-European pantheon also links it to the Greek, Roman, and
Iranian gods (see [[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]] and [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]).
