# Second Apology of Justin Martyr

A shorter companion to the [[first-apology-justin|First Apology]], addressed "to the Roman Senate." Written c. 160 CE by [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr]] (c. 100–165 CE) in Greek. Most scholars consider it either an appendix to the First Apology or a separate composition prompted by specific recent events of Christian persecution. Translation: Philip Schaff (*Apostolic Fathers with Justin Martyr and Irenaeus*, Eerdmans, repr. 2001). 15 chapters; shorter and more personal than the First Apology.

## Canon and Reception

Not canonical scripture; treated as authoritative patristic literature in Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant traditions. Important primarily as evidence for Christian self-understanding and the *logos spermatikos* theology in mid-2nd century Rome.

## Overview and Structure

### 1. Introduction and the Case of Ptolemaeus (Chs I–II)

Justin opens by citing recent persecutions under Urbicus, the city prefect, as the occasion for writing. He narrates the "case of Ptolemaeus" in detail: A Christian woman converted from an intemperate lifestyle and attempted to reform her husband. Failing, she divorced him. The husband, unable to prosecute her (she had petitioned the emperor for time to settle her affairs), had her Christian teacher **Ptolemaeus** arrested and interrogated solely on whether he was a Christian. When Ptolemaeus confessed, Urbicus condemned him. A bystander, **Lucius**, protested the injustice publicly and was also condemned. A third unnamed person was likewise condemned. Justin's point: the condemnation was solely for the name "Christian," not for any demonstrated wrongdoing — a reductio of Roman judicial procedure.

### 2. Justin and Crescens (Ch. III)

Justin anticipates his own death at the hands of **Crescens**, a Cynic philosopher who publicly denounced Christians in Rome. Justin challenges Crescens' credibility: either Crescens has not read Christian teachings (in which case he is "thoroughly depraved"), or he has read them and does not understand them, or he understands them and denounces Christians out of fear of public association. Justin claims to have cross-examined Crescens and found "he knows nothing." The passage is evidence of public philosophical debate between Christians and pagans in Rome c. 160 CE.

### 3. Why Christians Do Not Commit Suicide (Ch. IV)

Addressing the implied objection ("if you want to die and be with God, why not kill yourselves?"), Justin argues: God made the world for the human race and takes pleasure in those who imitate his virtues; suicide would deprive the world of teachers and worshippers of God, acting contrary to God's will. Christians confess openly, not to court death, but because they "count it impious not to speak the truth in all things."

### 4. Angels, Demons, and the Fall (Ch. V)

Justin provides his demonology most fully here: God appointed angels over the care of humanity and the created order. "The angels transgressed this appointment, and were captivated by love of women, and begat children who are those that are called demons" (alluding to Gen 6:1–4 / *1 Enoch*). The demons then enslaved humanity through "magical writings, and fears and punishments, and by teaching them to offer sacrifices, and incense, and libations." From this demonic seduction came murders, wars, adulteries, and "all wickedness" — and the pagan myths ascribed these actions to gods because the mythmakers did not know they were attributable to angels and their offspring. See [[fallen-angels|Fallen Angels]] and [[1-enoch|1 Enoch]].

### 5. Names of God and of Christ (Ch. VI)

"To the Father of all, who is unbegotten, there is no name given. For by whatever name He be called, He has as His elder the person who gives Him the name." The titles Father, God, Creator, Lord, Master "are not names, but appellations derived from His good deeds and functions." Christ is called *Christos* "in reference to His being anointed and God's ordering all things through Him." The name "Jesus" (*Iēsous* = Saviour) has meaning as man's name. This is a significant statement on divine naming and ineffability.

### 6. World Preserved for Christians; Free Will (Ch. VII)

God delays the final conflagration and destruction of the world "because of the seed of the Christians, who know that they are the cause of preservation in nature." The world would have been destroyed already were it not for Christians' presence. This differs from Stoic conflagration doctrine: for Justin, God the Creator is superior to created things and is not "resolved into fire." Free will is again asserted against Stoic determinism: "it is by free choice they both walk uprightly and stumble." Socrates, Musonius, and Heraclitus suffered persecution for living "according to the Logos" — showing that demons specifically target those who live by reason.

### 7. Logos Spermatikos — The Seed of the Word in All Men (Chs VIII, X, XIII)

The most sustained development of the *logos spermatikos* doctrine:

**Ch. VIII**: "Those of the Stoic school... were admirable... on account of the seed of reason [the Logos] implanted in every race of men." Heraclitus, Musonius, and others were persecuted like Christians because they lived by the Word. The demons hate all who live by the whole Word more than they hate those who live by a fragment.

**Ch. X**: "Our doctrines, then, appear to be greater than all human teaching; because Christ, who appeared for our sakes, became the whole rational being, both body, and reason, and soul. For whatever either lawgivers or philosophers uttered well, they elaborated by finding and contemplating some part of the Word. But since they did not know the whole of the Word, which is Christ, they often contradicted themselves." Socrates knew Christ "partially" — "for He was and is the Word who is in every man, and who foretold the things that were to come to pass both through the prophets and in His own person." Socrates died for the Logos; Christ is the fullness of the Logos who died for all.

**Ch. XIII**: "For each man spoke well in proportion to the share he had of the spermatic word... Whatever things were rightly said among all men, are the property of us Christians." Plato, Stoics, poets, and historians each grasped fragments. "The seed and imitation impacted according to capacity is one thing, and quite another is the thing itself, of which there is the participation and imitation according to the grace which is from Him."

### 8. Death as Freedom and Conclusion (Chs XI, XII, XIV–XV)

Justin invokes the allegory of Hercules at the crossroads (from Xenophon) — the choice between Vice and Virtue — as an analogy for the Christian contempt of death (Ch. XI). Christians proved innocent by their willingness to die: no person given to sensual pleasure would court death (Ch. XII). Justin closes by requesting the work be published and restating the claim that Christian doctrines "are not shameful, according to a sober judgment, but are indeed more lofty than all human philosophy" (Ch. XV).

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Logos Spermatikos as apologetic strategy**: Justin's claim that all truth belongs to Christians because all partial truths derive from the implanted Word is both an inclusive move (honoring pagan wisdom) and an exclusive one (subordinating it to Christ as the Logos in full). It avoids dismissing pagan philosophy while asserting Christian superiority. See [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]].

**The demonic origin of pagan religion**: Justin consistently explains pagan analogies to Christianity — and pagan persecution of Christians — through demonic agency. This produces a coherent if circular apologetic: pagan myths imitate prophecy (demons heard the prophets), pagan worship enslaves (demons instituted it), and pagan persecution is demonic (demons manipulate rulers). The framework appears in both Apologies and the *Dialogue with Trypho*.

**Angelic fall and demonology (Ch. V)**: Justin's use of the *1 Enoch* tradition (angels lusting after women, begetting demons) as a standard explanation for the origin of demonic power shows that this tradition was theologically functional in mid-2nd century proto-orthodox Christianity, even as it was beginning to be questioned in some circles.

## Hermeneutical Framework

**Philosophical-rational**: More philosophical in tone than the First Apology, with greater emphasis on Socrates as a proto-Christian who knew Christ "partially" through the Logos. Justin reads the history of Greek philosophy through the lens of progressive partial participation in the Logos, with Christ as the culminating fullness.

**Apologetic**: Specific incidents (the Ptolemaeus case) are deployed rhetorically to indict Roman justice. Justin's appeal to reason, justice, and philosophical consistency is the primary rhetorical mode throughout.
