# Paradiso (*Divine Comedy*, Cantica 3)

The third and final cantica of [[divine-comedy|the *Commedia*]] (33 cantos): the
ascent of the character [[dante-alighieri|Dante]], guided by [[beatrice|Beatrice]]
(and, at the end, by [[bernard-of-clairvaux|St Bernard]]), through the nine
heavenly spheres to the Empyrean and the final vision of God. Read here in Robin
Kirkpatrick's translation with his per-canto notes; Kirkpatrick stresses the
Paradiso is **collaborative and experimental**, not a series of magisterial
expositions, and still awaits full appreciation.

## Structure: The Ascent

Dante rises through the Ptolemaic spheres, each displaying a category of the
blessed — though a key doctrine (Canto 4) is that **all the blessed actually dwell
in the Empyrean**; their appearance in lower spheres is a pedagogical accommodation
to human cognition. The universe is "nothing more or less than a metaphor (like
Dante's text itself)."

| Sphere | The blessed shown | Key figures |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Moon | Those who broke vows | [[piccarda-donati|Piccarda Donati]], Empress Constance |
| Mercury | The ambitious / doers of justice for fame | [[justinian|Justinian]] (the Roman Eagle, Atonement) |
| Venus | Lovers | Charles Martel, Cunizza, Folco, Rahab |
| Sun | The wise (theologians) | [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] (life of Francis), Bonaventure (life of Dominic), Solomon |
| Mars | Warriors of the faith | [[cacciaguida|Cacciaguida]] (exile prophecy; poem's commission) |
| Jupiter | Just rulers (the Eagle) | David, Trajan, Ripheus (the virtuous pagans saved) |
| Saturn | Contemplatives | Peter Damian, St Benedict |
| Fixed Stars | The Church Triumphant | Christ & Mary; examinations by Peter (faith), James (hope), John (love); Adam |
| Primum Mobile | The angelic orders | the point of light; the nine choirs |
| Empyrean | All the blessed (the Celestial Rose) | [[bernard-of-clairvaux|St Bernard]]; the Virgin; the final vision |

## Selected Cantos and Figures

- **Cantos 1–5 (Moon).** The proem and *trasumanar* ("transhumanation," beyond
  language); Beatrice's cosmology of natural ascent. **[[piccarda-donati|Piccarda
  Donati]]** and the Empress Constance, forced from their vows — the doctrine of the
  **absolute vs. conditioned will** ("In His volition is the peace we have," 3:85)
  and of **free will as God's greatest gift** (Canto 5).
- **Cantos 6–7 (Mercury).** **[[justinian|Justinian]]**'s sweeping discourse on the
  **Roman Eagle** as the providential instrument of universal justice (Canto 6),
  then Beatrice's exposition of the **Atonement** on Anselmian *Cur Deus Homo*
  lines: the Cross was just (punishing fallen nature) and unjust (an infinite Person
  bore it); God chose both amnesty and satisfaction by becoming man (Canto 7).
- **Cantos 8–9 (Venus).** **Charles Martel** on providence working through nature —
  hereditary diversity is willed by God but misallocated by human institutions
  ("you… make a king of someone who should preach," 8:147); Cunizza, the
  troubadour-bishop Folco of Marseilles, and **Rahab**, type of the saved gentile.
- **Cantos 10–14 (Sun).** Two rings of wise spirits. **[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]**
  (a Dominican) narrates the life of **St Francis** and Lady Poverty, then condemns
  his own order's decline (Canto 11); **Bonaventure** (a Franciscan) reciprocally
  narrates **St Dominic** and attacks Franciscan corruption (Canto 12) — a
  deliberate cross-praise. Aquinas on **Solomon**'s kingly (practical) wisdom and
  against rash judgment (Canto 13); Solomon's hymn to the resurrection body (14).
- **Cantos 15–17 (Mars).** The cross of warrior-saints; **[[cacciaguida|Cacciaguida]]**,
  Dante's crusader great-great-grandfather, who paints an idealized austere old
  [[florence|Florence]] against the corrupt present, **prophesies Dante's exile**
  ("You'll leave behind you all you hold most dear… you will taste the saltiness of
  bread / when offered by another's hand," 17:55–60), and **commissions the poem**
  ("make plain what in your vision you have seen, / and let them scratch wherever
  they may itch," 17:127–129).
- **Cantos 18–20 (Jupiter).** The souls of the just spell *DILIGITE IUSTITIAM* and
  form an **Eagle** that speaks as one. The Eagle on the **inscrutability of divine
  justice** and the problem of the **virtuous pagan** ("A man is born beside the
  Indus… unbaptized… Where is the justice that condemns him?", 19:70–78); resolved
  not by universalism but by sovereign grace in the extraordinary cases of **Trajan**
  (returned to life by Gregory's prayers) and **Ripheus** of Troy (granted
  pre-Christian faith). "Predestination! How remote your root…" (20:130).
- **Cantos 21–22 (Saturn).** The contemplatives on Jacob's golden ladder; **Peter
  Damian** and **St Benedict**, with fierce denunciations of fat prelates and
  degenerate monks — "the white original turned dark" (22:93). Beatrice no longer
  smiles (it would consume Dante); ascent to the Fixed Stars and the view back at
  the "little threshing floor" of earth.
- **Cantos 23–26 (Fixed Stars).** The **Triumph of Christ and the Virgin Mary**,
  crowned by Gabriel (Canto 23). Dante's examination in the three theological
  virtues: **St Peter on faith** (Hebrews 11:1; escaping the circular-argument trap
  by the miracle of the world's conversion, Canto 24), **St James on hope** (Peter
  Lombard's definition, Canto 25), **St John on charity** (Canto 26) — John correcting
  the legend of his own bodily assumption. **Adam** answers four questions, revising
  Dante's own earlier theory: language and the names of God change naturally; Babel
  is not the origin of diversity.
- **Canto 27 (Primum Mobile).** **St Peter's** furious denunciation of the usurping
  papacy — "He who on earth has robbed me of my place… has made / of my own burial
  ground a shit hole" ([[catholicism|Boniface VIII]] as Satan's tool); Beatrice on
  the Primum Mobile, where time "takes root," and on cupidity.
- **Cantos 28–29.** God as an infinitely intense **point of light** ringed by the
  nine angelic orders (Dionysius's hierarchy preferred to Gregory's); **beatitude as
  sight first, then love** (Dante siding with Aquinas/the Dominicans, 28:109–11);
  Beatrice on simultaneous creation, Lucifer's near-instant fall through pride, and
  grace preceding merit; satire of "poppycockery" preachers (Canto 29).
- **Cantos 30–33 (Empyrean).** Ascent to "pure light of intellect, all love"; the
  **river of light** becomes the **Celestial Rose** of the blessed (the empty throne
  awaiting Henry VII; Clement V and Boniface condemned). [[beatrice|Beatrice]]
  returns to her throne and **[[bernard-of-clairvaux|St Bernard]]** becomes the final
  guide (Canto 31), surveying the Rose and the order of the saints — predestination
  and infant salvation by **grace alone** (Canto 32). Bernard's **Hymn to the Virgin**
  ("Virgin and mother, daughter of your son," 33:1) and her intercession win Dante
  the **final vision of God**: creation gathered "in a single book," the **three
  circles of the Trinity**, and the **human image** in the second circle (the
  Incarnation), which defeats intellect "as some geometer" fails to square the
  circle — resolved not by argument but by a flash of grace, ending with the will
  harmonized by "the love that moves the sun and other stars" (33:145).

## Theological Notes

- **Light metaphysics.** The whole cantica is structured by divine light
  "penetrat[ing] the universe and… reflected more in one part, less elsewhere"
  (1:1–3).
- **The [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]]** and its **ineffability** — the
  recurring topos that the vision exceeds memory and language — culminate in Canto 33.
- **Divine justice, predestination, the virtuous pagan.** The Jupiter cantos
  (18–20) and Canto 32 insist that justice and election derive wholly from God and
  exceed creaturely comprehension; salvation requires faith in Christ, yet sovereign
  grace can reach the righteous outsider (Rahab, Trajan, Ripheus).
- **Free will.** Affirmed as "the greatest gift that God… made" (5:19), and (Canto
  22) reconciled with astral influence: freedom is the willing alignment of the self
  with its providential nature, not autonomy.
- **Ecclesial corruption.** Denounced repeatedly — the papacy (Cantos 18, 27, 30),
  the friars (11–12), and the monks (21–22) — placed pointedly at the threshold of
  the divine.
