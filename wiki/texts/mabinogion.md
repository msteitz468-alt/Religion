# The Mabinogion

The **Mabinogion** is the collection of **eleven medieval Welsh prose tales** that is the principal repository
of **Brythonic (Welsh) [[celtic-religion|Celtic]] mythology**. The name was popularized by **Lady Charlotte
Guest**, whose 19th-century translation (the wiki's source) first made the tales widely known in English. They
are not scripture but **heroic and mythic romance**, in which the old British gods survive as kings, queens,
and enchanters.

## Contents and structure

The heart of the collection is the **Four Branches of the Mabinogi**, the most mythologically loaded portion:

1. **Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed** — [[pwyll|Pwyll]] exchanges places with **[[arawn|Arawn]]**, king of
   **[[annwn|Annwn]]** (the Otherworld); he wins **[[rhiannon|Rhiannon]]**, who rides an uncatchable white
   horse and is later falsely condemned for killing her vanished son **Pryderi**.
2. **Branwen daughter of Llŷr** — the giant-king **[[bran-the-blessed|Brân]]** marries his sister
   **[[branwen|Branwen]]** to the king of Ireland; her humiliation triggers a war of mutual annihilation, the
   **cauldron of rebirth**, and Brân's severed, still-speaking **prophetic head**.
3. **Manawydan son of Llŷr** — the enchantment that empties Dyfed, and its lifting.
4. **Math son of Mathonwy** — **Math** and **Gwydion** conjure the flower-bride **Blodeuwedd** for
   **Lleu Llaw Gyffes** (the Welsh **[[lugh|Lugh]]**), whose fated, almost-impossible death and resurrection as
   an eagle form the climax.

Surrounding these are the **native tale "Culhwch and Olwen"** (the earliest Arthurian story, with its great
list of Arthur's warriors and the hunt for the boar Twrch Trwyth), **"The Dream of Rhonabwy,"** and the
three **Arthurian romances** (Owain, Peredur, Geraint) that parallel Chrétien de Troyes.

## Mythological cruxes

- the identity of the **family of Dôn** (Gwydion, Arianrhod, Math, Gofannon) as euhemerized deities cognate to
  the Irish **[[the-tuatha-de-danann|Tuatha Dé Danann]]** (Dôn ≈ Danu);
- **[[rhiannon|Rhiannon]]** as a **sovereignty / horse-goddess** (≈ Gaulish Epona, "Great Queen" Rigantona);
- the **cauldron of rebirth** as an ancestor of the **Grail**;
- **Brân's talismanic head** and the Otherworld feast as a Celtic afterlife motif.

## Manuscript tradition

The tales survive complete in two great manuscripts — the **White Book of Rhydderch** (*Llyfr Gwyn
Rhydderch*, c. 1350) and the **Red Book of Hergest** (*Llyfr Coch Hergest*, c. 1382–1410) — with fragments
earlier. The myths they preserve are far older than their parchment.
