The **Bhagavad Gita** ("Song of the Lord") is the best-known Hindu scripture — 700 verses set within
the epic *Mahabharata*, the second of the **prasthana-trayi** ([[upanishads|Upanishads]], Gita,
[[brahma-sutras|Brahma Sutras]]). Technically **smriti**, it functions as effectively canonical
scripture across [[hinduism|Hinduism]].

## The Dialogue

On the eve of battle, the warrior **Arjuna** refuses to fight kinsmen and turns to his charioteer,
**[[krishna|Krishna]]** — revealed as the supreme God in human form. Krishna's teaching synthesizes the
paths of Hindu spirituality:

- **Karma yoga** — disciplined action without attachment to its fruits (the Gita's signature ethic).
- **Jnana yoga** — the path of knowledge (the Upanishadic self/Brahman insight).
- **Bhakti yoga** — loving devotion and self-surrender to God, which the Gita exalts as supreme (ch.
  9–12; the cosmic theophany of ch. 11).

## Why Every Vedanta School Must Claim It

As part of the prasthana-trayi, the Gita is expounded by all three schools, and its very richness lets
each find itself there: [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] reads Krishna's "I am the Self in all beings" as
non-dual identity; [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]] and [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]] read its
intense theism and bhakti as a personal God really distinct from the devotee; and
[[kashmir-shaivism|Kashmir Shaivism]] ([[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]]) reads it as supreme Śaiva
non-duality "more esoteric than Vedanta." One text, [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|four incompatible
commentaries]].

## The Commentary Battleground

The Gita's interpretive history is a contest to control it, fought through *bhāṣyas*:

- **[[shankara|Śaṅkara]]'s *Gītā-bhāṣya*** ([[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]) — the oldest surviving
  commentary. It reads the Gita as a treatise on **knowledge of the actionless Self**; Śaṅkara begins
  his comment only at **2.10** (treating chapter 1 as preamble) and holds that **knowledge alone
  liberates**, with action merely purifying the mind. The Gita's devotional and active language is
  provisional teaching for the unqualified. (Wiki source: [[gambhirananda-bhagavad-gita|Gambhirananda's
  translation]].)
- **[[ramanuja|Ramanuja]]'s *Gītā-bhāṣya*** ([[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]]) — reads the
  same text as exalting **bhakti** and *prapatti* (loving surrender) to a personal God, **Krishna =
  Vishnu/Narayana**, with whom the soul attains eternal communion, never identity.
- **[[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]]'s *Gītārtha-saṃgraha*** ([[kashmir-shaivism|Kashmir Shaivism]]) —
  comments only on verses with a **hidden (esoteric) meaning**, reading the Gita's yoga and sacrifice
  language as **tantric Śaiva practice** and its goal as recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) of the Self as
  Śiva. (Wiki source: [[marjanovic-abhinavagupta-gita|Marjanovic's translation]].)

The shared crux is the relation of **knowledge and action** — see
[[jnana-karma-samuccaya|jñāna-karma-samuccaya]].

## Doniger's Reading (Modern Critical — *The Hindus*, Ch. 11)

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 11) reads the Gita primarily as a
**response to historical pressures** — Buddhism, Jainism, and the challenge of the renunciant ideal to
the householder-king ethos — rather than as a timeless philosophical text.

**The three-path synthesis.** The Gita resolves the dharma/moksha tension by forming a **triad** with
**[[bhakti|bhakti]]** as the mediating third: karma-yoga (works, sva-dharma), jñāna-yoga (knowledge,
Vedāntic moksha), and bhakti-yoga (devotion). Each adherent regards their own path as supreme; the
text is structured to accommodate all three simultaneously — hence its inexhaustibility as a commentary
target.

**"Karma without kāma."** The Gita's signature ethic: act without attachment to the results of action.
Doniger: "It means not that one should not desire certain results from one's actions, but merely that
one should not expect the results… or regard the results as the point; it's the journey that counts,
not where you end up." This "moral Teflon" allows a warrior to kill his kinsmen with a renunciant's
state of mind.

**Arjuna's quasi-Buddhist hesitation.** Arjuna's opening refusal to fight is the text's setup: he
raises something like an ahimsa objection, invoking the horror of killing kinsmen. Krishna dismantles
it not by arguing for violence but by arguing that since the soul cannot be killed, killing the body
"is not really killing." The Gita then uses Buddhist terminology (*nirvāṇa*) while insisting it is
addressing a tension *within Hinduism*, not a debate with Buddhism.

**Ahimsa in the Gita.** The Gita mentions ahimsa only in **four lists of conventional virtues**
(10.5, 13.7, 16.2, 17.14) and never discusses it as a principle. "Had the Buddha been the charioteer,
the message would have been different" (Romila Thapar, cited by Doniger).

**The cosmic theophany (Ch. 11).** When abstract argument fails to move Arjuna, Krishna shows his
"doomsday form": Arjuna sees all warriors rushing blindly into Krishna's gaping mouths "like moths
rushing to their death in a blazing fire." The vision blasts away the moral impasse rather than
resolving it. Then Arjuna apologizes for past informality ("Hey, Krishna! Hey, pal!") and begs him
to resume the familiar human form — which Krishna consents to do. "The worshiper is comforted by
the banality, the familiarity of human life."

**Political use vs. philosophical use.** In India, the Gita is generally **taken out of context** and
used for its philosophy alone — "which can be used to support arguments for peace, as, notably, in
the hands of Gandhi." The Mahābhārata as a whole is "passionately against war, vividly aware of the
tragedy of war, despite the many statements that violence is necessary."
