# Fragments of Clement of Alexandria (Delphi collection)

The minor and fragmentary works of [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]] gathered in the
Delphi edition, together with the short *Exhortation to Endurance*. Translated by William Wilson.

> **Note on contents.** This Delphi "Fragments" collection consists of the Latin *Adumbrationes*
> ("Comments") preserved via Cassiodorus, catena fragments (Nicetas on Job and Matthew, the catena on
> Luke), the **Hypotyposes** fragments (via Oecumenius, Eusebius, Moschus, Macarius), and fragments
> from *On Providence*, *On the Soul*, *On Slander*, *On Marriage*, the Passover treatise, the
> Prodigal Son homily, and other lost books. It does **not** contain the *Excerpta ex Theodoto* or the
> *Eclogae Propheticae* — Clement's main Valentinian sources, which are not part of this edition. No
> Valentinian aeon-system material (Pleroma, Sophia, Demiurge, the elect/psychic/material triad,
> Theodotus) appears in this collection.

## Exhortation to Endurance, or To the Newly Baptized

A short hortatory fragment ("Precepts of Clement") found in the Escurial, conjecturally identified with
the work Eusebius (*H.E.* vi.13) lists under this title. It is a portrait of the "true Christian
gentleman": "Cultivate quietness in word, quietness in deed, likewise in speech and gait; and avoid
impetuous eagerness" (l. 30742). The mind "seated on high on a quiet throne looking intently towards
God, must control the passions" (see [[apatheia|apatheia]]). It counsels gentle speech, modesty,
gladness in learning and teaching, submission to elders, an ascetic vegetarian-leaning diet ("Be not a
flesh-eater nor a lover of wine, when no sickness leads you to this as a cure"), trust in God for
bodily needs, and courage in suffering — closing in hope of "eternal blessedness when the Son of God
shall appear."

## Adumbrationes ("Comments" on the Catholic Epistles)

Verse-by-verse Latin glosses (via Cassiodorus) on 1 Peter, Jude, 1 John, 2 John. Doctrinally notable:
- **The soul not naturally immortal** — "the soul is not naturally immortal; but is made immortal by
  the grace of God, through faith and righteousness, and by knowledge" (l. 31118). (This stands in
  tension with a fragment *On the Soul* asserting "All souls are immortal, even those of the wicked,"
  l. 31693 — recorded as an internal contradiction in the fragment corpus.)
- An ordered angelology of "first-created" powers (protoctists).
- On Jude: affirmation of the [[1-enoch|Enoch]] prophecy and the dispute over "the body of Moses."
- Strong Logos-Christology: the Son "co-existent with the Father... eternal and uncreate" (l. 31302),
  with the famous near-docetic tradition that the Apostle John, "touching the outward body itself, sent
  his hand deep down into it, and that the solidity of the flesh offered no obstacle" (l. 31312).
- Clement's own positive [[gnosis|Gnostic]] ideal in orthodox key: the perfect man "fulfils his works
  by love" (l. 31367) — distinct from heretical Gnosticism.
- A universalism-leaning note: Christ "saves all; but some... converting them by punishments; others
  who follow voluntarily with dignity of honour."

## The Hypotyposes (Outlines) — reception-critical fragments

The most historically valuable cluster, from a lost eight-book work of running scriptural commentary,
known only through quotation:
- **Order of the Gospels** (Eusebius, *H.E.* vi.14): "the Gospels containing the genealogies [Matthew,
  Luke] were written first"; Mark wrote at the entreaty of Peter's Roman hearers; "But John, the last
  of all, seeing that what was corporeal was set forth in the Gospels... composed a spiritual Gospel"
  (l. 31664).
- **Canon scope:** Clement "has not passed over the disputed books — I mean Jude and the rest of the
  Catholic Epistles, and Barnabas, and what is called the Revelation of Peter" (l. 31621) — i.e. he
  commented on the [[epistle-of-barnabas|Epistle of Barnabas]] and the **Apocalypse of Peter** as
  scripture-tier. He held [[hebrews|Hebrews]] to be Paul's, "written in Hebrew and translated by Luke."
- **Esoteric transmission of gnosis:** "To James the Just, and John and Peter, the Lord after His
  resurrection imparted knowledge (τὴν γνῶσιν). These imparted it to the rest of the apostles, and the
  rest... to the Seventy, of whom Barnabas was one" (l. 31647) — an apostolic-succession-of-secret-
  knowledge claim relevant to [[gnosis|gnosis]] and to the lost work's later suspect reputation.
- James the Just made bishop of Jerusalem; his martyrdom anecdote; the baptism-succession of the
  apostles (Christ baptized Peter, Peter Andrew, etc.).

## Other Fragments

- **On Job** (Nicetas): "naked came I out of the womb" read as nakedness "of evil and sin," not of
  possessions — "of one possession alone, that is godliness, are we properly owners."
- **The Prodigal Son homily** (Macarius): allegorical — best robe = "the robe of immortality," ring =
  "the mystery of the Trinity," shoes = "the preparation of the Gospel of peace," fatted calf = Christ.
  Contains explicit polemic against rigorist denial of post-baptismal repentance, naming "Novatus"
  (an anachronism — Novatianist rigorism postdates Clement by ~40 years, suggesting later editing of
  this catena fragment), affirming that "whom Christ finds lost, after sin committed since baptism"
  he restores (see [[second-repentance|second repentance]]).
- Gnomic sayings on slander, repentance, and a strong anti-coercion principle: "it is not those that
  abstain from wickedness from compulsion, but those that abstain from choice, that God crowns" (see
  [[freedom|free will]]).
- A Passover fragment harmonizing the Synoptics and John on the date of the crucifixion; an
  "Against Judaizers" fragment on the Temple as Christ's body; allegorical-cosmological exegesis (the
  seven-branched candlestick = seven planets).

## Canon and Reception

Patristic fragments, non-scriptural. Their chief value is (a) Clement's canon testimony in the
*Hypotyposes* (Barnabas, the Apocalypse of Peter, Hebrews-as-Paul's, the disputed Catholic Epistles)
and (b) the heterodox-tending speculations (soul not naturally immortal; the tactile body of Christ)
that contributed to the *Hypotyposes'* later suspect reputation in Photius and others.
