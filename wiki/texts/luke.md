The Gospel of Luke is the third canonical Gospel and the longest book in the New Testament. It is the first volume of a two-part narrative — Luke-Acts — that together constitutes approximately one-quarter of the entire NT by word count. Green's NICNT commentary (1997) reads it as a carefully crafted piece of Greco-Roman historiography, organized around the salvific purpose of God and read best alongside the Acts of the Apostles as its necessary sequel.

## Textual Overview

Luke is a Greek narrative (διήγησις, 1:1) of Jesus from birth through ascension, continuous with the [[acts|Acts of the Apostles]] as volume two of one story. Green’s outline (NICNT): Prologue (1:1–4); Birth and childhood (1:5–2:52); Preparation (3:1–4:13); Galilean ministry (4:14–9:50); Travel to Jerusalem (9:51–19:48); Temple teaching (20:1–21:38); Passion (22:1–23:56); Exaltation (24:1–53). Distinctive Lukan material includes the long Travel Narrative, the birth songs, Nazareth as programmatic first sermon, and Jerusalem-centered resurrection appearances.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

The following interpretive frameworks have been applied to this text by ingested commentators:
- **Narrative Criticism & Discourse Analysis**: [[joel-green|Joel B. Green]] (NICNT 1997; fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18, [[green-luke-nicnt]]) reads the Gospel as a unified literary whole, focusing on final form, narrative sequence (co-text), and rhetorical aim rather than atomistic form or redaction criticism. Co-text / intertext (esp. LXX) / context (honor-shame, patronage, purity, kinship) structure his reading.
- **Social-Scientific Criticism**: Green employs culture-critical analysis of first-century Mediterranean scripts assumed and subverted by the Gospel.
- **Theocentric historiography**: Green classifies Luke-Acts primarily as Greco-Roman historiography undercut as pure biography by God’s primacy as actor; narration itself is proclamation.
- **Bock (Acts)**: more traditional evangelical-historical approach to volume two — methodological contrast on reliability and authorship emphasis; see [[acts]], [[darrell-bock]].

Luke opens with a formal Greek preface (1:1–4) — the most polished literary opening in the NT — in which the author identifies his work as a "narrative" (διήγησις) addressed to "most excellent Theophilus," grounds it in eyewitness tradition, and declares his purpose: to provide an "orderly account" so that Theophilus may know the certainty of the things about which he has been informed. For Green, this preface is itself proclamation — "for Luke, narration is proclamation."

## Author, Origin, and Date

**Authorship**: The gospel is formally anonymous — the author does not name himself within the text, and the title "according to Luke" was added by others. Green's commentary explicitly treats the author's identity as **irrelevant to interpretation**: the author's perspective is available to us only through the narrator's choices, and information about Luke's vocation (e.g., physician, Gentile, Pauline companion) should not be smuggled into the commentary. The "Luke" of this commentary refers to the narrator, not the historical person. The most likely historical candidate — Luke the physician and sometime companion of Paul (Phlm 24; Col 4:14; cf. 2 Tim 4:11) — remains the traditional identification, but knowing it does not illuminate the text.

**Date and provenance**: Green treats the dating question briefly, noting that the gospel was written sometime in the latter decades of the first century CE. Conzelmann's connection to the fall of Jerusalem (70 CE) as a key factor in dating and shaping the gospel is part of the redaction-critical paradigm Green deliberately moves away from.

**Theophilus**: A real person, not a symbolic name (though "Theophilus" = "lover of God" / "dear to God"). The address "most excellent" may indicate a Roman official or a person of high status. Theophilus's role seems to have been patron-dedicatee — one who would secure wider circulation for the book. He has some existing knowledge of the Christian movement but requires fuller instruction.

## Genre: Luke-Acts as Greco-Roman Historiography

Green classifies Luke-Acts as Greco-Roman historiography rather than biography, for two reasons: (1) Luke-Acts taken as a whole cannot be reduced to a life of Jesus — it is **theocentric**, focused on God's redemptive purpose rather than on any individual; (2) Acts, as the story of the early church, is not a life of any single figure. While biography (*βίος*) influenced Luke's Gospel taken alone (as France argues for Mark and Matthew), the larger two-volume unit points to the historiographical genre.

This identification carries interpretive consequences: the reader should expect a narrative concerned with causation, teleology, and the "orderly" presentation of events in service of a persuasive theological argument — "to show how the unfolding events in this world of ancient Galilee and Judea are of universal significance."

Lena Alexander's alternative identification of Luke 1:1-4 with the "scientific tradition" (technical/professional writing on medicine, mathematics, etc.) is considered and partially accepted: it captures Luke's social location (educated, urban, connected to artisan culture). But "scientific" understates the scale and ambition of the work.

## The Unity of Luke-Acts

Green's most fundamental methodological commitment: **Luke and Acts are one story, told in two physical volumes**. The division was pragmatic (each volume fitting one papyrus roll). Key evidence:

- Both volumes are of nearly equal length (Luke ~19,400 words; Acts ~18,400 words)
- Acts 1:1 explicitly refers to "the first account" as everything "Jesus began to do and to teach" — "began" implies continuation
- Both begin in Jerusalem; both have commission narratives associated with the ascension
- Luke 24 and Acts 1 overlap in content (the ascension is narrated in both)
- The parallel structure of Jesus's ministry in Luke with the apostles' ministry in Acts is deliberate — Peter reenacts Jesus; Paul reenacts Peter

The gospel is therefore **incomplete in itself**. Luke 2:32 (Simeon: "a light for revelation to the Gentiles") anticipates a Gentile mission that never materializes in the Gospel — it is realized only in Acts. Reading Luke without Acts is like reading the first half of a sentence.

Green reads the narrative as theocentric historiography rather than biography. God's purpose (expressed through δεῖ, "it is necessary") and the Holy Spirit drive the plot from beginning to end. Narration itself is proclamation: the polished Greek preface (1:1–4) presents an "orderly account" (διήγησις) so that Theophilus may know the certainty of the things he has been taught. The story is addressed to a real high-status patron but serves the wider church by showing how the events in Galilee and Judea have universal significance.

## Key Narrative Structures

Green provides dedicated literary analysis of major blocks:

- **Birth Narrative (1:5–2:52)**: Carefully structured with parallel annunciations (John and Jesus), paired songs (Magnificat, Benedictus, Gloria, Nunc Dimittis), and social location in an agrarian society of power, privilege, purity, and kinship. The temple and incense offering frame Zechariah's encounter; the songs function as theological commentary on God's reversal of the lowly and the powerful.
- **Galilean Ministry (4:14–9:50)**: Organized around Jesus' programmatic Nazareth sermon (4:16–30), which announces "good news to the poor," release, and the Jubilee year of the Lord's favor. This section establishes the theocentric character of the mission and the pattern of acceptance and rejection.
- **Travel Narrative (9:51–19:48)**: Luke's most distinctive literary creation. The long journey to Jerusalem is not mere transition but a sustained block of teaching on discipleship, possessions, prayer, and reversal. It is framed by the "setting his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51) and repeated passion predictions, building inexorable tension toward the cross.
- **Jerusalem and Passion**: The city is the goal and center. The story moves from Galilean openness to Jerusalem confrontation, yet the resurrection appearances and commission send the witnesses back out from Jerusalem.

See also: [[luke-acts-unity|Luke-Acts as a Two-Volume Work]], [[acts|Acts of the Apostles]], [[jubilee|The Jubilee]].

## Narrative Method and Social-Scientific Lens

Green employs discourse analysis (narratology combined with social-scientific criticism) rather than traditional redaction or source criticism. He reads Luke on its own terms, without constant cross-reference to Matthew or Mark, to show how the narrative "works" for its first readers. Social scripts of honor-shame, patronage, purity, and kinship are assumed and often subverted by the Gospel's presentation of God's favor toward the marginalized, women as models of faithful response, and holistic salvation that refuses any dichotomy between social and spiritual dimensions.

The **Travel Narrative** (9:51–19:48) is the most distinctive Lukan section — ten chapters with no close parallel in Mark. It begins with the programmatic phrase "he set his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51) and contains most of Luke's unique material: the Good Samaritan, the parables of the Lost (ch. 15), the Rich Man and Lazarus, Zacchaeus, and major discipleship teaching.

## The Birth Narrative (1:5–2:52)

Luke's narrative properly begins with the intertwined stories of two couples: Zechariah and Elizabeth, and Joseph and Mary. Green observes that this opening section anchors the Gospel firmly in the piety of Second Temple Judaism and the socio-political realities of first-century Roman occupation (evidenced by references to Herod, Caesar Augustus, and the census).

**Key Narrative Features**:
- **Parallel Structure**: The announcements, conceptions, and births of John and Jesus are told in deliberate parallel. However, the comparisons always elevate Jesus: John is the "Prophet of the Most High," but Jesus is the "Son of the Most High." 
- **Status Reversal**: Zechariah and Elizabeth hold high religious status but suffer the social disgrace of barrenness. Mary is an obscure peasant girl of low status. God's miraculous intervention reverses their social standing, validating the programmatic message of the *Magnificat*: God lifts up the lowly.
- **Eschatological Anticipation**: The birth narrative is saturated with the presence of the Holy Spirit and angels (Gabriel). The characters—Zechariah, Elizabeth, Mary, Simeon, and Anna—represent the faithful remnant of Israel awaiting the "consolation of Israel" and the "redemption of Jerusalem," expectations that encompass both spiritual renewal and liberation from foreign oppression.
- **The Songs (Canticles)**: The narrative is punctuated by prophetic poetry (Mary's *Magnificat*, Zechariah's *Benedictus*, Simeon's *Nunc Dimittis*). These songs interpret the unfolding events not merely as personal blessings but as the fulfillment of God's ancient covenant with Abraham.

## The Programmatic Text: The Nazareth Sermon (4:16–30)

The most important single pericope in Luke — the interpretive key to the entire gospel. Jesus reads from Isaiah in the Nazareth synagogue and declares, "Today this scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing."

**The Isaiah text (Isa 61:1–2 + 58:6)**:
> The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim release to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to let the oppressed go free, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor.

Green's five observations:

1. **"The poor" are those of low-status honor** — not merely the economically destitute but anyone relegated to the margins of the community: the sick, the unclean, sinners, women, Samaritans, Gentiles, tax collectors. The first-century Mediterranean definition of "poor" encompasses social marginalization in all its forms.

2. **"Release" (ἄφεσις) is the governing term**, deliberately repeated twice (Luke combined Isa 61 with Isa 58:6 to achieve this). "Release" operates at three levels in Luke: (a) forgiveness of sins (release from guilt/debt); (b) liberation from diabolic bondage (all healings and exorcisms are acts of release); (c) Jubilee — the cancellation of economic debts and the return of land (Lev 25).

3. **"Year of the Lord's favor" = the Jubilee epoch** (see [[jubilee|The Jubilee / Year of the Lord's Favor]]). Isaiah 61 is a prophetic interpretation of Leviticus 25's Jubilee legislation in eschatological terms. Jesus announces that the eschatological Jubilee has arrived. The Qumran text 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) provides a close parallel: it combines Lev 25, Deut 15, and Isa 61 to describe an eschatological deliverer who inaugurates the final release — for Qumran it is Melchizedek, for Luke it is Jesus.

4. **"Today"** — Luke's characteristic eschatological marker. The kingdom of God is not merely future; in Jesus's presence, the age of salvation has been inaugurated. This "today" recurs at 19:9 (Zacchaeus: "Today salvation has come to this house"), 23:43 (to the penitent thief: "Today you will be with me in Paradise"), and throughout Acts.

5. **The Elijah/Elisha examples (4:25–27)** reveal that the beneficiaries of Jesus's mission are not restricted to Israel. Elijah was sent to a Gentile widow in Sidon; Elisha healed the Syrian leper Naaman. The people of Nazareth understand the implication — God's favor extends to outsiders — and respond with murderous rage. Jesus's escape from the cliff prefigures his resurrection ("passing through the midst of them").

## Theology: God as the Primary Agent

Green's central hermeneutical claim: **Luke's narrative is theocentric, not biographical**. God is the hidden primary character whose purpose drives every scene. This is expressed through:

- **δεῖ ("it is necessary / must")** — a recurring Lukan word for divine necessity: "I must proclaim good news to other cities also" (4:43); "the Son of Man must suffer" (9:22); "Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things?" (24:26). This is Luke's language for an unalterable divine plan.
- **The Holy Spirit** — the agent who puts the divine will into effect. The Spirit descends at Jesus's baptism (3:22), drives him into the wilderness (4:1), empowers his ministry (4:14, 18), and fills the disciples at Pentecost (Acts 2). The Spirit is behind every decisive turn in Luke-Acts.
- **Scripture fulfillment** — events are narrated as fulfillment of the divine plan visible in the OT. "The events that have been fulfilled among us" (1:1).
- **The Magnificat as theological thesis statement** (1:46–55): "He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty." This is not metaphor but program — a preview of every reversal the gospel will enact.

## Salvation: Holistic, Present, Embodied

Green argues that Luke's salvation is not privatized or eschatologically deferred. It encompasses:

- **Physical healing**: sickness is not merely biological but a form of social exclusion; healing restores persons to community
- **Social restoration**: tax collectors, sinners, women, Samaritans are not healed and then sent back to the margins — they are incorporated into the community of disciples
- **Economic dimensions**: the Jubilee motif; Green's treatment of possessions (see below)
- **Liberation from diabolic powers**: every exorcism is an act of the kingdom against the rule of Satan
- **Forgiveness**: release from guilt understood as restoration to the community of God's people, not merely pardon

"The Third Evangelist knows nothing of such dichotomies as those sometimes drawn between social and spiritual or individual and communal."

## Possessions and Wealth

A persistent Lukan theme, treated inconsistently (Johnson: "Luke speaks about possessions consistently but not consistently about possessions"). Green's synthesis:

1. **Wealth is not inherently evil, but it cannot be neutral**: "Wealth masters if it is not mastered." The drive for social prestige through wealth is the specific danger — it is the "rule of Money" that oppresses.
2. **Wealth and social relations are inseparable**: To share wealth is to treat others as kin; to withhold is to treat them as outsiders. But Jesus's mission is directed precisely to outsiders.
3. **Giving must not create social obligation**: Luke's ethic explicitly disrupts the patron-client dynamic — "lend, expecting nothing in return" (6:35). The kingdom economy overturns the Mediterranean gift/reciprocity system.

Key texts: Zacchaeus's restitution (19:1–10); Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31); the Widow's offering (21:1–4); Levi leaving his tax booth (5:27–28); the women who fund Jesus's ministry from their own resources (8:1–3).

## Women in Luke

Luke has the most prominent female voices of any Gospel:
- **Mary** (1:46–55): the Magnificat as the first NT proclamation of the gospel; a peasant girl declares the social revolution of the kingdom
- **Elizabeth** (1:41–45): Spirit-filled prophet who recognizes the Messiah before anyone else
- **Anna** (2:36–39): the first person explicitly called a prophet in the NT; she speaks "to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem"
- **The widow of Nain's son** (7:11–17): echoes Elijah/Elisha; underscores Jesus as eschatological prophet
- **The sinful woman** (7:36–50): model of extravagant devotion; contrast to the Pharisee host
- **Women traveling with Jesus** (8:1–3): unique to Luke — women fund the movement from their own resources; named disciples before the Twelve are sent out
- **Mary and Martha** (10:38–42): Mary's posture as a disciple ("at the Lord's feet") is defended by Jesus against the conventional female role
- **Women at the cross** (23:49); women as first witnesses of the empty tomb (24:1–12)

This pattern is continuous and deliberate, not incidental.

## The Travel Narrative (9:51–19:48)

Luke's most distinctive literary creation — ten chapters in which Jesus "sets his face to go to Jerusalem." The travel is narratively implausible (the journey should take days, not chapters), signaling that this section is a literary-theological construction. Its main functions:

- **Discipleship teaching**: the primary audience is the disciples-in-training, preparing for their role in Acts
- **Unique parables**: Good Samaritan (10:25–37 — the definition of "neighbor" overturns ethnic and religious boundaries); Parables of the Lost (ch. 15 — the father's reception of the prodigal as portrait of God); Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31 — the reversal of the Magnificat worked out in narrative); Persistent Widow (18:1–8 — persistent prayer); Pharisee and Tax Collector (18:9–14 — the reversal of religious merit)
- **Jerusalem as goal**: everything is oriented toward the coming confrontation; the city and its temple both attract and repel Jesus throughout

## Table Fellowship

Jesus at meals is a recurring Lukan motif — unique in its frequency and theological weight:
- Jesus eats with Pharisees (7:36–50; 11:37–54; 14:1–24) and with tax collectors and sinners (5:29–32; 15:1–2; 19:1–10)
- He is accused: "Here is a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" (7:34)
- The banquet parables (14:15–24; 15) show the table as an image of the inclusive kingdom
- The Last Supper (22:14–38) and the Emmaus meal (24:28–35) frame the Passion and resurrection
- The "breaking of bread" (24:35) becomes the technical phrase for the eucharist in Acts

Table fellowship in the Mediterranean world was one of the most status-laden social rituals — eating with someone confirmed social equality and mutual belonging. Jesus's practice systematically dismantled these status markers.

## Jerusalem as Goal and Center

Unlike Mark (where the risen Jesus returns to Galilee) and Matthew (where the Great Commission is given on a Galilean mountain), Luke's narrative begins and ends in Jerusalem:
- Zechariah's vision in the Jerusalem temple (1:5–23)
- Simeon and Anna in the temple (2:25–38)
- The 12-year-old Jesus "in my Father's house" (2:46–49)
- Jesus "sets his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51)
- The Passion in Jerusalem
- The resurrection appearances in and around Jerusalem (24)
- The ascension from Bethany (near Jerusalem; 24:50–53)
- Acts begins: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8)

Jerusalem is the city of destiny: both the place where the divine purpose is consummated (cross, resurrection) and the place where the new community is launched (Pentecost).

- **Canonical for** the major Christian streams that receive the fourfold Gospel (Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Protestant): see `canon_scope` frontmatter.

- **Luke-Acts and the canon:** Green notes that placing Luke among the Four and Acts as bridge to Paul obscures the two-volume literary unity for modern readers ([[luke-acts-unity]]).

## Scholarship and Historiography

Positions (not adjudications):
- **Critical consensus dating** often ca. 80–85 CE post-70; some evangelical and traditional arguments favor the 60s. Green treats date as interpretively secondary to narrative reading.
- **Authorship:** traditional identification with Luke the physician and Pauline companion (Phlm 24; Col 4:14; 2 Tim 4:11); Green holds identity “unimportant to its interpretation” and reads the anonymous narrator. Redaction-critical schools (Conzelmann and heirs) periodize John/Jesus/church; Green rejects dual-epoch staging of John vs Jesus baptism as salvation-historical ages.
- **Genre:** historiography (Cadbury; Aune general history) vs biography vs Alexander’s scientific-preface analogues — Green qualifies all three and prioritizes narrative reading of Luke-Acts as theocentric historiography.
- **Synoptic problem:** Green accepts written predecessors (1:1) including Mark but does not exploit Q or redaction differentials in the commentary method.

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **Nazareth program (4:16–30):** “the poor,” double ἄφεσις, Jubilee “year of the Lord’s favor,” Elijah/Elisha outsiders, “today” — Green’s master key ([[jubilee]]).
2. **Luke without Acts:** whether the Gospel is theologically complete alone (Green: no for the redemptive purpose for all peoples).
3. **Possessions:** renunciation vs stewardship vs status-critique (Green: wealth cannot be neutral; kingdom economics subvert patron-client reciprocity).
4. **Widow’s mite (21:1–4):** exemplary piety vs indictment of a system that “devours widows” (Green favors system critique in temple conflict co-text).
5. **Passion christology:** martyr typology vs messianic necessity (δεῖ) and Scripture fulfillment; Green stresses divine purpose, innocence motif, and bridge to Acts witness.

## Fidelity re-ingest anchors (Green NICNT 1997)

Grounded claims from the 2026-07-18 full-body re-read (raw 122–196 + 3522–10757; digests R0–R8 ~351 claims). Attributions are to Green’s exposition unless noted as survey.

### Method and introduction
- **Discourse analysis** = narratology + culture-critical analysis; not form/redaction/source criticism as primary tools.
- **διήγησις / orderly account:** Luke self-categorizes as narrative, not “Gospel”; order (καθεξῆς) controls meaning; isolated pericopes will not do.
- **Genre:** Greco-Roman historiography (Cadbury consensus; Aune general history); Alexander scientific-preface affinities noted and qualified; pure *bios* undercut by theocentrism and Acts.
- **Luke-Acts unity:** one story, two rolls (~19,400 / ~18,400 words); Acts 1:1 “began”; Gospel incomplete for full salvation-to-all purpose (e.g. 2:32 → Acts Gentile mission).
- **Co-text / intertext / context:** local sequence; LXX echo chamber (Abraham, Moses, Elijah/Elisha); socio-historical scripts affirmed or subverted.
- **Historicity:** commentary prioritizes *signification* over verification; for healings, contemporaries contest source/interpretation more than bare occurrence.
- **Authorship:** identity unimportant to interpretation; “Luke” ≈ narrator; traditional physician-companion remains most likely historical candidate.
- **Purpose:** primarily ecclesiological — strengthen the movement under opposition; ensure God’s redemptive purpose and call to faithfulness/witness.
- **Salvation theme:** theocentric; Spirit enacts will; holistic present restoration — “knows nothing of… dichotomies… social and spiritual or individual and communal.”
- **Money as rival:** chief competitor for loyalty; near-nobodies often model faithfulness.
- **Prologue:** narration is proclamation; Theophilus needs certainty (ἀσφάλεια) via persuasive order more than bare “did it happen?”

### Narrative blocks (body)
- **Birth (1:5–2:52):** promise–fulfillment–praise; imbalanced John||Jesus parallel elevating Jesus; Spirit density; honor/purity/kinship world; temple inclusio; Magnificat as program (God alone subject of revolutionary verbs); Benedictus holds socio-political and spiritual salvation together; shepherds first hear; Simeon light to Gentiles + division in Israel; boy Jesus in the Father’s house.
- **Preparation (3:1–4:13):** John as social-renewal prophet; fruits of repentance; Spirit anointing (not dunking-centered); genealogy to Adam; testing as true Son where Israel failed (Deut); devil’s alternative aim.
- **Nazareth + early Galilee (4:14–6:11):** 4:16–30 programmatic (poor = low-status honor; double release; Jubilee; Elijah/Elisha; today); Capernaum release-healings; call of disciples; table fellowship and Sabbath controversies under kingdom authority.
- **Late Galilee (6:12–9:50):** Sermon on the Plain reversal (poor/rich as power-privilege); enemy-love / merciful Father; women patrons 8:1–3; hear-and-do kinship; faith over fear; ch. 9 christology–discipleship (confession, daily cross, transfiguration/exodus).
- **Travel (9:51–19:27):** literary-theological journey (ἀνάλημψις), not itinerary; hearing-and-doing; Samaritan neighbor; prayer/Fatherhood; possessions; Lost (divine joy); mammon; Rich Man and Lazarus; Zacchaeus “today salvation”; pounds against immediate kingdom-at-Jerusalem.
- **Jerusalem entry + temple (19:28–21:38):** royal entry; weeping over unrecognized visitation; temple reclaim for revelatory teaching; authority conflicts; widow’s gift as system critique; temple fall ≠ immediate End; readiness.
- **Passion–exaltation (22–24):** farewell table / new covenant; δεῖ and Scripture; Satan–Judas–alliances; innocence through trials; “today… Paradise”; veil; empty tomb; Emmaus (Messiah must suffer then glory); embodied resurrection; witnesses + Spirit promise + Jerusalem hinge to Acts; ascension blessing and temple joy.

## Luke as Seen from Mark: France's NIGTC (2002)

[[rt-france|R. T. France]]'s NIGTC *Mark* (full re-ingest 2026-07-18; see
[[france-mark-nigtc]]) records a series of Luke/Mark observations. All claims are France's.

- **The "Great Omission."** Mark 6:45 "marks the beginning of Luke's 'Great Omission'" —
  Luke has nothing of Mk 6:45–8:26. Relatedly, France resolves the Bethsaida geography
  problem (Mk 6:32, 45) by discounting Luke's placing of the feeding: "An alternative and
  probably better solution is to discount Luke's placing of the incident."
- **Style.** Mark uses the historic present at least 150 times; Luke has "hardly any." In
  Mark (unlike Luke) οἱ μαθηταί and οἱ δώδεκα are functionally equivalent.
- **Jerusalem as home.** Matthew intensified Mark's [[galilee|Galilee]]/[[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]
  symbolism, "but not… Luke": Luke (and [[acts|Acts]]) instead depicts Jerusalem as the
  church's true home.
- **Christological hints.** France suggests Mark's ἐξῆλθον (1:38) may already hint at the
  coming into the world of the preexistent Son of God, "as Luke 4:43 apparently takes it."
  Luke reassigns the abuse of Mk 14:65 from Sanhedrin members to the guards, and Luke's
  ordering of the denial scene lacks Mark's sharp Jesus/Peter contrast.
- **Separate traditions.** Luke's anointing account (Lk 7:36-50) is probably a separate
  tradition from the Bethany anointing (Mk 14:3-9); Mark's καλέσαι (2:17) is best glossed by
  Luke's εἰς μετάνοιαν. The swordsman of Mk 14:47 was, in Mark's intention, a bystander —
  contrast Luke and Matthew, who imply a disciple.
- **Textual note.** The clause "whom he also named apostles" in Mk 3:14 is "probably not
  original in Mark," suspect as harmonisation to Luke.
- **The Longer Ending of Mark.** France's literary analysis finds Mk 16:9-20 "a pastiche of
  elements drawn from the other gospels and Acts" — including the Emmaus story (vv. 12-13)
  and the ascension (v. 19 // Lk 24:50-51) — with a "secondhand flavour." See
  [[mark-ending|The Ending of Mark]] and [[synoptic-problem|The Synoptic Problem]].
