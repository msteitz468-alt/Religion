# Second Clement

## Textual Overview

*Second Clement* (2 Clem.) is **not by Clement and not a letter**. It is, in fact, the oldest surviving complete Christian sermon — a "word of exhortation" (*paraklēsis*) — outside the New Testament. The document is anonymous; it was attributed to Clement of Rome because it was transmitted alongside [[1-clement|First Clement]] in the manuscripts, and copyists or archivists eventually assumed a common authorship.

**Nature**: A sermon or homily, probably read aloud in a congregational setting (cf. 2 Clem. 17.3: "while the elder reads"). The "reader" (19.1) of the sermon may have been the presider at worship; the "elder" who composed it (17.3) was the anonymous author.

**Date**: Highly uncertain. Proposals range from ca. 95 CE (making it nearly contemporary with 1 Clement) to ca. 170 CE. The most plausible proposals: (a) Corinth, ca. 120–140 CE (Lightfoot); (b) Rome, ca. 100 CE (Frend); (c) Alexandria, ca. 150 CE (Koester, based on the use of the *Gospel of the Egyptians*). The anti-gnostic elements (stress on physical resurrection and judgment; 9.1–5) suggest the second quarter of the 2nd century is most likely.

**Occasion**: The congregation is being urged to repent, to "keep our baptism pure and undefiled" (6.9), and to hold to the confession of Christ while resisting both gnostic-style spiritualizing and complacent worldliness.

## Content and Structure

The sermon is 20 chapters and moves as a sustained call to repentance, ethical seriousness, and eschatological hope.

**Opening (1.1–2.7)**: "Brothers and sisters, we ought to think of Jesus Christ as we do of God, as judge of the living and the dead." The functional equation of Christ with God is the theological starting point. Drawn from Isa. 54:1 (applied to the barren church now bearing many children) and a saying of Jesus (2.4 = Matt. 9:13).

> **2 Clem. 1.1** — "We ought to think of Jesus Christ as we do of God (ὡς περὶ θεοῦ)" — one of the most striking early Christological statements; Jesus is here ascribed the same honor and cognitive weight as God the Father.

> **2 Clem. 2.4** — Matt. 9:13 ("I have come not to call the righteous but sinners") is introduced with "and another scripture says" — the **earliest instance of a NT passage being cited as "scripture"** (γραφή) in surviving Christian literature.

**Call to repentance (3.1–9.6)**:
- Do not merely call Christ "Lord" but obey his commands
- The present world is small and transitory; Christ's promise is great
- Postbaptismal sin is addressed: as long as we are in this world, we can repent (8.1–3)
- The physical resurrection is affirmed against spiritualizing: "the flesh is the church, the spirit is Christ" (14.2–4); the judgment will be bodily

**Use of non-canonical sources (12.2, 5.2–4)**: At least four of the sayings of Jesus quoted in 2 Clement (out of at least nine total) do not appear in the canonical Gospels. The most notable:

> **2 Clem. 12.2**: "When the Lord was asked when his kingdom would come, he said, 'When the two become one, and the outside like the inside, and the male with the female, neither male nor female.'" This saying also appears in the *Gospel of Thomas* (22) and is attributed to the *Gospel of the Egyptians* by Clement of Alexandria.

This reflects the author's use of oral or written gospel traditions not preserved in the canonical four. He knows Matthew and Luke; his relationship to non-canonical sources suggests an Alexandria or Corinth provenance where such texts circulated.

**Eschatological urgency (5.1–6.9)**: The present age and the age to come are two enemies; the Christian cannot be friends with both. The body/soul, perishable/imperishable contrasts drive toward ethical urgency.

**The competition metaphor (7.1–6)**: The Christian life as an athletic competition (*agōn*); the heavenly crown is what matters. The earthly games are an analogy: those who cheat or fail to compete properly receive no crown.

**The repentance window (8.1–3)**: "We are clay in the craftsman's hand. If a potter reshapes a broken vessel while still working it, he can repair it; but once it has been fired, it cannot." A strong argument for the urgency of repentance while still alive.

**Ecclesiology (14.1–5)**: The church pre-exists creation ("the church was not made now, but was from the beginning"); it existed as spiritual but has now been revealed in the flesh of Christ. This pre-existent church theology is a distinctive feature, showing theological sophistication.

## Major Themes

**Christology**: Christ equated with God in honor and authority; judge of the living and the dead; his suffering for us is the ground of our obligation.

**Repentance and baptismal integrity**: The sermon's controlling practical concern. The community has been baptized; they must keep that baptism "pure." Postbaptismal repentance is available but urgent — do not delay.

**Anti-docetic/anti-gnostic elements**: The physical reality of the resurrection and judgment; the body as the vehicle of holy conduct (9.3); explicit reference to "the flesh" as the site of salvation. These are directed against those who spiritualize or deny the resurrection.

**The two ages**: The present evil age vs. the coming age of righteousness. Christians are transient residents in this world, not at home here.

**First use of NT as "Scripture"**: The citation of Matt. 9:13 as "scripture" (2.4) is the earliest known instance of this practice in surviving Christian literature — a data point directly relevant to the canon formation timeline.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**2 Clem. 1.1 (Christ = God)**: "We ought to think of Jesus Christ as we do of God (ὡς περὶ θεοῦ), as judge of the living and the dead." Is this a full identification of Christ with God, or a functional claim about honor and role? The text has been read both as early Christological high Christology and as a rhetorical elevation that need not imply full divine identity. Given its isolation in an otherwise unreflective, practical sermon, it likely represents the presupposition of the community rather than a worked-out doctrine.

**2 Clem. 2.4 (First NT scripture citation)**: The precise significance depends on how we date the sermon. If ca. 140 CE, the practice of treating NT texts as "scripture" (γραφή) had emerged by that point but was not yet universal. If ca. 100 CE, the implication is earlier.

**The non-canonical sayings**: How dependent was the sermon's author on non-canonical gospel sources? The sayings from the *Gospel of the Egyptians* and parallels with the *Gospel of Thomas* suggest a milieu where these texts were in use — a provenance argument for Alexandria or Corinth.

**The pre-existent church (ch. 14)**: "The living church is the body of Christ" and "the church was created before the sun and moon, and it was revealed in the last days to save us." This is theologically unusual — a Platonizing or apocalyptic theology of a pre-existent heavenly church. Does it reflect gnostic-influenced thought that the author is redirecting, or is it an independent development?

## Canon and Reception

Transmitted alongside [[1-clement|1 Clement]] (it follows it in Codex Alexandrinus and the later
Apostolic-Constitutions orbit), 2 Clement is the **oldest surviving Christian sermon** outside the
NT but was never a serious canon candidate. It was valued and read in some 2nd–4th century
communities ([[proto-orthodox-christianity|proto-orthodox]]) yet **excluded** from the settled
canon of all later Christian streams.
