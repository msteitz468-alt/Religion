# Mahābhārata

The **Mahābhārata** ("the Great Story of the Bhāratas") is the longer of the two great Sanskrit **itihāsas**
and one of the longest literary works in human history — approximately **75,000 verses** or 3 million words,
roughly fifteen times the combined length of the Hebrew Bible and New Testament, or seven times the Iliad
and Odyssey combined. It describes itself as the **Fifth Veda** (*pañcama-veda*) and includes the
**[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]]** as its most celebrated embedded text.

The text is attributed to the sage **Vyāsa**, who is also a character within it — a recursion the text
uses to authorize itself. No single author could have composed it; it is, in Doniger's phrase, "like an
ancient Wikipedia" to which anyone who knew Sanskrit could add material, yet it has a genuine integrity
"that the culture supports" — a brilliantly orchestrated hybrid with no single party line on any subject.

## Narrative Summary

The five **Pāṇḍava brothers** (sons of the dead king Pāṇḍu, each actually fathered by a god) share a
single wife, the extraordinary **[[draupadi|Draupadī]]**. Their cousins the **Kauravas** usurp their
kingdom through a dice game. After twelve years of exile and one year in disguise, the Pāṇḍavas return
and fight the **Battle of Kurukṣetra** — a cataclysmic civil war in which almost every warrior on both
sides is killed. The god **[[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]]**, who serves as Arjuna's charioteer, delivers his counsel
in the embedded **[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]]**.

After the war, the victorious but morally devastated **Yudhiṣṭhira** (the eldest Pāṇḍava) performs a
**horse sacrifice** to expiate his sins, and eventually all five brothers and Draupadī ascend into heaven
— accompanied, in the final test, by a stray dog who turns out to be the god Dharma himself.

## Major Themes

**"Dharma is subtle."** The Mahābhārata's central phrase (*dharmo hi sukṣmaḥ*) — every moral choice
turns out to be either wrong or impossible. Unlike the Rāmāyaṇa ("there is a perfect man, his name is
Rāma"), the Mahābhārata deconstructs dharma: "What is dharma?" asked Yudhiṣṭhira, and did not stay for
an answer.

**Violence and nonviolence (ahimsa).** The text is simultaneously about "grotesque, sanctioned violence"
and an extended meditation on whether violence is ever avoidable. "Matsya-nyāya" (fish eat fish — the
big fish eat the little fish) is cited both to justify coercive kingship and, by Arjuna, to justify
the inevitability of violence: "I see no being that lives in the world without violence." Yet the
Mahābhārata is also, at multiple points, passionately against war.

**Animals as moral agents.** Dogs, snakes, mongooses, hawks, and doves populate the text's ethical
debates. Yudhiṣṭhira's stray dog (the god Dharma in disguise) is refused entry to heaven by Indra on
the grounds that dogs pollute sacrifice — Yudhiṣṭhira refuses to abandon him anyway. This is what
passes for a moral victory. "The story shows just how rotten the caste system is but does not change it.
No dogs get into heaven."

**Caste, class, and social mobility.** The origin of the four class colors (varna) is explained in the
Mahābhārata not by divine creation but by karmic *fall*: originally everyone shared Brahmin dharma; the
other classes emerged when groups took up different occupations and their karma stained them different
colors. Nishadas (tribal outsiders) and Pariahs are the persistent marginalized in the text's social
imagination. Stories like the **house of lac** (Nishadas as expendable sacrificial substitutes for the
Pāṇḍavas) and **Ekalavya** (Nishada archer who gives his thumb to protect Arjuna's status) illustrate
the text's deep ambivalence: it sees the injustice and paints it without resolving it.

**The transfer of karma.** The Mahābhārata "totters on the brink" of a full concept of transferred
merit (proto-bhakti). Yudhiṣṭhira in hell discovers that his mere presence brings relief to his
suffering brothers — a "cool sweet breeze" — but the god Dharma insists "no one experiences another
person's deed." The tension is not resolved, only tested.

**The Bhagavad Gita's synthesis.** Krishna's teaching resolves the dharma/moksha tension by forming a
**triad** with bhakti as mediating third term: karma-yoga (works/sva-dharma), jñāna-yoga (knowledge/
moksha), and bhakti-yoga (devotion). "Karma without kāma" — acting without attachment to results —
is the Gita's signature solution. Ahimsa appears in the Gita only in four lists of conventional
virtues; the Gita never properly addresses it.

## Structural Notes

- The Mahābhārata calls itself both *itihāsa* (history) and *dharma-śāstra* (dharma text). "To say
  that the sermons on dharma are digressions is like saying the arias in a Verdi opera are unwelcome
  interruptions."
- The text is "a work in progress" — no single authoritative manuscript; hundreds of versions in
  Sanskrit and vernacular languages. The contradictions in it are "not the mistakes of a sloppy editor
  but enduring cultural dilemmas that no author could ever have resolved."
- The Mahābhārata frames itself with **Janamejaya's snake sacrifice** (an antisacrifice — killing the
  wrong animal) and closes with Yudhiṣṭhira's horse sacrifice undermined by a mongoose who declares the
  whole ceremony "not equal to one grain of barley given by a gleaner during famine."

## Historical Context

Composed roughly entre deux empires — after the Mauryans (c. 185 BCE) and before the Guptas (c. 320 CE).
Its complex narrative of Brahmin/Kshatriya tension, the rise of sectarian Hinduism (Vaiṣṇavas and
Śaivas), and the Buddhist/Jaina challenge to Vedic sacrifice is a snapshot of one of India's most
turbulent intellectual centuries. The figure of **Yudhiṣṭhira** has been read as a response to Aśoka
(Buddhist compassion; guilt at military victory) and to Pushyamitra (Brahmin assertion against
Buddhism). Both marks are genuine.

## Comparison with the Rāmāyaṇa

| | Rāmāyaṇa | Mahābhārata |
|---|---|---|
| Length | ~20,000 verses | ~75,000 verses |
| Tone | triumphalist | tragic |
| Dharma | affirmed | deconstructed |
| Time-frame | Treta Yuga (2nd age) | Dvāpara/Kali cusp (3rd–4th) |
| War | against foreigners/ogres | civil war (family) |
| Central question | Is there a perfect man? (Yes: Rāma) | What is dharma? (No answer) |

Both texts are in constant intertextual conversation; there is a Sanskrit poem that, depending on how
compounds are divided, tells the story of *either* the Rāmāyaṇa *or* the Mahābhārata.

## Canon and Reception

**Smriti** (remembered), not **shruti** (revealed), but functions with near-canonical authority across
virtually all Hindu communities. Called the "Fifth Veda" by the text itself. Received as history,
as dharma instruction, and as devotional literature simultaneously. The Bhagavad Gita is the single
most widely read section and one of the most contested texts in the history of Vedanta.
