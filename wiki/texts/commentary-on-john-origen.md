# Commentary on the Gospel of John (Origen)

[[origen|Origen]]'s *Commentary on the Gospel of John* was originally 32 books — the most extensive patristic commentary on John. Only a fraction survives: Books I, II, IV–VI, X, XIII, XIX, XX, XXVIII, and XXXII in the Greek; the Schaff/ANF edition includes Books I, II, IV–VI, X. The commentary was begun in Alexandria before 231 CE and continued at Caesarea. It represents Origen's highest-level allegorical exegesis, with the Johannine Prologue receiving exhaustive treatment.

## Book I — The Eternal Gospel and the Prologue

### Superiority of John's Gospel

[[origen|Origen]] opens Book I by ranking the four Gospels and singling out John as the highest. Matthew writes for Hebrews tracing the Davidic genealogy, Mark narrates "the beginning of the Gospel," and Luke records the acts and teachings of Jesus, but "none of these plainly declared His Godhead, as John does when he makes Him say, *I am the light of the world, I am the way and the truth and the life, I am the resurrection, I am the door, I am the good shepherd*" (lines 31615–31617). On this basis Origen concludes: "We may therefore make bold to say that the Gospels are the first fruits of all the Scriptures, but that of the Gospels that of John is the first fruits" (lines 31619–31620). The required qualifications for interpreting John match this exaltation: "No one can apprehend the meaning of it except he have lain on Jesus' breast and received from Jesus Mary to be his mother also" (lines 31620–31622). The interpreter must become "another John," having Christ himself reveal the Gospel's meaning — an explicitly pneumatic and transformative standard, not merely scholarly.

### The Gospel as First Fruits of Scripture

Origen develops a careful distinction between "first growth" and "first fruits." First fruits are offered after all the fruits are ripe; first growth comes before them all. He applies this: "one would not be wrong in saying that the first growth is the law of Moses, but the first fruits the Gospel. For it was after all the fruits of the prophets who prophesied till the Lord Jesus, that the perfect word shot forth" (lines 31536–31538). The four Gospels form "the elements of the faith of the Church, out of which elements the whole world which is reconciled to God in Christ is put together" (lines 31595–31597). Origen extends the category of "Gospel" broadly: all apostolic writing is Gospel in a secondary sense. Even Paul's letters count: "What he preached and said was the Gospel; and what he preached and said he was also in the habit of writing, and what he wrote was therefore Gospel" (lines 31643–31644). The "good things" the Gospel announces are ultimately identical with Christ himself: "the good things the Apostles announce in this Gospel are simply Jesus" (lines 31790–31791), since Jesus is the Resurrection, the Way, Truth, and Life, and the Door (lines 31791–31845).

### The Eternal Gospel vs. the Somatic Gospel

Origen draws a sharp distinction, grounded in Revelation 14:6, between two levels of Gospel. The received narrative Gospels form the "somatic" or bodily Gospel, serving "those who are carnal." But there is also "what John calls the eternal Gospel, and what may properly be called the spiritual Gospel, [which] presents clearly to those who have the will to understand, all matters concerning the very Son of God, both the mysteries presented by His discourses and those matters of which His acts were the enigmas" (lines 31747–31752). Those "perfected in the spirit" are to be "made to partake of that Word which, after it was made flesh, rose again to what it was in the beginning, with God" (lines 31773–31775). At the cosmic level, Christ himself is the Gospel: "What we have now to do is to transform the sensible Gospel into a spiritual one. For what would the narrative of the sensible Gospel amount to if it were not developed to a spiritual one? It would be of little account or none" (lines 31782–31785).

### The Logos Christology as Key to the Gospel

At sections 11 and 23, Origen catalogs the divine names of the Son — Life, Light, Truth, Way, Resurrection, Door, Shepherd, Wisdom, Power, and the Word — and insists each must be interpreted as pointing to a real soteriological function, not dismissed tropically. He criticizes those who fixate on "Word" while treating the other titles allegorically: "I do not mince matters; it is nothing but stupidity" (lines 32258–32259). The Logos title receives sustained investigation through sections 16–22, as Origen works through six senses of *arche* (beginning). His key conclusion: "God created me the beginning of His ways, for His works, so that the Word might be in an *arche*, namely, in wisdom" (lines 32170–32172). The theological priority of Wisdom over Logos is explicit: "wisdom is anterior to all the thoughts that are expressed in the titles of the first-born of every creature" (lines 32208–32210), and "not even as the Word is He the *arche*, for the Word was in the *arche*" (lines 32207–32208). Among all the titles, Wisdom alone properly designates the Son as a beginning. The Christ-figure called Logos "takes away from us all that is irrational, and makes us truly reasonable, so that we do all things, even to eating and drinking, to the glory of God" (lines 33050–33052). Origen also defends the Son's distinct hypostatic existence against those who collapse the Son into mere divine utterance: "let them say that God the Word is a separate being and has an essence of His own" (lines 32379–32380).

### Resolving Apparent Contradictions: John and the Synoptics

Origen frames the synoptics as operating at the somatic level while John transcends them with the eternal Gospel. He notes that Matthew traces the Davidic genealogy while John "speaks of him whose genealogy had already been set forth, but which begins to speak of him at a point before he had any genealogy" (lines 31603–31604). Mark's "beginning of the Gospel" finds its ultimate referent in John: "we may perhaps find what he aims at in John; in the beginning the Word, God the Word" (lines 31610–31611). The apparent difference in scope between synoptic genealogies and John's cosmic opening is resolved by recognizing that John operates at the level of the eternal Gospel. Origen also deploys John against Marcionite dualism: "These words, were there no others, are enough to convict them of their error. For how can John be the beginning of the Gospel if they suppose he belongs to a different God" (lines 31977–31981) — since Malachi's prophecy of the forerunner and the voice in the wilderness bind the two Testaments as a single economy.

## Book II — The Word and John the Baptist

### John 1:1–7: In the Beginning Was the Word

Book II opens with the clause "And the Word was with God, and the Word was God" and immediately poses the question of how the Logos was "with" God. Origen draws a comparison with the prophetic formula "The Word of the Lord came to Hosea / Isaiah / Jeremiah": the Logos "came" to prophets but was "always with the Father," so that "The Word was always with the Father; and so it is said, And the Word was with God. He did not come to God, and this same word *was* is used of the Word because He was in the beginning at the same time when He was with God, neither being separated from the beginning nor being bereft of His Father" (lines 33240–33244). The eternal co-presence of the Logos with the Father is thus distinguished from his temporal missions to the prophets.

Origen analyzes John's Greek grammar carefully: John uses the article with *ho Logos* and with *ho Theos* (the Father), but omits the article when calling the Logos "God": "He uses the article, when the name of God refers to the uncreated cause of all things, and omits it when the Logos is named God" (lines 33272–33274). The conclusion is that "God on the one hand is Very God (*Autotheos*, God of Himself) ... but that all beyond the Very God is made God by participation in His divinity, and is not to be called simply God (with the article), but rather God (without article)" (lines 33289–33293). The Son is therefore "a being of more exalted rank than the other gods beside Him," but he holds his divinity from the Father and maintains it "by remaining always in uninterrupted contemplation of the depths of the Father" (lines 33305–33308). This carefully avoids both Sabellian collapse and subordinationist denial of the Son's deity.

Origen also identifies four propositions in John 1:1–2 and shows that the fourth ("He was in the beginning with God") sums up the prior three: "He now points out by this word *He*, the Word who is God, and gathers up into a fourth proposition the three which went before" (lines 33415–33416). The Logos "is manifestly older than those things which were made at the beginning, older not only than the firmament and the dry land, but than the heavens and earth" (lines 33424–33426).

### The Divine Names of the Son

Book I had already cataloged the names exhaustively (see above), and Book II presupposes that framework. In Book II Origen discusses how the divine names condition what each class of beings can receive from Christ. He distinguishes: those who know the Logos in the beginning and was with God and was God (the prophets, who "declared that the Word of the Lord, or the Logos, had come to them"); those who "know nothing but Jesus Christ and Him crucified, considering that the Word made flesh is the whole Word" (the mass of believers); philosophers who follow subordinate logoi; and those in outright error (lines 33371–33382). The Logos is one and not many — "Any one will confess that there is only one truth; it could never be said in this case that there is one truth of God, and another of the angels, and another of man" (lines 33438–33440) — and the same principle applies to Wisdom and Logos. Life, Light, and the other titles are in each case functions of the single Logos distributed according to the recipient's capacity (lines 34056–34091).

### Word (*Logos*) vs. Reason (*Logismos*)

Origen distinguishes sharply between the Logos who was with God and the "reason" or "logoi" immanent in all rational creatures. The Logos with the article is "the source of that reason (*Logos*) which dwells in every reasonable creature; the reason which is in each creature is not, like the former called *par excellence* The Logos" (lines 33278–33281). He further distinguishes "the Logos in the beginning who was with God, God the Word" from "the Logos (reason) without whose presence none of our sins are committed" — the immanent reason that convicts sinners by being present but unheeded (lines 33944–33946). The Logos in every reasonable being functions as an inner law: "He is present with those who sin, for their condemnation, and He condemns the man who does not prefer Him to everything else" (lines 33934–33935). Origen draws on Romans 10:6–8 ("The Word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart") to argue that the Apostle does not treat these as two different beings (lines 33950–33953) — yet the distinction between the transcendent Logos and the immanent logismos remains operative throughout.

### John the Baptist: Voice vs. Word

One of the most precise arguments in Book II is the identification of John the Baptist as "voice" and Christ as "Word" (speech). Origen writes: "the Saviour is, in one view of Him, speech, and John differs from Him; for as the Saviour is speech, John is voice. John himself invites me to take this view of him, for to those who asked who he was, he answered, *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare the way of the Lord!*" (lines 34603–34607). The phenomenological analysis is pressed further: "A voice must be perceived with the ears if the mind is afterwards to receive the speech which the voice indicates. Hence, John is, in point of his birth, a little older than Christ, for our voice comes to us before our speech. But John also points to Christ; for speech is brought forward by the voice" (lines 34610–34614). Zacharias's temporary loss of speech at the annunciation is read as a sign: he "lost his voice at the birth of the voice which points out the Word of God, and only recovered it when the voice, forerunner of the Word, was born" (lines 34607–34609). Christ's baptism by John follows the same logic: "with men speech is purified by voice, though the natural way is that speech should purify the voice which indicates it" (lines 34616–34617). In summary: "when John points out Christ, it is man pointing out God, the Saviour incorporeal, the voice pointing out the Word" (lines 34618–34620).

### Pre-existence of the Baptist's Soul

Origen proposes that John the Baptist was sent into this world from a prior existence. He reads "There was a man sent from God, whose name was John" (John 1:6) as pointing beyond a simple prophetic commission: "the careful student will, therefore, enquire from what quarter John was sent, and whither... he will perhaps declare that as it is written of Adam, *And the Lord sent him forth out of the Paradise of pleasure to till the earth*, so also John was sent, either from heaven or from Paradise, or from some other quarter to this place on the earth" (lines 34462–34476). The argument is supported by John's prenatal response to Mary's greeting: "He who sedulously guards himself in his dealings with Scripture against forced, or casual, or capricious procedure, must necessarily assume that John's soul was older than his body, and subsisted by itself before it was sent on the ministry of the witness of the light" (lines 34509–34511). Origen goes further and entertains (on the basis of the apocryphal *Prayer of Joseph*) that John may have been an angel who assumed a body: "we ask if it can be one of the holy angels who is sent down on this ministry as forerunner of our Saviour. No wonder if, when the first-born of all creation was assuming a human body, some of them should have been filled with love to man and become admirers and followers of Christ, and thought it good to minister to his kindness towards man by having a body like that of men" (lines 34549–34554). Origen is explicit that this is inference from the text and apocryphal tradition, not a settled dogma, and notes the parallel with the saying "This is Elijah which is to come" (Matthew 11:14) as raising the same question about pre-existence (lines 34512–34516).

## Books IV–V — Fragments

Both books survive only as brief fragments, probably excerpted by a later compiler.

**Book IV** (lines 34862–34902) consists of three leaves from the book's beginning. Origen defends the Gospel's "rudeness of language" by invoking Paul's distinction of "earthen vessels" from the treasure they carry: "By earthen vessels we understand the humble diction of the Scriptures, which the Greek might so readily be led to despise, and in which the excellency of God's power appears so clearly" (lines 34883–34885). The argument is that plainness of style was providentially chosen so that the Gospel's spread could not be attributed to rhetorical skill: "if the Scripture possessed, like the works the Greeks admire, elegance and command of diction, then it would be open to suppose that not the truth of them had laid hold of men, but that the apparent sequence and splendour of language had carried off the hearers, and had carried them off by guile" (lines 34898–34902).

**Book V** (lines 34903–35107) survives only as its preface, addressed to Ambrose. Origen begins by citing Ecclesiastes ("My son, beware of making many books; there is no end of it") as an apparent warrant for not writing, then turns this worry into a sustained meditation on whether all Scripture constitutes *one* book. His resolution: "whoever speaks that which is foreign to religion is using many words, while he who speaks the words of truth, even should he go over the whole field and omit nothing, is always speaking the one word" (lines 35003–35006). He argues that the entire Bible is "one book" because it is all written about Christ — Christ is written of "even in the Pentateuch; He is spoken of in each of the Prophets, and in the Psalms, and, in a word, as the Saviour Himself says, in all the Scriptures" (lines 35016–35019). He also defends continued writing against the Gnostic heterodox who produce voluminous anti-Church expositions: "I fear that if I should be silent and should not put before our members the saving and true doctrines, these teachers might get a hold of curious souls" (lines 35087–35089). The fragment ends without reaching any commentary on the Gospel text proper.

## Book VI — John the Baptist's Testimony

Book VI is the fullest surviving portion of the commentary and covers John 1:19–29, [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]]'s testimony before priests, Levites, and the Pharisees' envoys.

### Preface: Interrupted Composition

Origen opens with an autobiographical preface explaining that Books I–V were completed despite "the storm in Alexandria" — a violent interruption that drove him from the city — and that he now resumes writing at a place of calm, noting that his earlier Alexandrian draft of Book VI was not brought with him (lines 35189–35196).

### John 1:19 — The Three Testimonies

Origen distinguishes three discrete testimonies of [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] in the Gospel: the first (John 1:6–18) asserting Christ's divinity; the second (John 1:19–27) disposing of the suspicion that John himself might be the Christ, Elijah, or the prophet; and the third (John 1:24–28) addressed to the Pharisees' envoys, "declaring one who was already present with men although they saw Him not, and whose coming was no longer in the future" (lines 35475–35478). Against the Gnostic commentator Heracleon, Origen argues that the words "Of his fullness all we received, and grace for grace; for the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ" belong to John the Baptist, not to the Evangelist (lines 35200–35212, 35431–35432).

Origen also uses John 1:19 as an occasion for an extended argument that the Old Testament prophets knew the same mysteries the Apostles knew, though they did not witness their fulfilment in the same sense: "those who were made perfect in earlier generations knew not less than the Apostles did of what Christ revealed to them, since the same teacher was with them as He who revealed to the Apostles the unspeakable mysteries of godliness" (lines 35284–35287).

### Priests and Levites as Types

Origen allegorizes the two embassies in John 1:19–24. The first group — priests and Levites from Jerusalem — show "gentleness and a willingness to learn" suited to "the character of true and careful servants of God," while the Pharisees' envoys, sent afterward, "assail the Baptist, as it were, with arrogant and unsympathetic words" (lines 35486–35499). Origen applies a hermeneutical rule: "The student who desires to understand the Scripture must always proceed in this careful way; he must ask with regard to each speech, who is the speaker and on what occasion it was spoken. Thus only can we discern how speech harmonizes with the character of the speaker, as it does all through the sacred books" (lines 35499–35503). He further notes that what the Jews do to John — sending envoys to inquire — John in turn does to Christ (sending his own disciples from prison to ask "Are you He that should come?"), showing the structural parallel between the two scenes (lines 35515–35521).

### The Identity of Elijah

The question of whether John is [[elijah|Elijah]] (John 1:21) receives the commentary's most elaborate section (lines 35567–35760). Origen presents two positions without finally adjudicating between them. The first, held by those who believe in *transcorporation* (metempsychosis), holds that "John did not know that he was Elijah. This will be the explanation of those who find in our passage a support for their doctrine of transcorporation, as if the soul clothed itself in a fresh body and did not quite remember its former lives" (lines 35585–35587). The second position — that of "our churchman" — rejects transmigration of souls and appeals instead to the angel's words at John's birth: John was to come "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), not *as* Elijah's soul. Origen explains: "it can be shown from thousands of texts that the spirit is a different thing from the soul, and that what is called the power is a different thing from both the soul and the spirit" (lines 35604–35607). On this reading, "there is nothing absurd in supposing that John, in the spirit and power of Elijah, turned the hearts of the fathers to the children, and that it was on account of this spirit that he was called Elijah who was to come" (lines 35615–35617).

Origen's own preferred resolution is that the priests' question "Are you Elijah?" was understood by John to mean: "are you that very Elijah who was taken up, and who now appeared according to the expectation of the Jews without being born?" — to which John rightly answers No, since "he who was called John was not Elijah who was taken up, and had not changed his body for his present appearance" (lines 35642–35647). Christ's statement that John *is* Elijah (Matt 11:14) is true in the sense of "spirit and power," not identity of soul or body.

### John the Baptist as Voice and Logos

Origen exegetes John's self-identification as "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (John 1:23) through the Logos/voice distinction already developed in Book II. Here he specifies: "He, then, understanding in this way the prophecy about himself spoken by Isaiah the prophet, says he is a voice, not crying in the wilderness, but *of one* crying in the wilderness, of Him, namely, who stood and cried, If any man thirst, let him come unto Me and drink" (lines 35818–35822). Zacharias's punishment — losing his own voice when he doubted the angel's announcement of John's birth — is read typologically: "for his want of faith with regard to the birth of the voice, he is himself deprived of his voice" (lines 35832–35834). The punishment fits because Zacharias failed to believe in the birth of the one whose entire nature *is* voice.

### The Shoe-Latchet: Origen's Typology of the Incarnation and the Descent into Hades

Origen treats John's disclaimer "the latchet of whose shoe I am not worthy to unloose" (John 1:27) as concealing a "riddle" about the Incarnation: "he conveys, as in a riddle, that he is not fit to solve and to explain the argument about Christ's assuming a human body, an argument tied up and hidden (like a shoe-tie) to those who do not understand it" (lines 36325–36329). Further, from Mark's variant "to stoop down and untie," Origen derives a two-shoe allegory: "I consider that the inhumanisation when the Son of God assumes flesh and bones is one of His shoes, and that the other is the descent to Hades" (lines 36448–36451). These two journeys — Incarnation and descent to Hades — are the shoe-latchets no human is adequate to undo without divine assistance.

### Bethabara and Proper Names

Origen reports that he personally investigated the site of John's baptism and concluded that the correct reading is "Bethabara," not "Bethany" as most manuscripts read: "We have visited the places to enquire as to the footsteps of Jesus and His disciples, and of the prophets" (lines 36657–36658). He argues the etymology supports the identification: "Bethabara … yields the meaning *House of preparation*, while Bethany means *House of obedience*. Where else was it fitting that he should baptize, who was sent as a messenger before the face of the Christ, to prepare His way before Him, but at the House of preparation?" (lines 36665–36670). He uses the discussion to articulate a hermeneutical principle that proper names in Scripture must be checked carefully against the Hebrew and the most accurate Greek copies (lines 36676–36720).

### The Lamb of God (*Amnos Tou Theou*)

"Behold the Lamb of God, which takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29) receives extended typological treatment (lines 36960–37376). Origen first explains why the title *lamb* is used among all the sacrificial animals: the lamb is the animal of the daily (continual) sacrifice, offered morning and evening, symbolizing the Word always being offered to the soul: "what other continual sacrifice can there be to the man of reason in the world of mind, but the Word growing to maturity, the Word who is symbolically called a lamb and who is offered as soon as the soul receives illumination" (lines 37091–37094).

In a second reading, the lamb signifies specifically Christ's human nature: "we may take our stand at the dispensation of the bodily advent of the Son of God in human life, and in that case we shall conceive the lamb to be no other than the man. For the man was led like a sheep to the slaughter, and as a lamb, dumb before his shearers … Hence, too, in the Apocalypse a lamb is seen, standing as if slain" (lines 37119–37125). The "taking away of sin" is not limited to believers but extends to "the whole world": Origen cites 1 John 2:1–2 — "He is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only, but for those of the whole world" — and 1 Timothy 4:10 ("Saviour of all men, especially of them that believe") against any reading that would restrict the Lamb's work to the Church (lines 37204–37209, 37360–37362). He also links the sacrifice of martyrs to the same pattern, arguing that "the death of the noble martyrs … their patience, their confession, even unto death, and their zeal for piety blunted the edge of the onset of evil powers against the sufferer" (lines 37169–37172).

### Typological Reading of the Jordan

Origen gives the Jordan River an elaborate typological reading anchored in etymology: "Jordan means their going down … what river will their going down be, to which one must come to be purified … what but our Saviour who separates those who received their lots from Moses from those who obtained their own portions through Jesus (Joshua)?" (lines 36723–36736). The Jordan thus is the Word made flesh: "Under the Jordan, accordingly, we have to understand the Word of God who became flesh and tabernacled among us, Jesus who gives us as our inheritance the humanity which He assumed" (lines 36741–36743). Origen extends the typology through multiple Old Testament crossings: the Red Sea (Moses / baptism into fear), the Jordan under Joshua (baptism into the sweet water of the Gospel), Elijah and Elisha crossing together (lines 36783–36875), and the cleansing of Naaman the Syrian — who preferred "Arbana and Pharpha, rivers of Damascus" only to be told by Origen that "none is good but the Jordan, nor able to cleanse from his leprosy him who with faith washes his soul in Jesus" (lines 36917–36919). The Jordan's contrast with the rivers of Babylon (captivity and exile) and the rivers of Egypt (whose dragon is the devil) makes the typology explicit: the Father is in the Son as God is in the Jordan, and "those who are carried captive, on account of their wickedness, when they taste other waters after sacred Jordan, are led to remember with longing their own river of salvation" (lines 36921–36923).

## Book X — Temple Cleansing

Book X of the [[commentary-on-john-origen|Commentary on John]] opens with John 2:12–25 — the journey to Capernaum, the Passover approach, the Temple cleansing, and the sign of the temple's raising. [[origen|Origen]] uses this dense pericope to develop three interlocking interpretive moves: a theory of Gospel harmony, a layered spiritual reading of the [[temple-jerusalem|Temple]] and its purging, and a meditation on Christ's omniscience.

**Gospel Discrepancy and Spiritual Harmonization**

[[origen|Origen]] notes immediately that the Temple cleansing appears in John at the *beginning* of the ministry, while Matthew, Mark, and Luke place it at the end, linked to the triumphal entry. He is blunt about the insolubility of this discrepancy at the literal level: "If the discrepancy between the Gospels is not solved, we must give up our trust in the Gospels, as being true and written by a divine spirit, or as records worthy of credence, for both these characters are held to belong to these works" (lines 37455–37457). Faced with this, [[origen|Origen]] argues that the Evangelists deliberately used historical material to convey spiritual truths, and "where this was not possible it was their intention to prefer the spiritual to the material. The spiritual truth was often preserved, as one might say, in the material falsehood" (lines 37566–37568). The solution is not harmonization on the literal plane but recognition that "the truth of these matters must lie in that which is seen by the mind" (line 37454). [[origen|Origen]] further observes that John's Gospel, which "goes more to the root of the matter and tells of the Word becoming flesh," places the Temple cleansing early as its second sign, while the Synoptics connect it to the passion narrative — each positioning reflects the Evangelist's theological intent rather than strict chronology (lines 37663–37666; cf. section 15, lines 38147–38266). He concludes that the student is left with a choice: either "renounce the attempt to find all the Gospels true" or "accept the four, and will consider that their truth is not to be sought for in the outward and material letter" (lines 37495–37499).

**The Temple Purging as Spiritual Allegory**

The core of [[origen|Origen]]'s reading in sections 16–19 (lines 38270–38770) is a thoroughgoing spiritual interpretation of the cleansing. He identifies the Temple with the soul, with the Church, and ultimately with each believer: "the natural temple is the soul skilled in reason, which, because of its inborn reason, is higher than the body; to which Jesus ascends from Capernaum, the lower-lying place of less dignity, and in which, before Jesus' discipline is applied to it, are found tendencies which are earthly and senseless and dangerous, and things which have the name but not the reality of beauty, and which are driven away by Jesus with His word plaited out of doctrines of demonstration and of rebuke" (lines 38334–38344). The animals and merchants represent different classes of spiritually disordered impulse: "The ox is symbolic of earthly things, for he is a husbandman. The sheep, of senseless and brutal things, because it is more servile than most of the creatures without reason. Of empty and unstable thoughts, the dove. Of things that are thought good but are not, the small change" (lines 38345–38349). Beyond the individual soul, [[origen|Origen]] also reads the cleansing as typological of the end of the Levitical sacrificial order — "when Jesus casts out the oxen and sheep, and orders the doves to be taken away, it was because oxen and sheep and doves were not much longer to be sacrificed there in accordance with Jewish practices" (lines 38324–38327), the "sacred office being transferred to those from the Gentiles who believed, and the kingdom of God being taken away from the Jews and given to a nation bringing forth the fruits of it" (lines 38331–38333). He also contests Heracleon's reading throughout sections 9, 14, 19: where Heracleon treats Capernaum as symbolic of "the farthest-out parts of the world, these districts of matter," [[origen|Origen]] counters that the Synoptics report extensive miraculous activity there, and Heracleon "must either give two meanings to Capernaum, and show us his reasons for them, or if he cannot do this he must give up saying that the Saviour visited any place to no purpose" (lines 38806–38809).

**"Destroy This Temple" — Body, Church, and Resurrection**

[[origen|Origen]]'s reading of John 2:19 ("Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up") develops in sections 20–26 (lines 38771–39233) into one of the most extensive passages in Book X. He takes the Johannine gloss — "He spoke of the temple of His body" (John 2:21) — as an opening to a double referent: the body Jesus received from the Virgin, and the collective body of the Church. "Both of these two things, the temple and the body of Jesus, appear to me, in one interpretation at least, to be types of the Church, and to signify that it is built of living stones, a spiritual house for a holy priesthood, built on the foundation of the Apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus being the head corner-stone; and it is, therefore, called a temple" (lines 38791–38795). The three days of the temple's raising become a figure of the Church's eschatological resurrection: "the temple will be raised again, and the body will rise again on the third day after the day of evil which threatens it, and the day of consummation which follows. For the third day will rise on the new heaven and the new earth, when these bones, the whole house of Israel, will rise in the great Lord's day, death having been overcome" (lines 38802–38807). [[origen|Origen]] connects this to Ezekiel's valley of dry bones (Ezek 37) and to the Pauline teaching that believers are "members of His body" (1 Cor 12:27). He also defends the distinction between "in three days" (John 2:19) and "on the third day" as theologically precise: "the rising again of the temple takes place on the first day after it has been destroyed and on the second day, and its resurrection is accomplished in all the three days. Hence a resurrection both has been and is to be" (lines 38881–38885). Against those who use the "I will raise it up" to collapse Father and Son into a single subject, [[origen|Origen]] insists the Son raised Himself with power granted by the Father (lines 38893–38912). The full spiritual significance of Solomon's Temple building in 1 Kings 5–6 is then expounded at length as a figure of the Church's construction from "living stones" — a task [[origen|Origen]] acknowledges "is far beyond our powers of language" and requires "that wisdom which is hidden in a mystery" (lines 39020–39028). He also notes that the false witnesses at Jesus's trial (Matt 26; Mark 14) distorted this very saying — taking "Destroy this temple" to refer to the stone sanctuary rather than the body — and that this incident "must, I think, necessarily have reference to the text now before us" (lines 38935–38936).

**"He Knew What Was in Man" — Christ's Omniscience**

Sections 28–30 (lines 39294–39384) address John 2:24–25: many at Jerusalem "believed in His name," but "Jesus did not trust Himself to them, because He knew all men." [[origen|Origen]] draws a sharp distinction between believing *in His name* and believing *in Him*: "believing in His name is a different thing from believing in Him. He who will not be judged because of his faith is exempted from the judgment, not for believing in His name, but for believing in Him; for the Lord says, He that believes in Me is not judged, not, He who believes in My name is not judged; the latter believes, and hence he is not worthy to be condemned already, but he is inferior to the other who believes in Him. Hence it is that Jesus does not trust Himself to him who believes in His name" (lines 39303–39311). The crowd's faith, grounded only in signs, does not rise to full trust in the person of Christ; Jesus therefore withholds Himself from them. The phrase "He knew all men" then prompts an inquiry into the scope of Christ's knowledge: it extends beyond men to "all beings who are without such bodies as ours," since he "was greater than those who reproved and judged by prophesying, and who brought to the light the secret things of the hearts of those whom the Spirit suggested to them" (lines 39374–39376). Finally, the clause "He knew what was in man" is read as referring to the spiritual powers — divine and demonic — that inhabit human beings: "The words, He knew what was in man, could also be taken as referring to the powers, better or worse, which work in men. For if any one gives place to the devil, Satan enters into him; thus did Judas give place, and thus did the devil put it in his heart to betray Jesus... But if any one gives place to God, he becomes blessed; for blessed is the man whose help is from God" (lines 39377–39383). The book closes with the invocation: "You know what is in man, Thou who know all things, O Son of God" (line 39383).

## Key Themes in Origen's Johannine Commentary

**1. The eternal Gospel and the hierarchy of the Gospels.** Origen's Commentary is governed by the conviction that John's Gospel is uniquely pneumatic — "the first fruits of all the Gospels" and the only text that "plainly declared His Godhead." The synoptic Gospels are "somatic"; John's is "spiritual." Behind all four lies the "eternal Gospel" that will replace the temporal one at the consummation.

**2. The Logos as organizing category.** Every divine name (Wisdom, Life, Light, Resurrection, Way, Truth, Son) is a distinct mode by which the one Logos distributes himself to recipients according to capacity. The anarthrous *theos* of John 1:1c indicates participative rather than essential deity, maintaining subordinationism while asserting genuine divine status.

**3. Voice and Word: the Baptist's ontological status.** The Baptist as "voice" (subordinate to the Word) generates one of the commentary's most philosophically precise distinctions. Voice is the instrument of speech, prior to speech but replaced by it. The Baptist's soul pre-existed; Origen entertains (from the *Prayer of Joseph*) that he may have been an angelic being incarnate.

**4. Allegorical harmonization of the Gospels.** Origen consistently resolves apparent contradictions between John and the Synoptics (the Temple cleansing's placement; differences in the Baptism narrative) by arguing that the Evangelists deliberately sacrificed historical sequence to preserve spiritual truth — "spiritual harmonization."

**5. The Jordan as Logos typology.** The Jordan = the descending Word (etymology: "their going down"). Origen's typological reading extends through Red Sea, Joshua's crossing, Elijah/Elisha, and Naaman's healing, contrasting the Jordan with rivers of Egypt (the devil's dragon) and Babylon (captivity).
