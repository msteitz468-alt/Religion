The Mishna is the first major written codification of the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]], compiled and edited by [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi]] (c. 200 CE). It forms the first layer of the [[talmud-bavli|Babylonian Talmud]] and the [[talmud-yerushalmi|Palestinian Talmud]], providing the text upon which the Gemara's commentary is built. The language is Mishnaic Hebrew, a distinct post-Biblical dialect.

## Structure

The Mishna contains **6 orders** (*sedarim*) and **63 tractates** (*masekhtot*), subdivided into individual paragraphs also called *mishna* (pl. *mishnayot*).

| Order | Hebrew | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| Seeds | *Zeraim* | Agricultural tithes, prayer |
| Appointed Times | *Moed* | Sabbath, festivals |
| Women | *Nashim* | Marriage and divorce law |
| Damages | *Nezikin* | Civil, criminal, and court procedure |
| Holy Things | *Kodashim* | Temple rites and sacrifices |
| Purities | *Tohorot* | Ritual purity |

*Nezikin* includes the tractate *Avot* (Sayings of the Fathers), a collection of ethical maxims often studied independently.

## Compilation History

The Mishna was the endpoint of a long oral transmission. The **Zugoth** (pairs of leading scholars, c. 150 BCE–10 CE) transmitted and disputed the Oral Torah. Their successors, the **Tannaim** (c. 10–200 CE), are the named authorities whose rulings fill the Mishna. Earlier attempts at codification existed — the *Mishna of Rabbi Akiva* is cited — but Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi's edition became authoritative and superseded earlier collections.

The Mishna records not only accepted rulings but also rejected minority opinions. This deliberate preservation of dissent is a structural feature: it leaves open the possibility that a minority position may later be validated.

## Hermeneutical Character

The Mishna operates through a **case-based, inductive method**. It presents rulings on specific situations rather than general principles. General principles are almost never stated; they must be inferred by the reader from the accumulation of cases. This is the opposite of Roman law's deductive, principle-first method.

The Mishna rarely cites Scriptural proof-texts for its rulings, in contrast to the later Gemara. This economy reflects its function as a legal handbook: it presents conclusions, not derivations. The Gemara supplies the derivation work.

## Relationship to the Gemara

The Gemara (completing the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]]) was composed as a running commentary on the Mishna by the **Amoraim** (c. 200–500 CE). The Babylonian Gemara covers 37 of the 63 tractates; the Palestinian Gemara has broader coverage but less analytical depth. The Mishna text itself is printed in Aramaic-dominated Talmud pages in Mishnaic Hebrew, creating a multilingual document.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Date and authority of the oral tradition**: The Mishna presents itself as transmitting laws originating at Sinai, but critical scholarship dates much Mishnaic material to Second Temple and post-70 CE innovation. The [[oral-torah-authority|authority debate]] turns on this question.

**The Sanhedrin and missing tractates**: Several Mishnaic tractates deal with institutions (Temple service, Sanhedrin courts) that no longer existed by 200 CE. Whether these sections preserve historical practice or are largely theoretical reconstructions is disputed.

**Relationship to the Dead Sea Scrolls and Second Temple diversity**: The Mishna presents a unified rabbinic tradition, but the Scrolls reveal a much more diverse Second Temple legal landscape. The extent to which Mishnaic law represents one stream among many, elevated to canonical status after 70 CE, is a live critical question.

## Key Commentators

- [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi]] — editor and compiler
- [[rashi|Rashi]] — commented on most tractates
- [[maimonides|Maimonides]] — *Commentary on the Mishna* (Arabic, c. 1168); later superseded by his *Mishna Torah*
