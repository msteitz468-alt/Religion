# The Vedas

The **Vedas** are the oldest scriptures of India — "generally believed to be the earliest literary record
of the Indo-European race" ([[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]], Vol. I, Ch. II). They are
**śruti** ("that which is heard"), regarded as authorless and eternal, revealed to the
**seers** (*mantra-dṛṣṭā*) and transmitted **orally** with extraordinary fidelity for some three thousand
years before being written. They are the supreme authority to which every later Hindu system appeals — and
the authority whose rejection defines the heterodox systems (see [[saddarshana|ṣaḍ-darśana]]).

> **Dating.** No date can be fixed with certainty; Dasgupta records the classic guesses — Max Müller
> 1200 BCE, Haug 2400 BCE, **B. G. Tilak 4000 BCE** — and notes the Hindus themselves held the Vedas to be
> beginningless, revealed afresh at each creation.

## The Four Saṃhitās

"Veda" names not a book but the **literature of an epoch**. The four collections (*Saṃhitās*):

- **[[rig-veda|Ṛgveda]]** — the oldest and most important; hymns to the gods, arranged by deity.
- **Sāmaveda** — the book of **chants**; almost entirely verses drawn from the Ṛgveda, set to melodies for
  the Soma sacrifice.
- **Yajurveda** — the **sacrificial prayers** (*yajus*), arranged in the order of ritual use, with original
  prose formulas.
- **Atharvaveda** — **spells and incantations**; later in form and, per Macdonell (quoted by Dasgupta), "a
  much more primitive stage of thought" — a book of magic appealing to the demon world.

## The Four Strata

Each Veda accreted four layers, marking a movement from ritual toward philosophy (Dasgupta, Ch. II–III):

1. **Saṃhitā** — the hymn collections themselves.
2. **Brāhmaṇas** — prose treatises on the **sacrifice** and its meaning; "all intellectual
   activity concentrated on the sacrifice" (Macdonell). Closing by c. 500 BCE.
3. **Āraṇyakas** ("forest treatises") — for those withdrawn to the forest; here **meditation on ritual
   symbols** begins to supplant the sacrifice itself (e.g. meditating on the dawn as the head of the
   sacrificial horse).
4. **[[upanishads|Upaniṣads]]** ("Vedānta," the end of the Veda) — the philosophical capstone, turning from
   the path of works (*karma-mārga*) to the path of knowledge (*jñāna-mārga*).

## Key Themes Tracked in the Wiki

- The **Vedic gods** as personified natural powers, and the **[[henotheism|henotheistic]]** stage of
  belief — each god exalted in turn ([[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]]).
- The growth of a **monotheistic tendency**: [[prajapati|Prajāpati]], Viśvakarman, Hiraṇyagarbha — and the
  emergence of **[[brahman|Brahman]]** as the supreme principle in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa.
- **Sacrifice as mechanical potency** and **[[rita|ṛta]]** (cosmic order) — together the first rudiments of
  the Law of **[[karma|Karma]]**.
- The **cosmogonic hymns** — the **Nāsadīya** (Ṛgveda X.129, "Then there was neither being nor not-being")
  and the **Puruṣa-sūkta** (X.90) — and the development of **[[atman|ātman]]** from "vital breath" to the
  self of the world.

## Canon and Reception

The Vedas are fully canonical *śruti* for [[vedic-religion|Vedic religion]] and [[hinduism|Hinduism]];
acceptance of their authority is the very definition of an *āstika* (orthodox) system. They are **rejected**
by the *nāstika* systems — [[buddhism|Buddhism]], Jainism, and Cārvāka materialism — whose denial of Vedic
authority marks them as heterodox.
