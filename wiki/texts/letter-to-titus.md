# Letter to Titus

## Overview

Titus is the shortest of the three Pastoral Epistles (659 words in Greek), addressed to Paul's coworker Titus, whom Paul has left on the island of Crete to "put in order what was left unfinished" and "appoint elders in every town" (1:5). Despite its brevity, Titus contains some of the PE's most concentrated theological content: the most explicit identification of Christ as "God and Savior" in the Pauline corpus (2:13); a dense statement on the *didactic* function of saving grace (2:11–14); and the fullest treatment of Spirit-effected regeneration in the PE (3:5–6).

Crete had a Jewish presence from early: Cretan Jews were present at Pentecost (Acts 2:11) and may have carried the gospel back. The island had a reputation in the ancient world for dishonesty — the "Cretan liar" paradox, and the citation in 1:12 of a Cretan prophet (generally identified as Epimenides): "Cretans are always liars, evil brutes, lazy gluttons." Paul uses this self-description to underscore the magnitude of the task awaiting Titus. The opponents (1:10–16) are "of the circumcision group" (1:10) and promote "Jewish myths" (1:14) — probably similar to the Ephesian false teachers but with a more explicitly Jewish-legalist character.

## Historical Situation

The letter presupposes Paul's freedom of travel and activity beyond what is described in Acts, consistent with a post-Acts release from a first Roman imprisonment and renewed missionary travel. Titus is known from Galatians (2:1–3) as Paul's uncircumcised Greek companion at the Jerusalem council (ca. AD 47), where he stood with Paul against "false brethren" pressing for his circumcision. He is also prominent in 2 Corinthians as a key emissary and negotiator between Paul and the Corinthian church over both their rocky relationship and the Jerusalem collection (2 Cor 2:13; 7:6–7, 13–15; 8:6, 16–24). 

By the time he receives this letter as the delegate charged with church-planting consolidation on Crete, Titus is a seasoned veteran with nearly two decades of ministry experience. Yarbrough notes that the brevity, challenging vocabulary, and idiomatic shorthand of the letter reflect their long shared association and mutual background as native Hellenistic Greek speakers. Titus was not a novice, so Paul could give rapid-fire, demanding instruction.

The opponents in Crete are "many rebellious people, full of meaningless talk" (1:10), particularly "those of the circumcision group" who "teach things they ought not to teach" to gain money (1:11). They claim to know God but deny him by their actions (1:16). The specific content is Jewish-mythological: "Jewish myths" (*mýthois Ioudaikois*) and "commands of those who reject the truth" (1:14). Schlatter: the opposition reflects the same dynamic of speculative, knowledge-claiming, and morally disordered teaching found in the other PE.

## Structure

1. **Greeting** (1:1–4) — an unusually elaborate Pauline preamble, the third-longest in the Pauline corpus (after Romans and Galatians). It sandwiches dense theological history between the sender and recipient: Paul identifies himself unusually as a "servant of God" (matching Peter, James, and Jude), grounds his mission in the hope of eternal life promised by a non-lying God "before the beginning of time," and addresses Titus in "our common faith."
2. **Elder Qualifications and False Teaching** (1:5–16) — appointment of elders; the Cretan context; silencing the opponents
3. **Pastoral Direction by Age and Status** (2:1–10) — Titus's teaching to older men, older women, younger women, younger men, slaves
4. **Motivation for Ministry: The Two Epiphanies** (2:11–15) — the saving grace that "appeared"; training for godliness; waiting for "the blessed hope"; Christ's self-giving
5. **Standing Orders for Believers** (3:1–11) — submission to authorities; gentleness toward all; regeneration by the Spirit; "trustworthy saying"; divisive person to be warned twice, then rejected
6. **Closing Requests and Blessing** (3:12–15)

## Major Themes

### 1. The Two Epiphanies (2:11–14)

The theological centerpiece of Titus. Paul describes the structure of Christian life as bracketed by two divine *epiphaniai* (appearances):

- **The first epiphany (past)**: "The grace of God has appeared (*epephanē*), bringing salvation to all people" (2:11) — the incarnation. Yarbrough: the aorist *epephanē* denotes "a unique, great fact of salvation history" (Siebenthal). This grace *teaches* (*paideuō*) believers to renounce ungodliness.

- **The second epiphany (future)**: "The appearing (*epiphaneia*) of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (2:13) — the parousia. Believers live "in this present age" (v. 12) defined and energized by both.

The word *epiphaneia* (rather than *parousia*) emphasizes the resplendent, dazzling, unmistakable character of the divine appearing — glory breaking visibly into history. This is consistent throughout the PE (1 Tim 6:14–16; 2 Tim 1:10; 4:1, 8).

### 2. Christ as "Our Great God and Savior" (2:13)

"The blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (2:13). This is one of the clearest NT affirmations of Christ's full divinity, secured by Granville Sharp's canon: when a single Greek article governs two nouns joined by *kai* (both referring to personal beings), they designate the same person. "Our great God and Savior" = Jesus Christ. Wallace's study of Sharp's canon confirms this is "as secure as any verse in the canon when it comes to identifying Christ as *theos*." Most commentators through history (including Chrysostom and Calvin) have read it as a Christ-as-God text.

### 3. Regeneration by the Spirit (3:5–6)

"He saved us, not because of righteous things we had done, but because of his mercy. He saved us through the washing of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out on us generously through Jesus Christ our Savior." This is the PE's fullest pneumatological statement. The "washing of rebirth" (*loutron paliggenesias*) may refer to baptism as the external sign of the inner renewal wrought by the Spirit; "renewal by the Holy Spirit" as the effective spiritual transformation. The Trinitarian structure is explicit: saved through the Spirit, poured out through Christ Jesus, whose work is the Father's saving act.

### 4. *Sōtēr* Concentration

Titus has the highest concentration of *sōtēr*-word-group language in Paul: 10.62 occurrences per thousand words (compared to 0.70 in Romans). God is Savior three times (1:3; 2:10; 3:4); Christ is Savior twice (1:4; 3:6); "our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" equates them (2:13). The *sōtēr* concentration is not incidentally about salvation but about the *identity* of the God who saves — Father and Son mutually described as *sōtēr* in a way that the NT parallels most closely in the LXX's description of YHWH.

### 5. Good Works as Fruit

*Ergon* (work/deed) is the second-ranked vocabulary cluster in Titus after God-language. Good works are not meritorious but are the expected and exuberant outcome of salvation (2:14: Christ purified "a people that are his very own, eager to do what is good"). The Cretan context makes this emphasis contextually appropriate: opponents who "claim to know God but by their actions they deny him" (1:16) must be countered by communities whose actions declare what their words proclaim.

## Interpretive Cruxes

1. **Titus 2:13 — "Our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ"**: Does *megas theos* ("great God") refer to the Father or to Christ? Granville Sharp's canon and the majority of interpreters favor Christ as the referent. A minority reads it as "the great God [the Father] and [of] our Savior Jesus Christ" — making two separate persons. This is a significant Christological controversy. See the note on the Granville Sharp canon above.

2. **Titus 1:12 — The Cretan liar**: Paul cites Epimenides' self-referential paradox ("Cretans are always liars") approvingly. Does this raise the liar's paradox? The standard resolution: Paul is not endorsing the self-referential universal; he is endorsing the general reputation of Cretans as practically accurate about typical Cretan moral tendencies.

3. **Titus 2:11 — "Salvation to all people" (*pāsin anthrōpois*)**: Is this universal salvation or salvation universally available? Yarbrough: salvation is "universally available" (the possibility is extended to all) but not universally actualized (some reject it, as 1:15–16 makes clear). "Just as the one sun and its light are for all human beings" (Schlatter) — the grace has appeared to and for all, but not all receive it.

4. **Titus 3:5 — "Washing of rebirth" (*loutron paliggenesias*)**: Does this phrase refer to baptism (sacramental reading) or to the Spirit's inner work (nonsacramental reading)? The connection between baptism and regeneration is debated across the tradition. Yarbrough leaves the sacramental question open while noting the priority of the Spirit's renewal in the passage.
