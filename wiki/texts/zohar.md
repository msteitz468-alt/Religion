# The Zohar (Sefer ha-Zohar)

*The Book of Radiance.* The foundational canonical text of Jewish mysticism (Kabbalah), comprising a vast running commentary on the Torah together with a rich body of homily, narrative, theosophic speculation, and ethical instruction. The Zohar exceeds the dimensions of a normal book; it is a literature, comprising more than twenty discrete sections, the bulk of which is a Torah commentary from Genesis through Deuteronomy. In traditional piety it was ranked alongside the Bible and Talmud as a divinely revealed source of religious truth.

The Pritzker Edition translation and commentary (Stanford University Press, 2004–) is the first scholarly English translation based on a newly constructed critical text from original manuscripts. Volumes I–IX (translated by [[daniel-matt|Daniel C. Matt]]) cover the main running Torah commentary from Genesis through Deuteronomy. Volume X (translated by Nathan Wolski) presents the Midrash ha-Ne’lam, the earliest stratum of the Zoharic corpus. The wiki has ingested the main Torah commentary in **Volumes I–IX** and additional material in **Volume X**:
- **Volume I** — the **Haqdamah** (Proem), **Be-Reshit** (Gen 1:1–6:8), and **Noaḥ** (Gen 6:9–11:32)
- **Volume II** — **Lekh Lekha, Va-Yera, Ḥayyei Sarah, Toledot, Va-Yetse** (Gen ~12–32): the Abraham, Isaac, and early Jacob material
- **Volume III** — the close of Genesis: **Va-Yishlaḥ, Va-Yeshev, Mi-Qets, Va-Yiggash, Va-Yeḥi** (Gen ~32–50): the Jacob and Joseph cycles
- **Volume IV** (ingested 2026-06-07) — the opening of Exodus: **Shemot, Va-Era, Bo, Be-Shallaḥ, Yitro** (Exod 1:1–20:23). Covers Pharaoh’s enslavement of Israel, the birth and call of Moses, the ten plagues and Passover, the deliverance from Egypt and the miraculous splitting of the Red Sea (with the Song of the Sea and the revelation of the 72-letter Name), wilderness provision and the war with Amalek, Jethro’s arrival and counsel, and the theophany at Sinai with the Ten Commandments.
- **Volume V** (ingested 2026-06-07) — continuation into the covenant laws and Tabernacle: **Mishpatim** (Exod 21:1–24:18, including the major embedded *Sava de-Mishpatim*, "Old Man of Mishpatim") and **Terumah** (Exod 25:1–27:19, beginning of the Dwelling instructions), plus *Sifra di-Tsni’uta* ("Book of Concealment").
- **Volume VI** (ingested 2026-06-07) — the Golden Calf crisis and Tabernacle completion: **Ki Tissa** (Exod 30:11–34:35, including the Golden Calf as rejection of Shekhinah and Moses’ intercession invoking the patriarchs) and **Va-Yaqhel / Pequdei** (Exod 35:1–40:38, the actual construction under Bezalel and the glory of YHWH filling the completed Dwelling).
- **Volume VII** (ingested 2026-06-07) — early-to-mid Leviticus: **Va-Yiqra and Tsav** (sacrificial offerings as sefirotic symbols, e.g. the *olah* as Shekhinah ascending to unite with the blessed Holy One), **Shemini** (Nadab and Abihu’s death read as failure to include Shekhinah and to imitate the divine male-female union through marriage), **Tazria/Metsora** (purity laws as sefirotic balance), and **Aḥarei Mot** (Yom Kippur and holiness requirements). Emphasizes theurgy sustaining the *zivvuga qaddisha*.
- **Volume VIII** (ingested 2026-06-07) — completion of Leviticus (**Qedoshim, Emor, Be-Har, Be-Ḥuqqotai**: the Holiness Code’s ethical and social demands read as practical sanctification and maintenance of divine union) and opening of Numbers (**Naso**, including the *Idra Rabba*; Torah study as theurgic act superior to sacrifice; conjugal union timed to arouse supernal holiness; nazirite and priestly blessing as channels of theurgy). Continues the theme of human action (study, ethics, ritual, union) affecting the upper worlds during the wilderness journey.
- **Volume IX** (ingested 2026-06-07) — completion of the main Torah commentary: remainder of Numbers (**Shelach Lekha** and following: the spies/scouting of Canaan read as mystical drama with bodies dying in the desert while souls resemble denizens of the Garden; other parashot on rebellion, purity, leadership) and the entirety of Deuteronomy (Moses’ farewell addresses as theurgic covenant renewal; visionary and soul themes in Rav Metivta; the Yanuqa/Child as precocious teacher; theurgic dimensions of Shema, land, blessings/curses, Moses’ death and ascent). With this volume, Matt’s Pritzker Edition Volumes I–IX cover the Zohar’s full running commentary on the entire Torah (Genesis through Deuteronomy). The Zohar’s main Torah commentary is now complete in the wiki.
- **Volume X** (ingested 2026-06-07) — the **Midrash ha-Ne’lam** (“the Concealed Midrash” or “Midrash of the Concealed”), recognized as the earliest layer of the Zoharic literary corpus. This volume presents a distinct, more midrashic and “concealed” stratum (primarily on Genesis and the patriarchs), with strong emphasis on the soul, the fate of the righteous and wicked, divine justice and accountability (the “Bookkeeper”), hidden wisdom conveyed to the righteous, and the body-soul analogy for God’s relationship to the generation. It stands at the origin of the Zoharic tradition, offering a different hermeneutical voice — more classical midrash with Kabbalistic concealment — compared to the full theosophic/theurgic running commentary of Vols. I–IX.
- **Volume XI** (ingested 2026-06-07) — **Midrash ha-Ne’lam and Zoharic Compositions** (trans. and annot. Joel Hecker). This volume presents additional strata of the *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* on the Megillot (Song of Songs / Shir ha-Shirim, Ruth, and Lamentations / Eikhah) together with the independent “Zoharic Compositions” — concentrated theosophic units including *Matnitin* (“Teachings”), Toseftot, a *Zohar on Song of Songs*, and above all the *Idra Zuta* (“Small Assembly”). Many of these texts were first printed under the misleading title *Zohar Ḥadash* (“New Zohar”); Hecker emphasizes that they comprise some of the *earliest* Zoharic material. The *Idra Zuta* is the dramatic account of Rabbi Shim’on bar Yoḥai’s deathbed discourse, in which the Holy Lamp discloses the most recondite secrets as his soul departs. These compositions represent a distinct genre of mystical assembly and concealed midrash, distinct from (yet complementary to) the running Torah commentary.
- **Volume XII** (ingested 2026-06-07) — **Zoharic Compositions** (trans. and annot. Nathan Wolski and Joel Hecker). This final volume of the Pritzker Edition completes the presentation of the independent “Zoharic Compositions” (the non-running-commentary, esoteric strata begun in Vol. XI). It carries a joint Introduction by Wolski and Hecker. Wolski translated and annotated the Heikhalot Be-Reshit, Heikhalot Pequdei, and Piqqudin units; Hecker the remainder (Raza de-Razin, Sitrei Otiyyot, Qav ha-Middah, Merkevet Yeḥezqel, Zohar al ha-Parashot, and passages mislabeled as Tosefta). The Introduction stresses that many of these sections, though printed under the title *Zohar Ḥadash* (“New Zohar”), comprise some of the *earliest* Zoharic material — a bibliographic misnomer. Major units: the *Heikhalot* (two versions; theurgic prayer praxis in which the worshiper adorns Malkhut/Shekhinah’s halls/maidens for union with the sefirot and Binah — the “seventh with seventh”); *Raza de-Razin* (“Secret of Secrets,” physiognomy, metoposcopy, and chiromancy for reading character and fate from the body, with distinctive emphasis on the mutability of physical features through sin or *teshuvah*); *Sitrei Otiyyot* (secrets of the YHVH letters that engrave and generate the sefirot as divine “chariots”); *Qav ha-Middah* (the luminous Line of Measure emanating from Ein Sof that gives scale, shape, light, color, and permanence to the sefirot); plus Piqqudin (commandments in festival/theurgic context), Merkevet Yeḥezqel, Zohar al ha-Parashot, and Tosefta-style addenda (Torah’s apotropaic power, Moses’ staff, blending of Compassion and Judgment, supernal shofar as Binah on Shabbat eve). These self-contained theosophic treatises emphasize letter and name mysticism, theurgy of prayer and adornment for divine union (*zivvuga qaddisha*), Ein Sof emanation, and embodied esoteric knowledge. See [[heikhalot|Heikhalot]] and [[raza-de-razin|Raza de-Razin]].

> ✅ **Genesis coverage complete** (Vols. I–III). **Volume IV** brought the foundational redemption narrative and initial Sinai covenant. **Volume V** advanced into the laws and Tabernacle instructions (Mishpatim + Terumah as Shekhinah dwelling, *Kallat Moshe*, gilgul via Sava). **Volume VI** completes the Exodus arc with the Golden Calf crisis (Shekhinah rejected and departing) and the theurgical building + dedication of the Tabernacle (glory filling as full indwelling restored). **Volumes VII–VIII** complete the Zoharic treatment of Leviticus (sacrifices, purity, and the full Holiness Code as theurgy of Shekhinah union) and begin Numbers. **Volume IX** completes the main Torah commentary (remainder of Numbers through end of Deuteronomy). **Volume X** adds the earliest stratum of the Zoharic corpus: the Midrash ha-Ne’lam (concealed midrashic layer on Genesis, souls, hidden wisdom, and divine justice). **Volume XI** (Hecker) adds further *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* on the Megillot (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations) plus the independent Zoharic Compositions — including the *Idra Zuta* (Small Assembly), the dramatic theosophic deathbed discourse of Rashbi — representing some of the earliest and most esoteric strata of the corpus. **Volume XII** (Wolski and Hecker) completes the Zoharic Compositions (Heikhalot theurgic prayer praxis with two versions; Raza de-Razin physiognomy with emphasis on mutability through teshuvah; Sitrei Otiyyot on YHVH letters generating sefirot as chariots; Qav ha-Middah from Ein Sof; plus Piqqudin, Merkevet Yeḥezqel, Zohar al ha-Parashot, and Tosefta-style addenda). The main Torah commentary (Pritzker Vols. I–IX by Matt) is complete; Vols. X–XII introduce the concealed midrashic and independent composition strata (many mislabeled “New Zohar” in print but among the earliest), bringing the full Pritzker Edition (I–XII) into the wiki. No further Zohar material from the Pritzker set remains uningested. See [[heikhalot|Heikhalot]] and [[raza-de-razin|Raza de-Razin]].

## Structure of Volume XI (Joel Hecker): Midrash ha-Ne’lam and Zoharic Compositions

Volume XI (translated and annotated by [[joel-hecker|Joel Hecker]]) marks a deliberate shift in the Pritzker Edition: the first volume to present a variety of Zoharic *genres* rather than the continuous running Torah commentary of Vols. I–IX or the Genesis-focused *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* of Vol. X. As Hecker writes in the Preface:

> This eleventh volume in the series is the first to present a variety of Zoharic texts, reflecting the range of genres within the Zoharic library. Many of these sections first appeared in print in the publication that was titled *Zohar Ḥadash*, or *New Zohar*; this title is misleading, however, in that the volume comprises some of the earliest Zoharic material.

The volume is divided into two main parts:

### Additional Midrash ha-Ne’lam on the Megillot
- **Midrash ha-Ne’lam on Song of Songs (Shir ha-Shirim)**: A relatively short but dense unit. It does not offer a conventional running commentary on the Song; instead it uncovers concealed layers of meaning — the love between the divine and Israel / the soul read through the lens of the sefirotic drama and the inner life of the Godhead.
- **Midrash ha-Ne’lam on Ruth**: Opens with the verse “In the days when the judges judged …” (Ruth 1:1) and immediately links it to cosmic angelic praise: “The rabbis explained the verse *When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy* (Job 38:7). Who are the morning stars? Holy angels ruling by day. Who are the sons of God? Angels ruling by night. … Everything that the blessed Holy One created, He created only for His glory, as is said: *Everyone who is called by My name, whom I created for My glory* (Isaiah 43:7).” The stratum continues the soul- and glory-centered concealed midrash seen in Vol. X, now applied to a book of exile, ḥesed, and redemption.
- **Midrash ha-Ne’lam on Lamentations (Eikhah)**: Applies the same concealed mode to the book of destruction and exile, deepening the Zohar’s portrait of Shekhinah in exile and the hidden divine response to catastrophe.

These units extend the “earliest” midrashic-concealed voice (proto-theosophic, focused on soul, glory, angelic realms, and divine justice) beyond Genesis into the Megillot.

### Zoharic Compositions
The second half presents the independent, non-commentary “Compositions” that form some of the most concentrated and dramatic expressions of Zoharic thought:
- *Matnitin* (“Teachings”) and Toseftot (supplements): Short, aphoristic or supplementary theosophic teachings, often extremely dense and allusive.
- A *Zohar on Song of Songs* in this compositional mode.
- **The *Idra Zuta* (“Small Assembly”)**: The crowning unit. In this intimate gathering of Rabbi Shim’on bar Yoḥai (the “Holy Lamp,” *Botsina Qaddisha*) with his closest Companions on the day of his death, the most recondite secrets of the Godhead are disclosed. The discourse itself is theurgic and revelatory; Rashbi’s soul departs in the midst of the revelation. The *Idra Zuta* is frequently regarded as the literary and theological apex of the entire Zoharic corpus — a mystical “assembly” in which the circle enacts and unveils the inner dynamics of the sefirot, the erotic union of the divine, and the ultimate fate of the soul. It stands in deliberate contrast (and complementarity) to the more expansive *Idra Rabba* encountered in Vol. VIII (Naso).

These compositions are not verse-by-verse exegesis of the weekly parashah but self-contained theosophic events. Their form — the gathering of the *Ḥavrayya*, the charged atmosphere, the departure of the master amid secrets — itself models the theurgic power of mystical fraternity and Torah discourse.

**Relation to prior volumes**: Vol. XI complements the main running commentary (I–IX, the systematic theosophic/theurgic reading of the entire Torah) and the Genesis *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* (X, earliest concealed midrash on creation and patriarchs) by displaying the full *generic* diversity of the Zoharic library from its earliest phases. The “New Zohar” label is shown to be a misnomer for some of the most primordial strata. The volume deepens the wiki’s tracking of Sod hermeneutics (now in concealed-midrash and assembly modes), the centrality of Shekhinah and *zivvuga qaddisha*, the theurgic efficacy of the mystical circle, and the figure of Rashbi (whose death in the *Idra Zuta* becomes the supreme moment of disclosure). It also enriches treatment of the Megillot (Ruth, Lamentations, Song of Songs) with Zoharic readings that resonate with exile, divine glory, and the soul’s journey.

Hecker’s scholarly apparatus (introductions, notes, and critical framing) provides the English reader with precise orientation to how these units sit within the broader evolution of 13th-century Castilian Kabbalah.

## Structure of Volume XII (Wolski and Hecker): Zoharic Compositions

Volume XII (translated and annotated by [[nathan-wolski|Nathan Wolski]] and [[joel-hecker|Joel Hecker]]) completes the Pritzker Edition’s coverage of the independent “Zoharic Compositions” — the non-running, highly esoteric strata of the corpus whose presentation began in Vol. XI (Hecker). The volume carries a joint Introduction by Wolski and Hecker. Wolski translated and annotated the Heikhalot Be-Reshit, Heikhalot Pequdei, and Piqqudin units; Hecker handled Raza de-Razin, Sitrei Otiyyot, Qav ha-Middah, Merkevet Yeḥezqel, Zohar al ha-Parashot, and the passages (mis)labeled as Tosefta. As the final volume, it underscores that “the Zohar” is a library of distinct genres and voices: the systematic running Torah commentary (Vols. I–IX), the concealed midrashic layers (Vols. X–XI), and these independent compositions (Vols. XI–XII).

The joint Introduction explicitly frames much of the material as belonging to the earliest phases of the Zoharic corpus:

> This volume presents a variety of Zoharic texts, reflecting the range of genres within the Zoharic library. Many of these sections appear in *Zohar Ḥadash*, or *New Zohar*; this title is a misnomer, however, since the volume comprises some of the earliest Zoharic material. Other than the masterly study and partial translation of *Sitrei Otiyyot* by Steven Wald, the vast majority of the material in this volume appears here in English for the first time.

### Major Units and Themes
- **Heikhalot (Halls/Palaces)**: Two versions, the first (Be-Reshit) much shorter than the second (Pequdei, more than three times the length and including halls of impurity). The divine halls are imagined as Malkhut/Shekhinah’s maidens or chambers arrayed for union with the upper sefirot and Binah (the “seventh with seventh”). Prayer and ritual are theurgic acts of adornment and preparation: “the worshiper arranges and adorns them in preparation for their union with the sefirot, and ultimately for the union of the male and female grades of Divinity.” The seventh hall is hidden and concealed; a multi-colored pillar stands at its center. Souls, patriarchs, and Israel in distress arouse Shekhinah for protection. This reimagines classical Heikhalot literature through Zoharic theosophy and deepens the central drama of *zivvuga qaddisha* and the divine feminine as the locus of encounter and blessing. See [[heikhalot|Heikhalot]].

- **Piqqudin (Commandments)**: Theurgic and festival-oriented treatment of commandments (e.g., the Ten Commandments situated in the context of Shavu’ot rather than weekly parashah exegesis; the supernal shofar symbolizing Binah sounded on Shabbat eve with halakhic parallels).

- **Raza de-Razin (“Secret of Secrets”)**: A treatise on physiognomy (reading temperament, character, and fate from physical appearance), metoposcopy (forehead lines and features), and chiromancy (palm and finger marks). Linked to the pseudepigraphic *Secretum secretorum* (Aristotelian tradition). It extends earlier Zoharic physiognomic passages and treats the body as a text revealing hidden spiritual realities — a form of Sod-level esoteric science. Distinctive Zoharic emphasis: physical features are mutable as a direct result of virtuous or sinful behavior and *teshuvah*, manifesting the tradition’s perennial pietism and opportunities for devotion. See [[raza-de-razin|Raza de-Razin]].

- **Sitrei Otiyyot (Secrets of the Letters)**: Describes the genesis of the sefirot from the letters of the Tetragrammaton (YHVH) “inscribed ethereally within the highest reaches of Divinity.” The letters engrave the sefirot on a heavenly tablet; the sefirot then function as “chariots” supporting and crowning the supernal letters. Opens with rhetorical questions from Psalms and Jeremiah on God’s ineffability and power. A core text for letter mysticism and theogony.

- **Qav ha-Middah (Line of Measure)**: The luminous, invisible divine instrument emerging from Ein Sof that “measures” — giving scale, shape, light, color, and permanence to the sefirot. Biblical resonances (Jeremiah 31:39, Zechariah 2:5 on measuring Jerusalem) are transmuted into a cosmogonic process: the “measuring rope” from the Infinite structures the divine realm itself.

- **Merkevet Yeḥezqel, Zohar al ha-Parashot, and Passages (Mis)Labeled as Tosefta**: Supplementary theosophic treatments of Ezekiel’s chariot vision, Torah portions, and editorial addenda on themes including Torah’s apotropaic/protective power against prosecuting angels and enemies; the staff of Moses (one of the ten primordial items, transformed at the burning bush, engraving of Judgment); and the blending of Compassion and Judgment in Torah and Moses.

These units emphasize theurgy (prayer as adorning Shekhinah for union; Torah and ritual as apotropaic and unitive), letter and name mysticism (YHVH generating the sefirotic structure), Ein Sof as source of emanation and measure, and esoteric physiognomic knowledge as part of the sod tradition.

**Relation to prior volumes and the Pritzker project**: Vol. XII completes the Zoharic Compositions begun in Vol. XI and, together with Vols. X–XI, supplies the non-running-commentary strata that complement the full Torah commentary of I–IX. Wolski’s two volumes (X and XII) bracket the Matt volumes with the earliest midrashic-concealed voice and the final theosophic compositions; Hecker’s contributions to both XI and XII further display the corpus’s generic and chronological range. The edition as a whole reveals the Zohar’s modular, multi-generic character from its Castilian origins. Hecker and Wolski’s annotations provide critical orientation to how these units evolved and were transmitted (including their appearance in *Zohar Ḥadash* prints) and situate the material within the broader development of Kabbalah, underscoring letter mysticism, the feminine divine grades, and the theurgic efficacy of both ritual and esoteric contemplation.

## Textual Overview

The Zohar was composed in medieval Castile, Spain, toward the end of the thirteenth century, written in a unique artificial Aramaic modeled on the Babylonian Talmud and Targum Onqelos but enriched with neologisms, puns, and deliberate obscurities. No single complete manuscript has ever existed; the text circulated in sections and booklets before being collated for print editions (Mantua, 1558–60; Cremona, 1559–60). Matt's Pritzker Edition reconstructs a critical text from approximately twenty manuscripts, restoring readings suppressed or smoothed over by the printed editions.

**Primary attributed speaker:** Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai (Rashbi; 2nd-century CE Tanna, student of Rabbi Akiva), whose circle of Companions (*Ḥavrayya*) wander the hills of the Galilee sharing Torah secrets. This attribution is a literary fiction (see [[zohar-authorship|Authorship Controversy]]).

**Actual authorship:** Scholarly consensus since Gershom Scholem (1941) identifies [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] (c. 1240–1305) as the primary author/compiler, with the text possibly being a collaborative circle product edited over several generations.

## Structure of Volume I

### Haqdamah (Proem)
The opening section introduces the work through a series of meditations on the verse *"The enlightened will shine like the radiance (*zohar*) of the sky"* (Daniel 12:3), from which the book takes its name. The Haqdamah contains some of the Zohar's most technically dense material, including:
- The "Thirteen Petals" homily — Shir ha-Shirim as a parable of divine-human union
- A cosmological meditation on the primordial dark light (*botsina de-qardinuta*, "a spark of impenetrable darkness") from which creation emanates
- The Rav Metivta section — a visionary account of heavenly academies
- An extended series of commandments (*mitsvot*) derived from the creation narrative, framing the entire Torah as a theurgical handbook

### Parashat Be-Reshit (Genesis 1:1–6:8)
The Zohar's interpretation of the creation account is its most celebrated exegetical achievement. Key sections:
- **Genesis 1:1** — The foundational re-reading: *bereshit bara Elohim* construed as "With beginning, It [Ein Sof] created Elohim [Binah]." Creation is *theogony*, the self-emanation of the divine, not cosmogony. God (Elohim/Binah) is the object of the first verse, not the subject. See [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]] and [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]].
- **Genesis 1:2** — Tohu, bohu, darkness, and wind mapped to divine names (Shaddai, Tseva'ot, Elohim, YHVH) and correlated with the Elijah theophany at Horeb (1 Kings 19:11–12)
- **Genesis 1:3–5** — The primordial light as a concealed treasure; the generation of the letters; the formation of Day (Yesod, Foundation) and Night (Shekhinah, Moon)
- **Genesis 1:6** — The firmament as the left side arousing conflict; the origin of Gehinnom (Hell) from the left's blazing judgment
- **Genesis 3** — Adam's sin as the separation of Shekhinah from Tif'eret; the diminishment of the moon; cosmic exile
- **Genesis 6:1–4** — The sons of God (fallen angels) and the root of the generation of the Flood's sin; sexual immorality as cosmic disruptor

### Parashat Noaḥ (Genesis 6:9–11:32)
- Noah as a righteous man of limited stature — unable to abolish death because Torah had not yet been given; the slime of the serpent (yetser ha-ra' as cosmic contamination) not yet removed
- The Flood as cosmic punishment specifically tied to the "wasting of seed" — the male and female waters failed to unite properly, so the punishment mirrors the crime
- Noah in the ark as hidden from the Destroyer (*Mashḥit*) — a typology of how the righteous survive judgment by concealment, not by direct combat
- The post-Flood world as still incomplete; completion awaited Israel at Sinai
- Elaborate discussions of the soul's tripartite structure (*nefesh*, *ruaḥ*, *neshamah*) and their origins in the sefirotic system
- Hell (*Gehinnom*): seven levels; relief on Sabbath and new moon; Kaddish as a theurgical release mechanism

## Structure of Volume II (Patriarchal Genesis, Gen ~12–32)

Volume II covers the Abraham, Isaac, and early Jacob narratives. Major loci:

### Parashat Lekh Lekha (Gen 12–17)
- **[[circumcision|Circumcision]] as the opening to the divine**: "before [[abraham|Abraham]] was circumcised, one rung spoke with him; once circumcised, **all those rungs rested upon him**." Circumcision = the covenant of [[tsaddiq|Yesod]] that admits the full sefirotic influx and guards against the [[sitra-ahra|Other Side]].
- Abraham = [[abraham|Ḥesed]] "aroused from the East," drawing the lower world (Malkhut) into a "sea"; the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] dwelling with [[sarah|Sarah]].
- **[[melchizedek|Melchizedek]]** (Gen 14): the meeting of "two worlds" (Malkhut and the supernal world); bread and wine; the priestly linking of the lower world to the Right.
- [[ishmael|Ishmael]]'s circumcision at a lower, outer grade.

### Parashat Va-Yera (Gen 18–22)
- The three visitors and the annunciation to [[sarah|Sarah]] "behind the Shekhinah."
- **[[lot|Sodom]]'s overthrow** as executed **Din**; Abraham's intercession as Ḥesed tempering judgment.
- **The Akedah** (Gen 22): "water versus fire" — [[abraham|Abraham]]/Ḥesed against [[isaac|Isaac]]/Gevurah — harmonized only when [[jacob|Jacob]] (Tif'eret) appears; Isaac (37) willingly bound. See [[akedah-interpretation|The Akedah]].

### Parashat Ḥayyei Sarah (Gen 23–25)
- [[sarah|Sarah]]'s death and burial in the cave of **Machpelah** (the gateway to the Garden of Eden — see [[zoharic-afterlife|The Soul and the Afterlife]]).
- The betrothal of [[rebekah|Rebekah]].

### Parashat Toledot (Gen 25–28)
- The twins struggling in [[rebekah|Rebekah]]'s womb: holy seed ([[jacob|Jacob]]) vs. the dross ([[esau|Esau]]/the Other Side).
- Rebekah's *din*-discernment securing the blessing for Jacob.

### Parashat Va-Yetse (Gen 28–32)
- **Jacob's ladder** at Bethel — the sefirotic axis joining the worlds.
- The **two wives = two worlds**: [[rachel|Rachel]] (lower, revealed — Malkhut/[[shekhinah|Shekhinah]], "You") and [[leah|Leah]] (upper, concealed — Binah/Jubilee, "He"); "through the revealed one reaches the concealed."
- The birth of the tribes from the four mothers.

## Structure of Volume III (Close of Genesis, Gen ~32–50)

Volume III carries the Torah commentary through the end of Genesis, dominated by the [[jacob|Jacob]] and [[joseph|Joseph]] cycles. Major loci:

### Parashat Va-Yishlaḥ (Gen 32–36)
- **Jacob wrestling the angel** (Gen 32:25): the "man" is **Samael, the heavenly Prince of [[esau|Esau]]**, champion of the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]]; Jacob's angels withdraw so he can wrestle the Prince and **force confirmation of the blessings**. The wounded thigh-sinew (*gid ha-nasheh*); healing by "the sun that was hidden away"; Jacob arrives *shalem* (complete).
- **Shekhinah couples with Jacob**; the holy camps; the "angel of YHVH" = Shekhinah.
- **Dinah and Shechem** read as the assault of the "impure side."
- **Rachel's death** so that the house could be "arrayed in twelve tribes"; the **twelve tribes as the array of [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]**; Reuben's disturbance of the bed (Gen 35:22) as a disgracing of the Shekhinah.
- **Esau/Edom and the eschaton**: Esau relinquishes his share of the cave of Machpelah and "the faith," clinging to this world; future judgment of Edom (Isa 63; Obadiah) inaugurates redemption.

### Parashat Va-Yeshev (Gen 37–40)
- **[[joseph|Joseph]] the Righteous = [[tsaddiq|Yesod]]**: "the righteous one is the foundation of the world" (Prov 10:25); Yesod as the all-gathering pillar, identified with **Zion**.
- **Resisting Potiphar's wife**: the image of [[jacob|Jacob]] appears; Joseph guards the **sign of the covenant** ("this covenant is the mystery of Joseph").
- **Judah and [[tamar|Tamar]]** (Gen 38): Tamar's act as righteous, "out of wisdom"; she and **[[ruth|Ruth]]** as "the two women through whom the seed of Judah was established… King David, King Solomon, King Messiah." **Er and Onan**'s wasted seed feeds the Sitra Aḥra.

### Parashat Mi-Qets & Va-Yiggash (Gen 41–47)
- Joseph's rise as the providential "raising of the head of the Righteous"; the "place of forgetting" (the cupbearer) as the end of the left/darkness.
- [[judah|Judah]]'s self-offering for Benjamin; Jacob's descent to Egypt "so that Shekhinah would be present there."

### Parashat Va-Yeḥi (Gen 47:28–50:26)
- **"Jacob did not die"**: he was "gathered to the moon" ([[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]); the sun (Tif'eret) does not die but goes to the moon. See [[zoharic-afterlife|The Soul and the Afterlife]].
- **The death of the righteous** shields the generation (Isa 57:1; [[tsaddiq|Tsaddiq]]).
- **The soul's afterlife journey**: the tripartite soul (nefesh/ruaḥ/neshamah); the cave of **Machpelah** as gateway to the **lower Garden of Eden**; the **dew of resurrection** waking the patriarchs.
- **Jacob's blessings** of the twelve sons, each mapped to its sefirotic/tribal root.

## Structure of Volume IV (Opening of Exodus)

**Parashat Shemot, Va-Era, Bo, Be-Shallaḥ (Beshallaḥ), Yitro** (Exodus 1:1–20:23)

This volume marks the wiki’s first Zoharic material on the Book of Exodus and the core redemption narrative. Per Matt’s preface to the volume, it treats “Pharaoh’s enslavement of the Israelites, the birth of Moses, the deliverance from Egypt, the crossing of the Red Sea, and the Revelation at Mount Sinai.” The Zohar explores the mystical dimension of these events with special attention to the nature of evil and its relation to the divine realm, the romance of Moses and Shekhinah, and the inner meaning of the Ten Commandments. A signature disclosure at the sea is the **Name of 72** (a 216-letter divine name of 72 triads, constructed from Exodus 14:19–21), revealed in the context of the splitting of the Red Sea.

### Parashat Shemot (Exod 1:1–6:1)
- Oppression in Egypt (Mitzrayim = “narrow straits”); the cry of Israel reaches the sefirotic structure.
- Birth and hiddenness of Moses; the daughter of Pharaoh and the “daughter of the Supernal King.”
- The burning bush and the progressive revelation of the divine name; Moses’ reluctance and the signs.

### Parashat Va-Era and Bo (Exod 6:2–13:16)
- The ten plagues as a sefirotic or cosmic-judgment process against the “Other Side” embodied in Egypt and its gods; Pharaoh’s hardened heart read as resistance or as the left side (Din / Sitra Aḥra) asserting itself.
- Passover and the “passing over”: protection tied to the covenant sign and the alignment of Israel with the holy side.
- The exodus itself as a historical and theogonic event — Israel’s “going out” affects the upper worlds and begins the redemption of Shekhinah from exile.

### Parashat Be-Shallaḥ (Exod 13:17–17:16)
- The splitting of the Red Sea (Yam Suf): the waters of the left side / Din parted so that the people (bride / Malkhut) may pass; the “right hand” or Ḥesed victorious.
- The **Song of the Sea** (Exod 15) as theurgic praise that crowns or unites the sefirot; Miriam leading the women as participation in the divine feminine.
- Wilderness trials: manna as bread from the higher sefirot (“bread of angels” / influx from Binah or Tif’eret); water from the rock (often identified with Shekhinah or the Tsaddiq).
- **War with Amalek** (Exod 17): Amalek as the archetypal embodiment of the Sitra Aḥra / Samael in history — the one who “cools” faith, attacks the “heels” (weak / Yesod / Shekhinah), and must be blotted out. “The Lord will have war with Amalek from generation to generation” is read as the ongoing cosmic struggle.

### Parashat Yitro (Exod 18:1–20:23)
- Jethro (priest of Midian): the righteous convert or outsider who recognizes YHWH, brings practical wisdom (the judicial hierarchy mirroring sefirotic order), and whose counsel is accepted by Moses.
- The theophany at Sinai and the Ten Commandments: the “ten words” correspond to the ten sefirot or the ten fingers of the Primordial Human; the revelation as the marriage or union of the divine with Israel; the inner (mystical) meaning of each commandment as theurgical maintenance of the sefirotic structure.

The volume thus applies the full Zoharic symbolic repertoire — sefirot, Shekhinah’s romance and exile, the Sitra Aḥra, theurgy, letter- and name-mysticism — to the historical narrative of liberation and covenant-making.

## Structure of Volume V (Mishpatim, Tabernacle Beginnings, Sava, Sifra di-Tsni’uta)

**Parashat Mishpatim** (Exodus 21:1–24:18) and **Parashat Terumah** (Exodus 25:1–27:19), together with the embedded *Sava de-Mishpatim* and *Sifra di-Tsni’uta*.

This volume shifts from the dramatic narrative of liberation and theophany (Vol. IV) into the detailed covenant laws and the instructions for building the portable Dwelling (*mishkan*). Per Matt’s description in the edition, the focus is on the *mishkan* as the symbol of Shekhinah, the feminine divine presence who “dwells” among the people, ensuring intimacy especially with Moses — hence the epithet **Kallat Moshe**, “Bride of Moses.” The construction of the sanctuary is a theurgical act to draw the Shekhinah into earthly union.

### Parashat Mishpatim and Sava de-Mishpatim
- The civil, criminal, and ritual laws of the covenant (slavery, personal injury, property, festivals, etc.) receive mystical reinterpretation.
- The long independent composition **Sava de-Mishpatim** ("Old Man of Mishpatim") is one of the most celebrated strata of the Zohar. It features a mysterious old donkey-driver who engages the Companions with riddles and then reveals profound secrets. 
  - Famous parable of **Torah as a beautiful maiden** hidden in her palace: the devotee circles the palace, catches glimpses through the window, and gradually wins greater intimacy as she reveals herself — modeling the layered, progressive revelation of the Torah’s inner (Sod) meanings.
  - **Gilgul** (reincarnation / transmigration of souls): the first extensive treatment in Jewish literature. The old man links the biblical law of levirate marriage (*yibbum*) with the soul’s need for repair (*tikkun*) across lifetimes. A soul that has not fulfilled its task may return in another body (often through the levirate mechanism) to complete its rectification. This introduces a major new dimension of Zoharic anthropology and theodicy: suffering and childlessness can be read in light of prior-life debts or unfulfilled sparks.
- The Sava’s style is distinctive — more narrative, parabolic, and “troubadour-like” than the usual Zoharic homily.

### Parashat Terumah and the Dwelling
- Detailed instructions for the *mishkan* (Tabernacle/Dwelling), its courtyard, and furnishings: the Ark, Table, Menorah (lampstand), altar, curtains, boards, etc.
- For the Zohar, every element and material corresponds to sefirotic realities. The *mishkan* as a whole **symbolizes Shekhinah** (Malkhut), the indwelling presence. Its construction on earth enables the divine feminine to “dwell” with Israel and especially with Moses, enacting the *zivvuga qaddisha* (holy union) in the wilderness camp.
- The raw materials (gold, silver, bronze; blue, purple, crimson yarns; fine linen, goat hair, ram skins, acacia wood) are not merely practical but charged with symbolic power for drawing down the Shekhinah.
- The portable nature of the Dwelling underscores that divine presence travels with the people rather than being fixed to a single site.

### Sifra di-Tsni’uta (The Book of Concealment)
- A dense, poetic, highly esoteric composition included in the volume. It meditates on the most concealed aspects of the divine structure (often the “head” or upper sefirot, the primordial “skull,” hairs, eyes, and the balance of the sefirotic tree). It is one of the most technically difficult and influential sections of the Zohar, frequently commented upon in later Kabbalah (e.g., by Luria and others).

The volume as a whole deepens the theurgical and feminine dimensions of the Zohar: the laws and the sanctuary are not external commands or structures but means by which the Companions (and Israel) participate in the inner life of the sefirot and the union of the divine masculine and feminine.

## Structure of Volume VI (Golden Calf Crisis and Tabernacle Completion)

**Parashat Ki Tissa, Va-Yaqhel, and Pequdei** (Exodus 30:11–40:38).

This volume completes the great Tabernacle arc of Exodus in the Zohar. It treats the catastrophic interruption of the instructions by the Golden Calf and the subsequent theurgical work of actually constructing and dedicating the Dwelling (*mishkan*) so that the divine presence can fully indwell. Per Matt’s framing and the text itself, the Golden Calf is given special treatment as a rejection of Shekhinah, while the building under Bezalel and the final filling with glory represent the restoration of the union.

### Parashat Ki Tissa (Exod 30:11–34:35)
- The Golden Calf receives special Zoharic attention as a direct **rejection of Shekhinah**. Normally She would have restrained the wrath of the divine masculine aspect and prevented destruction; having been rejected by Israel, She departs, leaving the people vulnerable to judgment.
- Moses’ intercession is portrayed as heroic and theurgical: by invoking the three patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob), he “pins God’s arms, as it were,” immobilizing the wrath and saving Israel. This continues the motif of Moses as unique mediator and partner of Shekhinah.
- The crisis forces the proclamation of the Thirteen Attributes (Exod 34:6-7) and the renewal of the covenant — read in light of the need to restore the divine feminine presence.

### Parashat Va-Yaqhel and Pequdei (Exod 35:1–40:38)
- The actual construction of the Tabernacle: the willing donation of materials, the skilled work of Bezalel (filled with the spirit of God, wisdom, understanding, and knowledge in all craftsmanship), and the precise assembly of the structure, furnishings, and priestly garments according to the divine pattern given earlier.
- For the Zohar, this building is the theurgical counterpart to the instructions (Vol. V): it is the concrete act by which Shekhinah is drawn down and housed among the people. The *mishkan* is the realized symbol of Malkhut/Shekhinah; every vessel and curtain participates in the sefirotic array.
- The climax: the Tabernacle is erected and “the glory of YHWH filled the Dwelling” (Exod 40). This is the fulfillment of the promise “I will dwell among them” — the full indwelling and union restored after the rupture of the calf. The portable sanctuary travels with Israel as the locus of divine presence.

The volume thus dramatizes the central Zoharic tension and resolution: human sin (especially idolatry as rejection of the feminine divine) causes Shekhinah to withdraw; human theurgical action (covenant renewal, willing building according to the pattern) restores Her presence and the sefirotic harmony.

## Structure of Volume VII (Leviticus: Sacrifices, Priesthood, Purity, and Holiness)

**Parashat Va-Yiqra, Tsav, Shemini, Tazria, Metsora, and Aḥarei Mot** (Leviticus 1:1–18:30).

This volume marks the wiki’s first Zoharic material on the Book of Leviticus, shifting from the Tabernacle’s construction and dedication (Vols. V–VI) to the sacrificial system, priestly ordination, purity laws, and the demand for holiness that allow the divine presence to remain among the people. The Zohar consistently spiritualizes the rituals as symbols and mechanisms of the sefirotic drama—especially the union (*zivvuga qaddisha*) of the masculine and feminine divine (Tif’eret and Shekhinah).

### Parashat Va-Yiqra and Tsav (Lev 1–8)
- The five main offerings (olah/ascension, minḥah/grain, shelamim/peace, ḥaṭṭat/sin, asham/guilt) are read as dynamic interactions within the sefirot.
- The *olah* (completely consumed, “that which ascends”) symbolizes **Shekhinah** (the last sefirah) ascending to unite with Her beloved, the blessed Holy One (Tif’eret). Sacrifices are not merely gifts or substitutions but theurgic acts that stimulate and maintain the upper divine union.
- The detailed laws of the offerings and the ordination of Aaron and his sons (ch. 8) establish the priesthood as the human channel for this theurgy.

### Parashat Shemini (Lev 9–11)
- The inauguration of the sanctuary (Lev 9) is a moment of great joy for the blessed Holy One: when the Dwelling below is erected, a corresponding Dwelling is erected above. Moses cannot initially enter (Exod 40:35), but God calls to him (Lev 1:1), signaling acceptance.
- Nadab and Abihu’s offering of “alien fire” (Lev 10) receives special Zoharic interpretation. Their death is linked to:
  - Failure to marry (remaining “incomplete,” failing to imitate the divine male-female union).
  - Failure, in their contemplation of the divine qualities, to include **Shekhinah**. “Without Her, God is incomplete.”
- The clean/unclean food laws (Lev 11) are tied to maintaining the boundaries that allow Shekhinah’s presence and Israel’s distinctiveness.

### Parashat Tazria, Metsora, and Aḥarei Mot (Lev 12–18)
- The purity laws (childbirth, skin diseases/*tzara’at*, bodily discharges) are read as disruptions or restorations of sefirotic flow and balance. *Tum’ah* (impurity) is not merely physical or moral but affects the ability of Shekhinah to dwell.
- Aḥarei Mot includes the Yom Kippur ritual (Lev 16), which purifies the sanctuary and removes sin (the scapegoat to Azazel), ensuring the continued indwelling. Sexual and blood prohibitions (chs. 17–18) protect the holiness required for the divine union.

The volume as a whole presents Leviticus as the practical theurgy for sustaining the Tabernacle’s purpose: through sacrifice, priesthood, and purity, Israel participates in the inner life of the sefirot, draws Shekhinah down, and maintains the *zivvuga qaddisha*. The famous Zoharic saying “The blessed Holy One, Torah, and Israel are all one” is illustrated here—ritual action below affects and is affected by the divine above.

## Structure of Volume VIII (Leviticus Completion and Opening of Numbers)

**Parashat Qedoshim, Emor, Be-Har, Be-Ḥuqqotai (Leviticus 19–27) and Parashat Naso (Numbers 4:21–7:89, including the *Idra Rabba*).**

This volume completes the Zohar’s commentary on the Book of Leviticus and inaugurates its treatment of Numbers. Building on Vol. VII’s focus on sacrifices and purity as mechanisms for sefirotic union, Vol. VIII extends the theurgical lens into the ethical, social, and priestly commandments of the Holiness Code and into the wilderness legislation and profound theosophic disclosures of early Numbers. The emphasis remains on human action—ethical vigilance, properly timed conjugal union, Torah study, and ritual—as the means of arousing, sustaining, and participating in supernal holiness and the *zivvuga qaddisha* during Israel’s journey.

### Parashat Qedoshim (Lev 19:1–20:27)
- The famous opening “You shall be holy, for I, YHWH your God, am holy” (19:2) is read as a call to active participation in the divine structure. Holiness is not merely separation but imitation and stimulation of the upper union.
- The juxtaposition of the portion on sexual licentiousness with Qedoshim is deliberate: “Whoever is vigilant toward such licentiousness has surely been generated in holiness—all the more so, if he sanctifies himself with the holiness of his Lord.”
- Conjugal union itself is theurgic when performed at the proper hour: “One who desires to sanctify himself according to the will of his Lord should perform conjugal union only from midnight on, or at midnight; for at that hour the blessed Holy One appears in the Garden of Eden, and supernal holiness is aroused, so that is the time to sanctify oneself.” Human *zivvug* at the right time participates in and arouses the divine male-female coupling.
- “Holiness of Torah is a holiness transcending all others, and holiness of supernal concealed Wisdom surpasses all.”

### Parashat Emor, Be-Har, and Be-Ḥuqqotai (Lev 21–27)
- Priestly laws (Emor) and the regulations for festivals, sabbatical, and Jubilee (Behar) extend holiness into the social and economic order. The Jubilee in particular embodies a cosmic rhythm of release and restoration that mirrors sefirotic balance and the return of Shekhinah from exile.
- Be-Ḥuqqotai’s blessings and curses frame the consequences of alignment or misalignment with the divine flow: observance maintains the channels of blessing; violation disrupts the union and invites the Sitra Aḥra.

### Parashat Naso (Num 4:21–7:89) and the *Idra Rabba*
- The volume moves into Numbers with the laws of the nazirite vow (a special consecration whose theurgic weight parallels priestly service), the priestly blessing (*Birkat Kohanim*), and the ordeal of the *sotah* (jealousy ritual)—all read as mechanisms affecting the sefirotic economy.
- Embedded or closely associated is the *Idra Rabba* (“Great Assembly”), a climactic theosophic revelation in which Rabbi Shim’on bar Yoḥai and the Companions disclose profound secrets of the divine before a momentous departure. The wilderness setting underscores that deeper knowledge of the divine is required for the journey.
- A signature teaching: Torah study surpasses sacrifice. “Even if punishment is decreed upon someone from above, if he has engaged in Torah, this is better for him than all offerings and sacrifices of the world, and that punishment is torn up. Because he studies [Torah] for her own sake, the blessed Holy One is reconciled with him.”

The volume as a whole presents the end of Leviticus and the beginning of Numbers as the portable continuation of the Tabernacle theurgy: in the absence of the fixed land, study, ethical sanctification (especially sexual and social holiness), and ritual acts (including the timing of human union) become the means by which Israel keeps the divine presence present and the upper worlds in harmony throughout the long journey.

## Structure of Volume IX (Remainder of Numbers and Deuteronomy)

**Parashat Shelach Lekha through the end of Numbers and the entirety of Deuteronomy (Numbers 13–36; Deuteronomy 1–34).**

This volume completes the main running Torah commentary of the Zohar (the bulk of the work translated by Matt in Pritzker Volumes I–IX). It carries the Companions’ exegesis from the middle of Numbers (beginning with the spies in Parashat Shelach Lekha) through the conclusion of Deuteronomy. The emphasis remains on Sod-level theosophy and theurgy: human actions, words (especially Moses’), study, and ritual affect the sefirotic structure, Shekhinah’s presence, and the soul’s journey. The volume includes major visionary and narrative sections (Rav Metivta, the Yanuqa) that explore the afterlife, the soul, and the transmission of secrets.

### Parashat Shelach Lekha and subsequent Numbers parashot (Num 13–36)
- The spies sent to scout Canaan (Num 13) are read as a mystical drama. The “bodies” of the generation die in the desert (as decreed), but “as for the souls, no—like the denizens of the Garden.” The failure of the spies is tied to lack of faith in the upper worlds and Shekhinah’s protection; the punishment and the promise of the land are interpreted in light of sefirotic inheritance and the Other Side (Sitra Aḥra).
- Later parashot treat rebellion (Korah), leadership transitions, purity in the camp, the bronze serpent, Balaam’s oracles (as forced blessing from the side of holiness), and the final preparations for entry into the land. Throughout, the wilderness journey is theurgic: the people’s actions (or failures) maintain or disrupt the union above, with Moses as the unique mediator who reaches the level of Tif’eret.

### Deuteronomy (Devarim)
- Moses’ farewell addresses are theurgic acts of covenant renewal. The book as a whole is read as Moses (aligned with Tif’eret) transmitting the mystery of the divine name, the Shema as unification of the sefirot, and the land as the locus where the lower and upper worlds can be joined through observance.
- The Rav Metivta (Master of the Academy) section is a major visionary narrative: Rabbi Shim’on and the Companions journey to the Garden of Eden, encounter the souls of the righteous, and learn secrets of the afterlife, the structure of the soul, and the fate of the wicked. It provides one of the Zohar’s richest treatments of zoharic eschatology and the tripartite soul (nefesh, ruach, neshamah) in relation to the sefirot and the Pleroma-like realms.
- The Yanuqa (the Child) story: a precocious, somewhat rascally boy encountered by the Companions who alternately amazes, teases, and teaches them with profound (and sometimes cheeky) interpretations. It models the transmission of secrets across generations and the surprising sources of wisdom.
- Signature themes include peace (especially Sabbath) as that which sustains the world (“The world exists only by peace”); ritual acts (e.g., the shoe-striking in levirate/halitzah contexts) that calm the body and call down mercy on the soul; the cup of blessing in Grace after Meals as an “invitation” for the blessed Holy One; Esau as the “shell” (Other Side) that must be removed for the “kernel” (holy seed/Israel) to emerge.
- The conclusion (Moses’ death, the Torah as inheritance) is read as the ultimate theurgical handover: Moses’ soul ascends or unites, leaving the written Torah as the enduring channel for divine influx and the maintenance of the zivvuga qaddisha.

## Structure of Volume X (Midrash ha-Ne’lam)

**The Concealed Midrash — the earliest stratum of the Zoharic literary corpus.**

Volume X of the Pritzker Edition (translated and annotated by Nathan Wolski) presents the *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* (“the Concealed Midrash,” or “the Midrash of the Concealed”). All Zohar scholars recognize this as the earliest layer of the Zoharic corpus, standing at the very origin of the tradition. Unlike the main running Torah commentary of Vols. I–IX (the full theosophic and theurgic exegesis of the parashot), the Midrash ha-Ne’lam is a distinct, more midrashic and “concealed” voice — primarily focused on Genesis (and related biblical material), with a strong emphasis on the soul, the fate of the righteous and the wicked, divine justice and accountability, hidden wisdom, and allegorical reinterpretations of the patriarchal narratives.

This layer often employs a classical midrashic style (homilies on verses, dialogues among rabbis) infused with Kabbalistic concealment: secrets of the soul’s journey, reward and punishment in the afterlife (the “Bookkeeper”), and the hidden dimensions of creation and the righteous. It is less explicitly emanationist or sefirot-focused than the later main body, but proto-theosophic in its treatment of divine wisdom, the concealed God, and the soul’s relationship to the divine.

### Key Themes and Samples
- **Concealed wisdom and the righteous**: “The blessed Holy One removed wisdom above—it is His Name—to convey to His righteous servants, and two remained in the world; they are its existence.” Wisdom (often identified with the divine name or Hokhmah) is largely hidden, accessible only to the righteous as the sustaining force of the world.
- **Body and soul analogy**: “Like the conduct of the body with the soul, so the blessed Holy One deals with the generation.” God’s relationship to humanity (or the generation) mirrors the soul’s governance of the body — a key motif for understanding divine providence, reward, and the inner life.
- **Accountability and the afterlife**: Rabbi Yehudah laments, “It would be better for us had we never come into the world, and not have to give an account to the Bookkeeper!” (then recants). The “Bookkeeper” represents divine justice and the soul’s reckoning after death, a recurring concern with the fate of the righteous and wicked.
- **Creation by the word**: Discussions of creation “by a single utterance” (citing Ps 33:6), tying the hidden divine word to the structure of reality and the role of the righteous in sustaining it.

### Relation to the Main Commentary
The Midrash ha-Ne’lam offers an earlier, more concealed and midrashically oriented lens on the same Genesis material covered in Vols. I–III. It complements the full theosophic/theurgic reading by highlighting the soul’s hidden journey, divine justice, and the transmission of concealed wisdom to the righteous — themes that resonate with (and in some cases anticipate) later Zoharic developments like gilgul (see Vol. V) and the visionary/eschatological material in Vol. IX (Rav Metivta). Its presence at the “origin” of the Zohar literature underscores the tradition’s roots in midrashic exegesis while pointing toward the mature Kabbalistic theosophy of the main body.

Wolski’s edition provides the critical English access to this foundational stratum, with annotation situating it within the development of Zoharic thought.

## Major Themes

**1. Creation as divine biography (theogony)**
The Zohar reads the Torah not as the story of the world but as the story of God — specifically the story of how the infinite divine (Ein Sof) came to express itself in the ten sefirot, and how those sefirot relate to creation and to Israel. The first verse of Torah encapsulates this: Ein Sof (unknowable) → Ḥokhmah (first point, "beginning") → Binah (palace, womb, "God") → the seven lower sefirot → creation.

**2. The Divine Feminine (Shekhinah)**
The tenth sefirah, Malkhut/Shekhinah, is the divine Bride, the Moon, the Assembly of Israel, the channel through which divine blessing flows to the world. She is a full divine persona, distinct from the "blessed Holy One" (Tif'eret, the personal God of prayer). The Zohar's central theological drama is the *zivvuga qaddisha* (holy union) of Tif'eret and Shekhinah — a union that sustains cosmic order and whose disruption (by human sin) constitutes the catastrophe of exile. See [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]].

**3. Theurgy — commandments as cosmic maintenance**
Every mitsvah operates on the sefirotic structure. The Zohar supplies *ta'amei ha-mitsvot* (reasons for commandments) that are theurgical: circumcision binds the soul to the living being (Shekhinah) on the eighth day; Sabbath rest allows the Sitra Aḥra to hide while holiness rules; tefillin correspond letter-by-letter to the divine name and clothe the human in the image of Adam Qadmon (the Primordial Human). Religious practice is not merely covenantal obedience but metaphysical maintenance of the divine organism.

**4. The Other Side (Sitra Aḥra)**
Evil is not external to the divine but emerges from within it — specifically from the excess of Gevurah (divine judgment/left side) when it operates untempered by Ḥesed (love/right side). The demonic realm is a shadow-emanation of the sefirot, ontologically dependent on divine energy and human sin. Lilith, Samael, the serpent, and the Angel of Death all derive from this "other side." See [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]].

**5. The Zohar as Torah commentary**
Despite its dense mystical symbolism, the Zohar remains rooted in the biblical text. Every teaching emerges from the opening of a specific verse. The Companions model the practice of *pithḥa* (opening a verse) and then exploring its inner meaning through verbal analogy, letter-combination, numerical equivalence, and sefirotic correspondence. This is the Sod (secret) level of the [[pardes|PaRDeS]] hermeneutical system practiced at its highest and most demanding form.

## Hermeneutical Method

The Zohar operates almost exclusively at the **Sod** (סוד, secret/mystical) level of biblical interpretation. Key moves:
- **Re-parsing the verse order**: "bereshit bara Elohim" → "With beginning, It created Elohim" (subject and object inverted)
- **Identifying divine names with sefirot**: YHVH = Tif'eret; Elohim = Binah (or Malkhut); El = Ḥesed; Shaddai = Yesod; Adonai = Malkhut; Tseva'ot = Netsaḥ/Hod
- **Letter mysticism**: individual letters and vowel-points carry sefirotic meaning; the *yod* = Ḥokhmah; the *he* = Binah
- **Verbal analogy** (*gezerah shavah*): shared vocabulary across widely separated verses implies sefirotic identity
- **Narrative expansion**: Torah scenes are re-enacted in the heavenly realm simultaneously
- **Apocryphal citation**: the Zohar appeals to *The Book of Enoch*, *The Book of King Solomon*, *The Book of Adam*, and *The Book of Mysteries* as sources of hidden tradition, almost certainly pseudepigraphical devices rather than actual texts

The Zohar's language is deliberately obscure — oxymorons ("a spark of impenetrable darkness"), neologisms, grammatical inversions — because language can only *hint* at the ineffable sefirotic reality. As the Zohar says: translation always betrays; the Aramaic itself is "suited to the soul" even when not understood.

## Interpretive Cruxes

**"A spark of impenetrable darkness" (botsina de-qardinuta)**: The first movement of divine self-expression. Intensely bright yet invisible because beyond all categories. This oxymoron is the Zohar's signature theological move: the divine reveals itself by concealing itself more deeply. Cross-link: [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]].

**"It split and did not split its aura"**: The transition from Ein Sof to the first sefirah is both happening and not-happening — pure actuality in the moment before distinction is possible. This is the Zohar's version of the creatio ex nihilo problem: nothing precedes the first point, not even nothingness as a state.

**The diminution of the moon (Genesis 1:16)**: The moon was originally equal to the sun; God diminished her because she complained about sharing. The Zohar reads this as an allegory for Shekhinah's diminishment through sin and her eschatological restoration. The full moon = the perfected divine marriage; the waning moon = exile.

**Adam's sin as cosmic divorce**: "He drove out Adam" (Gen 3:24) — the Zohar asks: who divorced whom? The deeper reading: Adam drove out Shekhinah, separating her from Tif'eret, fragmenting divine unity. The first sin is not disobedience but the fracturing of divine integration.

**The generation of the Flood and sexual sin**: The Flood is specifically tied to the "waste of seed" — the misalignment of the cosmic sexual union mirrored in human sexual disorder. Water punishes water-related sin.

## Manuscript and Publication History

- No single complete manuscript of the Zohar exists; it was always distributed in sections
- Oldest surviving manuscripts: 14th–16th century, held in Oxford (Bodleian), Cambridge, Paris (BnF), Munich, Rome, Vatican, Parma, Toronto, JTS
- First print editions: Mantua (1558–60) and Cremona (1559–60), both constructed critical texts from multiple manuscripts
- Modern critical text: the Pritzker Edition (Matt, Stanford UP) — based on approximately twenty manuscripts, restoring older readings suppressed by the printed editions. The only scholarly critical edition in existence.
- Standard pagination: Mantua edition pagination (e.g., "1:34b") used as canonical reference

## Key Commentators and Scholars

- [[shimon-bar-yochai|Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai]] — attributed author (legendary frame)
- [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] — actual primary author/compiler (scholarly consensus)
- [[daniel-matt|Daniel C. Matt]] — translator, Pritzker Edition; constructed the critical Aramaic text
- Moses Cordovero (*Or Yaqar*, 16th c.) — the most systematic Zoharic commentator; Safedian Kabbalah
- Isaac Luria (the Ari, 1534–1572) — Lurianic Kabbalah transforms the Zohar's framework with tsimtsum (contraction), shevirat ha-kelim (breaking of the vessels), and tikkun (repair)
- Gershom Scholem (1897–1982) — father of modern academic Kabbalah study; established Moses de León's authorship (1941)
- Isaiah Tishby — *Mishnat ha-Zohar* (The Wisdom of the Zohar), the comprehensive thematic anthology with analysis
- Yehuda Liebes — key figures in contemporary Zohar scholarship; Pritzker Academic Committee
