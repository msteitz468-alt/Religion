# Kitáb-i-Íqán (Book of Certitude)

*All claims from Smith, Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000), lines 18784–18901.*

## Overview

The Kitáb-i-Íqán (Persian: "Book of Certitude") is considered by [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]] scholars and by [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] to be Bahá'u'lláh's outstanding doctrinal work and "the basis for unity between the religions of the world" (l. 18789). Shoghi Effendi described it as "the most important and influential of Bahá'u'lláh's early writings" (l. 18787).

## Composition and Circulation

Composed in Persian in Baghdad in 1278 AH / 1861–62 CE (l. 18791–92), it was written in response to questions from one of the as yet unconverted maternal uncles of the Báb, Hájí Mírzá Sayyid Muḥammad (l. 18794–96). It was soon widely circulated among the Bábís in Iran and was one of the first Bahá'í books to be printed (by lithography in India), perhaps as early as 1882 (l. 18799–18800). The first English translation was published in 1900; the present official translation by Shoghi Effendi was published in 1931 (l. 18772–18775).

## Contents and Argument

Asked to reconcile the Báb's claim to be the Qá'im with traditional Shí'í expectations, Bahá'u'lláh argues that contemporary rejection of the Báb echoes similar rejections throughout religious history (l. 18802–18810). His central contentions include:

- **Pattern of rejection**: In every age, people expected God's messenger but opposed him when he appeared. Religious leaders ignored truth either through ignorance or fear of losing their authority; others blindly followed these leaders rather than investigating truth for themselves (l. 18811–18820).
- **Literalism as veil**: Persistent literal interpretation of scripture (e.g., biblical verses about "stars" falling or the promised one coming in "clouds of heaven") obscured meaning that was to be understood metaphorically (l. 18821–18827).
- **Divine freedom**: God is not bound by human expectations — he chose Moses, a murderer, and Jesus, with no known father, as messengers, and directed Muhammad to change the Qiblah (l. 18828–18836). True understanding belongs only to those who trust God rather than human standards (l. 18836–18843).
- **Proof of messengers**: The essential proof of a divine messenger is his own self and the divine word revealed in his scripture; supporting signs include constancy under opposition, the transforming influence on followers, followers' willingness to die for the cause, and fulfilment of prophecy (l. 18846–18856).
- **Spiritual sovereignty**: Prophets do not exercise worldly kingship; their sovereignty is spiritual and transcendent, exercised over "all in heaven and on earth" (l. 18858–18862).
- **He Whom God Shall Make Manifest**: The text cryptically alludes to a future Bábí messianic figure, perhaps making covert reference to Bahá'u'lláh's own claims, shortly to be made explicit (1863) (l. 18872–18879).

The book also discusses [[manifestation-of-god|knowledge]], the spiritual path, and demonstrates Bahá'u'lláh's knowledge of the Bible and sympathy for Christianity — "both unusual in a 19th-century Islamic context" (l. 18882–18884).

## Significance

The Íqán became an important means of attracting Bábís and others to Bahá'u'lláh (including the uncle for whom it was written, l. 18888). Its appeal rested in part on its straightforward Persian prose style, contrasting markedly with existing Bábí writings and making it accessible to ordinary Iranians without clerical education (l. 18890–18894). Although written prior to Bahá'u'lláh's open claim to prophetic status, it was accorded the status of divine revelation by Bahá'ís (l. 18899–18901).

## Canon and Reception

Canonical for the Bahá'í Faith. Used in Bahá'í apologetics and in theological arguments for [[progressive-revelation|progressive revelation]]. Smith notes a "growing secondary literature" presenting the Bahá'í Faith as the prophetic fulfilment of other religions, which this text underpins (l. 18756–18758).

## Key Hermeneutical Moves

The Íqán is the locus classicus of Bahá'í scriptural hermeneutics: it argues against literalism, defends metaphorical/spiritual interpretation of prophecy (see l. 18743–18752), and insists that the "essential proof" of a messenger is his self and scripture, not miracles or worldly power.
