# The Letter to the Ephesians

The most expansive and ecclesiologically focused of the Pauline letters. If Galatians is Paul's most polemical letter and Romans his most systematic, Ephesians is his most doxological — sustained praise, intercession, and benediction inform its every movement. It is structured as a movement from divine indicative (God's cosmic work in Christ, chs. 1–3) to ethical imperative (how believers must therefore live, chs. 4–6), and its central concern is the unity of Jews and Gentiles in the body of Christ as a cosmic demonstration of God's wisdom to heavenly powers (3:10). The letter contains some of the longest and most syntactically complex sentences in the NT (1:3–14 is 202 words in Greek; 1:15–23, 2:1–7, 3:2–13, and 4:11–16 are similarly dense). This dense, liturgical quality has prompted both suspicion about Pauline authorship and admiration for the letter's theological depth.

## Authorship, Date, and Destination

**The authorship question** is the most contested introduction issue in the letter. Three positions:

- **Pauline authorship** (Thielman, Hoehner, O'Brien, Best in part): Paul wrote the letter c. AD 62, near the end of his Roman imprisonment (Acts 28), simultaneously with Colossians and Philemon. The unusual style is explained by the circumstances of dictation under imprisonment and the unusually reflective, non-polemical occasion. The long sentences are not without Pauline precedent (2 Thess 1:3–12; Rom 5:12–18; 2 Cor 2:12–7:7). The varied use of key terms (*mystērion*, *oikonomia*, *plērōma*) from Colossians is consistent with a highly educated author writing in related but distinct contexts.
- **Pseudonymous authorship** (Lincoln, MacDonald, Schnackenburg, Käsemann): A disciple of Paul wrote after the apostle's death (c. AD 80–90), synthesizing and updating Pauline theology for a changed situation. The letter's church-institutionalizing tendencies, its "early catholicism," and its use of Colossians suggest a later hand. The puzzling distance between the author and "Ephesian" readers is more intelligible on this view.
- **Pauline authorship with amanuensis**: Some argue Pauline thought mediated through a secretary (a minority position that satisfies neither camp fully).

See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

**The destination** is textually uncertain. The phrase "in Ephesus" (*en Ephesō*, 1:1) is absent from the three oldest manuscripts (𝔓46, א, B) and from what Basil called "the old copies." Origen and Basil both omit the phrase. The overwhelming majority of other witnesses — including some with Alexandrian affinities (A, 33, 81, 1175) — include it, and all manuscripts, including those that omit it in 1:1, give the letter the title "To the Ephesians." [[frank-thielman|Thielman]] defends the reading "in Ephesus" as original, arguing that omitting the phrase produces "almost unintelligible Greek" and that the erasure probably reflects a scribal tendency to universalize the letter (paralleled in Codex G's omission of "in Rome" from Rom 1:7). Whether the letter went exclusively to Ephesus or circulated more broadly as a general letter is debated; the letter's distinctly impersonal character (Paul "has heard" of his readers, 1:15; "assumes" they know his calling, 3:2) fits a wide circular better than a letter to a church where Paul had labored three years.

**If Pauline (c. AD 62)**: Paul had been absent from Ephesus for 7+ years and imprisoned for nearly 5 years. The Ephesian house churches had received little contact with Paul. Diversity had grown: some Jewish Christians never closely affiliated with Paul's Gentile mission; some house churches may have been drifting in ascetic-speculative directions (anticipating the situation of 1 Timothy). The letter addresses a community that is discouraged by Paul's imprisonment (3:13), disunified across ethnic and household lines, and under pressure to assimilate to the Greco-Roman culture of Ephesus — a city dominated by the Artemis cult, magical practices, and the imperial cult.

**Tychicus** (6:21–22) is commended as the letter carrier, in language that overlaps almost exactly with Col 4:7–9 (a 32-word stretch of consecutive agreement). Thielman sees this as evidence of Pauline authorship: Ignatius used identical commendations for the same courier in letters written close together.

## The Cultural Setting

**The Artemis cult**: Ephesus was the seat of the goddess's most famous temple (one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world). The city's economy partly depended on the Artemis trade (Acts 19:23–27). The late Paul of Acts left the city amid a riot sparked by his disruption of this trade. The population's intense devotion to spiritual powers (*archai*, *exousiai*, *kosmokratores*) forms the background for Ephesians' cosmic categories.

**The imperial cult**: The province of Asia reorganized its calendar (c. 9 BC) to center on Augustus's birthday, calling him *sōtēr* ("savior"), *theos* ("god"), and declaring his birth the beginning of *euangelia* ("good tidings") to the world. Ephesus built a temple to "Artemis, Emperor Caesar Augustus son of god, Tiberius Caesar son of Augustus, and the *dēmos* of the Ephesians" (c. AD 11–13). Visual imagery of Rome "trampling" conquered peoples underfoot (cf. Eph 1:22: God put all things "under his feet") permeated the urban landscape. Thielman argues that Paul's gospel vocabulary (*euangelion*, *sōtēr*, *kyrios*, "under his feet," the administration of "times" and "ages") functions as a counter-imperial claim: Christ, not Augustus, is lord of time, lord of history, and lord over all powers.

## Narrative Context and Major Figures

While Ephesians is highly theological and doxological, it is anchored in a specific narrative framework revolving around its author and his envoy:

- **[[paul-apostle|Paul]] the Prisoner**: Paul frames his current imprisonment not as a tragedy, but as a deliberate suffering "on behalf of you Gentiles" (3:1, 13). His status as an ambassador in chains (6:20) underlines his apostolic authority to declare the mystery of Gentile inclusion. He presents his sufferings as structurally necessary for the church's cosmic witness.
- **[[tychicus|Tychicus]] the Envoy**: The letter is carried by Tychicus, a "beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord" (6:21). His mission is pastoral and communicative: he is sent to "let you know how I am" and to "encourage your hearts" (6:22). Tychicus acts as the crucial narrative link connecting the imprisoned apostle in Rome to the congregations in Asia Minor.

## Structure

Paul moves from praise (chs. 1–3) to exhortation (chs. 4–6), the whole letter organized around what God has done in Christ (chs. 1–3) and how believers must therefore live (chs. 4–6).

**I. Prescript (1:1–2)** — Paul, apostle of Christ Jesus, to the saints in Ephesus

**II. The Great Benediction (1:3–14)** — 202-word blessing of God for every spiritual blessing in Christ. Divided by *en Christō/en hō* hinges into four sections: (A) Election and adoption (vv. 3–6, ending "in the Beloved"); (B) Redemption and revelation of the mystery (vv. 7–10, ending "in him"); (C) Inheritance and hope (vv. 11–12, ending "in Christ"); (D) Application to readers — hearing, believing, sealing with the Spirit (vv. 13–14). Each section of A-C includes a *kata* phrase (according to his will) and a purpose clause. Trinitarian in structure but heavily christocentric (10 "in Christ" phrases). See [[frank-thielman|Thielman]] for structural analysis.

**III. Thanksgiving and Intercession (1:15–23)** — Paul thanks God for the readers' faith and love; intercedes for their deeper understanding of "the immeasurable greatness of his power toward us who believe" (1:19). The power he prays they would understand is the same power that raised Christ and seated him far above all rulers and authorities (1:20–22) and made him head over the church, which is his body, "the fullness of him who fills all in all" (1:23). See [[principalities-powers|Rulers and Authorities in the Heavenly Places]].

**IV. From Death to Life (2:1–10)** — All humanity was "dead in trespasses and sins," following "the ruler of the realm of the air" (the devil), living as "children of wrath" (2:1–3). The divine response: "But God, being rich in mercy" (2:4) — made us alive together with Christ, raised us, and seated us with him in the heavenly places (2:5–6). The ground is grace through faith, "not of yourselves, it is the gift of God, not as a result of works" (2:8–9). Believers are "his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (2:10).

**V. The New Humanity (2:11–22)** — The theological heart of the letter. Gentile readers are reminded of their former status: "outside Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (2:12). The divine solution: Christ "is our peace" (2:14). His death tore down "the middle wall" — identified by Thielman with the Mosaic law as a fence enclosing Israel from Gentiles (Letter of Aristeas 139) — and created "one new human being" (*heis kainos anthrōpos*, 2:15) in himself. Both Jews and Gentiles are reconciled to God "in one body through the cross" (2:16). The result: Gentile believers are "no longer strangers and aliens but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (2:19), built on the foundation of apostles and prophets with Christ as the cornerstone, growing into "a holy temple in the Lord" (2:21). See [[eschatological-temple|Eschatological Temple]] and [[ephesians-middle-wall|The Middle Wall in Eph 2:14]].

**VI. Paul's Apostolic Task (3:1–13)** — Paul as "prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles" (3:1). The revelation of the *mystērion* (secret/mystery): the Gentiles are "fellow heirs, members of the same body, and partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (3:6). This was "hidden for ages" but "has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit" (3:5). Paul's specific vocation: to make all people see "the plan of the mystery" and "to bring to light for everyone what is the plan of the mystery hidden for ages in God" (3:9). The purpose: "that through the church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places" (3:10). See [[mysterion|The Mystery (*Mystērion*) in Paul]].

**VII. Intercession and Doxology (3:14–21)** — Paul's second prayer: that readers might be "strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner person," that "Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith," and that they might "know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge" (3:17–19). Doxology: "to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all we ask or think" (3:20).

**VIII–XIII. The Ethical Section (4:1–6:24)** — Grounded in the indicative of chs. 1–3, the imperative of chs. 4–6 moves through:
- **Unity and gifts** (4:1–16): "eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (4:3); Christ's ascension gifts — apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers — for the building up of the body to maturity (4:11–16)
- **The new humanity** (4:17–5:2): "put off the old self, put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (4:24)
- **Light and darkness** (5:3–14): avoid sexual immorality and greed; walk as children of light
- **The Household Code / *Haustafel*** (5:15–6:9): mutual submission (5:21); wives and husbands (5:22–33, with the husband-wife relationship interpreted through the Christ-church analogy); children and parents (6:1–4); slaves and masters (6:5–9). See [[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]].
- **The Armor of God** (6:10–20): stand against "the schemes of the devil"; the enemy is not "blood and flesh but the rulers, the authorities, the cosmic powers over this present darkness, the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places" (6:12). Six pieces of armor: belt = truth; breastplate = righteousness; shoes = readiness of the gospel of peace; shield = faith; helmet = salvation; sword = word of God/gospel.

## Major Themes

**1. Cosmic Christology and the Summing Up of All Things (*Anakephalaiōsis*)**
God's ultimate purpose is "to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth" (1:10, *anakephalaioō* — a term unique to this verse in the Pauline corpus). Christ is installed "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (1:21) with "all things under his feet" (1:22). This cosmic scope distinguishes Ephesians from Galatians and 1 Corinthians, where Christ's lordship is announced but not articulated in quite this sweeping spatial-temporal way.

**2. The Church as Cosmic Actor**
The church's role is not simply to receive salvation — it is to demonstrate "the manifold wisdom of God" to "the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places" (3:10). The church is thus a cosmic proclamation: its unity across ethnic, social, and gender lines announces the defeat of the powers that sustain division. Thielman: "Christians in Ephesus needed to work together harmoniously to bear an effective witness to the inimical powers that their efforts to frustrate God's work were doomed to failure."

**3. The New Humanity**
The *heis kainos anthrōpos* ("one new human being," 2:15) created by Christ's death is a corporate new-creation concept: Jews and Gentiles are not simply two groups that come to share a common faith, but are reconstituted into a third entity — a new humanity that has never before existed. This is the specifically Ephesian development of the new-creation theme (cf. 2 Cor 5:17; Gal 6:15). See [[new-creation|New Creation]].

**4. Election, Grace, and Predestination**
The benediction of 1:3–14 grounds everything in divine initiative: "he chose us in him before the foundation of the world" (1:4); "he predestined us for adoption to himself" (1:5). The purpose of this election is ethical holiness ("that we should be holy and blameless before him in love," 1:4) and doxological ("to the praise of his glorious grace," 1:6). Thielman follows the tradition (Calvin) that God's pre-creational choice is a demonstration of grace specifically because it precedes anything believers could do to earn it.

**5. The *Mystērion* (the Divine Secret Disclosed)**
The word *mystērion* appears eight times in Ephesians (1:9; 3:3, 4, 6, 9; 5:32; 6:19), more than in any other Pauline letter. In Ephesians its primary referent is the inclusion of Gentiles as full heirs alongside Jews, previously hidden but now revealed through the apostles and prophets. In 5:32 Paul applies it to the husband-wife relationship as a type of Christ-church relationship. The Ephesian *mystērion* is not esoteric knowledge reserved for initiates (against Gnostic readings) but a divine plan that was once hidden and is now publicly disclosed through the gospel. See [[mysterion|The Mystery (*Mystērion*) in Paul]].

**6. The Heavenly Places (*En Tois Epouraniois*)**
A phrase unique to Ephesians in the NT (1:3, 20; 2:6; 3:10; 6:12). It refers to a "dimension" of existence rather than a spatial location: God, the risen Christ, and believers-in-Christ occupy the heavenly places (1:3, 20; 2:6), but so do the hostile spiritual powers (6:12). The phrase signals that the struggle believers face is not merely social or moral but participates in a cosmic drama. See [[principalities-powers|Rulers and Authorities in the Heavenly Places]].

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. Pauline or Pseudonymous?**
See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

**2. The "Middle Wall" (2:14)**
What exactly was torn down? The Jerusalem temple's balustrade? The cosmic barrier between heaven and earth? The Mosaic law? Thielman's reading: the Mosaic law, understood as an enclosing fence that separated Israel from Gentiles (citing Letter of Aristeas 139 and Jewish first-century practice). See [[ephesians-middle-wall|The Middle Wall in Eph 2:14]].

**3. The Household Code (5:21–6:9)**
The extent of mutuality in 5:21 ("submitting to one another"), the nature of husbandly headship, and whether the code reinforces or subverts Greco-Roman patriarchal norms remain contested. See [[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]].

**4. The "In Ephesus" Textual Problem (1:1)**
See above under authorship/destination.

**5. The *Plērōma* of Christ (1:23)**
What does it mean that the church is "the fullness (*plērōma*) of him who fills all in all"? Is the church what completes Christ, or what is filled by Christ? Most interpreters favor the passive sense: the church is the entity filled by Christ, not the entity that completes him.

**6. The Spirit as *Arrabōn* (Seal and Down Payment, 1:13–14)**
The Spirit is given as "the promised Holy Spirit" and functions as a "pledge" (*arrabōn*) of the inheritance — the same commercial term ("deposit") Paul uses in 2 Cor 1:22 and 5:5. The Spirit is the first installment of the eschatological consummation, guaranteeing full future possession.
