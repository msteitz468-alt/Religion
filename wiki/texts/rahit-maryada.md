*Sikh Rahit Maryada* is the authoritative Khalsa code of conduct, codified under Tat Khalsa influence and administered by the SGPC. Per [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]], it is "a result of that influence" — of the Tat Khalsa's success in defining normative Sikhism over against both Sanatan Sikh diversity and Hindu practice (McLeod ~L391–394).

## Nature and Authority

*Sikh Rahit Maryada* is the modern, authoritative synthesis of the earlier *rahit-namas* (older codes of Khalsa conduct). McLeod notes that "the rahit-namas … are definitely outside the bounds of canonical scripture" (~L1256, L2176–2178). The *Sikh Rahit Maryada* itself is not scripture, but it is the supreme normative guide to Sikh practice: it is what McLeod calls the document in which "the Guru Panth is identified with the Khalsa" (~L580–582), and it treats the SGPC as "the ultimate authority in the Sikh religion" (~L641–644).

McLeod describes it consistently as defining Sikh obligations and prohibitions across the full range of life events. It "speaks of the Panth" as meaning "the Guru Panth, one of the two receptacles to receive the function and authority of the Guru when the last personal Guru died in 1708. The other receptacle was the sacred scripture" (~L566–570).

## Canon and Reception

*Sikh Rahit Maryada* is not itself scripture. The *rahit-namas* as a genre are explicitly placed "outside the boundaries of scripture" by McLeod (~L1256). What the *Sikh Rahit Maryada* does is govern the *reading and use* of scripture (the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]) and the conduct of all who belong to the [[khalsa|Khalsa]]. Its authority is **normative** — it defines what it means to be an Amrit-dhari Sikh — rather than **scriptural**.

The document recognizes only the *Adi Granth* and the works of Guru Gobind Singh as the "sacred scripture which is construed to mean the Adi Granth and the works of Guru Gobind Singh" (~L569–571). *Sikh Rahit Maryada* itself mentions the works of Bhai Gurdas and Nand Lai "as suitable for singing in gurdwaras, other than those contained in the sacred scriptures" (~L2119–2121).

## Domain: Ritual and Life-Cycle Rites

*Sikh Rahit Maryada* governs a broad scope of Sikh life. McLeod cites it repeatedly as the authoritative standard for:

**Marriage (Anand ceremony)**: it specifies that "no account should be taken of caste; a Sikh girl should be married only to a Sikh husband; and Sikhs should not be married as children." It forbids consulting horoscopes, worship of ancestors, and burning ritual fires — all practices aimed "squarely at Hindu conventions" (~L448–462).

**Funeral rites**: it "begins and ends the funeral procedure by specifically forbidding practices associated with Hindu custom" — in particular, gathering residual bones and depositing them at sacred river locations (~L544–549).

**Panthic injunctions**: It defines the bounds of the Guru Panth as the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] — "only those Sikhs who have been initiated and who diligently uphold the Rahit. Loyal Amrit-dhari Sikhs constitute the Khalsa and the Khalsa is the Guru Panth" (~L581–594). Panthic injunctions (*gurmatd*) may only be promulgated on issues concerning "the dignity and status of the Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib, the correctness of the sacred text, the form of the initiation ceremony, the content of the Rahit, the organization of the Panth" (~L608–614).

**Offences and penances**: An offence against the Rahit by an Amrit-dhari Sikh is a *tanakhah*. Several are spelt out explicitly in the [[amrit-sanskar|amrit sanskar]] order. A Sikh convicted of one of the four *kurahit* (cardinal prohibitions) is branded *patit* (apostate) (~L647–690).

## Tat Khalsa Influence and Historical Consolidation

The ritual of Khalsa initiation "demonstrates Tat Khalsa influence at its greatest" and its format, though retaining existing elements, had "numerous details added" under Tat Khalsa guidance (~L391–398). For marriage rites, Tat Khalsa influence was "considerably greater"; for funeral rites, "the influence of the Tat Khalsa theorists has been limited … Ancient Indian customs have proved much stronger" (~L393–396).

The *Anand Marriage Act* of 1909 gave legal standing to the Anand marriage ceremony championed by the Tat Khalsa, demonstrating the codification of *Sikh Rahit Maryada*'s principles into civil law (~L404–405).
