# On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy (*Peri tes Ekklesiastikes Hierarchias*)

**On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy** is the second of the two "hierarchical" treatises of
[[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]], addressed to Timothy. It applies the
same framework of graded hierarchical mediation developed in the [[celestial-hierarchy|
Celestial Hierarchy]] to the visible church: its sacraments, ordained orders, and lay orders.
The work is the most influential patristic treatment of Christian liturgy as *theophany* —
a divinely ordered system through which the sensible symbolically mediates the intelligible and
divine illumination descends to the human.

## Structure: The Three Triads of the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy

Dionysius organizes the church's life into two sets of triads:

### Triad of Initiating Rites (Sacraments)
1. **Baptism** (*theleseis* / Illumination)
2. **Eucharist** (*synaxis* / Communion)
3. **Chrism** / Holy Unction (*myron*)

### Triad of Ordained Orders (Clergy)
1. **Bishop** (*hierarch*) — highest; illuminates and perfects
2. **Priest** (*hiereus*)
3. **Deacon** (*leitourgos*)

### Triad of the Illuminated (Laity)
1. **Monks** (*therapeutai*)
2. **People** (*laos*)
3. **Catechumens / Energumens / Penitents** (those being purified)

## Structure and Contents

### Caput I — What is the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy; What Its Goal

Addressed to Timothy. The treatise opens with the theological framework that parallels the
*Celestial Hierarchy*: the ecclesiastical hierarchy is "an inspired and Divine and Deifying
science, and of operation, and of consecration" (Caput I §I). Jesus himself — "the most
supremely Divine Mind and superessential, the Source and Essence, and most supremely Divine
Power of every Hierarchy" — illuminates "the blessed Beings who are superior to us, in a
manner more clear, and at the same time more intellectual, and assimilates them to His own
Light, as far as possible" (Caput I §I).

The goal of every hierarchy is stated precisely: "the assimilation to, and union with, God,
as far as attainable, is deification. And this is the common goal of every Hierarchy, — the
clinging love towards God and Divine things divinely and uniformly ministered" (Caput I §III).
The mechanism: "the supremely Divine Blessedness, the essential Deity, the Source of
deification, from Which comes the deification of those deified, bequeathed, by Divine
Goodness, the Hierarchy, for preservation, and deification of all rational and intellectual
Beings" (Caput I §IV).

The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy differs from the Celestial in medium: the celestial beings "as
minds, think, according to laws laid down for themselves; but we are led by sensible figures
to the Divine contemplations, as is possible to us" (Caput I §II). The church's sacramental
symbols are accommodations to human embodied nature; they point beyond themselves to the
intelligible.

Dionysius distinguishes two channels of hierarchical tradition: written Oracles ("Letters of
the Word of God") and unwritten oral transmission "from mind to mind, through the medium of
speech" (Caput I §IV) — the latter being closer to the immaterial mode of the celestial
hierarchy and reserved for the initiated.

### Caput II — The Sacrament of Illumination (Baptism)

Baptism (*theleseis* = "Illumination") is treated as the foundational Mystic Rite because it
is the precondition for all others: "the very earliest step of the religious reception towards
the religious performance of the Divine commandments is the unutterable operation of our being
from God" (Caput II §I). One cannot participate in any other rite without first receiving
divine birth.

**The rite** (Mysterion of Illumination): The candidate is brought by a sponsor to the
Hierarch. The Hierarch assembles the congregation, chants psalms, kisses the altar, and
questions the candidate. The candidate confesses ignorance of the beautiful and insufficiency
for life in God. The Hierarch lays his right hand on the candidate's head and seals him. The
candidate is stripped of clothing, faces west (rejection of darkness), makes a threefold
renunciation of Satan with breath and hands, then turns east (toward divine light), professes
faith in Christ, and makes a threefold profession. The Hierarch anoints with the oil of
chrism; priests complete the anointing of the whole body. The font is consecrated by the
Hierarch with three cruciform effusions of the *Muron* and three injections, and prophetic
psalms. The candidate is immersed three times in the name of the Trinity, received by priests,
clothed in new white garments, sealed with the *Muron*, and admitted to the Eucharist
(Caput II §VII).

**Theological contemplation**: The three immersions image "the supremely Divine death of the
Life-giving Jesus, Who spent three days and three nights in the tomb" (Caput II §VII). Death
is not annihilation but "the separating of things united, leading to that which is invisible
to us" (Caput II §VII). Baptism is death to sin and rebirth. The white garments signify the
soul's new luminosity; the *Muron* sealing "makes the man initiated of good odour, for the
holy perfecting of the Divine birth unites those who have been perfected to the supremely
Divine Spirit" (Caput II §VIII). The rite as a whole "imitates, so far as Divine imitation is
attainable to men, by the three immersions in the water, the supremely Divine death of the
Life-giving Jesus" (Caput II §VII).

The west–east movement symbolizes the renunciation of darkness and the embrace of divine light:
"the complete separation from baseness; and receiving his sacred promises of entire consort
with the One, since he has become uniform through love of the truth" (Caput II §V).

### Caput III — The Sacrament of the Synaxis (Eucharist)

The Eucharist (*Synaxis* = "Communion" / "Gathering") is described as "initiation of
initiations" (Caput III §I, citing Hierotheus). It is called *synaxis* because it "collects
our divided lives into uniform deification, and gives communion and union with the One, by the
Godlike folding together of our diversities" (Caput III §I). "It scarcely ever happens that
any Hierarchical initiation is completed without the most Divine Eucharist, as head of the
things done in each" (Caput III §I).

**The rite** (Mysterion of Synaxis): The Hierarch incenses the altar and the whole sanctuary.
He chants psalms with the congregation, followed by Scripture readings. Catechumens, the
possessed, and penitents are dismissed. Leitourgoi bring the bread and cup to the altar while
the whole church sings the *Symbolum* (Creed/Hymn of Praise). The Hierarch offers a sacred
prayer and proclaims holy peace; all exchange the kiss of peace. The holy tablets (diptychs of
the departed) are recited. Priests and Hierarch wash their hands; the Hierarch stands at the
altar surrounded by priests and chosen deacons. He sings the sacred works of God, ministers
"things most divine," and brings the eucharistic symbols to view. He first receives and then
distributes the communion, concluding with thanksgiving (Caput III §II).

**Theological contemplation**: The Hierarch's procession from altar to the furthest bounds of
the sanctuary and return images divine philanthropy — God descends to created beings without
leaving his own unmoved identity: "the Blessedness, supremely Divine above all, even if,
through Divine goodness, It goes forth to the communion of the holy who participate in It,
yet It never goes outside its essential unmoved position" (Caput III §III). The unveiled bread
and divided cup make visible what the *Synaxis* performs spiritually — the One descending into
the many and drawing the many back into unity: "the 'one,' and 'simple,' and 'hidden,' of
Jesus, the most supremely Divine Word, by His incarnation amongst us, came forth, out of
goodness and love towards man, to the compound and visible, and benevolently devised the
unifying communion" (Caput III §XII).

The Hierarch must partake first before distributing — "the reverend Leader should first
partake, and be filled with the gifts, to be imparted, through him, from God to others"
(Caput III §XIV) — modeling the downward mediation of divine light.

The dismissal of catechumens and the possessed before the consecration illustrates the
graduated hierarchy of the initiated: only the fully born-of-God may witness and receive the
most holy mysteries. "The supermundane Service of the Divine Mysteries excludes those under
penitence… proclaims… 'I am unseen and uncommunicated by those who are in any respect
imperfectly weak as regards the summit of the Divine Likeness'" (Caput III §VII).

The eucharistic rite also commemorates "the most Divine Supper, and arch-symbol of the rites
performed, agreeably with which the Founder of the symbols Himself excludes… him who had
supped with Him on the holy things, not piously" — an allusion to Judas (Caput III §I). The
register of the holy dead (*diptychs*) is read to signify "the indivisible conjunction of
their supermundane and sacred union with Him" (Caput III §IX).

### Caput IV — The Sacrament of the Myron (Chrism)

The *Muron* (consecrated fragrant oil) is a perfecting rite "of the same rank" as the
Synaxis. The Hierarch takes the *Muron*, places it veiled under twelve sacred wings
(*an image of the Seraphim around the divine presence*) on the altar, prays over it, and uses
it for "almost every Hierarchical consecration" (Caput IV §II).

**Theological meaning**: The composition of fragrant oils images Jesus himself as "superessentially
of good savour, filling the contemplative part of ourselves by bequests of Divine sweetness for
contemplation" (Caput IV §IV). The twelve wings enclosing the *Muron* during consecration
image the Seraphim around the divine presence: the highest Seraphic order "recognizes,
reverently, Him lowering Himself in our belongings" — that is, Jesus descending to be
sanctified as man — "and when viewing Him sanctified, in a manner befitting man, by the Father
and Himself and the Holy Spirit, recognized its own supreme Head as being essentially
unchanged" (Caput IV §X). The cruciform effusions of *Muron* into the baptismal font image
Christ's death: "the Lord Jesus descending even to death itself through the cross" (Caput IV
§X). The *Muron* seals the baptized with the Holy Spirit: "the perfecting unction of the
Muron gives to him who has been initiated… the abiding of the supremely Divine Spirit" (Caput
IV §XI). The consecration of the altar with *Muron* signifies that "our most Divine Altar is
Jesus — the supremely Divine sanctifying of the Godly Minds — in Whom… being sanctified and
mystically offered as a whole burnt-offering, we have the access" (Caput IV §XII).

The *Muron*'s fragrance is hidden under wings to teach that holy persons "have their beautiful
and well-savoured assimilations in virtue to the hidden God not seen for vain glory" (Caput IV
§I), imitating the divine hiddenness.

### Caput V — The Sacerdotal Orders and Their Consecrations

Having treated the three sacraments, Dionysius turns to the triad of ordained ministers. Each
of the three orders (*Hierarch*, *Priest*, *Leitourgos*) corresponds to one of the three
hierarchical functions:
- **Hierarch** (Bishop): perfecting — consecrates, perfects, reveals the sciences of the holy
  mysteries; the only one who can consecrate the *Muron*, ordain priests, and consecrate the
  altar. "The Divine Rank of the Hierarchs, then, is the first of the God-contemplative Ranks"
  (Caput V §V).
- **Priest**: illuminating — conducts the initiated to the visions of the Mystic Rites, ministers
  the sacraments under the Hierarch.
- **Leitourgos** (Deacon): purifying — strips the candidate at baptism, dismisses the impure
  from the assembly, stands at the doors, registers the baptized.

The consecration rite is common to all three: conducting to the Divine Altar, kneeling, the
Hierarchical hand, the cruciform seal, the announcement of name, and the completing salutation.
Distinctive: the Hierarch alone has the Gospel Book laid on his head (*"the Divinely-transmitted
Oracles"*); Priests bend *both* knees; Leitourgoi bend *one* knee only (Caput V §§I–II).

The Hierarch does not ordain by his own authority: "the God-beloved consecrator is manifestor
of the supremely Divine choice — not of his own accord or by his own favour leading those who
are ordained to the sacerdotal consecration, but being moved by God" (Caput V §V). Dionysius
cites Moses, the Melchizedek typology applied to Jesus, and Peter's apostolic lot for Matthias
as exemplars of God-moved consecration. The "lot" cast for Matthias is interpreted not as
chance but as "a certain supremely Divine gift, pointing out to that Hierarchical Choir him
who was designated by the Divine election" (Caput V §V).

The bending of knees at the altar symbolizes placing one's whole life "under God, as source
of consecration" (Caput V §III). The cruciform seal marks "the inaction of all the impulses
of the flesh, and the God-imitated life looking away unflinchingly to the manly most Divine
life of Jesus, Who came even to Cross and death" (Caput V §IV).

### Caput VI — The Ranks of the Initiated

The three lay ranks mirror the three sacerdotal orders in their receptive (rather than
distributing) role:
- **Monks** (*Therapeutae*): highest of the initiated; "entirely purified purification, through
  complete power and perfect chastity"; "conducted by the most perfecting powers of the
  Hierarchs." Named *Therapeutae* ("servants") or *Monks* "from the undivided and single life,
  as it were unifying them, in the sacred enfoldings of things divided, into a God-like Monad,
  and God-loving perfection" (Caput VI §III). Their consecration is performed by a Priest with
  the monastic tonsure, renunciation of all divided "not only lives, but even imaginations,"
  the cruciform seal, the stripping and reclobing, and admission to the Eucharist (Caput VI
  §II). They do not bend the knee (not ordained) but stand alongside the Priest; they are not
  leaders of others but models of singular deifying contemplation.
- **Holy People** (*laos*): middle initiated rank; cleansed from impurity, admitted to
  contemplation and communion of the Mystic Rites; assigned to the Priests for illumination
  (Caput VI §II).
- **Catechumens / Possessed / Penitents**: ranks under purification; in various stages of
  preparation; dismissed before the Eucharist; assigned to Leitourgoi for nursing and purifying.

### Caput VII — The Rite of Christian Burial

A theology of death and resurrection embedded in a description of the funeral rite. The
distinction between holy and unholy in death mirrors the distinction in life:
- The holy dead approach death "with firm and unfailing hope, in godly rejoicing, knowing that
  at the end of holy contests their condition will be altogether in a perfect and endless life
  and safety, through their future entire resurrection" (Caput VII §I).
- The unholy, by contrast, are "piteously and against their will, separated from this present
  life, conducted to no holy hope, by reason of their shameful life" (Caput VII §II).

Dionysius refutes four mistaken views of death: annihilation; permanent soul–body separation
as the blessed state; metempsychosis (reincarnation — "an injustice to them, that, after they
have laboured together with the godly souls… they relentlessly deprive them of their righteous
retributions"); and a material-istic eschatology that "say that the most holy and blessed
repose promised to the devout is similar to our life in this world" (Caput VII §II).

**The rite**: If the deceased was of sacerdotal rank, the body is placed before the Divine
Altar; if a monk or layperson, before the sanctuary entrance. The Hierarch offers prayer and
thanksgiving. Leitourgoi read scripture passages on the resurrection and chant psalms. The
first Leitourgos dismisses catechumens and reads the diptychs, naming the recently deceased
among the holy dead. The Hierarch offers prayer over the body. The Hierarch and all present
salute (kiss) the departed. The Hierarch pours oil over the body. The body is placed in a
chamber with others of the same rank (Caput VII §II).

**Theological contemplation**: The placement by rank in death mirrors placement by rank in
life: "in the regeneration, all will be in those chosen inheritances, for which they have
chosen their own life here" (Caput VII §III §I). The oil poured at burial corresponds to the
oil of chrism at baptism: "then indeed the anointing with the Oil summoned the initiated to
the holy contests; and now the Oil poured upon him shews the fallen asleep to have struggled,
and to have been made perfect, throughout those same contests" (Caput VII §IX).

The Hierarch's prayer for the departed is not intercessory in the ordinary sense — it does not
change God's just awards — but *declaratory*: "the Divine Hierarch, as the Oracles say, is
interpreter of the supremely Divine awards" (Caput VII §VI). He prays only for those who have
lived piously, pronouncing what God has promised to give them. He does not pray over the
unholy dead, because "he would deviate from his office of expounder, and would presumptuously
arrogate… a function of the Hierarchy, without being moved by the Supreme Legislator" (Caput
VII §VII). The prayer makes manifest God's justice and mercy simultaneously.

The body receives honor alongside the soul because it "contended with it throughout the devout
struggles"; in the resurrection "the Divine justice gives to it, together with its own body,
the retributive inheritances" (Caput VII §IX).

Infant baptism is also addressed here: infants receive the sacrament not because they
understand, but because a godfather (sponsor) promises to raise them in a "disposition for
Divine things" (Caput VII §XI). The Hierarch "imparts to the child the sacred symbols, in
order that he may be nourished by them, and may not have any other life but that which always
contemplates Divine things" (Caput VII §XI).

## Theology of Sacrament as Hierarchy

Each of the three Mystic Rites corresponds to one function of the triad: Baptism is the
*purifying* and *illuminating* rite ("exhibited in the Oracles as a purification and
enlightening illumination"); the Synaxis and the Myron are *perfecting* rites — "perfecting
knowledge and science of the works of God, through which the unifying elevation to the Godhead
and most blessed communion is reverently perfected" (Caput V §III). The sacraments do not
merely symbolize grace; they *convey* it in the only manner suited to embodied, sensible human
beings: "we are led by sensible figures to the Divine contemplations" (Caput I §II).

The central logic is that the Eucharist is the "head and tail of every initiation" — no other
rite is complete without it (Caput III §I). The Hierarch, by definition, is the one in whom
"the whole Hierarchy is clearly completed and recognized within himself" (Caput I §III).

## Influence on Liturgical Theology

The *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* established the vocabulary and conceptual framework through
which Eastern Christian liturgy was theorized as *theophany* and *theosis*-in-progress.
Maximus the Confessor's *Mystagogy* (7th c.) extends Dionysian principles. In the West,
the work deeply shaped Scholastic sacramental theology (Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* III; Hugh
of Saint-Victor), the understanding of ordination as conferring hierarchical power, and the
medieval identification of the sevenfold sacramental system with a graded system of grace.

## Connection to Celestial Hierarchy

The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy is explicitly described as the terrestrial image of the celestial:
"let us view our Hierarchy, conformably to ourselves, abounding in the variety of the sensible
symbols, by which, in proportion to our capacity, we are conducted, hierarchically according
to our measure, to the uniform deification" (Caput I §II). Where the celestial hierarchy
operates through purely intelligible illumination — "Almighty God does not move them to things
divine, from without, but intelligibly, since they are illuminated as to the most Divine will
from within, with brilliancy pure and immaterial" (Caput I §IV) — the ecclesiastical hierarchy
operates through material symbols (water, oil, bread, spoken words, vestments) that are
analogously transparent to the intelligible realities they convey. The three-triad structure
(rites, ordained, initiated) mirrors the three-triad celestial structure; each functions by
the same principle — purification, illumination, perfection — flowing from higher to lower in
graduated mediation.
