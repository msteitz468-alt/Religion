## Overview

The *Kalpasuta* is a text of central importance to [[svetambara|Svetambara]] Jainism, functioning simultaneously as biographical hagiography of [[mahavira]], monastic law, and above all as **living liturgical spectacle** in the annual festival of Paryusan. It exists in two distinct textual entities that share the same name (a potential source of confusion):

1. **The Kalpasuta within the Dasasrutaskandha** (first of the seven *Chedasutras* of the Svetambara canon): the eighth chapter of the Dasasrutaskandha (*Ayaradasao*), also called *Paryusanakalpa*. This is the text discussed here.
2. **A second Chedasutra also called Kalpasuta**: a short, independent text on monastic law (lines 1274–1277).

## Contents

The *Kalpasuta* contains material relating to: (a) the biography of Mahavira and the other fordmakers; (b) monastic regulations and history. Its account of [[mahavira|Mahavira]]'s *tirtha* (religious community) at his death is significant: it records 36,000 nuns and 14,000 monks, and 318,000 laywomen to 159,000 laymen — a community in which women substantially outnumbered men at every level (lines 446–452). The text also records that 1,400 women vs. 700 men achieved salvation during Mahavira's lifetime.

Hermann Jacobi included the *Kalpasuta* as one of four key texts in his *Jaina Sutras* (published in the "Sacred Books of the East" series), making it one of the first Jain texts presented to western audiences. Jacobi was presumably influenced by "the extraordinary proliferation of manuscripts of the text, far more than for any other Jain work, usually containing fine illuminations" (lines 850–855).

## The Kalpasuta at Paryusan

The *Kalpasuta*'s most important function for living Svetambara practice is its ritual recitation at **Paryusan**, "the most important event in the Svetambara Jain ritual calendar" (line 861). Paryusan is an eight-day festival celebrated in September at the climax of *caturmas*, the four-month monsoon period when ascetics temporarily settle among the laity.

Originally the recitation bound the ascetic community together. Its application to lay congregations — on days four through eight — was extended by an ancient teacher called Kalaka, though there is no unanimity about who was responsible for this change (lines 868–876).

**Structure of the recitation:**

- **Days 4–7:** Monks use a vernacular (Gujarati) commentary to explain the Prakrit text; this commentary is itself typically read rapidly.
- **Day 8 (the culminating day):** The Gujarati commentary is discarded. "Two monks taking turns proceed to recite the basic Prakrit text of the Kalpasuta" in a "breakneck gabble" that renders the text "completely incomprehensible even to those who know Prakrit, who would be minimally represented in the audience anyway" (lines 893–897). Simultaneously, a young layman (prompted by a monk's signals) displays illuminated manuscript pages illustrating scenes from Mahavira's life and from medieval commentaries.

**Other ritual elements:**

- **Days 3–4:** A copy of the *Kalpasuta* is paraded through the streets.
- **Day 5:** The passage culminating in Mahavira's birth triggers a major ceremony: silver images of the fourteen dreams of Mahavira's mother are lowered from the ceiling, and lay members bid in auction for the right to touch and garland them.

The eighth day's recitation functions primarily through spectacle and sound rather than comprehension: "the displayed pictures recapitulating a story with which the audience is already familiar and the monks sitting on a dais rushing through the recitation" (lines 920–923). The "sound and sight of it during this festival confer a particular merit" (lines 924–925). Dundas remarks: "The Protestant Christian-derived notion of scripture as involving meaning only is hardly appropriate here" (lines 927–929).

## Historical Notes on the Recitation

The use of a vernacular (Gujarati) commentary for days 4–7 seems connected to the breakdown of Svetambara monastic initiation in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when non-initiated clerics (*yati*) assumed religious leadership including *Kalpasuta* recitation, "many of whom lacked even a minimal ability to interpret the text from the original Prakrit or from a Sanskrit commentary" (lines 884–888).

## Canon and Reception

The *Kalpasuta* is canonical for the [[svetambara|Svetambara Murtipujaka Sangha]] and for Sthanakvasi and Terapanthi Svetambara sub-sects (it falls within the *Chedasutra* group, most of which these sects accept). The [[digambara|Digambaras]] reject the authority of the Svetambara canon as a whole, including the *Kalpasuta*.

As a manuscript object, "every copy of [the *Kalpasuta*] within a library is an evocative symbol of the tradition to which the Svetambaras belong" (lines 925–927). Its extraordinary manuscript proliferation — more copies than any other Jain work, typically with fine illuminations — makes it the most visually attested text in the Svetambara tradition.
