# Thanksgiving Hymns (Hodayot)
**Hebrew title**: *Hodayot* (הודיות) — "Thanksgivings"
**Sigla**: 1QH (the main Cave 1 scroll); 4Q427–32 (4QHa–f), 4Q433, 4Q433a

A collection of **thanksgiving psalms** or hymns in the first person, opening with the characteristic formula "I thank Thee, O Lord" (*'odekha Adonai*). The Thanksgiving Hymns are the most personal and intimate texts in the Qumran corpus and contain language suggestive of individual authorship by a towering spiritual figure — widely identified as the **Teacher of Righteousness**. Scholars distinguish two cycles within the collection.

## Canon and Reception
Purely sectarian; of no standing outside the Qumran community. Their value for understanding the psychology, self-understanding, and theology of the Teacher (or a figure modeled on him) is enormous.

## Two Cycles

### Hymns of the Teacher (Cols. X–XVII approximately)
A distinct group of hymns characterized by:
- **Intense personal suffering**: Persecution by enemies; isolation; near-death experience
- **Divine election from the womb**: "Thou hast known me from the womb"; "Thou didst establish my spirit before forming me"
- **Revelatory experience**: God has revealed hidden things to the speaker; he has been made a channel of divine wisdom
- **Conflict with a community of the wicked**: "A company of traitors surrounds me"; "The Spouter of Lies and those who seek smooth things"
- **Metaphors of spiritual birth**: The speaker experiences a kind of cosmic birth pangs — he is like a woman in labor bringing forth a community

Representative language:
> "I thank Thee, O Lord, for Thou hast not abandoned me while I sojourned among a people [laden with sin]… Thou hast placed me as a father to the sons of grace and as a foster-father to men of portent."

> "For in the mysteries of Thy understanding Thou hast allotted all these things to bring Thy glory to pass. What is flesh that [it should have insight into these matters]? How can dust direct its steps? Thou hast fashioned the spirit and ordained its work."

The **divine birth metaphor** (1QH XI, formerly III):
> "I was in distress as a woman in travail… I was afraid at the sound of the roaring… For the children have reached the waves of Death, and she who is pregnant with a man labors in her pains."

This metaphor of giving birth to the community through suffering is unique in Second Temple literature and anticipates the "labor pains" of the new age in Paul (Gal. 4:19; Romans 8:22).

### Hymns of the Community (Cols. I–IX and elsewhere)
More liturgical, less intensely personal. These hymns:
- Praise God's predestinarian sovereignty: "What can I plan unless Thou have decreed it, and what shall I do without Thy will?"
- Celebrate the community's election: "I know that righteousness belongs not to man, nor to a son of man perfect conduct. To God Most High belong all the acts of righteousness"
- Develop the **Two Spirits** anthropology: the spirit of flesh vs. the spirit of truth within the individual

The Community Hymns provided the liturgical matrix for communal worship — the congregation thanked God for election and affirmed divine grace over human merit.

## Theological Themes

**Predestinarianism and Grace**: The Hodayot present the most thorough pre-Pauline Jewish statement of divine grace overcoming human sinfulness. The speaker consistently denies human merit:
> "The secret of truth art Thou; the place where it is found, and those who seek it, are in Thy hand. Without Thee nothing can be done, and without Thy will nothing is known."

**Suffering and Vindication**: The Teacher/speaker experiences suffering and persecution as a *vocation* — through suffering he mediates divine wisdom to the community. God will vindicate him against his enemies. This pattern closely parallels the Servant Songs (Isaiah 40–55), and the Hodayot draw heavily on Servant language: "despised," "afflicted," "rejected."

**Knowledge and Mystery**: The speaker has received *da'at* (knowledge/gnosis) from God — hidden knowledge of divine mysteries. This knowledge is not general religious insight but specific revealed truth about the community's identity and eschatological situation. The *raz* ("mystery") language of Sapiential Work A is related.

## Relationship to Isaiah 53 and the Servant
The Teacher's hymns draw on the Suffering Servant imagery of Isaiah 40–55. The speaker presents himself as:
- "Despised and rejected" — Isa. 53:3
- One who "suffers the sorrows of affliction" — Isa. 53:4
- A channel of divine knowledge and healing for others

Whether the Teacher consciously identified himself as the Servant, or later editors read the Servant through the Teacher's experience, cannot be determined. The overlap is significant for understanding how the Servant figure could later be applied to Jesus in early Christianity.
