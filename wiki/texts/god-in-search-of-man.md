# God in Search of Man: A Philosophy of Judaism

A 1955 systematic work of religious philosophy by [[abraham-joshua-heschel|Abraham Joshua
Heschel]], the companion volume to his earlier *Man Is Not Alone* (1951). It is **not a
canonical or scriptural text** but a modern constructive theology / philosophy of Judaism;
it is widely esteemed in [[judaism|Jewish]] thought (especially Conservative and liberal
streams) and in Jewish–Christian dialogue, but holds no canon standing. The `canon_scope`
buckets are therefore empty by design — the book's authority is intellectual, not
scriptural.

**Source:** `raw/texts/God in Search of Man A Philosophy of Judaism (Abraham Joshua Heschel ...).txt`

## Argument and Structure

The book moves in three parts, mirroring the title's claim that revelation is **God in
search of man**, not merely man in search of God:

1. **God** — how we come to an awareness of God, beginning not from proofs but from
   *wonder*, *radical amazement*, awe, the sublime, and the sense of the ineffable
   mystery; the inadequacy of reason alone; "depth-theology."
2. **Revelation** — the prophetic event, the idea and paradox of revelation at Sinai,
   the Bible as God's anthropology rather than man's theology, midrash and the
   "understatement" of prophecy.
3. **Response** — the life of mitzvot (the "leap of action"), halakhah and agadah,
   kavanah, the problem of polarity, evil, freedom, and the meaning of Jewish existence.

## Key Themes and Cruxes

### Part I — God (chs 1–16)

Part I argues that awareness of God arises not from proofs but from a chain of
pre-conceptual acts, pursued by the method Heschel calls **[[depth-theology]]** (probing
"the act of believing," not its propositional content). Its first task is "to rediscover
the questions to which religion is an answer."

- **The starting point is wonder, not doubt.** "There is no word in Biblical Hebrew for
  doubt; there are many expressions of wonder." Heschel's **[[radical-amazement]]** is
  wonder that does not cease when explained — "the most incomprehensible fact is the fact
  that we comprehend at all."
- **The sublime, awe, mystery, glory.** Things point beyond themselves: "There are no
  sublime facts; there are only divine acts." This ascends through **[[awe]]** (*yir'ah*,
  sharply distinguished from fear) to the sense of **[[the-ineffable]]**, whose biblical
  name is the **[[kavod|glory]]** — "the presence, not the essence of God; an act rather
  than a quality; a process not a substance."
- **The mystery is honored, not solved or deified.** Against Hegel ("the enigma is not
  solved") and against nature-worship, Heschel's pivotal formula is "God is a mystery, but
  the mystery is not God"; behind the mystery is mercy. The Moses–Akiba legend (Menahot
  29b) models registering, not resolving, the mystery of divine justice.
- **The ontological presupposition.** Certainty of God is not the conclusion of an
  argument but its presupposition: "The ultimate or God comes first and our reasoning about
  Him second." Hence "we praise before we prove," and "ultimate concern is an act of
  worship."
- **God in search of man.** The book's thesis (**[[divine-pathos]]**): biblical faith is
  God seeking man, not man seeking God — "Faith in God is a response to God's question."
  God is "the ultimate subject"; we know Him only "by adverbs," and faith is "an act of the
  whole person … not the same as belief."

### Part II — Revelation (chs 17–27)

Part II turns from the awareness of God to **[[revelation|revelation]]** — the divine
initiative that answers the human quest. Its core moves:

- **Revelation is an event, not a process; an "ecstasy of God."** "The mystic experience is
  an ecstasy of man; revelation is an ecstasy of God." Because it is a unique event, Judaism
  is **[[religion-of-time|a religion of time]]**: God "spoke through events in history," and
  the patriarchs are "lives to be continued," not principles.
- **The paradox of Sinai and the language of understatement.** "To reveal He must conceal";
  the reality exceeds speech, so revelation is told only by understatement and *via
  negationis* (Elijah's "still small voice"). "As a report about revelation the Bible itself
  is a midrash"; "God spoke" is *more* than literal, not less — indicative language, not
  descriptive.
- **Co-revelation — "the word of God and man."** "Prophecy consists of a revelation of God
  and a co-revelation of man"; the Bible is "a record of both revelation and response."
  Authority is not chronological ("not because it is Mosaic, but because Moses was a
  prophet"); "God does not reveal Himself; he only reveals His way."
- **Faith with the prophets; the Bible as drama.** The goal is to move from *faith in* the
  prophets to *faith with* them — "the soul of the prophet is a mirror to God." Scripture is
  "not a book to be read but a drama in which to participate … itself an event"; "presence"
  matters more than "origin."
- **Continuous understanding; the Torah in exile.** "Judaism is based upon a minimum of
  revelation and a maximum of interpretation … The Bible is a seed, God is the sun, but we
  are the soil." The present Torah is partial and accommodated — "just as the Shechinah is in
  exile, so is the Torah in exile."

**Crux — the two-front battle.** Heschel positions his theory *between* fundamentalist
verbal-dictation literalism and rationalist/critical reduction of Scripture to myth:
"Philosophy of religion has to carry on a battle on two fronts." Biblical criticism may
correct transmission history but "the act of revelation remains beyond its scope." This
middle stance is in tension with strict Orthodox verbal inspiration (note his approving
citation that Moses may have "ascribed his order to the authority of the Lord") and with
liberal denial of revelation alike. He also argues from Israel's improbable survival as a
holy people ("Revelation to Israel became a revelation through Israel").

### Part III — Response (chs 28–43)

Part III turns to the human **response**: the life of [[mitzvah|mitzvot]] as the answer to
the God who searches for man.

- **The leap of action.** "A Jew is asked to take a leap of action rather than a leap of
  thought … to do more than he understands in order to understand more than he does" (Sinai's
  "we shall do and we shall hear"). Jewish law is "a science of deeds"; a mitzvah is "an act
  which God and man have in common" — the **partnership of God and man**.
- **More than inwardness; the deed redeems.** Against faith-alone soteriology (Paul, Luther,
  Barth) *and* against pure ethics of intention (Kant), Heschel rejects the faith/works
  dichotomy itself: "the deed is wiser than the heart"; "the good motive comes into being
  while doing the good." "Purity of motivation is the goal; constancy of action is the way."
  The needed inwardness is **[[kavanah]]** — "a mitsvah is like a musical score … one must
  live what he does."
- **The polarity of halakhah and agadah.** "[[halakhah|Halacha]] without agada is dead,
  agada without halacha is wild." Heschel rejects two symmetrical errors — **religious
  behaviorism / pan-halachism** (the Spinoza–Mendelssohn reduction of Judaism to law; the
  Septuagint's "fatal" *Torah = nomos*) and antinomian spiritualism.
- **Evil, the neutral, integrity.** Evil is real and "thrives in the disguise of the good,"
  yet is sustained by a "holy spark" (Zohar's *nogah*) — Heschel's pointed alternative to
  evil-as-privation (see [[nature-of-evil]]). "Neutrality is an illusion … man always emerges
  either as a priest or as a pirate." On integrity, *pure* motive is humanly unattainable
  ("the 'I' stands between God and man"); Job is read as **anthropodicy**, the vindication of
  man.
- **Freedom, the spirit of Judaism, the People Israel.** **[[freedom|Freedom]]** is "an
  event," grounded in creation (not Greek cosmos): "all freedom is a situation of God's
  waiting for man to choose"; the culminating claim is "God is in need of man." The spirit of
  Judaism is *theotropism* — the turning of all being toward God — epitomized by the
  untranslatable **Sabbath**, "holiness in time"; the ideal is "not priests, but a people of
  priests." The closing chapter on **the People [[judaism|Israel]]** defines it as "a
  spiritual order in which the human and the ultimate … enter a lasting covenant" — chosen
  *relationally*, not by superiority ("Israel was discovered by God"); "Israel is the tree,
  we are the leaves." A Jewish philosophy "must look for agreement with the men of Sinai as
  well as with the people of Auschwitz."

### Cruxes across Part I

**Cruxes / contradictions recorded.** Heschel's program runs against several positions:
classical **natural theology** and the argument from design (Paley's watchmaker "fails to
answer the religious problem"); Jewish **rationalism** (Hermann Cohen; and a critique of
[[maimonides|Maimonides']] Thirteen Principles for privileging ideas over events — "it is
in events, not only in ideas that ultimate reality comes to expression"); **God-as-symbol**
theologies (the Tillichian "ultimate concern" is named and rejected); and any
**apophaticism** that deifies the mystery (cf. [[apophatic-theology]]). Faith-as-attachment
sits in tension with creedal/propositional Judaism.
