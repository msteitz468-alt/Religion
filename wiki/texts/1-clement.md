# First Clement

## Textual Overview

*First Clement* (1 Clem.) is a long deliberative letter from the church at Rome to the church at Corinth, traditionally attributed to Clement, the third bishop of Rome (after Peter and Linus). The attribution to Clement is plausible but not universally accepted — the letter is anonymous; it never names its author. It circulated as "the letter of the Romans" before "Clement's letter" became the standard designation.

The letter addresses a specific crisis at Corinth: the congregation has deposed its established leadership (presbyters/bishops) in favor of younger members. The Roman church intervenes — not as a superior authority but as a concerned sister church — to urge restoration of the deposed leaders and a return to proper order.

**Authorship note**: Rome in the 90s CE was not yet governed by a single monarchical bishop. Clement may have been the leading presbyter among a college of presbyters, or the one who drafted the letter on behalf of the Roman community. The letter's authority is the Roman community's collective weight, not Clement's individual episcopal office (that institution had not yet developed there — Ignatius, writing ca. 107–117 CE, conspicuously does not mention a bishop when writing to Rome).

## Structure and Content

1 Clement is 65 chapters. Its broad movement:

**Chs. 1–3**: Opening; the Corinthians' former virtues; the current "abominable and unholy sedition"

**Chs. 4–36**: The root cause — *zelos* (jealousy, rivalry, faction). Illustrated through:
- OT examples of jealousy's destructive fruit: Cain and Abel, Jacob and Esau, Joseph and his brothers, Moses, Aaron and Miriam, Saul and David, Dathan and Abiram
- "Recent examples" close to the writers' own time — **the great climax**: Peter and Paul (ch. 5), and then unnamed Corinthian women, and then the "vast number" of martyrs in Rome

**Chapters 5–6 on Peter and Paul**: The single most significant passage for early church history:

> "Through jealousy and envy, the greatest and most righteous pillars were persecuted and battled to the death. Let us set before our eyes the good apostles: Peter, who because of unrighteous jealousy bore up under hardships not just once or twice, but many times; and having thus borne his testimony he went to the glorious place he deserved. Through jealousy and strife Paul showed the way to the prize for endurance... Having taught righteousness to the whole world and reached the limits of the West, and having borne his testimony before the rulers, he thus departed from the world and went to the holy place" (1 Clem. 5.2–7).

This passage is a primary witness to Peter and Paul's presence in Rome and their deaths there, though it does not name Rome explicitly ("limits of the West").

**Chs. 37–61**: The solution — cosmic order and proper hierarchy. The Roman army's discipline; the body's ordered members; the analogy of OT priestly hierarchy (chief priest, priests, Levites, laity); the principle that each person should remain in their appointed place.

**The succession argument (chs. 40–44)**: The apostles appointed bishops and deacons and established a principle that when these officers die, approved men should take their place. The Corinthians therefore cannot depose men appointed through legitimate succession.

**Chs. 59–61**: A lengthy concluding prayer — one of the earliest extant Christian liturgical prayers, addressed to God the Father; theologically significant for its relatively undeveloped Trinitarian language.

## Major Themes

**Order and cosmic hierarchy**: 1 Clement draws on Stoic ideas of cosmic harmony — the heavens, the seasons, the tides all work in appointed order. The church should do the same. This is the earliest explicit application of natural-law cosmology to church governance.

**Jealousy (zelos) as the anti-principle**: Faction, rivalry, and ambition are the root causes of all schism and persecution. The catalog of OT victims and perpetrators drives this theme relentlessly.

**Apostolic succession (nascent)**: 1 Clement implies but does not fully articulate the later doctrine of apostolic succession. The apostles appointed leaders; those leaders should not be removed without cause. This is the earliest sustained argument for the legitimacy of appointed church leaders, but it is not yet the full Irenaean theory (bishop-lists going back to the apostles).

**Peter and Paul in Rome**: The letter's reference to Peter's and Paul's deaths in the context of Roman persecution is one of the earliest and strongest witnesses to the Roman martyrdom tradition.

**Hermeneutical approach**: Primarily **literal-historical** use of the OT (examples of jealousy and order) with some **typological** moves (the priestly hierarchy of the OT as a type of the church's order). Heavy use of the LXX. Significant Pauline resonances (1 Corinthians, Romans, Hebrews — 1 Clement may know Hebrews).

## Canonical and Near-Canonical Status

1 Clement is the earliest non-NT Christian document with strong canonical claims:
- **Codex Alexandrinus** (5th century): Contains the NT followed by 1 Clement and 2 Clement — i.e., it is appended to the NT as a quasi-canonical supplement
- **Clement of Alexandria** cited it as authoritative
- **Eusebius** (*Church History* 3.38): Notes that it is "publicly used in most of the churches"; classifies it as "disputed but generally recognized" rather than outright non-canonical
- **Codex Hierosolymitanus** (1056): Contains the full Greek text, which was unknown to the Western church until its discovery by Bryennios in 1873

The letter was *read publicly in churches* during the 2nd century — treated as authoritative instruction even if not technically canonical.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1 Clem. 5 (Peter and Paul's martyrdom)**: The primary early witness to their Roman deaths. The language is circumspect — "pillar," "going to the glorious place," "limits of the West" — but the intended meaning is clear to a Roman audience. Date implications: the letter must postdate both deaths (Peter and Paul in the 60s CE under Nero); the letter is usually dated ca. 96 CE on the basis of the reference to "sudden and unexpected calamities and disasters" (ch. 1) being identified with Domitian's persecution.

**1 Clem. 40–44 (Order and succession)**: The theological and political heart of the letter. Does "appointed without their consent" (44.3-5) mean the Corinthians cannot depose anyone once appointed, or only those who are blameless? The logic has been read both as a radical statement of episcopal authority and as a more modest argument for legitimate process.

**1 Clem. 59–61 (Concluding prayer)**: Whether this prayer is liturgical (drawn from actual Roman worship) or literary (composed for the letter) affects our understanding of early Roman liturgy.

**The "jealousy" catalog and gender**: The catalog of victims of jealousy (chs. 4–6) conspicuously includes women — Danaids and Dircae (ch. 6) and unnamed Roman female martyrs — indicating that women participated in the Roman persecutions. The passage is notoriously difficult to interpret due to textual corruption.

## Connections to Other Apostolic Fathers

- **1 Clem. 13.2**: Quotes sayings of Jesus ("forgive and you will be forgiven") without attributing them to a written gospel — the tradition is known but not in a canonical-gospel form
- **1 Clem. 42–44**: The succession argument anticipates but does not yet match Ignatius' monepiscopate theology or Irenaeus' episcopal succession doctrine
- **Polycarp to the Philippians 11.3**: Polycarp quotes 1 Clement, indicating the letter circulated as authoritative instruction in Asia Minor within ca. 15 years of its composition
