# Samayasara ("Essence of the Doctrine")

The *Samayasara* (abbreviated *SS*; literally "Essence of the Doctrine") is the most famous and influential work of [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]], the early Digambara philosopher and mystic. Dundas describes it as "largely devoted to a discussion of the real nature of the self" (lines 865–866). It is the primary literary vehicle for Kundakunda's mystical, inward-focused reinterpretation of Jain doctrine.

## Canon and Reception

Within the [[digambara|Digambara]] community the *Samayasara* is regarded as fully canonical and among the most authoritative non-Agamic texts. The Digambara community "continues to regard all Kundakunda's writings as genuine and forming a unity" (lines 855–857) despite philological debates about interpolations. The influence of the *Samayasara* on later Digambara intellectual and devotional life is profound, and it remained foundational for movements such as the [[kanji-svami-panth|Kanji Svami Panth]] in the twentieth century (not in Dundas's account of this chapter).

The [[svetambara|Svetambara]] community was "generally ill at ease with what they perceived as Kundakunda's reductionist approach to reality" (lines 954–956), limiting the *Samayasara*'s cross-sectarian authority, though Svetambara intellectuals such as Haribhadra engaged with Kundakunda's ideas.

## Language and Style

The *Samayasara* is written in "Prakrit of the variety known as Jaina Sauraseni and [is] deceptively simple in style" (lines 858–860).

## Content and Teaching

The *Samayasara*'s central teaching is the radical primacy of the [[jiva|soul]] (*jiva*) as the only true ultimate category. Key positions found in the *Samayasara* (cited by Dundas with *SS* loci):

### Collapse of Moral Polarities
"The idea of polarities, moral or otherwise, is collapsed and Kundakunda asserts that good and bad, pleasure and pain, fasting and non-fasting and so on are of significance solely at the worldly level in the flawed realm of transmigration" (*SS* 306–7, lines 903–905).

### Scripture's Inability to Liberate
In a "statement deeply subversive of Jain tradition," the *Samayasara* declares that "scripture and its injunctions, generally regarded in Jainism as the source of correct understanding, are incapable of bringing [realisation of the soul] about because they cannot be said to know anything" (*SS* 360, lines 915–919).

### The Three Jewels Redefined
The Three Jewels of right faith, knowledge and practice, "which on the face of it represent the path to deliverance, are in reality reckoned to be merely another means of describing the soul" (lines 907–912).

### Inward Turn
The text exemplifies Kundakunda's "overall soteriological standpoint as involving a radical interiorisation of the religious life" (lines 870–872). From the *nishchaya* (certain) standpoint, "the sole object of omniscience is the soul itself" and "the worldly level of truth … is otherwise of no value" (lines 885, 912–913).

## Relationship to Kundakunda's Other Works

The *Samayasara* is the most famous of four principal works:
- *Niyamasara* ("Essence of Restraint") — ascetic practices
- *Pancástikaya* / *Pravachanasara* — ascetic behaviour and doctrine
- *Samayasara* — the real nature of the self (this text)

"All these writings, although they generally discuss different aspects of the Jain path, provide an exemplification of Kundakunda's overall soteriological standpoint" (lines 867–872).

## Hermeneutical Method

The *Samayasara* operates on **two levels of truth**: the *nishchaya* (ultimate/certain) standpoint and the *vyavahara* (transactional/worldly) standpoint. Dundas notes that the apparent paradoxes in the text — e.g., affirming both the irrelevance of external practice and its provisional utility — are "predictable that a two-levels-of-truth model will be manipulated to provide apparently paradoxical statements, for that is part of its function, and we should not expect total 'logical' consistency in its deployment on Kundakunda's part" (lines 928–931).

The text has been suspected of containing interpolations: "it has been suggested that Kundakunda's works deploy both a mystic and a non-mystic pattern, with verses expressing the latter standpoint which relates to conventional Jain theorisation possibly having been interpolated into the 'Essence of the Doctrine'" (lines 924–927).

## Influence

Later Digambara mystic **Yogindu** (perhaps tenth century CE) continued the *Samayasara*'s inward trajectory in Apabhramsha poetry. The seventeenth-century Svetambara **Yasovijaya** criticised the *Samayasara*'s influence via the Adhyatmika sect's "de-emphasis of the role of ritual and ascetics" (lines 960–963).
