# De Principiis (*Peri Archon* — On First Principles)

[[origen|Origen of Alexandria]]'s *De Principiis* (c. 220–230 CE) is the first systematic Christian theology — a four-book attempt to articulate the "first principles" (ἀρχαί / *principia*) underlying the faith. It addresses God and the Trinity, rational creatures and their creation, free will, the nature of the world, and the interpretation of Scripture. Most of the original Greek survives only in Rufinus's free Latin translation (early 5th c.), which softens Origen's more speculative positions; Jerome's more literal renderings of fragments allow some control. The work has attracted condemnation (Fifth Ecumenical Council, 553 CE) for pre-existence of souls and *apokatastasis*, and fascination for its intellectual boldness ever since.

## Preface — The Rule of Faith and the Open Questions

The Preface (lines 300–491) establishes both the authoritative baseline from which Origen works and the open questions he intends to investigate speculatively. Its governing concern is the diversity of opinion among professing Christians, and Origen's solution is to identify what the apostles delivered without ambiguity and to distinguish that from what they left undetermined.

**The Rule of Faith — What the Apostles Clearly Taught.** Origen opens by asserting that all genuine knowledge derives from "the very words and teaching of Christ" [line 304], understood broadly to include the pre-incarnate Word active in Moses and the prophets. Because even believers disagree on the most fundamental matters, Origen says it is necessary first to "fix a definite limit and to lay down an unmistakable rule regarding each one of these" [lines 329–330]. He then enumerates six heads of apostolic teaching:

1. *God*: "there is one God, who created and arranged all things, and who, when nothing existed, called all things into being" [lines 358–359] — the God of the patriarchs and prophets, Father of Jesus Christ, who gave Law, Prophets, and Gospels.
2. *Jesus Christ*: "born of the Father before all creatures" [line 371], servant of the Father in creation; he assumed a body, "did truly suffer, and did not endure this death common (to man) in appearance only, but did truly die" and "did truly rise from the dead" [lines 378–381].
3. *Holy Spirit*: "associated in honour and dignity with the Father and the Son" [lines 382–383]. Here Origen immediately notes a gap: "whether He is to be regarded as born or innate, or also as a Son of God or not: for these are points which have to be inquired into out of sacred Scripture" [lines 385–387].
4. *Soul and free will*: the soul has "a substance and life of its own" [line 392] and is rewarded or punished after death; "every rational soul is possessed of free-will and volition" [lines 402–403].
5. *Devil and opposing powers*: these "exist indeed; but what they are, or how they exist, it has not explained with sufficient clearness" [lines 424–426]. The common opinion is that the devil was once an angel who fell.
6. *Scripture's hidden sense*: the Scriptures "have a meaning, not such only as is apparent at first sight, but also another, which escapes the notice of most. For those (words) which are written are the forms of certain mysteries, and the images of divine things" [lines 437–440].

**The Open Questions.** The Preface explicitly maps the terrain Origen considers unsettled and therefore available for speculative inquiry. These include: the status of the Holy Spirit (born? innate?); the origin of the soul — "whether it is derived from the seed by a process of traducianism... or whether it has any other beginning... is not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church" [lines 417–422]; the nature of devil and angels; what existed before the world and what will come after, since "there is no clear statement regarding it in the teaching of the Church" [lines 433–435]; whether God and the soul are corporeal or incorporeal (Origen notes the Greek philosophical term ἀσώματον is not found in Scripture and is used differently there [lines 445–466]); and the nature of the sun, moon, and stars [lines 481–482].

**Method.** Origen states his investigative method plainly: the "elements and foundations" of the faith must be taken as given, but those who possess the gifts of the Spirit should work through them "by means of illustrations and arguments — either those which he has discovered in holy Scripture, or which he has deduced by closely tracing out the consequences and following a correct method" [lines 487–491]. This marks De Principiis as deliberate theological inquiry, not dogmatic pronouncement. Later in Book I chapter 6 he reinforces this: the present exercise "is to be conducted... in the style of a disputation rather than of strict definition" [lines 1906–1908].



## Book I — God, Trinity, Rational Creatures

Book I (lines 492–2434) moves through eight chapters: God (Ch. 1), Christ/Son (Ch. 2), Holy Spirit (Ch. 3), Falling Away (Ch. 4), Rational Natures (Ch. 5), the End or Consummation (Ch. 6), Incorporeal and Corporeal Beings (Ch. 7), and the Angels (Ch. 8). The cumulative argument moves from the unity and simplicity of God, through the eternal generation of the Son, to the structure of rational creation, its freedom, its fall, and the eschatological restoration to which that fall points.

**Chapter 1 — The Incorporeality and Incomprehensibility of God.** Origen's first move is to refute the view that God is a body. Against those who argue from "God is a consuming fire" (Deut) or "God is a Spirit" (John 4), he argues these are figurative: "God does indeed consume and utterly destroy; that He consumes evil thoughts, wicked actions, and sinful desires, when they find their way into the minds of believers" [lines 524–526]. The expression "spirit" in Scripture denotes not a subtle body but the opposite of the gross and literal: "It is the custom of sacred Scripture, when it wishes to designate anything opposed to this gross and solid body, to call it spirit" [lines 533–535]. The Holy Spirit cannot be a body because many saints participate in it simultaneously, and what is divided into parts cannot be shared whole [lines 551–554]. Origen then invokes the Neoplatonic concept of Monad: God "is in all parts [Μονάς], and, so to speak, mind and source from which all intellectual nature or mind takes its beginning" [lines 636–637]. God is "an uncompounded intellectual nature, admitting within Himself no addition of any kind" [lines 633–634], requiring no physical space for its operations [lines 639–640]. The positive conclusion: God is strictly "incomprehensible, and incapable of being measured" [lines 601–602], known only by analogy — as the sun is known through its rays, "the works of Divine Providence and the plan of this whole world are a sort of rays, as it were, of the nature of God, in comparison with His real substance and being" [lines 628–629]. Seeing God is actually knowing God: "to see and to be seen is a property of bodies; to know and to be known, an attribute of intellectual being" [lines 757–759], and "the mind bears a certain relationship to God, of whom the mind itself is an intellectual image" [lines 733–734].

**Chapter 2 — The Eternal Generation of the Son.** Origen identifies the Son under many titles — Wisdom, First-born, Word, Truth, Life, Way, Resurrection — and insists all these are "one and the same" person [line 812]. The crucial argument is that Wisdom subsists hypostatically: "the only-begotten Son of God is His wisdom hypostatically existing" [line 822, Greek ὑπόστασις]. This Wisdom was never not existent: "who in his sound senses ever sought for form, or colour, or size, in wisdom, in respect of its being wisdom?" [lines 826–827]. Eternal generation follows: "God is the Father of His only-begotten Son, who was born indeed of Him, and derives from Him what He is, but without any beginning" [lines 839–841], and therefore "Wisdom was generated before any beginning that can be either comprehended or expressed" [lines 845–846]. Origen presses that seeing cannot apply to the Father-Son relation; rather "the one is said to be known by the other" [lines 770–771]. He uses the simile of a statue too vast to see directly: the incarnate Son is a reduced but faithful replica, allowing those who cannot behold the Father to know him through the Son [lines 1033–1044]. The "brightness of glory" of Hebrews shows "the existence of the Son is derived from the Father but not in time, nor from any other beginning, except... from God Himself" [lines 1196–1198]. Origen explicitly rejects emanation in a divisive, material sense — "we must be careful not to fall into the absurdities of those who picture to themselves certain emanations, so as to divide the divine nature into parts" [lines 966–968] — and instead prefers the analogy of a will proceeding from a mind: "as an act of the will proceeds from the understanding, and neither cuts off any part nor is separated or divided from it, so after some such fashion is the Father to be supposed as having begotten the Son" [lines 973–975]. The Son's omnipotence is exercised "by reason and wisdom, not by force and necessity" [lines 1169–1170].

**Chapter 3 — The Holy Spirit.** The Spirit is third in dignity and function. Origen distinguishes the operation of Father and Son — which "takes place as well in saints as in sinners, in rational beings and in dumb animals; nay, even in those things which are without life" [lines 1392–1394] — from the operation of the Holy Spirit, which "does not take place at all in those things which are without life... nay, is not found even in those who are endued indeed with reason, but are engaged in evil courses" [lines 1395–1399]. The Spirit sanctifies: "those beings which are not holy in their essence may be rendered holy by participating in it" [lines 1521–1522]. The Spirit cannot be a created being, since no passage of Scripture speaks of it being "made or created" [lines 1320–1321]. Origen quotes his "Hebrew master" interpreting the two seraphim of Isaiah 6 as the Son and Holy Spirit [lines 1343–1345]. On the relation of Spirit to Son: the Spirit does not derive knowledge from the Son by progressive revelation, since "if the Holy Spirit knows the Father through the Son's revelation, He passes from a state of ignorance into one of knowledge; but it is alike impious and foolish to confess the Holy Spirit, and yet to ascribe to Him ignorance" [lines 1367–1370]. Origen closes the chapter by describing a graduated economy: God the Father confers existence; the Son, as Logos/Reason, confers rationality; the Holy Spirit confers holiness upon those already turning toward virtue, in proportion to their deserts [lines 1523–1532].

**Chapter 4 — Falling Away.** Origen uses the analogy of a geometer or physician: rational beings who do not maintain their exercise of wisdom gradually forget it [lines 1574–1589]. This chapter prepares the account of the origin of evil not in creation but in the freedom of creatures.

**Chapter 5 — Rational Natures: Their Fall and the Origin of the Cosmic Hierarchy.** All rational creatures (*logika*) were created equal. Their current differentiation into angels, humans, and demons is not intrinsic but moral. Origen argues from symmetry: if evil powers are not evil by nature (for that would make the Creator responsible), then holy powers are not holy by nature — "goodness exists in virtue of essential being" in the Trinity alone; for everything else it is accidental [lines 1948–1950]. The fall of opposing powers is demonstrated from Ezekiel 28 (the prince of Tyre: "You were stainless in your days, from the day when you were created, until iniquities were found in you" [lines 1787–1789]) and from Isaiah 14 (Lucifer: "How is Lucifer, who used to arise in the morning, fallen from heaven!" [lines 1832–1833]). Of Lucifer: "at one time he was light" [line 1857], and the Saviour said "I see Satan fallen from heaven like lightning" [lines 1855–1856]. The conclusion: "it lies within ourselves and in our own actions to possess either happiness or holiness; or by sloth and negligence to fall from happiness into wickedness" [lines 1884–1886], with the theoretical possibility of falling even into the condition of an opposing power [lines 1887–1889].

**Chapter 6 — The End or Consummation.** Origen here first articulates what will become his most controversial teaching: "the goodness of God, through His Christ, may recall all His creatures to one end, even His enemies being conquered and subdued" [lines 1912–1914], grounded in 1 Cor 15:28 and Ps 110. The subjection of enemies is understood not as coercive domination but as the same willing subjection by which the saints are subject to Christ [lines 1924–1928]. The end mirrors the beginning: "the end is always like the beginning: and, therefore, as there is one end to all things, so ought we to understand that there was one beginning; and as there is one end to many things, so there spring from one beginning many differences and varieties, which again, through the goodness of God, and by subjection to Christ, and through the unity of the Holy Spirit, are recalled to one end, which is like the beginning" [lines 1935–1940]. This passage provides the blueprint for [[apokatastasis|apokatastasis]]. Those who fell from the primal unity are placed in various orders according to the degree of their fall, but "those who have been removed from their primal state of blessedness have not been removed irrecoverably" [lines 1974–1975]. The material world will not be annihilated but transformed: "it is by no means an annihilation or destruction of their material substance... but a kind of change of quality and transformation of appearance" [lines 2050–2052].

**Chapter 7 — Incorporeal and Corporeal Beings; Sun, Moon, and Stars.** Origen raises the striking question whether the heavenly bodies are rational, ensouled beings: "I think it follows that they may be designated as living beings, for this reason, that they are said to receive commandments from God, which is ordinarily the case only with rational beings: I have given a commandment to all the stars, says the Lord" [lines 2151–2154]. Their regular, ordered movement implies reason. Job's words — "the stars also are not clean in Your sight" [lines 2128–2129] — suggest they are morally susceptible. Origen applies Paul's statement that "the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of Him who subjected the same in hope" (Rom 8:20) to the sun, moon, and stars, clothed in bodies (their "vanity"), which groan along with creation [lines 2221–2241]. At the final consummation even these will be delivered, "when God shall be all in all" [lines 2259–2260].

**Chapter 8 — The Angels.** The offices of angels (Raphael for healing, Gabriel for wars, Michael for prayer) are not accidental but were "obtained... by their own merits, and by the zeal and excellent qualities which they severally displayed before this world was formed" [lines 2268–2271]. Origen argues against Gnostic views that posit intrinsically differentiated spiritual natures created by different creators [lines 2292–2299]; the diversity of rational beings is due to their conduct, "not to any partiality on the part of the Disposer" [lines 2305–2307]. He identifies three orders of rational creatures: (1) those who have progressed into the angelic order; (2) those devoted to wickedness "in so headlong a course, that they are unwilling rather than unable to recall themselves; the thirst for evil being already a passion" [lines 2400–2402]; (3) human souls, who may through moral progress ascend to the angelic order [lines 2402–2419]. He rejects metempsychosis — the view that souls descend into irrational animals — as "contrary to our belief" [lines 2431–2432].

**Key Neoplatonic Vocabulary in Book I.** Origen deploys: Monad (Μονάς, line 636) for divine unity; hypostasis (ὑπόστασις, line 824) for the real subsistence of the Son; efflux (line 929) for the relation of Wisdom to divine glory; incorporeal (ἀσώματον) throughout Ch. 1; energy (ἐνέργεια, line 1199) for divine working. Origen explicitly names the question of divine incorporeality as one the philosophers address [line 468], and his demonstration of God as "uncompounded intellectual nature" draws on the same intellectual tradition as Middle Platonism, though he insists on grounding it in Scripture.

## Book II — The World, Soul, and Eschatology

### The World as Remedial School for Fallen Souls

Book II opens with Origen's account of the physical cosmos as a consequence of and remedy for the primordial fall of rational creatures. The diversity and variety of the world — different ranks of angels, conditions of human birth, varieties of bodies — arise not from the Creator's arbitrary will but from the differing degrees to which individual *logika* (rational natures) fell away from original unity with God. As Origen states: "what other cause than this ought to be assigned for the existence of the world … the diversity and variety in the movements and declensions of those who fell from that primeval unity and harmony" [lines 2455–2462]. God then adapts each creature's situation to its pre-existing merits, using the present order as a school of correction: "God, by the ineffable skill of His wisdom, transforming and restoring all things … recalls those very creatures which differed so much from each other in mental conformation to one agreement of labour and purpose" [lines 2467–2471]. The world is simultaneously just (each creature receives what its prior conduct merits) and remedial (the hardships of embodied existence serve therapeutic ends). On matter itself, Origen argues firmly against an uncreated *hyle*: matter was brought into being by God from nothing, citing 2 Maccabees and the Shepherd of Hermas [lines 2587–2600].

### The Soul: Nature, Faculties, and Etymology

Chapter 8 contains Origen's sustained inquiry into soul (*anima*, Greek *psychē*). He defines soul as "a substance *phantastikē* and *hormētikē*" — sensible and mobile [lines 3822–3824]. He observes that Scripture associates the *psychē* almost exclusively with suffering and perturbation, while the *pneuma* (spirit) is associated with the willing and the divine. In Christ, what is troubled is spoken of under the name of soul ("Now is My soul troubled"; "My soul is sorrowful, even unto death"), while what is commended to the Father is His *spirit* — "whence it appears that the soul is something intermediate between the weak flesh and the willing spirit" [lines 4003–4006].

Origen pursues an arresting etymology: the Greek word *psychē* may derive from *psychein* (to grow cold), suggesting that the soul is what has "cooled" from original participation in divine fire. As rational creatures are fire and warmth (God is a consuming fire; the angel of the Lord appeared in flame), those who fell away from love of God "have cooled in their affection for Him" [lines 3936–3937]. The soul, on this reading, is the *nous* (understanding) in a fallen, cooled condition: "the understanding, falling away from its status and dignity, was made or named soul; and that, if repaired and corrected, it returns to the condition of the understanding" [lines 3979–3981]. This is put forward tentatively — "not as dogmatic … but simply as opinions, treated in the style of investigation and discussion" [lines 3993–3995].

### The Resurrection Body (*Soma Pneumatikon*)

Book II, Chapter 10, contains Origen's most extended treatment of the resurrection body. He accepts bodily resurrection against both heretics who deny it and credulous believers who expect fleshly restoration. His argument turns on Paul's image of the seed: just as a bare grain of wheat dies and is raised by God into a body He pleases, so the body contains a "germ" that carries its identity through death and transformation: "our bodies are to be supposed to fall into the earth like a grain; and (that germ being implanted in them which contains the bodily substance) although the bodies die, and become corrupted, and are scattered abroad, yet by the word of God, that very germ which is always safe in the substance of the body, raises them from the earth, and restores and repairs them" [lines 4444–4450]. The resurrection body is genuinely a body, but transformed from "animal" (*sōma psychikon*) to "spiritual" (*sōma pneumatikon*): "it is sown a natural body, it will arise a spiritual body" [lines 4367–4368]. Its glory differs according to the moral quality of its occupant; even the body destined for punishment is "by the very change of the resurrection so incorruptible, that it cannot be corrupted and dissolved even by severe punishments" [lines 4460–4463]. Origen explicitly rejects millenarian literalism — the expectation of eating, drinking, marrying, and rebuilding Jerusalem — as a "Jewish sense" of Scripture [line 4685–4686], insisting the promises point to spiritual food: "the saints will eat indeed, but that it will be the bread of life, which may nourish the soul with the food of truth and wisdom" [lines 4688–4690].

### Multiple World-Ages (Aeons)

Chapter 3 of Book II takes up the question of worlds before and after the present one. Origen asserts that this present world is "the conclusion of many ages" [line 2822], citing Paul (Heb 9:26): Christ was manifested "once in the consummation of ages." Christ's incarnation took place uniquely in this age, not in the anterior ages: "the holy apostle teaches that in that age which preceded this, Christ did not suffer, nor even in the age which preceded that again" [lines 2824–2826]. Future ages also await: "after this age … there will be other ages again to follow … That in the ages to come He might show the exceeding riches of His grace" [lines 2831–2834]. Identical cyclic recurrence — the Stoic doctrine — is ruled out by free will: "souls are not driven on in a cycle which returns after many ages to the same round" [lines 2801–2802]. Instead, successive worlds differ from one another: "a diversity of worlds may exist with changes of no unimportant kind, so that the state of another world may be for some unmistakeable reasons better (than this), and for others worse, and for others again intermediate" [lines 2816–2819]. Origen confesses uncertainty about the number or measure of these world-ages [lines 2819–2821].

### Eschatological Hope and *Apokatastasis*

The eschatological vision of Book II structures the soul's ascent toward perfect knowledge of God. After death, the righteous dwell in an intermediate "paradise" understood as "some place of instruction, and, so to speak, class-room or school of souls" [lines 4828–4830], learning the reasons for earthly diversity. They then ascend through the "many mansions" of the heavenly spheres until the mind "growing by each individual step … is raised as a mind already perfect to perfect knowledge, no longer at all impeded by those carnal senses, but increased in intellectual growth; and ever gazing purely, and, so to speak, face to face, on the causes of things, it attains perfection" [lines 4872–4878]. The ultimate food of the perfected rational creature is "the contemplation and understanding of God" [lines 4888–4889].

Punishments are explicitly remedial rather than retributive. "Hell-fire" is not an external furnace but each sinner's moral history set ablaze: "every sinner kindles for himself the flame of his own fire, and is not plunged into some fire which has been already kindled by another" [lines 4469–4471]. The fire serves as medicine: "God our Physician, desiring to remove the defects of our souls … should employ penal measures of this sort" [lines 4533–4536]. Origen cites Isaiah's promise that "the Lord will sanctify in a burning fire" and Malachi's purifying refiner [lines 4563–4565]. The *apokatastasis* framing appears through 1 Cor 15:28: all things will be subjected to Christ, and through Christ to the Father, "when God is all in all" [line 2850], a state described as transcending all ages and ages of ages.

### The Old Testament and the New Testament

Chapter 4 of Book II is a sustained defence of the identity of the God of the OT with the Father of Jesus Christ, directed against Marcionite and Valentinian dualism. Origen marshals Gospel quotations in which Jesus appeals to OT authority: the formula "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by this or that prophet" demonstrates that "He who sent the prophets, Himself predicted what was to be foretold of Christ" [lines 3010–3012]. Jesus's citation of the Shema ("You shall love the Lord your God …") shows He commends love toward "the God of that law" [lines 3069–3071]. Paul's statement that he serves God "from my forefathers with pure conscience" links the apostolic Gospel to the Abrahamic heritage [lines 3079–3081]. Origen concludes: "the God of the law and the Gospels is one and the same, a just and good God, and that He confers benefits justly, and punishes with kindness; since neither goodness without justice, nor justice without goodness, can display the (real) dignity of the divine nature" [lines 3343–3347]. Apparent divine anger in the OT is not to be taken literally but spiritually — the same hermeneutical principle applies to parables in the Gospels [lines 3183–3190]. The Spirit who inspired the prophets is the same Spirit poured out after Pentecost; the grace of the Holy Spirit enables believers to see past "the mere corporeal meaning" of the law and discover the spiritual sense [lines 3734–3742].

### Subordinationist Statements in Book II

Book II contains several statements bearing on Trinitarian subordinationism. In the discussion of the soul of God (Ch. 8), Origen identifies the "soul of God" with "the only-begotten Son of God. For as the soul, when implanted in the body, moves all things in it … so also the only-begotten Son of God, who is His Word and Wisdom, stretches and extends to every power of God" [lines 4018–4022]. In the Incarnation chapter (Ch. 6), Christ is "the image of the invisible God, the first-born of every creature," with the Father explicitly named as His head: "The head of Christ is God" [lines 3449–3451]. The visibility/knowledge distinction reinforces subordination: even the Son does not *see* the Father (vision being a property of bodies), but *knows* Him — and this knowing is mediated ("No man knows the Father, save the Son, and he to whom the Son will reveal Him" [lines 3162–3163]). These formulations are consistent with the explicit subordinationism of Book I and contributed to later Arian and anti-Arian debates alike.


## Book III — Free Will, Sin, the Adversary

Book III opens with a sustained philosophical and scriptural defense of [[free-will|free will]] (*autexousion*) as the constitutive mark of rational nature. Origen's argument begins from the mechanics of motion: inanimate things are moved entirely from without; animals are "moved from themselves" when a *phantasia* (desire or impression) arises within them; but the rational animal possesses *reason*, which judges between phantasiai and selects among them. Because rational nature has "the power of distinguishing between good and evil, and while distinguishing possesses the faculty of selecting what it has approved, it may justly be deemed worthy of praise in choosing what is good, and deserving of censure in following that which is base or wicked" [lines 4993–4997]. To deny this, Origen says, is to reduce the human being to "pieces of wood, or stones, which have no motion in themselves, but receive the causes of their motion from without" [lines 5047–5048]. The existence of divine commandments is itself proof of freedom: commands directed at those incapable of obeying them would be absurd. Origen marshals Micah 6:8, Moses's "choose what is good" (Deut 30), Isaiah 1:19–20, and Paul's language of accountability (Rom 2:4–9) as scriptural witnesses that "to determine and approve what use we ought to make of those things which thus happen, is the duty of no other than of that reason within us, i.e., of our own judgment" [lines 5009–5011].

**Pharaoh's hardened heart and the rain analogy.** The most troubling objection is Exod 4:21 ("I will harden Pharaoh's heart"), which seems to transfer the cause of sin from the creature to God. Origen treats this at length (chapters 1.8–1.17 in the Rufinus version and its Greek parallel). His solution employs two analogies. First, rain falls equally on all ground, yet produces fruit in cultivated soil and thorns in neglected soil — "every one's will, if untrained, and fierce, and barbarous, is either hardened by the miracles and wonders of God, growing more savage and thorny than ever, or it becomes more pliant" [lines 5290–5293]. Second, the sun melts wax and hardens mud by the same heat: "one and the same working upon the part of God, which was administered by Moses in signs and wonders, made manifest the hardness of Pharaoh, which he had conceived in the intensity of his wickedness" [lines 5304–5307]. God did not create Pharaoh's obduracy; the signs and wonders revealed it. Origen adds a pastoral reading of the "hardening" idiom: a lenient master who tolerates a servant's misbehavior over a long time may be said colloquially to have "made him bad" by forbearing punishment — so God's patience with Pharaoh is what Scripture figuratively calls hardening. Providence also defers easy cures when a premature healing would leave deeper corruption unaddressed: "God deals with souls not merely with a view to the short space of our present life, included within sixty years or more, but with reference to a perpetual and never-ending period, exercising His providential care over souls that are immortal, even as He Himself is eternal and immortal" [lines 5444–5448].

**Jacob/Esau and pre-existing causes.** Origen applies the same logic to Rom 9 (Jacob loved, Esau hated before birth). The potter-and-clay image means that God, perceiving the free choices of souls already made in the pre-existent state, forms vessels to honour or dishonour accordingly — not arbitrarily. "It does not appear absurd that, in discussing remoter causes in the same order, and in the same method, we should come to the same conclusion respecting the nature of souls, and that this was the reason why Jacob was loved before he was born into this world, and Esau hated, while he still was contained in the womb of his mother" [lines 5867–5870]. The single lump of clay from which vessels of honour and dishonour are made represents the single nature shared by all rational creatures — confirming against Gnostic determinism that there are no souls of inherently different kinds.

**[[satan|Satan]], the opposing powers, and their role in temptation.** Chapter 2 surveys the devil's scriptural appearances: the serpent in Genesis (identified with the devil via the *Ascension of Moses*), Satan requesting power over Job, the lying spirit before Ahab, the devil entering Judas, and Paul's "principalities and powers" and "rulers of the darkness of this world" (Eph 6:12). Origen identifies Satan's original fall as pride: "the devil, who viewed as his own, and not as given him by God, the primacy which he held at the time when he was unstained; and thus was fulfilled in him the declaration, that every one who exalts himself shall be abased" [lines 5399–5403]. But Origen explicitly rejects the "simple" view that every human sin is caused by devil-instigation. Natural bodily appetites — hunger, thirst, sexual desire — arise from nature, not demonic suggestion. The devil's role is to seize on pre-existing natural transgressions and amplify them: "we receive certain initial elements, and, as it were, seeds of sins, from those things which we use agreeably to nature; but when we have indulged them beyond what is proper, and have not resisted the first movements to intemperance, then the hostile power, seizing the occasion of this first transgression, incites and presses us hard in every way" [lines 6869–6874]. The adversary thus operates through the opened door of the will, not as the door itself. God permits the adversary's activity within limits calibrated to each soul's strength (1 Cor 10:13), so that temptations are proportioned to capacity — the whole arrangement functioning within the economy of salvation as a kind of moral gymnasium.

**Stages of the soul's moral journey.** The soul's condition is not fixed: Origen insists throughout Book III that rational creatures move up and down a moral continuum across successive ages. Vessels of honour may become vessels of dishonour if they prove negligent; vessels of dishonour may, through cleansing, become vessels of honour "in the new world, sanctified and useful, and prepared unto every good work" [lines 5916–5918]. "It is more agreeable to the rule of piety to believe that every rational being, according to his purpose and manner of life, is converted, sometimes from bad to good, and falls away sometimes from good to bad: that some abide in good, and others advance to a better condition, and always ascend to higher things, until they reach the highest grade of all" [lines 5929–5933]. The telos of this long journey is described in chapter 6: when "all feeling of wickedness has been removed, and the individual has been purified and cleansed, He who alone is the one good God becomes to him all" [lines 7988–7990]. This universal restoration — [[apokatastasis]] — is grounded throughout Book III in the principle that the soul's immortality exceeds any single world-age, and that God's providential care operates across "countless and unmeasured ages" toward the ultimate goal of 1 Cor 15:28, "God all in all."

The book also treats (chapters 3–5) three kinds of wisdom (worldly / princely / divine), human temptations as distinct from demonic ones (natural bodily lusts versus hostile-power incitement), and the world's temporal beginning and eschatological end — all framed by the insistence that freedom of will is preserved at every stage, since "it is not by any accidental attacks that we are assailed" but by a providential arrangement that never overwhelms the creature's capacity to choose.

## Book IV — Scripture and Its Interpretation

Book IV is Origen's sustained methodological treatise on how Scripture must be read. It falls into two broad halves: first, an argument that Scripture is divinely inspired; second, a systematic account of the three senses of Scripture and the theory of intentional "stumbling blocks." The source presents both a Rufinus Latin version and a more literal Greek-based translation of the same material, here treated together. Citations track source line numbers.

### 1. The Argument for Divine Inspiration (§§1–7, lines 8190–8435)

Origen opens by grounding scriptural authority in two empirical facts: prophetic fulfillment and the transforming spread of the Christian message. Moses and Christ are without parallel among legislators in their power to draw foreign nations to their law. The rapid global spread of Christianity, despite persecution, torture, and death, is itself evidence of divine power: "it is not by human power or might that the words of Jesus Christ come to prevail with all faith and power over the understandings and souls of all men" (lines 8240–8242). Specific prophecies are marshaled: the cessation of Jewish kings from the line of Judah fulfilled at the temple's destruction ("For the children of Israel shall abide many days without king and prince: there shall be no victim, nor altar, nor priesthood, nor answers," lines 8268–8270), Daniel's weeks of years, Micah's Bethlehem prophecy, Isaiah's sign of the virgin.

Beyond prophecy, the reader who engages the prophets with due reverence will feel "his mind and senses touched by a divine breath, and will acknowledge that the words which he reads were no human utterances, but the language of God" (lines 8358–8364). Christ's advent "illuminating the law of Moses by the light of truth, has taken away that veil which had been placed over the letter (of the law), and has unsealed, for every one who believes upon Him, all the blessings which were concealed by the covering of the word" (lines 8365–8369). Citing Paul ("We have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the virtue of the divine power may shine out the more brightly," lines 8407–8410), Origen insists that the plainness and even awkwardness of scriptural language is itself evidence of divine design: if the books persuaded by rhetorical art, faith would rest on human wisdom rather than the power of God.

### 2. The Three-Level Hermeneutic and Its Anthropological Basis (§11, lines 8566–8615)

The hermeneutical system derives from Scripture itself. Proverbs enjoins that one should "describe these things to yourself in a threefold manner, in counsel and knowledge" (Prov 22:20–21, cited lines 8572–8574). From this Origen infers three senses corresponding to the three parts of the human person:

> "For as man is said to consist of body, and soul, and spirit, so also does sacred Scripture, which has been granted by the divine bounty for the salvation of man." (lines 8587–8590)

The **somatic** (bodily/literal) sense is "the common and historical sense," edifying "the more simple individuals." The **psychic** (soul) sense edifies those who have made "considerable progress" and can see something more. The **pneumatic** (spiritual) sense is for "those who are perfect," who receive edification from "the spiritual law itself (which has a shadow of good things to come), as if by the Spirit" (lines 8578–8588).

Origen illustrates the scheme through the Shepherd of Hermas: Hermas writes two books, one for Grapte (who admonishes widows and orphans), one for Clement (to send to cities abroad), while Hermas announces to presbyters by living voice. Grapte = the bare letter, admonishing the spiritually immature ("orphans" who cannot yet call God their Father); Clement = the soul-sense, for those already withdrawing from literalism; the Spirit-bearer's oral transmission to the presbyters = the pneumatic sense, conveyed "not by letter nor by book, but by the living voice, to the presbyters of the Church of Christ, i.e., to those who possess a mature faculty of wisdom, capable of receiving spiritual teaching" (lines 8612–8615).

A crucial qualification: not every passage contains all three senses. Some carry no literal/historical sense at all — "there are certain passages of Scripture where this body, as we termed it, i.e., this inferential historical sense, is not always found" (lines 8617–8619). The six water-vessels of John 2 (holding two or three firkins apiece) allegorize this: some passages yield two firkins (soul + spirit), others three (body + soul + spirit) (lines 8620–8648).

### 3. The Theory of "Stumbling Blocks" (*Skandala*) as Intentional Divine Pedagogy (§§15–18, lines 8759–8919)

This is Origen's most distinctive methodological contribution. The Holy Spirit deliberately inserted passages that are impossible or absurd when read literally, forcing the reader past the surface:

> "divine wisdom took care that certain stumbling-blocks, or interruptions, to the historical meaning should take place, by the introduction into the midst (of the narrative) of certain impossibilities and incongruities; that in this way the very interruption of the narrative might, as by the interposition of a bolt, present an obstacle to the reader, whereby he might refuse to acknowledge the way which conducts to the ordinary meaning; and being thus excluded and debarred from it, we might be recalled to the beginning of another way, in order that, by entering upon a narrow path, and passing to a loftier and more sublime road, he might lay open the immense breadth of divine wisdom." (lines 8764–8773)

The Spirit's method is dual: where historical events can carry spiritual sense, the Spirit uses them, "concealing the hidden meaning more deeply." Where the historical account cannot carry the spiritual meaning, "He inserted sometimes certain things which either did not take place or could not take place; sometimes also what might happen, but what did not" (lines 8782–8785). The same applies in the legislative portions — many laws are useful, but others have "no principle of utility" and some are outright impossibilities, placed there "in order that, seeing those events which lie on the surface can be neither true nor useful, we may be led to the investigation of that truth which is more deeply concealed, and to the ascertaining of a meaning worthy of God in those Scriptures which we believe to be inspired by Him" (lines 8792–8796).

Crucially, this is not limited to the Old Testament: "as being one and the same Spirit, and proceeding from one God, He dealt in the same way with the evangelists and apostles" (lines 8799–8801, §16).

### 4. Specific Examples of Allegorical Reading and the Critique of Literalism (§§16–18, lines 8808–8919)

Origen supplies a systematic catalogue of passages that cannot be read literally:

**From Genesis**: The first three days had "evening and morning" without sun, moon, or stars — "who is there, pray, possessed of understanding, that will regard the statement as appropriate, that the first day, and the second, and the third, in which also both evening and morning are mentioned, existed without sun, and moon, and stars— the first day even without a sky?" (lines 8808–8812). God planting a visible tree of life whose wooden fruit confers immortality when eaten "with bodily teeth" is impossible (lines 8812–8817). "No one, I think, can doubt that the statement that God walked in the afternoon in paradise, and that Adam lay hid under a tree, is related figuratively in Scripture, that some mystical meaning may be indicated by it" (lines 8817–8820). "The departure of Cain from the presence of the Lord will manifestly cause a careful reader to inquire what is the presence of God, and how anyone can go out from it" (lines 8820–8823).

**From the Gospels**: The devil leading Jesus "up into a high mountain, that he might show Him from thence all the kingdoms of the world" is impossible literally: "How could it literally come to pass… that the latter should show him all the kingdoms of the world (as if they were lying beneath his bodily eyes, and adjacent to one mountain) — i.e., the kingdoms of the Persians, and Scythians, and Indians?" (lines 8831–8836).

**From the Mosaic Law**: The command that uncircumcised eight-day-old males be "cut off from his people" is "exceedingly incongruous" if taken literally (lines 8844–8852). The goat-stag "cannot possibly exist" yet is listed among clean beasts (lines 8853–8855). The Sabbath command — "You shall sit, everyone in your dwellings; no one shall move from his place on the Sabbath day" — is "impossible to observe literally; for no man can sit a whole day so as not to move from the place where he sat down" (lines 8858–8861). Those who insist on literal Sabbath observance fall into "empty and trifling tales" — computing two thousand cubits for a permitted Sabbath journey, or (following Dositheus the Samaritan) requiring a person to hold whatever posture they occupied at Sabbath's onset for the entire day, and calling a shoe with nails a burden but one without nails not a burden (lines 8862–8881).

**From Gospel commandments**: Saluting no one on the road; possessing neither two coats nor shoes; presenting the left cheek when the right is struck; plucking out the right eye — all yield "not only absurdities, but impossibilities" if read literally (lines 8882–8919).

The polemic against literalism targets three groups: (1) **Jews**, who expected a literal political messiah and disbelieved because wolves and lambs had not literally lain together (lines 8444–8465); (2) **Gnostic/Marcionite heretics**, who read OT divine wrath passages ("A fire has been kindled in Mine anger," "I am a jealous God," lines 8466–8468) literally, concluded the OT God was inferior or evil, and invented a higher silent God — going wrong "by no other (cause) than the not understanding the Scripture according to its spiritual meaning, but the interpretation of it agreeably to the mere letter" (lines 8492–8495); (3) **simple church members**, who affirm the Creator but conceive of God in crudely anthropomorphic terms (lines 8488–8491). Origen insists throughout that most of Scripture is historically true and most commandments are to be literally obeyed — "the passages which hold good in their historical acceptation are much more numerous than those which contain a purely spiritual meaning" (lines 8935–8937).

### 5. Typological Relations Between the Testaments (§13, lines 8648–8695)

The spiritual sense reveals "what are the heavenly things of which these serve as the patterns and shadow, who are Jews according to the flesh, and of what things future the law contains a shadow" (lines 8648–8651). Paul is the model practitioner of this reading. The rock in the wilderness = Christ (1 Cor 10:4, cited lines 8659–8661). The tabernacle = the pattern shown to Moses on the mountain (Heb 8:5, lines 8662–8664). Abraham's two wives = two covenants: "Which things are an allegory: for these are the two covenants" (Gal 4:22–24, lines 8670–8673). Food, drink, new moon, Sabbaths = "a shadow of things to come" (Col 2:16–17, lines 8678–8680). The whole legislation serves "for an example and shadow of heavenly things" (Heb 8:5, lines 8681–8682).

OT geographical names are similarly allegorized: "Israel," "Jerusalem," "Egypt," "Babylon," "Tyre," and "Sidon" designate spiritual realities — souls, their heavenly or fallen conditions, the cosmic geography of rational creatures (§§20–23, lines 8960–9106). The heavenly Jerusalem of Paul (Gal 4:26; Heb 12:22) is the metropolis of a spiritual people; prophecy about earthly Jerusalem must be understood spiritually. Ezekiel's oracles against Pharaoh and the prince of Tyre cannot refer to human rulers — no man "fell from heaven" or "was Lucifer" (lines 9019–9026). The descent of seventy souls into Egypt and the entire Exodus narrative are treated as spiritual allegory (§24, lines 9890–9962).

### 6. The "Spiritual Law" and the "Eternal Gospel" (§25, lines 9963–9980)

Origen closes with an eschatological hermeneutic. "Deuteronomy" (the "second law") types a deeper reality: as the first Mosaic law was fulfilled by the Gospel (Joshua typifying the Saviour who enacts a "second legislation" — "by whose second law— that is, the precepts of the Gospel— all things are brought to perfection," lines 9960–9962), so the present Gospel — itself still "a shadow of good things to come" — will be superseded at the second advent by an "everlasting Gospel":

> "as in His coming now He fulfilled that law which has a shadow of good things to come, so also by that (future) glorious advent will be fulfilled and brought to perfection the shadows of the present advent… when He will more worthily transfer all the saints from a temporal to an everlasting Gospel, according to the designation, employed by John in the Apocalypse, of an everlasting Gospel." (lines 9972–9980)

This yields a three-stage hermeneutical eschatology: (1) the Mosaic law as shadow; (2) the present Gospel as fulfillment of that shadow, itself a further shadow; (3) the eternal Gospel as the final unveiled reality. The "spiritual law" is therefore not a timeless alternative to the letter but a stage in a progressive divine pedagogy moving toward a knowledge that surpasses all written mediation.

## Key Theological Positions

Six positions emerge across all four books as the theological signature of *De Principiis*:

**1. God as simple, incorporeal Monad.** Book I argues that God is "a simple intellectual nature," absolutely incomprehensible, without parts. The Son is "a second God" (*deuteros theos*) eternally generated from the Father, distinct in hypostasis but derivative in being; the Spirit sanctifies in descending rank. This subordinationist Trinitarianism contributed the eternal-generation formula ("always generated; never was a time when he was not") that Nicaea would refine.

**2. Rational creatures created equal, differentiated by fall.** All *logika* began equal and spiritual; their varying embodiments (angels, humans, demons) reflect degrees of prior falling-away from contemplative union with God. Pre-existence of souls — condemned in 553 CE.

**3. The world as remedial school; multiple world-ages.** The cosmos exists to rehabilitate fallen souls. Successive *aeones* each calibrate to the moral state of the souls in them; God's justice is always also therapy.

**4. Free will (*autexousion*) as ground of moral responsibility.** Rational nature is constitutively free. No divine decree overrides it; even Pharaoh's hardening is God's exposure of a freedom already misused. The soul is a battlefield of freedom and necessity across world-ages.

**5. *Apokatastasis* (universal restoration).** At the end of all ages every rational creature — including the Devil — will be restored to union with God, "God all in all" (1 Cor 15:28). Follows from divine love combined with rational capacity. Condemned; remains Origen's most discussed doctrine.

**6. The three-level hermeneutic as master key.** Book IV's somatic/psychic/pneumatic reading (literal/moral/spiritual) is the most systematic Christian allegorical method before the Quadriga. Origen's key innovation: "stumbling blocks" (*skandala*) — historically impossible or morally offensive passages — are deliberately inserted by the Spirit to compel the reader to the spiritual sense. The eternal Gospel is the eschatological fulfillment toward which temporal Gospel and Law point.

## The Subordinationist Controversy

Origen's Trinity is explicitly hierarchical: the Father is the sole *Monad* and source; the Son is "second God" (*deuteros theos*), eternally generated but ontologically subordinate; the Holy Spirit is third in rank. This **subordinationism** was later condemned as heterodox by Nicene orthodoxy, yet Origen's eternal generation formula ("always generated, never was when he was not") contributed positively to the Nicene *homoousion* debate.

**Controversy**: see [[origenist-condemnations|Origenist Condemnations]] — the subordinationism question was central to both the Arian crisis and the later anti-Origenist synods.

## Pre-existence of Souls and *Apokatastasis*

Two positions in *De Principiis* drew formal condemnation at the Fifth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople II, 553 CE):

1. **Pre-existence of souls**: all rational creatures were created equal and spiritual; their varying conditions (angel/human/demon) result from the degree of their original fall from contemplation of God
2. ***Apokatastasis*** (universal restoration): at the end of all ages, all rational creatures — including the Devil — will be restored to unity with God (1 Cor 15:28, "God will be all in all")

## Text Tradition and the Rufinus Problem

The original Greek *Peri Archon* is largely lost. The primary text is Rufinus of Aquileia's Latin translation (c. 397–398 CE), which explicitly states it has "smoothed out" passages he deemed later Origenist interpolations or scribal corruptions — making it impossible to know precisely what Origen wrote. Jerome's hostile literal renderings of selected passages provide some check. Modern scholarship (Crouzel, Simonetti; Butterworth's English trans.) reconstructs Origen's intent from the combination of Rufinus, Jerome's fragments, and Philocalia excerpts.

## Canon and Reception

**Not scripture in any tradition.** Reception:
- **Authoritative for**: no tradition; read as theological speculation even by admirers
- **Disputed by**: mainstream Christianity — the work's subordinationism and pre-existence doctrines make it controversial
- **Condemned by**: Fifth Ecumenical Council (553 CE), 15 anathemas
- Preserved and transmitted *despite* condemnation; studied by Cappadocians, Jerome, Rufinus; the *Philocalia* (Basil + Gregory of Nazianzus) excerpted it; Renaissance and modern theological revival
