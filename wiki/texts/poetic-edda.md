The **Poetic Edda** (or Elder Edda) is the **single most important source for Norse mythology** — a collection
of anonymous **Old Norse poems** preserved mainly in the Icelandic manuscript **Codex Regius** (c. 1270),
though the poems themselves are older and oral. Where the [[prose-edda|Prose Edda]] is one author's handbook,
the Poetic Edda is the **raw mythic and heroic poetry** itself.

## Content

It falls into two groups:

- **Mythological poems** — including the **Völuspá** ("Prophecy of the Seeress"), the majestic poem that
  narrates the **creation of the world and its end at [[ragnarok|Ragnarök]]**; the **Hávamál** ("Sayings of
  the High One"), [[odin|Odin's]] wisdom-poem (including his self-sacrifice to win the runes); the
  **Grímnismál** and **Vafþrúðnismál** (cosmological lore); the **Lokasenna** (Loki's flyting); the
  **Þrymskviða** (Thor recovering his stolen hammer); and the **Baldrs draumar** (Baldr's death-dreams).
- **Heroic poems** — the legends of Sigurd the dragon-slayer, the Völsungs, Brynhild, and the Niflungs (the
  Norse form of the Germanic legend behind the *Nibelungenlied* and Wagner's *Ring*).

## Significance

The Poetic Edda is the **foundation document** of [[norse-religion|Norse religion]] (and of modern
[[asatru|Heathenry]]). Its **Völuspá** gives the fullest mythic account of the cosmos's birth from
[[ymir|Ymir]], the world-tree [[yggdrasil|Yggdrasil]], and the doom and rebirth of the gods. Like all Norse
sources it was **written down in Christian Iceland**, raising the standard caution about Christian coloring of
a pagan inheritance.
