Jeremiah is the longest prophetic scroll in the Hebrew Bible (52 chapters; longer than Isaiah by verse count) and among the most theologically complex. It spans the last decades of the Kingdom of Judah — from Josiah's reform through the Babylonian conquest and fall of Jerusalem in 587 BCE — and contains the most personal prophetic witness in the OT, the most developed theology of divine-human relationship through unfaithfulness and restoration, and the central OT text for the New Testament theology of covenant: the New Covenant promise of 31:31-34, the longest OT quotation in the NT (Hebrews 8 and 10).

## Historical Background

Lalleman presupposes call in the thirteenth year of Josiah = **627 BC** and fall in the eleventh year of Zedekiah = **587 BC** (temple burned August 587, fifth month) (TOTC raw 897–898). Career span under that frame:

**Era 1: Josiah's reign (627–609 BCE)**  
Josiah's reform (622; 2 Kgs 22–23) and discovery of the Book of the Law (parts of Deuteronomy). Lalleman denies that Deuteronomy was written then "only in order to justify the reform" (raw 864). Jeremiah barely treats the reform; she reads 3:10 ("Judah did not return with her whole heart, but in pretence") as possibly referring to superficial Josianic response (raw 864–865).

**Era 2: Jehoiakim through the fall (609–587 BCE)**  
After Josiah's death at Megiddo, Jehoiakim resists Jeremiah. **Lalleman's structural hinge:** Jehoiakim burns the prophetic scroll (Jer 36; first scroll written 605; public reading December **604**). She states: "this event (604 BC) marked the 'point of no return' in Judah's history and the dynasty of David" (raw 870–871). Carchemish (605) brings Babylonian domination; deportations 597 and 587 follow. Zedekiah consults Jeremiah but fears officials; rebels 589; 18-month siege; fall 587 (raw 874–878).

**Era 3: Aftermath (587 onward)**  
Gedaliah murdered; remnant forces Jeremiah and Baruch to Egypt (Jer 43); idolatry resumes (Jer 44). "Nothing is known about the end of Jeremiah's life" (raw 880–881). Jehoiachin's release under Awel-Marduk is a "glimmer of hope" (raw 882; 52:31–34).

## Composition: The "Two Versions" Problem

Jeremiah is unique among prophetic books in having two substantially different ancient versions:
- **Masoretic Text (MT)**: The standard Hebrew Bible; approximately one-seventh *longer* than the LXX
- **Septuagint (LXX)**: A Greek translation preserving a shorter Hebrew Vorlage; the Oracles Against the Nations appear in a different location (after 25:13, not at chs. 46-51)

Dead Sea Scrolls fragments corroborate that both versions reflect distinct Hebrew originals, not merely translational differences. Goldingay's interpretation: both versions are authoritative — like having Matthew and Luke rather than just Mark. MT is "a bit like Luke-Acts" (expanded, takes the story further); LXX is "a bit like Matthew" (shorter, its own coherent shape). Neither is simply "better."

## Structure

Scholarship often places a major hinge at **ch. 25**. **Lalleman begs to differ**: she keeps **23–29** together because "the theme of 'false prophets versus true prophet' extends throughout these chapters" (raw 1542–1543). Her working outline (Analysis + intro raw 1545–1600; 1606–2055):

| Section | Chapters | Lalleman label / note |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | 1 | Call; inclusio with 52 |
| Calls to repentance | 2–6 | Pre-604 material; marriage lawsuit; *šûb* dense |
| Covenant broken; laments | 7–20 | Temple sermon; Confessions; drought; despair (20) |
| Concerning kings | 21–22 | Royal justice; Shallum, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin |
| True vs false prophecy | 23–29 | **Central block** in her reading; figs; 70 years; Hananiah; letter to exiles |
| **Book of Comfort** | **30–33** | Hope; new covenant; field purchase; David/Levi |
| Zedekiah and Jehoiakim | 34–38 | Slaves; Rekabites; **scroll burning (36)**; prison |
| Final days | 39–45 | Fall; Gedaliah; Egypt remnant; Baruch (45) |
| Oracles Against the Nations | 46–51 | MT order; Egypt…Babylon bookends |
| Epilogue | 52 | Fulfilment proof; Jehoiachin glimmer |

**Goldingay** works with the more common two-half / ch. 25 hinge picture and the MT/LXX "two versions" problem (see Composition above). Both readings are recorded; neither is promoted to sole wiki-voice structure.

## Key Theological Themes

### 1. The Verb *Šûb* (Turn/Return/Repent)

**Lalleman's word-study thesis** (TOTC Introduction §5, raw 997–1036): *šûb* ("to turn") runs from urgent human repentance (dense in 3:1–4:4; 8:4–5) to God as subject who restores fortunes and causes return from exile (24; 29:11–14; Book of Comfort 30–33, where *šûb* is "a key word for all of chaps. 30–33," citing Lundbom; raw 4483–4484).

> "before the burning of the scroll by Jehoiakim in 604 BC, the people could still return and repent, but this event marked a 'point of no return'. From then on, the only possible avenue of hope was God's promise that he would 'restore' his people…" (raw 1034–1035)

In 30–31 she stresses contrast with ch. 3: there the people are called to repent; here "God is the one bringing the people back… Only God can do this, as the people themselves are incapable of repentance" (raw 4483–4484). Wordplay at 31:18 (return/repent) and "faithless" from *šûb* (31:22) continue the arc.

### 2. The Marriage Metaphor (Divine-Human Covenant as Marriage)

Jeremiah is the second prophet (after Hosea) to develop the divine-human marriage metaphor extensively:
- 2:1-2: YHWH remembers Israel's early "bridal love" in the wilderness
- 3:1: The legal impossibility of taking back a divorced wife — yet God offers to take Israel back anyway
- 3:6-10: Two sisters (Israel and Judah) as faithless wives
- The entire first half of the book is structured as a covenant lawsuit: YHWH as wronged husband, Israel as adulterous wife

This directly extends the [[divine-human-marriage|Divine-Human Marriage Metaphor]] established by Hosea and developed in Ezekiel 16/23 and Isaiah 54.

### 3. Two Metaphor Clusters (Lalleman)

**Build/plant vs. tear down/uproot** (1:10): four negative verbs vs two positive ≈ more judgment than hope in the commission (raw 1044–1045). Applied to Judah and nations (12:14–17; 18:7–9). For Judah, tearing down first; building/planting through exile (24:6; 29:5; 31:27–28; 42:10; 45:4) — both metaphorical and literal (raw 1045–1058).

**Medical metaphors**: wound, healing, sickness, balm across judgment, hope, and laments (8:21–22; 10:19; 14:19; 15:18; 30:12–17; 33:6; 46:11; 51:8–9; false healers 6:13–14; 8:10–11) (raw 1060–1115). Lalleman links rare medical vocabulary to Hosea influence and priestly-family familiarity with purity language, while assuming Jeremiah did not serve as a cultic priest (raw 1118–1119).

### 4. The Confessions (Personal Laments)

Five units Lalleman lists (preferring "laments" to "Confessions," raw 1288): 11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:12–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18. Enemies oppose him *because* he prophesies; final Confession most desperate (fire in bones, 20:9). As mediator, "In Jeremiah's suffering we can even hear God's suffering on account of his people, and his compassion for them" (raw 1368).

**Goldingay's** formulation: "In Jeremiah, the message — the *word* (*haddābār*) — became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." The prophet's suffering and protests are themselves part of the revelation.

### 5. The "Both-And" Theology

The most theologically sophisticated feature of Goldingay's reading: the scroll refuses to speak univocally on major theological questions:
- Is judgment inevitable or can it be averted? Both — "It's never over until it's over" (18:1-11)
- Will judgment mean total destruction or just decimation? Both — "I will make a full end of you" (4:27) and "I will not make a full end of you" (5:18)
- Will the Davidic monarchy be restored? Both
- Does God detest Israel's worship or can it be redeemed? Both
- Are the nations simply doomed or is there hope for them? Both (most nations receive a restoration promise)

This is not incoherence. It is what Goldingay calls "both-and theology": the scroll equips its readers for contextual discernment — to recognize "which time it is" and bring out the needed truth for this moment. "A prophet is someone who knows what time it is."

## The New Covenant (31:31-34)

Theological climax of the Book of Comfort and central OT text for NT soteriology. Full concept treatment: [[new-covenant|New Covenant]].

**Lalleman** (comment on 31:31–34, raw 4546): Sinai terms remain (covenant, law, "their God… my people"), but "it will not be like that covenant" because **the people** broke Sinai — "God did not break it; the people did." Yet God does "what was impossible in treaties between human LORDs and vassals: this LORD makes a new covenant." Law is "internalized"; heart as seat of will/decision (OT anthropology); sin once engraved on hearts (17:1, 9) will be overcome by transformation; forgiveness removes hindrances; corporate "they will be my people" is not abolished. "There is continuity between the old and the new, and yet there will be a radical new beginning." Ch. 31 Meaning: 31:31–34 is "the summit of Jeremiah's message of hope"; NT application Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:23–25; Heb 8:8–12; 10:16–17; basis is "God's eternal love and compassion (31:3, 20)" (raw 4554–4555). Parallel restatement as **everlasting covenant** at 32:40 (raw 4620–4621).

## Jeremiah and the NT

- **Hebrews 8 and 10**: New Covenant (31:31-34) — longest OT quote in NT; foundational for atonement theology
- **Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25**: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" — Jesus directly cites Jer 31:31 at the Last Supper
- **2 Corinthians 3:3-6**: Paul contrasts the "letter that kills" with "ministers of a new covenant"
- **Matthew 2:18**: Rachel weeping for her children (Jer 31:15) — applied to the massacre of the innocents
- **Revelation 18**: Fall of Babylon echoes Jer 50-51 extensively
- **Luke 19:46**: "My house shall be a house of prayer" combines Isa 56:7 with Jer 7:11 ("den of robbers") — Jesus's Temple Sermon echoes Jeremiah's Temple Sermon

## Canon and Reception

- **Canonical for** rabbinic Judaism (Latter Prophets), Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant canons (`canon_scope` above).
- **MT vs LXX** reception: shorter Greek arrangement (OAN after 25:13a) vs longer Hebrew; DSS support dual Hebrew editions (Lalleman summarizing Tov, raw 912–917).
- **NT reception:** new covenant (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25; Heb 8; 10); Rachel (Matt 2:18); temple as den of robbers (Matt 21:13 // Jer 7:11); extensive Rev 18 // Jer 50–51 (Lalleman list raw 1497–1523).
- **Christian figural:** Lalleman reads Jeremiah as forerunner of the Suffering Servant / Christ while stressing he cannot "carry away the sins of the world" (raw 421–422, 1526).

## Scholarship and Historiography

- **Dates:** Lalleman 627–587 fixed frame (raw 897); Goldingay finalization positions recorded on frontmatter `date_range`.
- **Composition:** Lalleman — Baruch as precise scribe (ch. 36); growth during ministry; ch. 52 later vindication; rejects proof-light Deuteronomist schools (raw 930–981). Goldingay — curators/storytellers; dual-version authority.
- **Structure debates:** ch. 25 hinge (common) vs Lalleman's 23–29 true/false-prophecy centre (raw 1542–1543); Peels on MT OAN placement (raw 1404–1405).
- **Recent literary turn:** Lalleman cites Lundbom and Allen (final-form / "country house" growth) as closer interlocutors than pure source-criticism (raw 942–949).

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **Is judgment still avertible?** Lalleman: yes until 604 scroll-burning, then no (raw 870–871). Goldingay: both-and / "which time it is."
2. **Who authors the book?** Traditional Baruch+Jeremiah growth vs multi-stage Deuteronomistic editions — positions, not verdict.
3. **MT or LXX "better"?** Dual editions; Lalleman chooses MT for exegesis (raw 922–923); Goldingay both authoritative.
4. **New covenant continuity vs supersession:** Lalleman continuity of formula + radical internalization; Hebrews reads obsolescence of first covenant — keep streams separate.
5. **Confessions:** biographical raw pain vs liturgical persona — Lalleman: laments of a prophet opposed *because* he prophesies; suffering mediates God's (raw 1288–1368).

## Goldingay NICOT — Reingest Anchors (2026-07-18)

Fidelity re-ingest of [[goldingay-jeremiah-nicot|Goldingay, *Jeremiah* NICOT]] (Intro + Parts 1–5 + Epilogue). Selected load-bearing claims:

- **Composition (Intro):** Three horizons (delivery / 604 Baruch scroll / post-587→final not before 562; near-final ~550s–540s). **Curators and storytellers** (not “Deuteronomists”). **Gospels analogy:** 604 ≈ Mark, LXX ≈ Matthew, MT ≈ Luke-Acts; both versions authentic.
- **Structure:** Parts 1–5 + Prologue/Epilogue frame; **ch. 25 as hinge/central axis** (604; poetry→stories; disaster-half→more restoration); MT/LXX most spectacular divergence at 25 (OAN placement).
- **Temple sermon (7):** place not talisman; robbers’ cave; Shiloh precedent; “confessions” = rhetoric/proclamation, not spiritual diary.
- **Pledge language:** *bərît* often “pledge”; marriage/adoption analogies for Sinai.
- **604 / Jer 36:** column-by-column burning “nails the coffin shut” / point of no return (with Lalleman on hinge date; structure differs).
- **Book of Consolation (30–33):** “glowing centre”; **new pledge 31:31–34** — tôrâ on mind; husband/*bāʿal*; mysterious relation of Yahweh’s transform and Israel’s turn; field purchase (32); everlasting pledge (32:40).
- **OAN / Babylon:** integral; restoration last word for many nations; dual will (submit now / Babylon fall later); Seraiah colophon (51:59–64).
- **Epilogue 52:** frame with ch. 1; calamity short of annihilation; Jehoiachin release as glimmer of hope.

**Tension with Lalleman (record, do not adjudicate):** Goldingay **ch. 25 hinge/axis** vs Lalleman **23–29 true/false-prophecy centre** (and softer break at 25). Both treat 604 scroll-burning as decisive for Judah’s fate.
