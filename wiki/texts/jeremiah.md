Jeremiah is the longest prophetic scroll in the Hebrew Bible (52 chapters; longer than Isaiah by verse count) and among the most theologically complex. It spans the last decades of the Kingdom of Judah — from Josiah's reform through the Babylonian conquest and fall of Jerusalem in 587 BCE — and contains the most personal prophetic witness in the OT, the most developed theology of divine-human relationship through unfaithfulness and restoration, and the central OT text for the New Testament theology of covenant: the New Covenant promise of 31:31-34, the longest OT quotation in the NT (Hebrews 8 and 10).

## Historical Background

Jeremiah was called to prophesy in 627 BCE during the reign of Josiah (640-609 BCE). The 65 years of his career span three historical eras:

**Era 1: Josiah's Reign (627-609 BCE)**
Josiah's religious reform (initiated 622 BCE; 2 Kings 22-23) cleansed the temple and reimposed Torah observance. A "Book of the Law" was discovered in the temple — generally identified with some form of Deuteronomy. Jeremiah's relationship to this reform is complex: he critiques the people's superficial response (3:10: "Judah did not return with her whole heart, but in pretense"), suggesting the reform did not produce genuine change.

**Era 2: Jehoiakim through the Fall (609-587 BCE)**
After Josiah's death (killed by Pharaoh Necho at Megiddo), successive kings reverted to pre-Josianic practices. The pivotal moment: Jehoiakim's burning of Jeremiah's scroll (Jer 36, 604 BCE) — Lalleman identifies this as the book's "point of no return." After this, the call to human repentance (*šûb*) gives way to God's unilateral promise to restore. The Battle of Carchemish (605 BCE) brought Judah under Babylonian domination. Three deportations followed: 605, 597, and 587 (total destruction of city and temple).

**Era 3: Aftermath (587 BCE onward)**
After the fall, Jeremiah remained in the ruins under governor Gedaliah, then was taken against his will to Egypt by fleeing Judeans (Jer 43). Nothing is known of his death. The scroll was finalized in the decades following 587.

## Composition: The "Two Versions" Problem

Jeremiah is unique among prophetic books in having two substantially different ancient versions:
- **Masoretic Text (MT)**: The standard Hebrew Bible; approximately one-seventh *longer* than the LXX
- **Septuagint (LXX)**: A Greek translation preserving a shorter Hebrew Vorlage; the Oracles Against the Nations appear in a different location (after 25:13, not at chs. 46-51)

Dead Sea Scrolls fragments corroborate that both versions reflect distinct Hebrew originals, not merely translational differences. Goldingay's interpretation: both versions are authoritative — like having Matthew and Luke rather than just Mark. MT is "a bit like Luke-Acts" (expanded, takes the story further); LXX is "a bit like Matthew" (shorter, its own coherent shape). Neither is simply "better."

## Structure

The book falls into two halves with ch. 25 as hinge:

**First Half (chs. 1-25)**: Dominated by prophetic messages in poetry and prose, mostly warning and confrontation; organized around recurring assertions "The word of the LORD came to me..."

**Chapter 25**: Both concludes the message cycle and introduces the nations material

**Second Half (chs. 26-52)**: Dominated by narrative stories about Jeremiah; incorporates the Book of Comfort (chs. 30-33), the biography of the fall, oracles against nations, and the epilogue

## Key Theological Themes

### 1. The Verb *Šûb* (Turn/Return/Repent)

*Šûb* appears over 110 times in Jeremiah — more than any other prophetic book — and carries the book's entire theological development (Lalleman):

**Phase 1 (before 604 BCE)**: *šûb* in human imperative — "Turn back! Repent!" The call is urgent; judgment can still be averted. Jeremiah 3-4 is dominated by these calls.

**Phase 2 (after 604 BCE)**: *šûb* shifts to God as the grammatical subject — "I will *make* them return; I will restore their fortunes." Repentance and return are now God's gifts, not human achievements. The new covenant promise (31:18: "Restore me and I will return, for you are the LORD my God") is the clearest expression.

This shift from human *šûb* to divine *šûb* tracks the 604 BCE turning point: after Jehoiakim's burning of the scroll, human initiative has exhausted itself and only divine initiative can produce the promised restoration.

### 2. The Marriage Metaphor (Divine-Human Covenant as Marriage)

Jeremiah is the second prophet (after Hosea) to develop the divine-human marriage metaphor extensively:
- 2:1-2: YHWH remembers Israel's early "bridal love" in the wilderness
- 3:1: The legal impossibility of taking back a divorced wife — yet God offers to take Israel back anyway
- 3:6-10: Two sisters (Israel and Judah) as faithless wives
- The entire first half of the book is structured as a covenant lawsuit: YHWH as wronged husband, Israel as adulterous wife

This directly extends the [[divine-human-marriage|Divine-Human Marriage Metaphor]] established by Hosea and developed in Ezekiel 16/23 and Isaiah 54.

### 3. Two Metaphor Clusters

**Build/Plant vs. Tear Down/Uproot** (from 1:10): Jeremiah's commission encompasses both destruction and restoration. Four verbs for judgment; two for hope. The negative majority reflects the proportion of the book's content. Both are applied to Judah and the nations. Crucially: the exile itself is the *means* of building and planting — the exiles in Babylon are the "good figs" (ch. 24), the remnant through whom the new future comes.

**Medical Metaphors**: Judah is an "incurably wounded" nation (8:21-22; 30:12-13); Egypt and Babylon are also fatally ill; God alone is the physician. "Is there no balm in Gilead?" (8:22) — the rhetorical question presupposes the answer: not from any human source. The same medical vocabulary appears in salvation passages: "I will restore you to health and heal your wounds" (30:17).

### 4. The Confessions (Personal Laments)

The most distinctive feature of Jeremiah among all OT prophets: a series of personal protest-prayers in which Jeremiah argues with God about his prophetic calling:
- 11:18-23; 12:1-6: Plot against his life; "Why does the path of the wicked prosper?"
- 15:10-21: "Woe is me, my mother, that you bore me"; God's response and recommissioning
- 17:14-18: "Heal me, O LORD"
- 18:18-23: Ultimate imprecatory prayer against his enemies
- 20:7-18: "You have deceived me, LORD... Cursed be the day I was born"

Goldingay's formulation: "In Jeremiah, the message — the *word* (*haddābār*) — became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." The prophet's suffering and protests are themselves part of the revelation. Jeremiah embodies what it costs to bear God's word in a resistant generation — and thus foreshadows the ultimate Word made flesh.

### 5. The "Both-And" Theology

The most theologically sophisticated feature of Goldingay's reading: the scroll refuses to speak univocally on major theological questions:
- Is judgment inevitable or can it be averted? Both — "It's never over until it's over" (18:1-11)
- Will judgment mean total destruction or just decimation? Both — "I will make a full end of you" (4:27) and "I will not make a full end of you" (5:18)
- Will the Davidic monarchy be restored? Both
- Does God detest Israel's worship or can it be redeemed? Both
- Are the nations simply doomed or is there hope for them? Both (most nations receive a restoration promise)

This is not incoherence. It is what Goldingay calls "both-and theology": the scroll equips its readers for contextual discernment — to recognize "which time it is" and bring out the needed truth for this moment. "A prophet is someone who knows what time it is."

## The New Covenant (31:31-34)

The theological climax of the book and the central OT text for NT soteriology. See [[new-covenant|New Covenant]] for full treatment.

> "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah... I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts. And I will be their God, and they shall be my people. And no longer shall each one teach his neighbor and each his brother, saying, 'Know the LORD,' for they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest, declares the LORD. For I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more." (31:31-34)

Four characteristics of the new covenant:
1. **Internalized Torah**: written on hearts, not tablets of stone
2. **Universal knowledge of God**: no need for mediated teaching; direct knowing
3. **Mutual relationship**: "I will be their God; they shall be my people" — the covenant formula, now finally fulfilled
4. **Forgiveness that forgets**: "I will remember their sin no more"

This passage is quoted in Hebrews 8:8-12 and 10:16-17 — together constituting the longest OT quotation in the entire NT — as the Christological and soteriological foundation for Jesus's high priesthood and the supersession of the Levitical system.

## Jeremiah and the NT

- **Hebrews 8 and 10**: New Covenant (31:31-34) — longest OT quote in NT; foundational for atonement theology
- **Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25**: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" — Jesus directly cites Jer 31:31 at the Last Supper
- **2 Corinthians 3:3-6**: Paul contrasts the "letter that kills" with "ministers of a new covenant"
- **Matthew 2:18**: Rachel weeping for her children (Jer 31:15) — applied to the massacre of the innocents
- **Revelation 18**: Fall of Babylon echoes Jer 50-51 extensively
- **Luke 19:46**: "My house shall be a house of prayer" combines Isa 56:7 with Jer 7:11 ("den of robbers") — Jesus's Temple Sermon echoes Jeremiah's Temple Sermon
