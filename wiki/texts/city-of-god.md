# The City of God (*De Civitate Dei contra Paganos*)

[[augustine|Augustine of Hippo]]'s twenty-two–book apologetic masterwork, "Against the Pagans,"
written **413–426 CE**. It is not scripture but the most influential work of patristic theology in
the Latin West; the translator's preface calls it "the first real effort to produce a philosophy of
history." This page covers **Part 1, Books I–X** (the refutation), **Part 2, Books XI–XVIII**
(the origin and historical course of the two cities), and **Part 3, Books XIX–XXII** (the ends of the
two cities), in the NPNF translation — the whole work read in full.

## Occasion and Design

The work was occasioned by the **sack of Rome by Alaric's Visigoths in 410 CE** — an event that
shook the whole known world, since many (pagans and Christians alike) believed Rome's fall presaged
the end of the world. Pagans blamed the catastrophe on the abandonment of the old gods and the
prohibition of their worship under a now-Christian empire. Augustine's friend **Marcellinus** (the
imperial commissioner at the 411 Carthage conference against the Donatists) urged him to answer the
charge. The result outgrew earlier apologetics (Justin, Tertullian, replies to Celsus/Porphyry/Julian)
into a comprehensive account of the relation of Christianity to the whole pagan order — moral,
political, philosophical, and religious.

The governing theme is the **two cities** ([[two-cities]]): the *civitas Dei*, the community of God's
people living by faith as a stranger in this world and awaiting its eternal seat, set against the
*civitas terrena*, the earthly city "mistress of the nations yet ruled by its lust of rule." Augustine
traces their entanglement from the fall of the angels to the last judgment.

## Structure

The twenty-two books divide into two great parts:

- **Books I–X — Refutation (Part 1, this ingest).** Against the pagans. Books **I–V** argue that the
  Roman gods never secured Rome temporal welfare (and that Rome suffered worse before Christ);
  Books **VI–X** argue that the gods cannot secure **eternal life** either, engaging Varro's civil
  theology and then the Platonists, demons, and the question of mediation.
- **Books XI–XXII — Construction.** The origin, history, and ends of the two cities. Books **XI–XIV**
  (Part 2a) treat the **origin** of the two cities — creation, the angels and their fall, time and
  eternity, the fall of man, and the two loves that found the cities. Books **XV–XVIII** (Part 2b)
  trace their **historical course** in parallel — sacred history from Cain and Abel through the
  patriarchs, kings, and prophets, set beside the world-empires (Assyria/Babylon, then Rome). Books
  **XIX–XXII** (Part 3) treat their **ends** — the supreme good, the last judgment, hell, and the
  eternal felicity of the city of God.

### Books I–X — argument by book

- **Book I** — Against pagans who blame Christ for Rome's sack. The barbarians spared those who fled to
  the churches and martyr-shrines (unprecedented in the history of war) for Christ's sake; the same
  affliction "proves, purges, clarifies the good, but damns, ruins, exterminates the wicked"; on the
  violated Christian women (purity is a virtue of the *soul*, not lost when the body is violated); the
  dilemma of [[lucretia|Lucretia]] and a sustained argument against **suicide** as homicide forbidden by
  "Thou shalt not kill" (with Cato and Regulus as foils).
- **Book II** — Rome's *moral* ruin under the gods before Christ. The gods issued no laws for a virtuous
  life but demanded **obscene theatrical games** in their own honor. Augustine deploys Cicero (*De
  Republica*) and Sallust as pagan witnesses that Rome's moral collapse — after the destruction of
  Carthage removed the restraining fear of an enemy — long predated Christianity; "true justice has no
  existence save in that republic whose founder and ruler is Christ."
- **Book III** — Rome's *temporal/physical* disasters under the gods before Christ: the two destructions
  of Troy (which worshipped the same gods as Greece), Romulus's unpunished fratricide, the rape of the
  Sabines, plagues, the Punic Wars (Regulus, Cannae, faithful **Saguntum** destroyed), the Mithridatic
  massacre, and the Marian–Sullan civil wars — "peace vied with war in cruelty, and surpassed it." The
  gods are convicted of being either absent or powerless.
- **Book IV** — The empire's greatness was not the gift of the swarm of petty Roman gods (three deities
  for a single door). "Justice removed, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (the Alexander-and-pirate
  anecdote). The prior Assyrian empire lasted ~1,240 years without Rome's gods; the one true God gives
  earthly kingdoms "to good and bad" by a hidden [[divine-providence|providential]] order.
- **Book V** — Against [[fate-moirai|fate]] and astrology (the argument from twins; Nigidius's
  potter's-wheel); God's foreknowledge is compatible with **free will** (our wills are themselves causes
  within the order God foreknows — against Cicero). The real cause of Rome's greatness was God rewarding
  genuine Roman civic virtue, the **love of glory** ([[gloria-love-of-praise|gloria]]), with earthly
  empire — "they have received their reward." On the true felicity of Christian emperors (Constantine,
  Theodosius).
- **Book VI** — The argument turns from temporal goods to **eternal life**, which the gods equally fail
  to give. Augustine deploys [[varro|Varro]]'s tripartition of theology — mythical, natural, civil
  ([[three-theologies]]) — to show the civil theology is no better than the theatrical; Seneca is a
  bolder witness who "worshipped what he censured."
- **Book VII** — Against the "select gods" of the civil theology (Janus, Jupiter, Saturn…) and Varro's
  physical/allegorical interpretations of them as natural forces — internally incoherent and terminating
  in "things corporeal, temporal, mutable, and mortal," never the true God. Closes with Numa Pompilius's
  buried books, burned by the senate because their (demonic) causes were more scandalous than the rites.
- **Book VIII** — **Natural theology** and the **Platonists**, "nearest of all to us," who grasped God as
  immaterial, immutable cause of being, truth, and good (a sketch of philosophy from Thales and Pythagoras
  to Socrates and [[plato|Plato]]; Exodus's "I AM WHO AM" as the nearest parallel). Yet they conceded
  worship to many gods; a sustained attack on [[apuleius|Apuleius]]'s doctrine of **demons as aerial
  mediators** subject to passions, and on Hermes Trismegistus.
- **Book IX** — Whether *good* demons can mediate: Apuleius's own definition collapses the good/evil
  distinction. Demons are "an eternal misery" — immortal in body (with the gods) yet wretched in soul
  (with men), "suspended head downwards." The true **Mediator** must share mortality with men and
  blessedness with God: Christ alone, "Mediator as He is man." Demons have "knowledge without charity."
- **Book X** — Worship and sacrifice (**latreia**) are due to the one God alone; the good angels refuse
  worship. True [[sacrifice]] is the contrite heart and the whole redeemed city offered through Christ
  the High Priest ("we, being many, are one body in Christ"); Old Testament sacrifices are its figural
  signs ([[typology]]). A sustained refutation of [[porphyry|Porphyry]]'s **theurgy**; Porphyry's
  "Principles" dimly glimpse the Trinity but his pride rejects the incarnate Mediator. Christ is the
  "universal way" of the soul's deliverance that Porphyry confessed he had not found.

### Books XI–XVIII — argument by book

- **Book XI** — The **origin of the two cities in the fall of the angels**. Augustine reads the six
  days of Genesis: time was created *with* the world, not in a pre-existing time ("the world was made,
  not in time, but simultaneously with time"); God's eternity is changeless. The angels are the
  "light" of Day One; the separation of light from darkness figures the division of the holy and the
  apostate angels. Evil is no nature but the **privation of good** ([[evil-as-privation]]) — "evil has
  no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name evil." The fallen angels were created
  good and fell by their own will (pride), against the [[manichaeism|Manichees]] (who made the devil
  evil by nature) and [[origen|Origen]] (who made bodies prison-houses for pre-existent souls).
  Trinitarian traces (*vestigia*) in creation and in the self (*esse, nosse, amare*).
- **Book XII** — Why some angels are good and others evil: not a difference of nature but of will. The
  evil will has **no efficient cause, only a "deficient" one** — "a defection from that which supremely
  is." Against the pagan doctrine of **eternal cycles / recurrence** (refuted philosophically,
  scripturally, and morally — cyclic return makes true blessedness impossible; "Christ died once");
  [[porphyry|Porphyry]] himself recoiled from it. Man was created as **one individual** (unlike the
  angels and animals made in numbers) to commend the **unity of the human race**; the world is less
  than 6,000 years old, against inflated Egyptian chronologies ([[apuleius|Apuleius]], the
  Alexander–Olympias letter). God alone is creator; angels create nothing.
- **Book XIII** — **Death is penal**, originating in Adam's sin, not natural to man. The taxonomy of
  death: the death of the soul (God forsaking it), of the body (the soul forsaking it), and the
  **second death** (eternal). The baptized still die so that faith may be exercised; the martyr's
  inversion — "Die, that thou sin not." The animal body of the first Adam (a "living soul") versus the
  spiritual body of the last Adam, Christ (a "quickening spirit"), expounding 1 Cor. 15.
- **Book XIV** — **The fall and the founding of the two cities.** Paul's "flesh" means not the body but
  **the whole man living by himself** — so the vices "of the flesh" (idolatry, envy, heresy) are of the
  soul; "the sinful soul made the flesh corruptible," reversing Platonic anthropology. The root of the
  fall is **pride** — "the evil act had never been done had not an evil will preceded it." Disordered
  **concupiscence** (the disobedient members, shame, the involuntariness of lust) is the punishment of
  the will's disobedience to God. The book closes with the defining formula: **"two cities have been
  formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by
  the love of God, even to the contempt of self."**
- **Book XV** — The two cities in history **from Cain and Abel to the flood**. **Cain** (the first-born,
  who built a city) founds the *civitas terrena*; **Abel** (the sojourner who built none) is the first
  citizen of the *civitas Dei* and a type of the slain Christ. The natural precedes the spiritual (1
  Cor. 15:46). Two genealogical lines — **Cain's line and Seth's line** ("sons of God" and "daughters
  of men") — carry the two cities to the deluge. Defense of antediluvian **longevity** and of biblical
  chronology, with a careful treatment of the **Hebrew–Septuagint numerical divergence** (a systematic
  copyist's design, not Jewish malice or translators' fraud). The "sons of God" are men of Seth's line,
  **not angels**; the *Book of Enoch* is non-canonical. **Noah's ark** is a figure of the Church and of
  Christ's body (its proportions those of the human body; the door in its side the wound of the
  Crucified); literal history and allegory are *both* required.
- **Book XVI** — The heavenly city **from Noah to the kings**, focused on the patriarchs. Noah's
  blessing/curse allegorized (Shem→Christ/Jews, Japheth→Gentile churches, Ham→heretics). **Nimrod**
  founds **Babylon** ("Confusion") and the tower of Babel — the earthly city's paradigm; Hebrew, the
  primal language, is preserved in **Heber's** line (whence "Hebrews") as immunity from the punishment
  of confused tongues. **Abraham**: the **twofold promise** (the land to the carnal seed of Israel; and
  "in thy seed shall all nations be blessed" to the spiritual seed of faith); the covenant and
  circumcision; **Sarah and Hagar** as the two covenants / two cities (Gal. 4); the **binding of
  Isaac** as type of Christ (Isaac carrying the wood = the cross; the ram in the thicket = the crown of
  thorns). Jacob and Esau ("the elder shall serve the younger" = the Jews serving the Christians);
  Jacob's ladder and wrestling (Israel = "seeing God"); Judah's blessing (Gen. 49) read as full
  prophecy of Christ.
- **Book XVII** — The city of God under **the kings and prophets, from Samuel to Christ.** Prophecy has
  a **threefold sense** — pertaining to the earthly Jerusalem, to the heavenly, or to both — against
  both the purely literal and the purely allegorical reading. Hannah's song and David's psalms are read
  as the voice of the **Church**. The **Davidic covenant** (2 Sam. 7) — the eternal "house" and throne
  — is fulfilled **not in Solomon** (whose house turned to idols) **but in Christ** and the Church, the
  temple "made of men, not of stones." The Psalms expounded Christologically (Pss. 16, 22, 45, 69, 110
  …); Solomon's canonical books distinguished from the Western-but-disputed Wisdom and Sirach.
- **Book XVIII** — The **parallel courses of the two cities** from Abraham to the present. The earthly
  city culminates in two great empires: **Assyria/Babylon, "the first Babylon of the East," and Rome,
  "a second Babylon in the west."** Sacred history is synchronized with pagan (the Argive and Sicyonian
  kings, the euhemerist origins of Isis, Serapis, and the Greek gods, Varro as source). The **Erythraean
  Sibyl** and her *ICHTHYS* acrostic are claimed as a Gentile witness to Christ. The **writing prophets**
  arose providentially as Assyria fell and Rome rose, each read for its testimony to Christ and the
  calling of the nations. A major defense of the **inspired authority of the Septuagint** (the seventy
  translators moved by the same Spirit as the prophets, so their divergences from the Hebrew are
  themselves prophecy, not error). Jacob's oracle (Gen. 49:10) is fulfilled when **Herod**, a foreigner,
  takes the throne — the sign that Christ should come. The **Jewish dispersion** is providential
  testimony; **Job** shows that the heavenly city has citizens outside Israel. The book ends with the
  Church's growth amid persecution and refutes the pagan oracle that Christianity would last only 365
  years.

### Books XIX–XXII — argument by book

- **Book XIX** — The **ends of the two cities**, beginning with the **[[supreme-good|supreme good]]**.
  Augustine reviews [[varro|Varro]]'s 288 philosophical sects on happiness and shows that no philosophy
  can locate the supreme good *in this life* (the miseries of body and society; the very virtues are a
  standing war with vice; the Stoics permit suicide). The supreme good is **eternal life and
  [[peace|peace]]**; the supreme evil the **second death**. The famous definition of **peace as "the
  tranquillity of order"** and its hierarchy from body to the heavenly city; even robbers and savages
  desire peace; war is waged *for* peace. The redefinition of **a people** as "an assemblage… bound
  together by a common agreement as to the objects of their love" (against Cicero — Rome was never a
  true republic, lacking true justice). The pilgrim city *uses* the earthly peace of "Babylon."
- **Book XX** — The **[[last-judgment|last judgment]]**, proved from both Testaments. God's present
  *hidden* judgments will be made manifest. The **two resurrections** (John 5): the first, spiritual and
  present; the second, bodily and final. The **thousand years** of Rev. 20 read as the present
  [[millennialism|Church age]] (Augustine's amillennialism, against the carnal **chiliasts**), with
  Satan bound then loosed, and **Gog and Magog** as the nations of the final assault. New Testament
  witnesses (Matt. 25; 1–2 Thess. on **Antichrist**, the "man of sin" — Augustine "frankly confess[es]"
  he cannot identify the restrainer) and Old (Isaiah, Daniel, the Psalms, Malachi's refiner's fire and
  promised **[[elijah|Elijah]]**). The tentative order of the last things: Elijah, the conversion of the
  Jews, Antichrist, Christ's manifest judgment, resurrection, the conflagration, the new heaven and
  earth.
- **Book XXI** — The **end of the earthly city: [[eternal-punishment|eternal punishment]]**. The central
  problem — how a body can burn forever unconsumed and feel pain without dying — answered from the soul's
  own immortality-amid-suffering and from a celebrated catalogue of **natural marvels** (the salamander,
  Etna, the peacock's incorruptible flesh, quicklime, the magnet, the diamond). The material fire torments
  even spirits; Matt. 25:46 makes both destinies equally "eternal." A sustained refutation of the
  **"merciful" (*misericordes*)** who soften hell, and of **[[origen|Origen]]'s**
  [[universal-salvation-controversy|universalism]]; Augustine grants only a limited pre-judgment
  **[[purgatory|purgatorial]]** cleansing. Faith without works does not save; being "saved by fire" means
  the fire of *tribulation*, not escape from hell.
- **Book XXII** — The **end of the heavenly city: eternal felicity and the [[resurrection|resurrection of
  the body]]**. The blessed (men joining the unfallen angels) fill up the number of the fallen angels.
  Defense of bodily resurrection against the **[[plato|Platonists]]** and **[[porphyry|Porphyry]]**
  (whose two errors, combined, yield the Christian teaching); the world's belief in the resurrection is
  itself a miracle. Objections resolved (infants, stature at ~30, hair and nails, women, the martyrs'
  scars, bodies eaten by beasts). A major argument that **miracles have not ceased**, with a personally
  attested catalogue at the **relics of [[stephen-protomartyr|Stephen]]** ([[relic-veneration|relic
  veneration]]). The **spiritual body**, the confirmed freedom *non posse peccare*, the
  **[[beatific-vision|beatific vision]]**, and the **eternal Sabbath** — closing on "**There we shall
  rest and see, see and love, love and praise. This is what shall be in the end without end.**"

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- The relation of the **earthly city** to the historical Roman empire (is Rome simply Babylon, or an
  ambiguous instrument of providence?).
- Augustine's treatment of **Lucretia** and the ethics of suicide (Book I).
- His qualified verdict on the **Platonists** — closest to Christian truth, yet damnably conceding
  worship to demons (Books VIII–X).
- **Evil as privation** and the "deficient cause" of the evil will — whether this adequately accounts
  for the origin of evil without making God its author (Books XI–XII).
- The **inspired authority of the Septuagint** alongside (and where divergent, over) the Hebrew, against
  Jerome's preference for the *hebraica veritas* (Book XVIII).
- Whether the **earthly city** is simply identical with Babylon and Rome, or whether the empires are
  ambiguous instruments within the parallel of the "two Babylons" (Book XVIII).
- The **amillennial reading of Revelation 20** — the thousand years as the present Church age rather than
  a future earthly reign — against the chiliasts (Book XX), the foundation of later Western eschatology.
- The **eternity of hell** and the reductio against [[origen|Origen]]'s universalism: whether Matt. 25:46
  truly requires that punishment be as endless as life ([[universal-salvation-controversy|controversy]],
  Book XXI).
- The germ of **[[purgatory|purgatory]]** in Book XXI and the interpretation of 1 Cor. 3:15 ("saved… as
  by fire") — read by Augustine as the fire of tribulation, later a Catholic–Protestant dividing line.
- Whether the saints will see God **with the bodily eyes** of the resurrection body or by the spirit
  alone — left open in the treatment of the [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]] (Book XXII).
- The status of **post-apostolic miracles**: Augustine's argument (Book XXII) that miracles continue
  through relics and sacraments, against the later cessationist view.

## Manuscript and Text

Ingested in the **NPNF1 vol. 2** edition (ed. Philip Schaff; English translation by Marcus Dods et al.,
Edinburgh/Glasgow 1871; public domain via CCEL). Augustine used the Old Latin version of Scripture, not
Jerome's Vulgate, and relied on Latin (not Greek) authorities throughout.

## Canon and Reception

*City of God* is **not canonical scripture** in any tradition; it is **authoritative theology** esteemed
across [[christianity|Christianity]] — Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant alike claim Augustine. Twenty
printed editions appeared between 1467 and 1500.
