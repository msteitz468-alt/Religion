# Tablets of the Divine Plan

*Populated from [[peter-smith|Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Overview

The Tablets of the Divine Plan are a series of fourteen letters by [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]] on the importance of [[teaching-bahai|teaching]] the Bahá'í Faith, addressed to the Bahá'ís of the United States and Canada during World War I (lines 30145–30149). The first eight letters were composed in 1916 (26 March–22 April), five of which were received and published before communications between Palestine and America were severed due to the war. A further six letters were composed in 1917 (2 February–8 March; lines 30150–30158). Letters were addressed to North American Bahá'ís as a whole as well as by region (Canada, North-Eastern, Southern, Central, and Western United States; lines 30159–30162). The complete set was ceremonially unveiled to the Bahá'ís at a special convention in New York in April 1919 (lines 30163–30166).

## Content and Instructions

'Abdu'l-Bahá's instructions were that Bahá'ís should:
- Systematically teach the Faith in American states and Canadian provinces where there were few or no Bahá'ís (lines 30167–30170)
- Establish the Faith throughout Latin America (line 30171)
- Propagate it throughout the world "as far as the islands of the Pacific" (lines 30172–30174)

Long lists of territories and islands to be covered were provided. The American continent was described as the place where God's light would be revealed; if the North American Bahá'ís responded, waves of spiritual power would emanate and they would become "established upon the throne of an everlasting dominion" (lines 30176–30182). Particular significance was attached to teaching Eskimos and indigenous Americans, and establishing the Faith in Alaska, Greenland, Mexico, Panama, and the Brazilian city of Bahia (Salvador; lines 30183–30186).

Teachers should become "Apostles of Bahá'u'lláh" — three conditions required: firmness in the Covenant; fellowship and love; and that teachers be dedicated, severed from the world, continually travelling, sanctified, and "like a shining torch in God's love" (lines 30188–30195). They should learn local languages; Bahá'í literature needed translation into the world's various languages (lines 30196–30198). The war had made peoples yearn for peace and become receptive to the Bahá'í message (lines 30199–30202). Specific prayers were given for teachers to use (line 30203).

## Historical Response

At first only a few Bahá'ís responded, including Martha [[root|Root]] (Latin America), John and Clara Hyde-Dunn (Australia), and Leonora [[armstrong|Armstrong]] (Brazil; lines 30207–30214). Only from 1937 onwards did [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] direct Bahá'ís to undertake a series of systematic teaching Plans intended to fulfil the objectives of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's letters (lines 30215–30219). Shoghi Effendi referred to the fulfilment of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's Divine Plan as passing through stages in the unfolding of time (lines 30219–30222).

## Significance

These tablets are the charter for Bahá'í global missionary expansion. All subsequent Bahá'í teaching plans (Seven Year Plan, Ten Year Crusade, etc.) are understood as stages in the implementation of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's Divine Plan.
