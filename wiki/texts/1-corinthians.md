# The First Letter to the Corinthians

One of Paul's longest and most wide-ranging letters, 1 Corinthians addresses a largely Gentile urban congregation struggling with the infiltration of pagan Corinthian social values into the life of the church. Its scope covers wisdom and factionalism, sexual ethics, marriage, food offered to idols, the Lord's Supper, spiritual gifts, and the bodily resurrection — making it a primary source for Pauline ethics, ecclesiology, and eschatology.

## Authorship, Date, and Occasion

Pauline authorship is not disputed. Paul founded the Corinthian church during his second missionary journey (Acts 18:1–18), staying approximately eighteen months. The congregation was drawn predominantly from the Gentile population of Roman Corinth, a prosperous, cosmopolitan city refounded as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar in 44 BC and noted for its commerce, social stratification, and status-consciousness.

The letter was written from Ephesus (1 Cor 16:8), most likely in the spring of AD 54 or 55, prompted by two sources: oral reports from "Chloe's people" (1:11) describing factions, and a written inquiry from the Corinthian church itself (7:1: "Now concerning the matters about which you wrote"). An earlier letter has been lost (5:9); 1 Corinthians is thus at least Paul's second letter to Corinth.

## Occasion and Purpose: Ciampa-Rosner's Thesis

[[roy-ciampa-brian-rosner|Ciampa and Rosner]] (PNTC, 2010) argue that the root cause of Corinthian problems is not overrealized eschatology (a popular older view) nor proto-Gnosticism, but the infiltration of specifically Corinthian social values into the church. Paul diagnoses the Corinthians as "worldly, acting like mere human beings" (3:3) — the Greek *sarkikoi*, "fleshly/worldly," a descriptor that encompasses their factionalism, litigation, sexual license, idol feasting, and disorder in worship alike.

The letter is not an ad hoc response to a random list of problems. Ciampa and Rosner demonstrate that Paul's response follows a coherent pattern drawn from his own biblical/Jewish framework: the proclamation of the death and resurrection of Christ inaugurates the eschatological age and calls former Gentiles to abandon the characteristic Gentile vices of sexual immorality and idolatry and to worship the one true God. The ultimate goal is the glory of God — God being "all in all" (15:28) through a worldwide community of worshipers drawn from all nations.

## Structure

The letter's structure reflects a Jewish moral framework applied to Gentile converts, organized around the two quintessential Gentile vices condemned in early Jewish literature: sexual immorality and idolatry. Each vice-topic receives a negative treatment (what to flee) followed by a positive treatment (what to pursue), with summary imperatives closing each unit.

**I. Letter Opening (1:1–9)**

**II. True and False Wisdom / Corinthian Factionalism (1:10–4:17)**
- Negative: "No more boasting about human leaders!" (3:21) — The wisdom of the cross vs. human wisdom; factions as worldliness
- Positive: "Let those who boast, boast in the Lord" (1:31) — Christ-centered wisdom; ministers as servants

**III. "Flee Sexual Immorality" and "Glorify God with Your Bodies" (4:18–7:40)**
- Negative: Flee sexual immorality (and greed) — incest (ch. 5), lawsuits (ch. 6:1–11), prostitution (6:12–20)
- Positive: Glorify God in marriage and sexual relationships (ch. 7)

**IV. "Flee Idolatry" and "Glorify God" in Your Worship (8:1–14:40)**
- Negative: Flee idolatry — food offered to idols, Israel's wilderness warning (chs. 8–10)
- Positive: Glorify God in proper worship — head coverings, the Lord's Supper, spiritual gifts, love (chs. 11–14)

**V. The Resurrection and Consummation (15:1–58)**
The letter's theological climax: bodily resurrection as the foundation of ethics and the goal of all history.

**VI. Letter Closing (16:1–24)**

## Major Themes

**1. The Cross as Wisdom and Power**
The proclamation of the crucified Christ reverses the world's value system: what appears foolish and weak (1:18–25) is in fact "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1:24). This reversal is the key to interpreting the Corinthians' status-seeking and factionalism.

**2. The Church as God's Eschatological Temple**
The church — both collectively (3:16: "you are God's temple") and individually (6:19: "your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit") — is the dwelling place of God's Spirit. This identity grounds nearly all of Paul's ethical instruction in the letter. See [[eschatological-temple|Eschatological Temple]].

**3. Sexual Purity as Holiness of the Temple**
The Jewish framework treats sexual immorality and idolatry as the two defining Gentile vices. Chapters 5–7 work through sexual immorality in all its manifestations (incest, prostitution, marriage) as a threat to the holiness of God's temple.

**4. Idolatry and Proper Worship**
Chapters 8–14 deal with idolatry: first the actual practice of eating at idol temples (8:1–11:1), then the proper ordering of the church's worship (11:2–14:40). The common thread is that worship must reflect the Lordship of Christ and the purity of God's people.

**5. The Lordship of Christ**
"Christ" (64×), "Lord" (66×), "Jesus" (26×) pervade the letter. In almost every case Paul addresses a Corinthian problem by pitting Christ against the prevailing cultural value: Christ's sacrifice grounds church discipline (5:7); union with Christ forbids prostitution (6:15); Christ is the reason the weak are protected (8:11); Christ's body and blood are what the Lord's Supper declares (11:29); resurrection in Christ is the basis of ethics (15:3–23).

**6. Glory of God as Ultimate Goal**
"Do everything to the glory of God" (10:31) is Paul's summary ethical principle. The letter climaxes in the vision of God as "all in all" (15:28) — the ultimate goal of creation, redemption, and the worldwide gathering of Gentile worshipers.

**7. The Resurrection and Eschatology**
Chapter 15 defends the bodily resurrection of the dead against apparent Corinthian skeptics. Paul argues: (a) Christ has already been raised (15:3–11); (b) without resurrection, the gospel collapses (15:12–19); (c) Christ's resurrection inaugurates the resurrection of all who belong to him (15:20–28); (d) the resurrection body is transformed, not abandoned (15:35–57). The resurrection is both the foundation of ethics (15:58) and the consummation of the divine glory.

**8. Love as the Criterion of Spiritual Gifts**
Chapter 13 interrupts the treatment of spiritual gifts (chs. 12 and 14) with the "hymn to love" — love (*agapē*) as the more excellent way. Tongues, prophecy, and knowledge without love are empty. Love does not seek its own (13:5), which directly addresses the Corinthian tendency toward self-assertion and peer-group status.

## OT Background (Ciampa-Rosner)

Two OT books form the primary template:

**Deuteronomy**: Paul's letter stands in an analogous canonical position — spelling out the ethical consequences of the new exodus deliverance (Christ's death and resurrection as the new Passover). Like Moses in Deuteronomy, Paul instructs newly redeemed people to shun idolatry and sexual immorality and maintain the holiness of the community. Israel's wilderness failures in 1 Cor 10:1–13 directly quote Deuteronomy and Numbers.

**Isaiah**: Paul's self-understanding as an eschatological herald (Isaiah's suffering servant imagery) and the letter's opening critique of human wisdom (Isaiah's two-wisdom contrast) both draw heavily on Isaiah. Paul's gospel is the convergence of Isaiah's Suffering Servant and Davidic Messiah in one person (1:22–24). Isaiah 66:18–24 provides the eschatological map for Paul's Gentile mission.

**Malachi 1:11** echoes in 1:2 ("all those everywhere who call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ") — the fulfillment of Malachi's prophecy of worldwide Gentile worship.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. The "Strong" and the "Weak" (chs. 8–10)**: May Christians eat food sacrificed to idols? Under what circumstances? The tension between "knowledge" (idol is nothing; 8:4) and love (don't wound the weak conscience; 8:10–13) and the direct prohibition of idol feasts (10:14–22) has generated debate about the unity of Paul's argument across these chapters. See [[food-offered-to-idols|Food Offered to Idols]].

**2. "Baptism for the Dead" (15:29)**: Over forty interpretations have been proposed for this cryptic reference. Paul's argument does not depend on the specific meaning; he appeals to it as evidence that those who "baptize for the dead" implicitly believe in resurrection.

**3. Women's Silence in the Churches (14:34–35)**: "The women should keep silent in the churches." One of the most contested passages in the NT. The instruction may concern specific interruptions of prophecy or teaching, may be a reference to the practice of all the churches, or (in some text-critical theories) may be an interpolation. Ciampa & Rosner's specific position requires reading their commentary exegesis. See [[women-and-worship|Women and Worship (1 Corinthians 14)]].

**4. "Every Man Should Have His Own Wife" (7:2)**: Is marriage Paul's concession to weakness, or a positively affirmed good? The chapter begins with what may be a Corinthian slogan ("It is good for a man not to have sexual relations with a woman," 7:1) and Paul's nuanced engagement with celibacy, marriage, separation, and remarriage.

**5. "The Spiritual Body" (15:44)**: Is the resurrection body physical? *Sōma pneumatikon* ("spiritual body") has been read as a non-material spiritual entity (Gnostic/idealist reading) or as a physical body animated and transformed by the Spirit (Ciampa-Rosner; most evangelical commentators). The latter is required by Paul's seed/plant analogy (15:36–44) and by "this perishable body must put on imperishability" (15:53).

**6. Head Coverings (11:2–16)**: The meaning of *kephalē* ("head") as "authority over" or "source" affects whether Paul is grounding his instruction in divine hierarchy or creation order. Whether the text refers to wearing a veil, a hairstyle, or something else is also contested. See [[women-and-worship|Women and Worship]].

**7. "All Things Are Lawful" (6:12; 10:23)**: Likely a Corinthian slogan that Paul cites and qualifies. The strategy of mirror-reading to reconstruct Corinthian positions is particularly active in this letter.

**8. Unity of the Argument in Chapters 8–10**: Are chapters 8–10 a literary unity or a composite of sources addressing food offered to idols from different angles? Ciampa-Rosner argue for unity: the three chapters form a sustained negative treatment of idolatry.
