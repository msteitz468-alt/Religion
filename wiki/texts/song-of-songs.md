The Song of Songs is the most interpretation-driven book in the Hebrew Bible. Its problem is not obscurity — the book is explicit and sensuous love poetry — but the opposite: what is plainly obvious about it has caused interpreters for two millennia to insist it must mean something else. As Rabbi Saadiah observed, "the Song of Songs resembles locks to which the keys have been lost." Longman's commentary answers: *the key is genre*. Proper genre identification unlocks the proper interpretation of the book, and the proper genre is an **anthology of love poems**.

The Song belongs to the Writings (*Kětûvîm*) as one of the five Megillot (festival scrolls), read on the eighth day of Passover in the Jewish liturgy — an association that arose from the historical allegorical interpretation (see below). In the Hebrew ordering of the Ketuvim, it follows Ruth and precedes Ecclesiastes, creating an intentional sequence: Proverbs 31 (the valiant woman) → Ruth (a virtuous, assertive woman) → Song of Songs (love between man and woman). In English Bibles it follows Ecclesiastes.

Longman (NICOT) treats the book as an anthology/cycle of love poems rather than a single drama or allegory. The structure is loose but artful, built on recurring motifs (searching and finding, praise of the body, invitations to the garden, the "do not awaken love" refrains). The garden is the central erotic paradise image. While the primary sense is a celebration of human sexual love as wisdom gift, the canonical context among the Wisdom books and the Solomonic attribution open typological readings (human love as image of YHWH's covenant love with Israel / Christ with the Church). The two-voice frame in the broader Wisdom corpus (cf. Ecclesiastes) keeps the celebration grounded in "the fear of the LORD."

## Title and Structure

**"Song of Songs"** (*šîr haššîrîm*): a Hebrew superlative construction ("the best of all songs") — the same pattern as "Holy of Holies," "servant of servants," "heaven of heavens." The book is both a single Song (one consistent persona, recurring refrains) and many Songs (an anthology with no strict narrative unity).

Two other titles:
- **Song of Solomon**: highlights the Solomonic connection (1:1 superscription); misleading insofar as it implies Solomonic authorship of the whole
- **Canticles**: from the Latin Vulgate (*Canticum canticorum*)

**23 poems** identified by Longman, ranging from a few verses to several chapters. The poems are loosely unified by:
- Recurring refrains: "I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles or the deer of the field, not to awaken or arouse love until it desires" (2:7; 3:5; 8:4)
- Consistency of characters (the man, the woman, the daughters of Jerusalem)
- Repetition of key images and metaphors (garden, vineyard, garden, fragrances)
- Echoes between poems (3:1-5 / 5:2-8; the *wasf* poems in 4:1-7, 5:10-16, 7:1-9)

But no plot, no stage directions, no narrative voice. About 10% of the verses are uncertain as to speaker — a feature impossible to disguise if the text had a dramatic intention.

## Genre: Anthology of Love Poetry

The long history of misreading the Song (see below) makes genre identification the commentary's central concern.

### What the Song Is Not

**Not an allegory**: No text-internal signal of a different meaning below the surface. The assignment of symbolic value in allegorical readings is completely arbitrary — "If two allegorizers ever agree on the interpretation of a verse it is only because one has copied another" (Keel). The two breasts in 4:5 have been variously identified as: the church from which we feed; the two testaments; the twin precepts of love of God and neighbor; the blood and the water; the outer and inner man; the Old and New Torah. The text gives no warrant for any of these.

**Not a drama**: No stage directions, no narrative voice, no plot that can be identified without importing material from outside the text. The two-character reading (Delitzsch: Solomon and the Shulammite) and three-character reading (Provan: king / woman / shepherd in a love triangle) both require identifying the man as Solomon throughout — but "king" and "shepherd" are epithets of respect and endearment, not royal titles. The king is mocked in 8:11-12 as trying to buy love.

**Not cultic poetry**: No evidence of a *hieros gamos* (sacred marriage) ritual in ancient Israel. The *marzeah* festival is mentioned in the OT only negatively (Amos 6:7; Jer 16:5) and is never mentioned in the Song.

### What the Song Is

An anthology of **lyric love poetry** that expresses the thoughts and feelings of its characters with the intention of evoking those thoughts and feelings in the reader. Its closest literary parallels are Egyptian Ramesside-period love poems (Chester Beatty Papyrus I, Papyrus Harris 500, Turin Papyrus, Cairo Love Songs) — also a collection of poems, non-cultic, intended for entertainment, with similar epithets ("sister"), imagery (garden, floral, water), and the *wasf* descriptive form.

Genre note: within the anthology, recognizable subforms include the *wasf* (descriptive poem of the lover's body: 4:1-7; 5:10-16; 7:1-9), the invitation (2:10-14; 4:8-9), the song of yearning (3:1-5; 8:1-4), the admiration song (1:15-17), and the dialogue (4:1-5:1; 8:13-14).

## Characters

**The Woman**: The dominant presence — speaks 61.5 of 117 verses. Young, coming to maturity. She initiates pursuit, overcomes obstacles (3:1-5; 5:2-8). Her self-view is modest (1:5-6; 2:1); through the man's eyes she is magnificent. Three times she warns the daughters of Jerusalem: "do not awaken love before its time."

**The Man**: Pursued by and pursues the woman. "King" and "shepherd" are metaphors of respect and desire, not titles. In 8:11-12 Solomon — if he is the referent — is a negative foil: one who tries to buy love but is ridiculed.

**The Daughters of Jerusalem**: Young city women, inexperienced in love. Serve as sounding board (5:9 → woman's description of the man, 5:10-16), contrast (they are city girls; she is associated with countryside and vineyard), and students. The woman instructs them in love through the recurring "do not arouse" refrains.

**The Brothers**: Appear at beginning (1:5-6) and end (8:8-9); represent the social control of female sexuality. The woman resists this control as she pursues her love — a thematic thread connecting the book's opening and closing.

**Absence of the Father**: Conspicuous; the mother is mentioned (3:4, 11; 6:9; 8:1), but not the father. Possibly because the woman's resistance to social norms (which the father would enforce) is the book's thematic undercurrent.

## History of Interpretation

The Song has the most dramatic and well-documented shift in interpretation history of any biblical book. For approximately 1,800 years, allegory dominated without serious challenge. Since the 19th century, the natural/literal reading has become scholarly consensus. Understanding the history illuminates the hermeneutical principles at stake.

### The Allegorical Tradition

**Why allegory arose**: Platonic/Neoplatonic philosophy introduced the conviction that the body and soul are separate substances and that the body is inferior or evil. In this framework, the Song's explicit sexuality could not be what it seemed. Allegorizing imported from Greek philosophical tradition (originally used to salvage the immoral mythological stories of Homer and Hesiod) provided a method of "repression": making the text say something different from what it plainly says.

**Jewish allegorization**:
- *Historical allegory* (the Targum, ca. 700-900 CE): Man = God; Woman = Israel. The Song narrates Israel's redemptive history from the Exodus to the Messianic era. Specifically: the kiss = Torah at Sinai; the woman's blackness = the golden calf sin; the bedroom = the promised land.
- *Philosophical/mystical allegory* (Gersonides, following Maimonides): Man = Active Intellect (God); Woman = material intellect (humanity). Their union = the highest human perfection. The daughters of Jerusalem = faculties of the soul.
- *Abravanel*: Man = Solomon; Woman = Wisdom. Their union = Solomon's divinely given wisdom.

**Christian allegorization**:
- *Hippolytus* (ca. 200 CE): First Christian interpreter; two breasts in 4:5 = Old and New Testaments
- *Origen* (185-253 CE): Most influential; 10 volumes on the Song. Man = Christ; Woman = Church or individual soul. The body's concerns are deliberately suppressed: "these things seem to afford no profit to the reader... It is necessary to give them all a spiritual meaning." Origem underwent castration; "what Origen did to his own body, he did via allegorical interpretation to the Song." Aware of Jewish allegorization through his rabbinic contacts; engaged in implicit debate with Rabbi Yohanan over the meaning of the breasts/wine (1:2).
- *Jerome* (331-420): Introduced and popularized Origen's approach in the Western Church. Recommended that a young woman read every book before the Song — ensuring she would never reach it.
- *Bernard of Clairvaux* (1090-1153): 86 sermons on the Song (through 3:1 only); the Song as the pinnacle of biblical teaching, reserved for spiritually mature monks; the literal meaning is "the outer husk of the dead letter."
- *Luther*: Rejected Origen's allegory, replaced it with a political one — the Song describes Solomon's relationship to God and his rule over God's people.
- *Westminster Assembly*: Condemned those who understood the Song as "a hot carnal pamphlet formed by some loose Apollo or Cupid."

### Why Allegory Failed

1. **No textual basis**: Without traditional authority, allegorical readings have no justification — pure ad hoc assignment of symbolic value
2. **Enlightenment epistemology**: Required textual argument rather than traditional fiat
3. **ANE discoveries**: Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Ugaritic love poetry (19th-20th centuries) showed the Song belongs to a recognized literary tradition of human love poetry
4. **Modern Arab customs**: Wetzstein (German consul to Syria, mid-19th century) witnessed wedding ceremonies including the *wasf* — a song describing the physical beauty of bride and groom — recognizably parallel to the Song
5. **New theology of the body**: Rejection of Platonic dualism; affirmation that the body is part of the image of God (Gen 1:27)

### Precursors to the Literal Reading

- Theodore of Mopsuestia (350-428): Read the Song as Solomon's love for the Egyptian princess; questioned its canonicity on that basis; condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople (553)
- Calvin: Believed the Song concerned physical love within marriage; defended its canonicity despite the explicit content; had his former student Castellio (who agreed on literal meaning but rejected canonicity) exiled from Geneva

## The Theology: Three Dimensions

The Song's theological contribution operates at three levels simultaneously. These are not allegorical layers; the human love meaning is primary and must stand. The other levels arise from reading the Song within its canonical context.

### 1. Human Love and Sexuality

The Song celebrates physical love, sensuality, and sexuality within the context of marriage. It addresses the Church's tendency to make sexuality taboo and society's tendency to make sex an idol.

Key features:
- Love is **mutual**: neither the man nor the woman dominates; pursuit flows both directions
- Love is **exclusive**: to these lovers there is only each other — a model violated by Solomon's actual marital history, which is why Solomonic authorship is unlikely
- Love is **potent and dangerous**: "Love is as strong as death, its jealousy unyielding as the grave. It burns like blazing fire, like a mighty flame. Many waters cannot quench love; rivers cannot sweep it away" (8:6-7) — a warning about love's power, not just its beauty
- The recurring refrain "do not arouse love before its time" (2:7; 3:5; 8:4) warns against premature entanglement with this potent force

The Song is poetry, not a sex manual. It evokes a mood and an emotional texture rather than mandating specific behaviors.

### 2. Sexuality Redeemed (Creation-Fall-Redemption Arc)

Longman's most distinctive canonical contribution, shaped by Phyllis Trible:

**Genesis 2 — Creation**: Adam and Eve naked, no shame. Complete vulnerability and intimacy. No power struggle. Adam's poetic response to Eve's creation emphasizes equality and delight: "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh." Not created from his head (to dominate) or feet (to be trampled) but from his side — to be equal.

**Genesis 3 — Fall**: The first sign of the Fall is sexual self-consciousness: "they realized they were naked, so they sewed fig leaves." Vulnerability replaced by self-protection. The key word: *tĕšûqâ* ("desire") in the curse on Eve (Gen 3:16): "your desire will be for your husband and he will rule over you." The same word appears in Gen 4:7 for sin crouching at Cain's door desiring to overtake him — a destructive desire to control and consume, not a tender desire. The curse replaces the egalitarianism of creation with gender power struggle.

**Song of Songs — Redemption**: The couple is in the garden again (garden imagery throughout), naked without shame, using the language of Genesis 2. And in Song 7:11 — "I belong to my lover, and *his desire* [*tĕšûqâ*] is for me" — the only third occurrence of that specific word in the entire OT. In a refrain of mutual love and possession, the word is used *positively* and *mutually* — his desire for her, not her being dominated by him. The curse of Gen 3:16 is being reversed.

This redemption is **already-not-yet**: the cautionary poems (2:15; the city as setting for failed intimacy in 3:1-5 and 5:2-8; the "do not arouse" refrains; the brothers' controlling presence) show that the Song doesn't claim full healing this side of the eschaton. Broken intimacy is healed but not consummated. The Song ends not with a climactic final embrace but with yearning: "Come away, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or like a young stag on the spice-laden mountains" (8:14). Always still reaching.

See also Luke 20:27-40: Jesus' answer to the Sadducees implies that marriage in heaven will be transformed but not abolished; the nature of human intimacy in the eschaton remains beyond precise description.

### 3. The Divine-Human Relationship

The allegorical tradition was not wrong to insist on the Song's relevance to the divine-human relationship. It erred by *suppressing* the human dimension and pressing details arbitrarily. Longman's synthesis: the Song is primarily human love poetry, but because Scripture consistently describes God's relationship with his people as a *marriage*, the Song illuminates that metaphor from the inside.

The canonical trajectory of the divine-human marriage metaphor:
- **Hosea 1-3**: God commands Hosea to marry "an adulterous wife" as a living parable of Israel's spiritual adultery; idolatry = adultery
- **Ezekiel 16, 23**: Two long chapters of extended sexual metaphor — Israel and Judah as unfaithful wives; God as their scorned husband; the language is deliberately shocking
- **Jeremiah 2-3**: God remembers Israel's early devotion ("like a bride's love"); now she has committed adultery on every high hill
- **Isaiah 50, 54**: God has not divorced his wife; the exile is a temporary separation; the restoration is a remarriage
- **Malachi 2:14**: Marriage itself is called a *bĕrît* (covenant) — the same term as Israel's covenant with God
- **Ephesians 5:21-33**: Paul explicitly maps Christ-Church onto husband-wife; "this mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the Church"
- **Revelation 19:7-9**: The eschatological climax is a wedding banquet — "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and his bride has made herself ready"

From all this: the more we understand the emotional intensity, exclusivity, intimacy, and mutual pursuit of the Song's lovers, the more we understand what it means for God to call his people his bride. There are only two exclusive relationships in human experience: one spouse / one God. The only two contexts where jealousy can be a positive emotion (Exod 20:5). The Song enriches both relationships simultaneously.

This canonical reading is *not* allegorization: it does not replace the human love meaning with a spiritual one. The human love meaning stands; the canonical extension adds a second dimension without destroying the first.

## Canon and Canonicity

Disputed in antiquity alongside Ecclesiastes and Esther. The dispute centered on whether the Song "defiled the hands" (a phrase for canonical status). Rabbi Aqiba's famous declaration — "all the Writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies" — was uttered in the context of defending its canonicity against those who sang it at banquet halls. The book almost certainly entered the canon before the allegorization was developed; allegorization was likely the *result* of its canonical inclusion, not its cause.

Qumran: Four manuscripts, Herodian period — the Song was already authoritative.

NT: The Song is never quoted in the NT, though the divine-human marriage metaphor it illuminates runs through the NT at multiple points (Eph 5; Rev 19; John 3:29; Matt 22:1-14).

## Scholarship and Historiography

1. **Longman (NICOT 2001):** Genre is the key (Saadia’s “lost keys”); 23 poems; superscription “concerns Solomon”; Egyptian love poetry parallel; functional decanonization warning (Carr).
2. **Allegorical majority to mid-19th c.:** Aqiba, Targum, Origen, Bernard; Keel on arbitrary agreement by copying.
3. **Literal/natural consensus since 19th c.:** ANE parallels + body-affirming theology drive shift.
4. **Drama schools:** Delitzsch two-character; Provan three-character triangle — positions recorded without adjudication.

## Zoharic reception — MhN fragment + Zohar on Song of Songs (Pritzker Vol. XI)

*Source: [[hecker-zohar-pritzker-vol11|Hecker Pritzker Vol XI]] (standards reingest 2026-07-18). Two distinct units — do not conflate with Longman’s erotic-anthology genre claim.*

### Midrash ha-Ne’lam on Song of Songs (short)

Does not run Song as continuous midrash. Opening: four *ruḥot* (Isa 11:2) complete in love’s kiss; large **ש (*shin*)** of Song 1:1 as upper chariot with large **ב** Genesis / **מ** Proverbs / **א** Chronicles as book-openers. Mostly Aramaic theosophy; non-sefirotic Ecclesiastes 12 close. Hecker: likely intro to a larger lost or unrealized work; cast not Rashbi’s Companions.

### Zohar on Song of Songs (mature composition)

Sustained **Rabbi Shim’on–Elijah** exchange on Song **1:1–11**. Hecker: mature Zoharic phase; Rashbi on ascent, Elijah on Other Side rupture/repair; later parts transpose romance onto masculine/feminine **letters**. Body authorship claim: Rashbi’s words written **above** before Ancient of Days; Elijah’s only **below**, by Rashbi’s hand. Dense letter mysticism (*bakh*, upright final letters, 72 names as wine); Pittum ha-Ketoret vs Accuser; Song 1:5 black/beautiful; Moon/*eikhah* Temple frame; cantillation/vowels as Song 1:11 adornments. Cordovero ranks Shir ha-Shirim among compositions of full-fledged commitment (Hecker preface / Cordovero). Practical fallout Hecker notes: **no tefillin on intermediate festival days**.

Do not collapse into Longman’s “anthology of love poems” or into medieval allegory as if identical; Zoharic Sod is its own register.
