# The Divine Comedy (*Commedia*)

The *Commedia* (the epithet "Divine" was added by later admirers, beginning
with Boccaccio) is a narrative poem in Italian vernacular by [[dante-alighieri|Dante Alighieri]]
(1265–1321), composed in exile c. 1307–1321. It recounts a first-person journey
through the three realms of the Christian afterlife — Hell, Purgatory, and
Heaven — undertaken by the character "Dante" over the three days of Easter,
guided first by the Roman poet [[virgil|Virgil]] (through [[inferno|Inferno]]
and [[purgatorio|Purgatorio]]) and then by [[beatrice|Beatrice]] (through
[[paradiso|Paradiso]]) toward a final vision of God.

## Status and Canon

The *Commedia* is **not scripture** and is canonical for no community; it is a
work of literary and theological imagination that *depicts* and dramatizes
Catholic doctrine (sin, the afterlife, providence, the [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]])
rather than constituting it. Its relationship to the tradition is one of
reception and synthesis: it draws constantly on Scripture (especially the
Psalms, the prophets, the Gospels, and Revelation), on [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]
and scholastic theology, and on classical poetry (Virgil, Ovid, Statius, Lucan).
The poem's anti-ecclesiastical and anti-papal passages later furnished
"ammunition to the Protestant movement" (Dante's *De Monarchia* was cited at the
court of Henry VIII), even though the work itself is thoroughly [[catholicism|Catholic]]
in its theology. (Kirkpatrick, Introduction.)

## Structure

Three *cantiche* (Inferno, Purgatorio, Paradiso) of 33 cantos each, plus an
introductory canto to the Inferno, totalling 100 cantos. The verse form is the
*terzina* (terza rima, rhyme scheme ABA BCB CDC…), an interlocking three-line
form Dante devised, in hendecasyllabic (eleven-syllable) lines. Cantos run no
more than ~160 lines — a deliberately short narrative unit, unlike the
book-length units of Virgil's *Aeneid*, forcing constant re-forging of
connection.

The cosmology: when [[satan|Satan]] fell from Heaven he plunged to the dead
centre of a spherical earth (Dante knew it was round), striking at the point
later named [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]; the displaced matter was extruded at the
antipodes to form **Mount Purgatory**, a mountain-island in the southern
hemisphere crowned by the Garden of Eden. Hell is a graded funnel of descending
circles within the earth; Purgatory ascends the mountain; Paradise ascends
through the planetary spheres (Moon to Saturn), then the fixed stars, the
Primum Mobile, and the Empyrean.

## Major Themes

- **Evil as privation.** Following [[evil-as-privation|the privation theology]]
  of [[augustine|Augustine]] and Aquinas, Dante presents evil not as a positive
  force but as the negation of being and activity. Satan, at the climax of the
  Inferno, is a deliberate **anti-climax**: spectacularly ugly but impotent, a
  three-faced parody of the [[trinity|Trinity]], capable only of weeping and
  chewing. Hell is the realm of "banal monotony"; goodness, not sin, is the
  source of variety and invention. (Kirkpatrick contrasts this with the
  post-Blakean cliché that evil is "interesting.")
- **The graded analysis of sin.** Hell's circles embody a rational diagnosis of
  [[sin|sin]] drawing on Aristotle and Cicero as much as Christian sources. The
  worst sin is **treachery** (punished in ice at Hell's centre), not lust or
  greed — sin seen less as offence against God than as the perversion of the
  rational and social bonds that constitute humanity.
- **The psychosomatic human being.** Dante is "no dualist": following Aristotle,
  the soul (*anima*) is the animating form of the body, and human flourishing is
  bodily as well as rational. The body is everywhere in the poem.
- **[[freedom|Freedom of the will]].** The central cantos of the Purgatorio (16–17)
  locate the core of human personality in free will; reason is oriented less to
  valid inference than to the pursuit of the good.
- **Reason and revelation.** Virgil allegorizes unaided reason (an inhabitant of
  [[limbo|Limbo]], "neither happy nor sad"); Beatrice, who supplants him at the
  summit of Purgatory, represents revelation and the joy of the "joyous Maker."
- **Doubt as productive.** Even in Paradise, "doubt springs like a growing shoot
  from the base of truth" (Par. 4:124–32); the poem's recurring interrogative
  "why?" invites collaborative inquiry rather than asserting certitude.

## Sources and Method

Dante's handling of sources runs on **assimilation and experiment**. He claims
Virgil as the teacher of his "fine-tuned style" and enrolls himself among Homer,
Ovid, Horace, and Lucan (Inf. 4), yet freely reshapes them: Statius is made a
secret Christian convert (Purg. 21–22); Ovid's *Metamorphoses* shapes the
transformation scenes (Inf. 25) and even Paradiso 1 (Glaucus). Scriptural
allusion, though usually less explicit, outnumbers the classical. Dante presents
himself as a **syncretist**, reconciling classical, Scriptural, and Christian
text as stages in the progressive historical revelation of divine truth.

## Allegory and Reading

Dante engaged the [[allegorical-reading|allegorical/typological tradition]] (the
*Convivio* reads his own canzoni allegorically; the disputed *Epistle to Can
Grande* applies fourfold reading to the Paradiso). But the *Commedia* resists
being read as straightforward medieval allegory: Dante increasingly privileges
the **literal** meaning (a planet remains a planet inviting the scientific eye).
His stated purpose (Purg. 32:103) is "to aid the world that lives all wrong" —
an ethical address to the reader's conscience, engaging all the faculties rather
than coding doctrine. The author-Dante / character-Dante distinction is
conventional but, Kirkpatrick stresses, the similarity often matters more than
the difference; the poem is "confessional writing" in the lineage of Augustine's
*Confessions*, not "mere fiction."

## Manuscript Tradition

No autograph survives. The Inferno circulated c. 1315, the Purgatorio c. 1320;
parts of the Paradiso may have reached patrons before Dante's death in 1321. The
oldest complete manuscript was produced in Florence c. 1330–31; ~900 manuscripts
existed before printed editions began in 1472. The standard modern Italian text
is Giorgio Petrocchi's *La commedia secondo l'antica vulgata* (Milan, 1966–67),
based on ~30 of the earliest Florentine manuscripts; it is the basis of the
Kirkpatrick translation ingested here.

## Reception

Boccaccio lectured on the poem and wrote a *Treatise in Praise of Dante*;
Botticelli, Michelangelo, Blake, Doré, and Tom Phillips illustrated it. After an
eclipse from the Renaissance to the Enlightenment (partly due to Petrarch's
"anxiety of influence"), the Romantics revived Dante; the nineteenth century was
drawn to the Inferno, the twentieth to the Purgatorio (Eliot, Beckett, Heaney),
with the Paradiso still awaiting full appreciation. Modern readers shaped by the
poem include Eliot, Joyce, Mandelstam, Primo Levi, Borges, and Walcott.

## Translation Ingested

Robin Kirkpatrick's verse translation (Penguin Classics Deluxe Edition) abandons
end-rhyme in favour of internal rhyme, assonance, and alliteration to reproduce
the "aural density" of the original, while preserving Dante's clearly-marked
syntactic logic. Kirkpatrick's introduction and per-canto notes constitute a
running commentary — see [[robin-kirkpatrick|Robin Kirkpatrick]].
