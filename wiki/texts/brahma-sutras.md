The **Brahma Sutras** (Vedanta Sutras) are the third of the **prasthana-trayi** and the **systematic**
foundation of [[hinduism|Vedanta]]: ~555 terse aphorisms (*sutras*) attributed to **Badarayana** that
organize and reconcile the teachings of the [[upanishads|Upanishads]] into a coherent doctrine of
[[brahman|Brahman]].

## Structure: Four Adhyāyas

The work is organized into four chapters (*adhyāyas*), each carrying a distinct task — and each a
battleground where the commentators diverge:

1. **Samanvaya** ("harmony") — that all Upanishadic texts converge on [[brahman|Brahman]] as the one
   cause. Most of the school-defining disputes are fought here.
2. **Avirodha** ("non-conflict") — refuting rival systems (Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Buddhism, Jainism) and
   removing apparent self-contradictions in scripture.
3. **Sādhana** ("means") — the path: meditation, the role of works, the soul's journey after death.
4. **Phala** ("fruit") — [[moksha|liberation]] and the condition of the freed soul.

## Why It Is the Battleground of Vedanta

The sutras are so compressed as to be **unintelligible without a commentary** — and that is exactly why
they became the decisive text. To *found a school of Vedanta is to write a bhashya (commentary) on the
Brahma Sutras.* The three classical commentaries on this one text are the three schools:

- [[shankara|Shankara]]'s *Sharira-bhashya* → [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] (non-dualism)
- [[ramanuja|Ramanuja]]'s *Sri-bhashya* → [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]] (qualified
  non-dualism)
- [[madhva|Madhva]]'s commentary → [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]] (dualism)

The very first sutras set the stage: *athato brahma jijnasa* ("now, therefore, the inquiry into
Brahman") and *janmady asya yatah* ("[Brahman is] that from which the origin etc. of this [world
proceed]"). Each commentator extracts a different metaphysics from the same words — the wiki's prime
exhibit of [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|one canon, irreconcilable readings]].

Tellingly, [[shankara|Śaṅkara]] does not even begin with sutra 1: he prefixes the whole *bhāṣya* with a
freestanding preamble on **[[adhyasa|superimposition]]** (*adhyāsa*), making the human problem one of
mistaken identity that only knowledge can cure — whereas [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]] opens his *Śrī-Bhāṣya* by
**attacking** that very preamble (the *Mahāpūrvapakṣa*). The schools diverge before the first aphorism.

[[madhva|Madhva]] diverges harder still, and word by word (as traced in
[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]], Vol. IV, Ch. XXVI). On *athāto brahma jijñāsā* he
re-keys the very particles theistically — *atha* is a name of Nārāyaṇa, *atas* means "by Viṣṇu's grace,"
*jijñāsā* means reasoned argument (not "desire to know"), and *Brahman* by its root sense of **fullness of
qualities** (*guṇa-pūrti*) cannot denote the *imperfect* soul. On *janmādy asya yataḥ* he expands
Śaṅkara's threefold "origin–maintenance–dissolution" into **eight** divine functions (adding control,
knowledge, ignorance, bondage, and release), all dependent on Viṣṇu. And on the *samanvaya* sutra (I.1.4)
he claims that **every word of the Upaniṣads ultimately denotes Viṣṇu** — so where Śaṅkara reads a
contested term (*ākāśa*, *prāṇa*, *jyotiḥ*…) as "Brahman," Madhva reads "Viṣṇu specifically," often citing
entirely different proof-texts. His charge throughout is that the *māyā*-doctrine is **more dangerous than
Buddhism** — it guts the Vedas from *within*.

## A Modern Critic's Verdict

The translator [[george-thibaut|George Thibaut]], reading as a historical critic rather than a
confessional Vedantin, argued that **Rāmānuja's theistic reading may sit closer to Bādarāyaṇa's original
intention** than Śaṅkara's non-dualism — i.e. that the sutras may have been a theistic text, with
Advaita the later (if more powerful) overlay. A clean example of the critical voice standing apart from
every traditional *bhāṣya*.
