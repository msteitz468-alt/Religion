# Tongshu — Penetrating the Scripture of Change (通書)

The **Tongshu** (通書, "Penetrating the Scripture of Change") is a text of 40 short, largely
independent sections by [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] (1017–1073), focused on the philosophy of
**sagehood** and moral cultivation. Originally titled *Yitong* (易通, "Penetrating the *Yi*"), it
is [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou's]] primary philosophical work and the text that [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] found most
personally significant for his own spiritual-intellectual development.

## Overview

Where the [[taijitu-shuo|Taijitu shuo]] provides a cosmological sequence, the *Tongshu* addresses
the practical-ethical question: what is sagehood, how is it constituted, and how can it be learned?
The central concept is **cheng** (誠, "being authentic" / actualizing principle), defined in the
opening section as "the foundation of the sage." The text also develops the concepts of *ji*
(incipience — the subtle moment when activity begins to emerge from stillness) and *shen* (spirit —
that which interpenetrates the material and the metaphysical).

In [[adler-reconstructing-confucian-dao|Adler's analysis]] (2014), the *Tongshu* was the text
primarily responsible for [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] intense interest in [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] — not
the *Taijitu shuo* alone. Specifically, **section 16 (Activity and Stillness)** provided the
cosmological verification of Zhu Xi's own resolution of the *zhong/he* (centrality/harmony) problem
that had preoccupied him through the 1160s.

## Key Sections (Summary)

### Section 1–3: Being Authentic (*Cheng*)
*Cheng* (誠) = "the foundation of the sage"; defined as *shi li* (actualized/real principle). The
sage completely actualizes this principle; the worthy recovers and holds it.

- "Being authentic is the foundation of the Five Constant Virtues and the source of the Hundred
  Practices."
- "In being authentic there is no [intentional] acting (*wuwei*)" — the sage acts from pure
  actualized nature without effortful striving.
- "In incipience (*ji*) there is good and evil" — at the subtle onset of activity, the distinction
  between the moral order and selfish desire begins.

### Section 4: Sagehood
The three characteristics of the sage: *authenticity* (jiran budong — "completely silent and
inactive"), *spirituality* (gan'er suitong — "penetrating when stimulated"), and *incipience*
(the between-state where activity is just beginning). These three together = sagehood.

### Section 16: Activity and Stillness (**Pivot of Zhu Xi's appropriation**)
> "That which has no stillness in activity and no activity in stillness is a **thing**.
> That which has no activity in activity, and no stillness in stillness, is **spirit**.
> It is not the case that having no activity in activity and having no stillness in stillness is
> neither activity nor stillness. Things, then, are not penetrating. **Spirit renders the myriad
> things subtle.**"

Zhu Xi's commentary: "There is stillness within activity, and activity within stillness." The
*taiji*, as metaphysical principle, is not limited to either mode; physical things are locked into
one mode at a time. This **interpenetration** of activity and stillness at the level of principle
was the cosmological ground Zhu Xi found for his 1169 "ji-chou enlightenment."

### Section 20: Learning to Be a Sage
The most practically significant section:
> "Can sagehood be learned? It can. Are there essentials? There are. To be **unified** (*yi*) is
> essential. To be unified is to have **no desire**. Without desire one is **vacuous** (*xu*) when
> still and **direct** (*zhi*) in activity. Being vacuous when still, one will be **clear** (*ming*);
> being clear, penetrating. Being direct in activity, one will be **impartial** (*gong*); being
> impartial, **all-embracing** (*pu*). Being clear and penetrating, impartial and all-embracing,
> one is almost [a sage]."

Zhu Xi's gloss (additional comment 1): "Unity" = Supreme Polarity; "vacuous when still" = stillness
of yin; "direct in activity" = activity of yang; "clear and penetrating, impartial and all-embracing"
= Five Phases. "Basically, all of Master Zhou's discussions connect the Supreme Polarity and the
various moral principles."

Zhu Xi notes (comment 2) that Zhou's prescription is "especially eloquent but unexpectedly difficult
to pin down" — and that Cheng Yi therefore emphasized the more practical concept of *jing*
(reverent composure) as the operative method.

### Sections 10, 23, 29: Master Yan (Yan Hui)
Three sections single out **Yan Hui** (Confucius's favorite disciple, d. 481 BCE) as the model
of sage-learning: he was "never unaware of what was not good in himself," he was "happy" despite
poverty because he "saw what was great," and through him "the comprehensiveness of the sage" was
made visible.

This motif — "What did Confucius and Master Yan enjoy?" — is the phrase the Cheng brothers credited
to Zhou Dunyi as his central teaching.

### Section 40: Keeping Still (*Gen*) — Emphasis on Stillness
The final section discusses the Yi hexagram *Gen* (艮, "Keeping Still") to illustrate "the
comprehensiveness of the sage and the idea of **emphasizing stillness**" (*zhu jing* 主靜 — the
same term that appears in the *Taijitu shuo* section G and that troubled Confucian readers as
Buddhist-adjacent). Zhu Xi's interpretation: *gen* (stopping at the right point) = true *jing*
(reverent composure) = not Buddhist quietism but resting precisely where principle calls.

## Zhu Xi's Commentary

Zhu Xi wrote a full commentary on the *Tongshu* (entitled *Tongshu zhu*, 通書註, completed 1187,
with preface dated 1187). The commentary:

1. Integrates the *Tongshu* with the *Taijitu shuo* at every major juncture, arguing the two texts
   "complement each other."
2. Translates Zhou's *cheng* framework into his own *li/qi* metaphysics.
3. Extensively cites his **Classified Conversations** (*Yulei*), compiled by disciples — the
   additional comments (assembled by Zhang Boxing) draw on hundreds of passages from Zhu Xi's
   teaching sessions on the *Tongshu*.
4. Connects Zhou's *cheng* (authenticity) to his own concept of *jing* (reverent composure) as the
   practical cultivation method.

**Zhu Xi's preface** (1187): "From my early years I was fortunate to receive his bequeathed writings
and tried to read them. At first I did not understand at all what he was saying. Worse yet, I could
not even punctuate them. In my thirties I had the opportunity to go study with Teacher Yanping [Li
Tong], and then I began to learn about 10 to 20 percent of [Zhou's] theory. In recent years, after
delving into it for a long time, I have gotten it, if only roughly."

## Editorial History and Editions

- Originally titled *Yitong* (Penetrating the Yi). The title change to *Tongshu* had occurred by
  the time Zhu Xi published his Jian'an edition (1169), though exactly when is uncertain.
- Hu Hong (1106–1161) produced an edition of the *Tongshu* that rearranged the section sequence
  and replaced section titles with "Master Zhou said"; Zhu Xi criticized this emendation and restored
  the original titles.
- **Jian'an edition** (1169): Zhu Xi's first published edition of Zhou's collected works.
- **Nankang edition** (1179): Revised edition published when Zhu was Prefect of Nankang.
- **Final commentary** (1187): Zhu Xi's completed annotated edition.
- **Zhang Boxing's edition** (*Zhou Lianxi ji*, 1708): The standard Qing-dynasty edition, incorporating
  Zhu Xi's commentary plus additional passages from the *Yulei*; still the reference text.

## The Tongshu and the Taijitu shuo: Which Comes First?

A persistent editorial question: which text is primary?

- The **manuscript tradition** had the diagram appended *after* the *Tongshu* — as if it were an
  appendix.
- **Zhu Xi** reversed this, arguing the *Taijitu shuo* should come first because it is the
  "essential idea" (the cosmological framework) that the *Tongshu's* words express.
- Zhu Xi's ordering has been standard ever since.

Adler's reading supports the view that the texts genuinely complement each other: the *Taijitu shuo*
provides the cosmological sequence (activity-stillness → yin-yang → Five Phases → ten thousand things
→ human nature); the *Tongshu* unpacks what it means to actualize that nature in sagehood.
