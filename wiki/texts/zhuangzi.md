The **Zhuangzi** is the second foundational classic of [[daoism|Daoism]] and its literary masterpiece —
a collection of stories, dialogues, and paradoxes attributed to **Zhuang Zhou** (c. 369–286 BCE), whose
"Inner Chapters" are the philosophical core. Where the [[daodejing|Daodejing]] is terse and oracular, the
Zhuangzi is exuberant, humorous, and skeptical.

## Major Themes

- **Skeptical relativism** — the conventional distinctions of true/false, right/wrong, useful/useless,
  big/small, even waking/dreaming, are perspectival and unstable. The famous **butterfly dream** (Zhuang
  Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, then wonders whether he is a butterfly dreaming he is Zhuang Zhou)
  dramatizes the point.
- **Effortless mastery** — the parables of skill (Cook Ding carving the ox, the wheelwright) show
  **[[wu-wei|wu-wei]]** as the spontaneity of a perfected craft, going with the grain of things.
- **Freedom (*you*) and the equality of things** — wandering free of fixed standards; the "fasting of
  the mind"; the usefulness of the useless; equanimity before death as a natural transformation.

## Relation to the Tradition

The Zhuangzi is the heart of [[philosophical-daoism|philosophical Daoism]] and a major source of Chan/Zen
sensibility. Its skeptical playfulness offers a cross-tradition counterpoint to wisdom literature such as
[[ecclesiastes|Ecclesiastes]] (the vanity of fixed striving) — a candidate comparison page.

## Composite-Text Status

Like the Daodejing, the Zhuangzi is not a single-author work. Merton (1965) and Ziporyn (2020) both
note that the **Inner Chapters** (chs. 1–7) are probably Zhuang Zhou's own; the Outer and Miscellaneous
Chapters (8–33) are largely by disciples. Chapter 33 — the survey of all the philosophical schools —
is the **first philosophical history in the Chinese tradition**. The whole book is "an anthology of the
thought, humor, gossip, and irony that were current in Taoist circles in the best period, the 4th and
3rd centuries BCE" (Merton). This makes the Zhuangzi a parallel case to the Daodejing's composite-text
problem: canonical but not single-author.

### Kuo Hsiang's Role (Kirkland)

[[russell-kirkland|Kirkland]] sharpens the composite-text problem significantly. Our received
33-chapter *Chuang-tzu* was assembled by **Kuo Hsiang** (died 312 CE), who selected from a 52-chapter
original and wrote a preface — long thought lost, but discovered by Japanese scholars at a temple in
Kyoto in the 20th century. In that preface, Kuo confesses that the original was so "full of daring
terms and lofty expressions" that much of it made no sense to him; he admits he edited toward "what
is easy to understand" and that his talent did not extend to "strange doctrines." 

This means that:
- Our "Chuang-tzu" is **Kuo Hsiang's bowdlerized selection**, not a clean original
- 19 chapters of the 52-chapter original were excised by Kuo
- The "philosophical" sensibility scholars have attributed to the "Inner Chapters" may partly reflect
  Kuo Hsiang's editorial preferences, not "Chuang Chou's" actual thought
- Kirkland compares the situation to scholars' acceptance or rejection of Gospel material as "authentic
  teachings of Jesus": ultimately an article of faith

The historical "Chuang Chou" as a biographical figure is similarly unverifiable. The characters in the
Zhuangzi — including "Chuang-tzu" himself — are literary constructs. Kirkland emphasizes that the
*Chuang-tzu* gives no biospiritual cultivation practices (unlike the Nei-yeh or later Taoist texts);
its contribution is primarily epistemological skepticism and the aesthetics of effortless skill —
*wu-wei* in a literary/philosophical register rather than a practical cultivation register.

## Key Passages and Themes (Merton's readings)

Merton (*The Way of Chuang Tzu*, 1965) provides the wiki's first thematic-reading pass:

- **Cutting Up an Ox** (Cook Ding / Pao Ting, iii.2): the definitive *wu-wei* parable — "I see nothing
  with the eye. My whole being apprehends. My senses are idle. The spirit free to work without plan
  follows its own instinct." The cook's blade has never dulled in nineteen years.
- **The Fasting of the Heart** (*xinzhai*, iv.1): Confucius to Yen Hui — "The goal of fasting is inner
  unity... hearing with the spirit... The hearing of the spirit demands the emptiness of all the faculties."
- **The Pivot** (ii.3): the complementarity of opposites; the wise person holds the still point where
  all Yes/No affirmations converge, rather than taking sides in the "Yes vs. No" dispute.
- **Two Kings and No-Form** (vii.7): the kings drill holes in No-Form to give him the senses men have;
  when the seventh hole is done, No-Form lies dead. "To organize is to destroy."
- **Duke Hwan and the Wheelwright** (xiii.10): "The men of old took all they really knew with them to the
  grave... what you are reading there is only the dirt they left behind." Tao cannot be transmitted in books.
- **The Lost Pearl** (xii.3): the Yellow Emperor sends Science, Analysis, and Logic to find his lost
  pearl; all fail. Nothingness finds it.
- **The Woodcarver** (xix.10): fasting for 7 days to forget gain, praise, body — "My own collected
  thought encountered the hidden potential in the wood."
- **When the Shoe Fits** (xix.12): "When the shoe fits, the foot is forgotten... forget the right way
  and forget that the going is easy."
- **The Empty Boat** (xx.2): "If you can empty your own boat crossing the river of the world, no one
  will oppose you, no one will seek to harm you."
- **Chuang Tzu's Funeral** (xxxii.14): "I shall have heaven and earth for my coffin... why are you so
  partial to birds?"

## Chuang Tzu's Critique of Confucianism

Merton's key interpretive contribution: Chuang Tzu's critique does not argue for **less** virtue than
Confucianism, but for **more**. The problem with Confucian moral cultivation is that it objectifies
virtue — treats the good as something to be attained from outside. Any time virtue is treated as an
object, its pursuit produces its opposite. Chuang Tzu's way begins with the good already present
in one's nature (*ziran*) and requires only **not obstructing** it.

This analysis also extends to politics: systematic benevolence and justice create their own exploitation
(the "Cracking the Safe" parable — organize the loot neatly and you serve the bigger thief).

## Zhuangzi and Zen

The Chinese Zen (Chan) Buddhists of the Tang period (7th–10th c. CE) are the historical inheritors
of Zhuangzi's sensibility: iconoclastic, humorous, totally practical — the same style of thought that
"transformed highly speculative Indian Buddhism into the... kind of Buddhism that was to flourish in
China and Japan" (Merton). Zen illuminates Zhuangzi and Zhuangzi illuminates Zen.
