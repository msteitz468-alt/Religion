# Shepherd of Hermas

## Textual Overview

*The Shepherd of Hermas* (*Herm.* or *Shepherd*) is one of the most enigmatic and most popular texts from the postapostolic period. Relatively simple in style and widely beloved in the 2nd and 3rd centuries, it preserves more early copies than many canonical writings — including multiple papyri from the 2nd century onwards — yet was ultimately excluded from the NT.

**Nature**: A Christian apocalypse set in Rome. The author, Hermas, receives a series of revelations — first from a woman who appears to be an aged lady (= the Church), then from an angelic figure in the form of a shepherd (= "the angel of repentance") — that address two central concerns: (1) whether postbaptismal sin can be forgiven, and (2) the behavior of wealthy church members toward the poor.

**Authorship**: The Muratorian Canon (oldest ca. AD 180–200) identifies Hermas as the brother of Pius, bishop of Rome (ca. 140–154 CE). This would place the final form of the text in the mid-2nd century. Internal evidence (reference to "Clement" in Vis. 8.3 = a figure possibly identified with the author of 1 Clement) suggests an earlier stratum of the Visions section may date to the late 1st or early 2nd century. The text is probably a composite, composed in stages over several decades by one author (or possibly two or three contributors working with common material).

**Social location**: Hermas represents lower-class freedmen (former slaves) in Rome — not the educated elite of [[1-clement|1 Clement]]. His anxieties are those of people whose business dealings with the pagan world (necessary for earning a living) constantly threaten their baptismal purity. This social location gives the *Shepherd* a different theological flavor than the other Apostolic Fathers.

## Structure

Two reference systems exist for the *Shepherd*. The older traditional system divides the text into:

| Section | Traditional | Chapter Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Visions 1–4 | *Vis.* 1–4 | *Herm.* 1–24 |
| Vision 5 (= Introduction to Part 2) | *Vis.* 5 | *Herm.* 25 |
| Mandates/Commandments (12) | *Mand.* 1–12 | *Herm.* 26–49 |
| Similitudes/Parables (10) | *Sim.* 1–10 | *Herm.* 50–114 |

The textual evidence suggests the *Shepherd* was originally two documents — Visions 1–4 (*Herm.* 1–24) circulating separately from the Mandates + Similitudes (*Herm.* 25–114) — later combined with *Parables* 9–10 added to unify them.

## Content: Key Sections

### Visions 1–4 (Herm. 1–24): The Elderly Woman and the Church

**Vision 1**: Hermas, a freedman in Rome, catches sight of his former owner Rhoda bathing in the Tiber and briefly entertains a desire for her. He is visited by Rhoda in a heavenly vision who accuses him before God. A female figure (= the Church) appears, aged and seated on a chair made of snow-white wool, and reads to him from a book. She announces that his family — not he personally — has sinned, and calls him to convert his children.

**Vision 2**: The same elderly woman appears again, younger. She gives Hermas a small book that he copies. Her message: repentance is possible, but the window is closing.

**Vision 3**: The woman appears yet younger still — a progressive rejuvenation that symbolizes the church's renewal through repentance. A vision of a tower being built from stones — those that fit are used; others are rejected (= unrepentant sinners) or set aside for later (= those who can still repent).

**Vision 4**: A great beast on the road (the coming great tribulation). Hermas trusts in God and passes safely. The church, now appearing as a young woman, interprets: his faith has preserved him.

**Vision 5 (Herm. 25)**: The "Shepherd" appears — an angelic figure in shepherd's clothing. This is the "angel of repentance," who will be Hermas' guide through the Mandates and Similitudes.

### Mandates (Herm. 26–49): The Twelve Commandments

Twelve commandments delivered by the Shepherd covering:
- Faith, continence, truth, purity, patience
- Fear of God vs. fear of the devil
- Grief as companion to doubt (*dipsychia*)
- The two spirits in each person (truth and deceit)
- Commandment 12 (Mand. 12): The two desires — this-worldly vs. heavenly

**The double-minded (*dipsychos*)** is a recurring figure: the person who doubts God's ability to rescue them is "double-minded" and cannot receive anything from God. This concept runs through both the Mandates and the *Didache* and James and may reflect a common Jewish-Christian moral tradition. (See [[double-mindedness|Double-Mindedness]] concept page.)

### Similitudes/Parables (Herm. 50–114)

Ten parables using allegorical analogies from Roman social life:

- *Sim.* 1 (*Herm.* 50): This world is not your city — you are a stranger here; don't buy property in the enemy's city
- *Sim.* 2 (*Herm.* 51): The elm and the vine — the wealthy (elm, unfruitful but supporting) and the poor (vine, fruitful but needing support) need each other
- *Sims.* 3–4 (*Herm.* 52–53): Trees in winter and summer — sinners and just alike in this world; different at judgment
- *Sim.* 5 (*Herm.* 54–60): The fasting parable — true fasting is keeping God's commandments; the slave who works for the master and earns freedom by doing more than required (Christological implications; the Spirit of God in the flesh)
- *Sim.* 8 (*Herm.* 67–77): The great willow tree and its branches — extended allegory of the church; branches of different conditions = Christians at different spiritual states
- *Sims.* 9–10 (*Herm.* 78–114): The tower revisited — the most elaborate and most analyzed section; the tower built from twelve different mountains (peoples) of different characters; the "virgins" (= virtues) who build the tower; extensive repentance theology

## Major Themes

### Postbaptismal Repentance

The *Shepherd*'s central theological question: **Is forgiveness available for sins committed after baptism?** The answer is yes — once, and for a limited time only. The opportunity is real but urgent; it will soon close. This "once-for-all" teaching about postbaptismal forgiveness was both deeply needed (Christian communities included people who had sinned significantly after conversion) and theologically controversial (Tertullian, having joined the Montanists, attacked the *Shepherd* as "the shepherd of the adulterers" for its permissive approach to postbaptismal sin).

The *Shepherd* thus represents an early attempt to navigate between two dangers: legalism (no forgiveness after baptism) and antinomianism (forgiveness always available, no urgency). Its solution — once, now, urgently — reflects a community that needed to address actual cases of serious postbaptismal sin without losing its moral seriousness.

### Wealth, Poverty, and Community

A second major concern: the behavior of wealthy church members. The rich are not condemned per se, but their wealth creates particular spiritual dangers (entanglement with the world; business dealings with idolaters; neglect of the poor). The elm-and-vine parable (*Sim.* 2) is the most positive statement: the wealthy and the poor need each other; the poor's prayers before God are powerful, and the wealthy should support the poor in exchange for those prayers.

This social theology is more egalitarian than [[1-clement|1 Clement]] and reflects a different (lower-status) membership base.

### Christology: The Spirit in the Flesh

The *Shepherd* has notably **minimal Christology** — Christ is rarely mentioned by name; the Spirit and angels perform many functions that other texts attribute to Christ. The most developed Christological passage is *Sim.* 5: a pre-existent holy Spirit (= the Son?) dwelled in a "servant" (= the flesh of Jesus), and because this Spirit was faithfully served by the flesh and did nothing wrong, God elevated the flesh to share in the divine community (*Sim.* 5.5–7). This is adoptionist or binatarian in structure — theologians have read it as subordinationist, adoptionist, or as simply theologically undeveloped.

### Genre: Jewish-Christian Apocalypse

Visions 1–4 follow the form of a Jewish-Christian apocalypse — revelation from God through a heavenly mediator, interpretation of visions, concern with coming tribulation. The closest formal parallels are 4 Ezra and 1 Enoch. The Mandates resemble a Jewish-Hellenistic wisdom homily. The Parables use allegorical similitudes characteristic of Jewish parabolic tradition.

## Canonical Status

The *Shepherd* was among the most widely read texts in the 2nd–3rd centuries that ultimately did not enter the NT canon:
- **Irenaeus**: Accepted as scripture
- **Clement of Alexandria**: Accepted as scripture
- **Origen**: Accepted as scripture (at least for a period)
- **Tertullian**: After joining the Montanists, attacked it as "the shepherd of the adulterers"
- **Athanasius**: Cited it positively but explicitly excluded it from the canon (*Festal Letter* 39, 367 CE); recommended it for reading by new converts
- **Didymus the Blind**: Included it in his canon
- **Codex Sinaiticus**: Present after the NT (after Barnabas) — reflects its quasi-canonical status in some communities
- **Muratorian Canon**: Explicitly excluded from the NT (not apostolic; too recent; not to be read publicly in church)

## Manuscript Tradition

The text of the *Shepherd* is poorly preserved — complex manuscript situation with no single complete witness:

| Witness | Date | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Codex Sinaiticus (S) | 4th century | Herm. 1.1–31.6 |
| Codex Athous (A) | 14th–15th cent. | Herm. 1.1–107.2 (main Greek witness for middle/later sections) |
| Michigan Papyrus 129 (M) | 2nd century | Herm. 51.8–82.1 (one of several papyri) |
| Old Latin / "Vulgate" (L¹) | Perhaps 2nd century | For Herm. 107.3–114.5 (where no Greek survives) |
| Sahidic Coptic (C¹) | 4th century | Selected portions |
| Ethiopic (E) | Perhaps 6th century | Selected portions |

For *Herm.* 107.3–114.5, there is no surviving Greek text — we depend entirely on the Old Latin translation.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**The single repentance**: Is the "one repentance" teaching meant universally (every Christian may repent once after baptism) or situationally (Hermas' community is receiving one special offer of repentance due to extraordinary circumstances)? The text seems to mean the former but has been read both ways.

**Christology**: Is the Spirit-in-the-flesh Christology adoptionist, pre-existent-Spirit theology, or simply underdeveloped? The *Shepherd*'s Christological vocabulary is distinctive and has generated sustained scholarly debate.

**Compositional history**: Were the Visions (1–4) written first and separately circulated? Does the Michigan papyrus beginning at *Vis.* 5 (*Herm.* 25) indicate that the two-part division was known in the 2nd century itself?

**"Clement" in Herm. 8.3**: Hermas is told to give a copy to Clement, who "will send it to the foreign cities." If this is the same Clement as the author of [[1-clement|1 Clement]], it provides a connection to the Roman church of ca. 96 CE and dates the earliest layer of the Visions to that period.
