# Sermon on the Mount

## Textual Overview

The Sermon on the Mount is the first and longest of the five major discourses of the
[[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]], running from 5:1 to 7:29 and closed by the formula at 7:28 that
recurs at the end of each of the five discourses.

[[rt-france|R. T. France]] declines the traditional title and calls the unit **"The Discourse on
Discipleship"**, on the ground that "Sermon on the Mount" too often conveys "the concept of a
general code of ethics rather than the specific demands of the kingdom of heaven." He also judges
the traditional title geographically misleading: he renders *eis to oros* "into the hills," taking
it as a general term for the hill country west and north of the lake, and holds that "no specific
'mountain' can be safely identified" — "To that extent 'Sermon on the Mount' is a misleading
description." Luke's "level place" he takes as supporting this.

France holds that the discourse is not the transcript of a single sermon but "an anthology of the
teaching of Jesus relating to discipleship." He generalizes this to all five Matthean discourses,
which he describes as "not so much transcripts of actual sermons as anthologies of the remembered
sayings of Jesus," the work of "a responsible anthologizer." On his account Matthew expanded an
existing sermon outline preserved in Luke 6:20-49 — same sequence, same opening and closing motifs
— by adding three major blocks: fulfilling the law (5:17-48), religious observance (6:1-18), and
material concerns (6:19-34). He quantifies the source relations: roughly 27 percent of the discourse
is shared with Luke 6:20-49, 33 percent has parallels elsewhere in Luke, 5 percent in Mark, and
35 percent has no Synoptic parallel at all. He argues that "it is the overall structure of the
'sermon' rather than its specific content that links Matthew with Luke," since the opening and
closing pericopes are similar in subject but conspicuously different in wording. See
[[synoptic-problem|the Synoptic problem]].

France flags as unresolved the question of the discourse's overall design: a satisfying outline of
the whole "continues to elude commentators."

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

### Audience and purpose

France insists the discourse is addressed to committed disciples rather than to the crowds: it is
"the instruction of those who have already responded to that proclamation," teaching them what life
in the kingdom of heaven is really about. The crowds of 5:1 and 7:28 he places "on the fringe of the
disciple group." He describes the disciples as "an alternative society, a 'Christian
counter-culture'," called to a radically new lifestyle in conscious distinction from surrounding
norms. His summary verdict is that "far from being a philosophical discourse on ethics, this is a
messianic manifesto," asserting absolute authority over people's lifestyle and destiny. At the
climax of ch. 5 he restates the purpose as "not to provide a suitable ethic for getting along
alright in the world but to challenge those who have accepted the demands of the kingdom of heaven
... by being different from other people." See [[discipleship|discipleship]].

France holds the demands "do not easily translate into a practical, day-to-day morality," naming
Tolstoy's experience; the standard is perfection and "its reach exceeds their grasp." He states the
programmatic thesis for his whole reading of ch. 5 thus: "The essence of life in the kingdom of
heaven is in fact the antithesis of a legalistic code, as 5:20 will state and 5:21-48 will
repeatedly illustrate."

### 5:17–20 as the interpretive crux

France treats 5:17-48 as a coherent unit on one theme — "the most extensive discussion of this issue
anywhere in the gospel tradition" — raising acutely the question of Jesus' messianic authority in
relation to the authority of the Torah and its authorized interpreters. He frames it as "a
presentation of the law of the new covenant, as both in continuity and in contrast with the OT law."

Against interpreters who treat 5:17-20 as originally separate sayings to be read in isolation,
France insists that "Matthew must have thought that they made coherent sense together, and it is our
responsibility to try to discern that sense." He concedes "there is tension and development" but
holds that "it is the responsibility of the exegete to try to make coherent sense of the text as it
stands, before charging the author with blatant contradiction or clumsy editing."

**The two-fronts thesis.** France argues Matthew confronts two opposite tendencies: an antinomian
one, that OT laws no longer matter — "in line with Paul's 'freedom from the law' teaching" — which
is confronted in vv. 17-19; and a scribal-literalist one, confronted in v. 20 and illustrated by
vv. 21-47. He describes v. 20 as "taking the battle onto a different front."

**"Fulfil."** France names the hinge explicitly: "The key to this issue must be what is meant by
Jesus 'fulfilling' the law and the prophets." He rejects the sense "obey/keep": that "would not be
the natural way to say that, and such a sense would not answer the charge of aiming to 'abolish.'"
Surveying every Matthean use of *plēroō*, he finds the dominant sense to be "the coming into being
of that to which Scripture pointed forward, whether by direct prediction or understood
typologically." He stresses that "this verse does not speak of Jesus 'fulfilling the law,' but
rather of his 'fulfilling the law and the prophets,'" so that the law is fulfilled in the same
forward-pointing mode as the prophets; his decisive supporting text is 11:13, "All the prophets and
the law prophesied until John." His strongest formulation follows: "The Torah, then, is not God's
last word to his people, but is in a sense provisional, looking forward to a time of fulfillment
through the Messiah." Yet he denies this abolishes it: "the authority of the law and the prophets is
not abolished. They remain the authoritative word of God. But their role will no longer be the
same." See [[fulfillment|fulfilment]], [[old-law|the old law]], [[new-law|the new law]].

France notes that this reading "has gained a considerable degree of assent in recent decades, over
against the older view of a legally conservative Matthew."

**The double "until" of 5:18.** France flags "not one small letter or a single stroke of the pen" as
"a vivid way of conveying that no part of it can be dispensed with," and reads "until heaven and
earth pass away" as conventional idiom for "never." He then calls the second "until" clause "a
striking and puzzling epigram," rejects the view that it merely repeats the first, and rejects the
view that it names a terminus at which the details were invalidated. His own solution: "The second
'until,' then, is not speaking of the time of their abandonment but of their intended goal." He
concedes the construction is "admittedly awkward."

**5:19.** France argues *lyō* here means undermining the commandments' authority by teaching that
they may be ignored, not disobeying them — "The translation 'breaks' in NRSV and NIV (but not
TNIV), or 'disobeys' in GNB, thus misses the essential point." He rejects the reading that "these
smallest commandments" are Jesus' own rather than the OT law's, since the connecting "So" rules it
out. Because sacrificial and food laws were already broadly abandoned in Matthew's day, he infers
that Matthew "had in mind a different kind of 'doing'" appropriate to the era of fulfilment — the
law as now interpreted by Jesus — cross-referring to 28:20, "where it is the 'commandments' of
Jesus, not those of the OT, which are to be the basis of Christian discipleship."

**5:20.** France argues the surpassing righteousness cannot mean out-performing the
[[pharisees|scribes and Pharisees]] at rule-keeping: "Jesus is not talking about beating the scribes
and Pharisees at their own game, but about a different level or concept of righteousness
altogether." His sharpest formulation is that "Those who can do no more than simply keep the rules,
however conscientiously, haven't even started as far as the kingdom of heaven is concerned." See
[[righteousness|righteousness]].

### Piety, possessions and anxiety (6:1–34)

France reads 6:1 as turning from the greater righteousness of 5:20-48 to a *wrong* kind of
righteousness, undertaken for human approval rather than to conform to God's will; the focus moves
"from ethical distinctives to the practice of religion." He analyses 6:1-18 as an introductory
exhortation (v.1) plus three matching contrasts on almsgiving, prayer and fasting (vv. 2-4, 5-6,
16-18), noting that v.1 is plural while the three examples are singular, so that v.1 functions for
6:2-18 as 5:20 did for 5:21-48. He observes that the three duties, together with recitation of the
creed and the pilgrimage to Mecca, constitute the Five Pillars of Islam.

France addresses at length the *prima facie* contradiction between 5:16 ("let your light shine
before others") and 6:1 ("do not practise your piety before others"), and offers two differences:
5:16 concerns whole character and lifestyle whereas 6:1-18 concerns specifically religious duties,
which are more fertile ground for false piety; and — for him the more important — 6:1 concerns "a
deliberate search for public recognition," whereas 5:16's visibility is unavoidable, being that of
a town on a hill, and issues in God being glorified rather than the disciple. He notes the verbal
play: "applauded" in 6:2 is the same verb *doxazō* used in 5:16 of glorifying God, and the switch of
object "speaks eloquently of the different perspectives." He also insists that in 6:2-18 no deceit
as such is alleged — "they presumably did give alms, pray, and fast; the problem was that they
wanted everyone to know it" — and that the Matthean hypocrite is generally "not so much [a]
deceiver ... as disastrously self-deceived."

On possessions France treats 6:19-24 as three originally separate sayings grouped by their bearing
on material goods, connected by the thought of single-mindedness which surfaces in the wordplay on
*haplous* in v. 22. He renders "Do not store up" as "Stop storing up" — "a call to reorientation" —
and refuses a one-way causal reading of v. 21: "the valuing of the treasure both follows from and
reveals the orientation of the heart." He explicitly limits the saying: "It is going beyond the
intention of the saying to use it as a basis for ruling out all material possessions." On 6:24 he
calls the traditional "No one can serve two masters" "patently untrue; we do it all the time," and
locates the force in *slavery* — total ownership — rather than employment.

On 6:25-34, France is candid about the theodicy problem: birds die in huge numbers, devout disciples
die in famine while the affluent live in excess, and it would be "grossly insensitive and blinkered"
to suggest they did not trust God enough. He concedes that "This teaching seems to envisage the
world as it should be rather than the world as it is," and declines to resolve it here: "We must
look elsewhere for a more wide-ranging theodicy." He locates the teaching's original force in the
itinerant, dependent lifestyle of Jesus' first disciples. He distinguishes worry from provision —
"It is, after all, worry about tomorrow, not provision for tomorrow, which v. 34 condemns" — and
limits the promise sharply: "The disciple is promised survival, not affluence; this is no carte
blanche."

### The golden rule and the closing warnings (7:1–29)

France reads 7:1-5 as addressing unfairly critical attitudes combined with naive lack of
self-criticism, threatening to disrupt a close-knit community; underlying it is a principle of
reciprocity linking 6:14-15, 7:1-2 and 7:12 — "We must expect to be treated as we treat other
people." He insists the passage forbids a fault-finding mentality, not the use of judgment as such,
since v.6 requires discrimination, vv.15-20 require judging by fruits, and 18:15-17 requires
correcting a sinning brother. He calls the speck-and-log scenario "deliberately ridiculous," robust
imagery from the carpenter's workshop, and judges it "more likely" that the critic fails to apply
his own standards to himself than that he conceals known failings, since "v. 3 speaks of 'failing to
notice' rather than of deliberate deception."

**7:6** France flags as a crux: "this apparently independent saying is couched only in metaphor,
with no indication how it might be applied to real-life contexts." He rejects the eucharistic
application of Did. 9:5 as anachronistic for a saying of Jesus during his lifetime, and rejects
reading dogs and pigs as Gentiles, since that "would conflict sharply with the much more pervasive
ideal of the inclusion of [[gentiles|Gentiles]] among the people of God which will culminate in
28:19." He prefers the mixed character of the disciple community as the setting but deliberately
declines a specific identification: "disciples are to be discriminating ... but to avoid offering a
more specific identification of who are to be regarded as unsuitable."

**7:12.** France reads "this is the law and the prophets" as echoing 5:17 and thereby closing the
main body of the discourse, so that vv.13-27 form "a coda calling for decisive response rather than
adding further instructions." He treats 7:12 as "a far-reaching and memorable summary of the ethics
of discipleship (the 'greater righteousness' of 5:20)," distinguishing it from 22:34-40 in that
there Jesus singles out two OT texts whereas here "the summary he offers here is of the spirit
rather than the actual words of the law." He concedes it does not cover every aspect of the law —
not the sacrificial rituals, nor love for God himself. He reports the Hillel parallel (b. Šabb. 31a)
and argues that Jesus' *positive* formulation, against the negative form found in Hillel, other
Jewish and pagan parallels, Did. 1:2 and Gos. Thom. 6, "even if not unprecedented, represents a more
demanding interpretation of love of one's neighbor than was normal among other teachers of the
time." He notes with surprise that the Didache, "normally thought to be dependent on Matthew," has
only the negative form.

**The four warnings (7:13-27).** France describes them as four short sketches of no uniform literary
form functioning as "a rhetorical conclusion to the discourse, aiming to motivate the hearers to
take appropriate action," traced by the key word *poieō*, "to do." He argues that they press
progressively closer to home: saved or lost; outsiders pretending to be insiders; those who *think*
they are insiders but are not; and a line drawn within the insiders. Each failure ends
catastrophically. On the two ways he insists the issue is "being either in or out, saved or lost,"
and draws a striking conclusion: "Matthew's Jesus does not seem to envisage the general conversion
of society." On 7:21-23 he argues against treating it as a subsection on false prophecy, since the
deception is of a wholly different kind — v.15 concerns deliberate deception by outsiders, vv.21-23
self-deceived insiders who are "apparently themselves more surprised than anyone when they find
themselves rejected." He states the central point: the basis of rejection is neither what they did
nor the allegiance they professed, but "I never knew you" — and concludes that "Even good works by
themselves are not enough," since "'Doing the will of my Father in heaven' is not a merely ethical
category; that will also includes to know and be known by Jesus the 'Lord.'" He acknowledges the
pastoral cost: the pericope "raises sharply the issue of assurance of salvation, and taken alone it
can be a cause of great distress to some more sensitive souls," pointing to 1 John as a pastoral
treatise written in the light of just such self-examination. On the two builders he stresses that
both men "have 'heard' Jesus' teaching," so that "to be there in the audience is no more guarantee
of salvation than to have called Jesus 'Lord! Lord!'"; and that the point "is not, as in
1 Cor 3:10-15, the suitability of the building material, but the solidity of the foundation."

### 7:28–29: authority

France reads 7:28-29 as forming with 5:1-2 a frame around the discourse, with the focus shifting
from disciples to crowds. He identifies the discourse-closing formula recurring at 7:28; 11:1;
13:53; 19:1; 26:1, whose first six Greek words are identical each time, and argues it is
deliberately archaic: *kai egeneto* renders the Hebrew *wayyehî*, is not natural Greek, and occurs
elsewhere in Matthew only at 9:10.

He calls the contrast with scribal authority "a bold claim," since scribes were the authorized
teachers of the law with a right by training and office to expect acceptance of their rulings. His
key distinction is that scribal rulings rested on the tradition of earlier interpreters, while
Jesus in 5:17-48 set himself up as an authority over against that tradition "on the basis not of
formal training or authorization but of his own confident, 'I tell you.'" He forecasts the
[[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] conflict as "a contest of authority, that of the established guardians of
legal tradition against that of the upstart Galilean preacher," and speculates that the crowds may
be "remembering how in 5:20 Jesus has declared the 'righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees'
inadequate." Adding Jesus' assumption that he is himself the proper object of allegiance and "the
arbiter of their destiny," France concludes "the crowd's astonishment is hardly out of place." He
closes his exposition by quoting W. D. Davies: "The Sermon on the Mount compels us, in the first
place, to ask who he is who utters these words."

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

France's most developed text-critical discussion in this discourse concerns 7:13. He reports a
variant governing the imagery: on the majority reading each road has its own gate, while on the
alternative the wide road has no gate — a reading yielding the homiletically attractive picture of
drifting with the crowd. He finds the alternative richer but notes that "most textual scholars opt
for the majority reading," and suspects the shorter reading's ancient popularity "may have more to
do with its homiletical possibilities than with its textual authenticity." He also notes manuscript
variation in the tense of the verb at 6:12, which he suggests may reflect ancient uncertainty over
whether human forgiveness is strictly prior to God's. See [[lords-prayer|the Lord's Prayer]] for the
doxology's textual status.

**Source limitation.** The plain-text conversion of France's commentary used for this wiki dropped
all footnote bodies. France's detailed text-critical argument for 7:13 is located in a footnote
(p. 284, n. 1) and is not recoverable; the same applies to his argument on *tou ponērou* at 6:13
(p. 231, n. 14) and on the doxology (p. 231, n. 15). Positions above are attributed to named
scholars only where the surviving body text names them.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

- **France's own frame** is grammatical-historical exegesis oriented to the final form of Matthew's
  text, with a declared preference for reading juxtaposed sayings as a coherent whole "before
  charging the author with blatant contradiction or clumsy editing." He supplements this with
  redaction-critical judgments about Matthew's compilation of an anthology from scattered tradition.
- **Fulfilment hermeneutic.** France's reading of 5:17 makes the law forward-pointing in the same
  mode as prophecy, so that the Torah is read prospectively and christologically:
  "it is Jesus, the fulfiller, rather than the law which pointed forward to him, who is the ultimate
  authority." See [[fulfillment|fulfilment]] and [[typology|typology]].
- **Moses typology, qualified.** France allows that Matthew "may well have intended *to oros* also
  to suggest a typological parallel with [[moses|Moses]]," alongside the geographical sense, but
  calls Davies and Allison's claim that "Mt 1-5 in all its parts reflects a developed exodus
  typology" a "rather exaggerated comment." Where the typology holds, he insists it is one of
  *contrast*: "Moses gave them the law; Jesus 'fulfills' it (5:17)."
- **Against a literal rulebook reading.** France's governing rule for applying the discourse is that
  "Jesus' illustrations of the 'greater righteousness' are not to be treated as if they were a new
  set of literal regulations to replace those of the scribes and Pharisees," and that application is
  left to the disciple: "It is for each disciple to work out for themselves how this principle can
  most responsibly be applied."
- **Against individualist reading.** France criticizes "modern Western individualism" for reading
  the light imagery as "a variety of little candles shining, 'you in your small corner, and I in
  mine'"; the point is "the collective light of a whole community."

## Canon and Reception

The Sermon stands within Matthew and shares its canonical status: canonical for Catholic, Eastern
Orthodox and Protestant communities alike. France's own reception-historical remarks are scattered
but pointed. He notes that Jesus' teaching on love of enemies "formed one of the most distinctive
traits of the early Christian movement, and has been widely influential ever since." He records that
"some early patristic interpreters took this verse as a ruling against the use of hair dye!" of the
oath by one's head. He reports that Did. 9:5 applied 7:6 to the Eucharist, restricted to the
baptized, and rejects the application. He cites the Didache's negative form of the golden rule
despite its presumed dependence on Matthew, traces the name "Golden Rule" traditionally to the
Roman Emperor Alexander Severus (A.D. 222-35), and illustrates Victorian British reception through
Charles Kingsley's *The Water Babies* and its fairy "Mrs Do-as-you-would-be-done-by." He names
Tolstoy as one who discovered in experience that the discourse's demands do not easily translate
into day-to-day morality.

## Scholarship and Historiography

Positions recorded here as positions, not adjudicated by this wiki:

- **Kingsbury's three-part structure.** France reports the proposal of N. B. Stonehouse, developed
  by E. Krentz and adopted by J. D. Kingsbury, that "From that time Jesus began" (4:17; 16:21) is a
  structural formula marking a three-part gospel. He rejects it: "a five-word phrase repeated once
  at a distance of twelve chapters is not an impressive basis," and it does not compare with
  Matthew's genuine repeated formulae at 1:22 and 7:28.
- **Exodus typology (Davies and Allison).** Quoted at 1:427 to the effect that "Mt 1-5 in all its
  parts reflects a developed exodus typology"; France calls the comment "rather exaggerated."
- **A legally conservative Matthew.** France states this as the position he will reject, conceding
  that "Verses 18 and 19 taken on their own can certainly give that impression." He objects on two
  grounds: it is "out of step with the overall thrust of NT Christianity" (citing Hebrews on
  sacrifice, Gal 5:1-6, Rom 7:1-6, Rom 10:4, and the abandonment of food laws at Acts 11:2-10 and
  Rom 14:14), and it "conflicts with clear pointers within his own gospel," since 15:11 "in effect
  undercuts the whole complex of Levitical laws of purity."
- **Meier on interiorization.** France quotes J. P. Meier approvingly: Jesus demands "a radical
  interiorization, a total obedience to God, a complete self-giving to neighbor, that carries the
  ethical thrust of the Law to its God-willed conclusion, even when this means in some cases
  abrogating the letter of the Law."
- **Betz on peacemaking**, endorsed by France, that the discourse "recognizes war, persecution and
  injustice as part of the evil world" and that peacemaking means "risking peacemaking out of a
  situation of powerlessness."
- **Markan priority.** France states that he "continue[s] to believe in the priority of Mark,"
  arguing that a Mark written with the fuller Matthew in front of him would have had to omit the
  entire Sermon while adding inconsequential narrative detail.

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **What "fulfil" means in 5:17** — France's answer is the forward-pointing sense; alternatives he
   rejects include "obey" and "keep."
2. **The double "until" of 5:18** — three readings canvassed; France chooses "intended goal" and
   concedes the awkwardness.
3. **The relation of 5:16 to 6:1** — France holds "the discrepancy is only on the surface."
4. **The salt metaphor** — France flags that "the precise nature of the symbolism is not certain,"
   and that either flavouring or preservation "would provide an appropriate sense here." He also
   notes the verb for losing savour literally means "becomes foolish," and that Hebrew/Aramaic
   *tāpēl* can mean both tasteless and foolish.
5. **The lamp-and-eye saying (6:22-23)** — France states flatly that "modern commentators have not
   been able to agree on how the image works," canvasses three constructions, and concludes with a
   deliberately minimal statement: "Perhaps we can be no more definite than to say that the imagery
   depends on light being necessary for the proper functioning of the body." On *haplous* he weighs
   "single-minded" against "generous" and resolves inclusively: "either single-mindedness ... or
   generosity, or more likely both."
6. **Who the dogs and pigs of 7:6 are** — France deliberately refuses to identify them.
7. **Whether the closing warnings' catastrophes are eschatological** — on the two builders France
   hedges: total collapse "probably suggests" final judgment, "but that setting is not emphasized,
   and the imagery applies equally to the testing which discipleship will repeatedly encounter."
8. **Practicability.** France concedes that the antithesis material is widely judged unworkable and
   answers not by softening but by reframing: "instead of therefore dismissing Jesus' teaching as
   starry-eyed utopianism, a proper response ... is to ask in what practical ways Jesus' radical
   principles can be set to work in our very different world."

France leaves several questions explicitly unresolved — the overall outline of the discourse, the
salt symbolism, the eye-lamp mechanism, the identity of the "dogs," and the wider theodicy raised by
6:25-34. These refusals are part of his exposition rather than gaps in it.
