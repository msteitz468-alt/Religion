# On the Incarnation (*De Incarnatione Verbi Dei*)

The treatise *On the Incarnation of the Word of God* by [[athanasius|Athanasius of Alexandria]],
an early work (c. 318 CE) that forms the second half of a **double work** — its companion, *Contra
Gentes* ("Against the Heathen"), is referred to as "our former book" in its opening lines (§1). It
is addressed to one **Macarius** ("true lover of Christ," §1; named again in the conclusion, §56),
otherwise unknown. It is not scripture but one of the most influential single statements of
patristic Christology and soteriology, prized in East and West alike. This page covers the whole
treatise (the nine chapters / §§1–57, in the translation introduced by **C. S. Lewis**), **read in
full**.

The Lewis introduction (a celebrated essay in its own right) is bound with this translation and
makes two claims worth recording as reception: that *De Incarnatione* reads "almost as easy as
Xenophon," a fourth-century "masterpiece" written "with such classical simplicity"; and that
Athanasius's epitaph is *Athanasius contra mundum* — he "stood for the Trinitarian doctrine… when
it looked as if all the civilised world was slipping back from Christianity into the religion of
Arius." (Lewis also notes that the so-called "Athanasian Creed" is "not exactly a creed and was not
by St. Athanasius.")

## Design and Argument

The treatise answers a single question — **why did the Word of God become man?** — and, secondarily,
why he died *on a cross* and *publicly*. Athanasius repeatedly grounds the whole in the goodness of
God and the unity of **creation and salvation**: "the renewal of creation has been wrought by the
Self-same Word Who made it in the beginning" (§1).

**Chs. I–III — Creation, Fall, and the Divine Dilemma (§§1–17).**
- *Creation out of nothing.* Against the Epicureans (chance), Plato (pre-existent matter), and the
  [[gnosticism|Gnostics]] (a Demiurge other than the Father of Christ), Athanasius asserts creation
  *ex nihilo* through the Word (§§2–3), citing Gen 1:1, *The Shepherd of Hermas*, and Heb 11:3.
- *Humanity and the [[imago-dei|Image]].* Humans were made mortal "by nature" (from nothing) but
  given a share in the Word — "the impress of His own Image" — so that by contemplation of God they
  might "continue for ever" incorruptible (§§3–5). The Fall reverses this: turning from God, humanity
  comes under "the law of death" and slides back toward **non-existence/corruption** (*phthora*),
  since "evil is non-being, the negation and antithesis of good" (§4).
- *The Divine Dilemma* (§§6–10), the structural heart of the work. God's **goodness** cannot allow
  His creatures to perish; yet His **truthfulness/consistency** cannot simply revoke the sentence of
  death He pronounced. Mere **repentance** is insufficient: it would leave God "untrue" and could not
  "recall men from what is according to their nature" (i.e., undo *corruption*). Only the Word — who
  made all from nothing — can "recreate all" and "maintain for the Father His consistency" (§7).
- *The solution.* The incorporeal Word takes a real body from a virgin "as an instrument" and offers
  it to death "instead of all" — "so that in His death all might die, and the law of death thereby be
  abolished" (§8). The death of the one body, indwelt by the Word "who was above all," is a
  "sufficient exchange for all" (§9), an idea Athanasius illustrates with the **great king dwelling
  in one house of a city**, honoring the whole city (§9).
- *The second purpose: renewing the knowledge of God* (§§11–16). Having defaced the Image,
  humanity could not regain knowledge of God through creation, the law, or the prophets — the "three
  ways" God had already provided (§12). So the Image himself came to **re-draw the portrait** on the
  same panel (the celebrated simile, §14: a defaced portrait requires the original sitter to return).
  The Word "meets their senses half way," becoming an object of sense so that those looking for God
  among created things might find the Father through his bodily works (§§15–16).
- *The paradox of the Uncontained* (§17): present in the body, the Word "was not hedged in by His
  body"; he simultaneously animated his body, sustained the universe, and abode in the Father —
  "as Man… living a human life, and as Word… sustaining the life of the universe, and as Son… in
  constant union with the Father." The sun-and-rays image: as the sun is undefiled by its rays
  touching earth, the Word is undefiled by the body, which is instead "cleansed and quickened."

**Ch. IV–V — The Death and Resurrection (§§18–32).**
- The "two opposite marvels": "the death of all was consummated in the Lord's body; yet, because the
  Word was in it, death and corruption were in the same act utterly abolished" (§20).
- *Why a public death inflicted by others* (§§21–24): had he died of sickness or privately, his
  power over death and the fact of his resurrection would be deniable; "death had to precede
  resurrection" and be **witnessed**. The **wrestler** simile (§24): a true champion does not pick
  his own opponent.
- *Why the cross specifically* (§25): to "become a curse" for us (Gal 3:13, "Cursed is every one that
  hangeth on a tree"); the **outstretched arms** draw the "ancient people" with one and the
  Gentiles with the other; the death "in the air" (the devil's sphere, Eph 2:2)
  cleanses the air and "re-opens the road to heaven." He kept his body **whole and undivided** (not
  beheaded like John, not sawn like Isaiah) "so that there should be no excuse… for those who would
  divide the Church."
- *Resurrection proved by present fact* (§§27–32): the **martyrs' and even children's contempt of
  death** ("Death has become like a tyrant… bound hand and foot"), the asbestos/fire simile, and the
  Word's ongoing efficacy — converting, expelling demons, toppling idols — show "this is the work of
  One Who lives, not of one dead."

**Chs. VI–VIII — Refutation of the Jews and of the Gentiles (§§33–55).**
- *Against the Jews* (§§33–40), entirely from their own Scriptures: the virgin birth
  (Isa 7:14), the suffering servant (Isa 53), the piercing of hands and feet (Ps 22), the universal
  hope of the nations (Isa 11:10), and — the "supreme point" — **Daniel's seventy weeks** (Dan 9:24–
  25): since "prophet and vision" and Jerusalem itself have ceased while the nations turn to the God
  of Israel, the Holy One of holies must already have come.
- *Against the Gentiles and Greeks* (§§41–55), on rational and factual
  grounds. If the Word already pervades the universe (itself "a body") and reveals the Father through
  it, there is nothing unfitting in his entering one human body, a part of that whole (§§41–43; the
  "personality actuates the whole body, even the toe" parallel). He came "not to make a display" but
  "to heal and to teach," meeting humanity "according as they could bear it" (§43). The **factual**
  proof (§§46–55): since the Word's coming, **idolatry, oracles, and magic are ceasing**, the Greek
  poets' "gods" are exposed as mortal men, and barbarians "turn from fighting to farming" (Isa 2:4) —
  results no Asclepius, Hercules, or Dionysus, and no philosopher, ever produced.

**Ch. IX — Conclusion (§§56–57).** A second coming "in His proper glory" as Judge; and the
hermeneutical coda (§57): "for the searching and right understanding of the Scriptures there is need
of a good life and a pure soul" — one must "cleanse his own life, and approach the saints by copying
their deeds" to understand them.

## The Theosis Formula (§54)

The treatise contains Athanasius's most quoted sentence, the classic patristic statement of
[[theosis-divine-nature|deification]] framed as an **exchange (the "wonderful exchange")**:

> "He, indeed, assumed humanity that we might become God. He manifested Himself by means of a body
> in order that we might perceive the Mind of the unseen Father. He endured shame from men that we
> might inherit immortality." (§54)

In Athanasius the "becoming God" is unpacked as **incorruption / immortality / sharing the life of
the Word** — soteriological and participatory, the recovery and perfection of the lost Image — not
an ontological merger of essences. See the discussion on [[theosis-divine-nature]].

## Hermeneutical Framework

Athanasius reads Scripture **typologically and prophetically** within a **theology of the Word
(Logos)**: the Old Testament is a "shadow"/"type" pointing forward (§40, "what need was there any
longer of the shadow?" once "the Truth had come"), and the entire created order is itself a kind of
text revealing its Maker. The argument is **apologetic** — proofs "from the Scriptures" against the
Jews and "from reason and from facts" against the Greeks — and the work belongs to the
pre-Nicene **Logos-Christology** tradition ([[logos-christology]]). Notably, the treatise itself
engages **Jews and Gentiles, not [[arianism|Arians]]**: its anti-Arian use is later, and its
Christology is read by later writers as material for the Nicene cause rather than as an explicit
intervention in the controversy.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- *§8–9 — "instrument" (organon) language.* The body as the Word's "instrument" and "temple" is
  characteristic Alexandrian/Athanasian Christology; later readers debate whether it adequately
  secures a complete human **soul/mind** in Christ (the question Apollinarianism would later raise).
  The treatise speaks of the Word animating the body directly and says little of a human rational
  soul — a silence later Christology had to address (cf. [[incarnation]] on "what is not assumed is
  not healed").
- *§4 — "evil is non-being."* The privation account of evil, shared with the Platonic tradition and
  later Augustine (see [[evil-as-privation]]).
- *§9, §25 — the mechanism of "exchange."* How the death of one body avails "for all" (solidarity in
  the Image / physical theory of redemption) versus juridical [[atonement|satisfaction]] models.

## Canon and Reception

A patristic treatise, **authoritative but not scriptural** across the historic churches — Catholic,
Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and the magisterial Protestant traditions all esteem and cite
it. It is foundational for **Eastern theosis** doctrine and is regularly read as a primary witness
in modern ecumenical and devotional contexts (hence the popular Lewis-introduced translation).
