# The Letter of James

The most intensely practical book in the New Testament, and the most controversial of the
"General" (Catholic) Epistles. James is a hortatory letter — it contains a higher frequency of
imperative verbs than any other NT book — addressed to "the twelve tribes scattered among the
nations" (1:1). It is famous (and, in Reformation history, notorious) for its insistence that
"a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24), a statement that stands in
apparent direct contradiction to Paul's "a person is justified by faith apart from works of the
law" (Rom. 3:28). [[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC, 2000) reads the letter as a sustained call to
*spiritual wholeness* — undivided commitment to God against "double-mindedness" (1:8; 4:8) and
"friendship with the world" (4:4) — grounded in the teaching of Jesus and the wisdom traditions
of Hellenistic Judaism.

Moo's PNTC volume is a substantial revision and near-doubling of his earlier Tyndale (TNTC)
commentary (1985). It is sympathetic, theologically integrative, and committed to the
traditional authorship and an early date — positions that put it at odds with the dominant
critical consensus. See [[james-the-just|James the Just]] (the author as figure) and
[[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]]. **Source page (fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18):**
[[moo-james-pntc|Moo, *The Letter of James* (PNTC, 2000)]] — full body re-read with coverage ledger
(~345 grounded claims); no major position inversions vs the 2026-06-06 summary.

## Why James Was Controversial

James "was not so much rejected as neglected" in the early church (Moo). As a "general" letter
with no fixed home congregation, it gained canonical recognition slowly — not firmly received
across both East and West until the fourth century. Origen is the first to cite it by name (early
3rd c.); Eusebius classed it among the *antilegomena* ("disputed books"); Jerome and Augustine
secured its place in the Western canon.

The sharpest challenge came at the Reformation. **Luther** called it "an epistle of straw" (*LW*
35:362), relegated it (with Hebrews, Jude, Revelation) to the end of his NT, and judged that it
"mangles the Scriptures and thereby opposes Paul" — because it appeared silent about, or hostile
to, justification by faith alone, "the doctrine on which the church stands or falls." But Luther
did *not* remove it from the canon and quoted it frequently. **Calvin**, by contrast, accepted
its full apostolic authority and argued that James and Paul could be harmonized ("it is not surely
required for all to handle the same arguments"). Calvin's approach became standard. See
[[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on Justification]].

## Authorship, Date, and Destination

Moo defends the traditional view: the author is **James the Lord's brother** (Gal. 1:19), leader
of the Jerusalem church (Acts 12:17; 15:13; 21:18), writing in the **middle 40s** — possibly the
earliest NT document, predating the Apostolic Council (Acts 15, c. AD 48–49).

The four standard critical objections (and Moo's replies):
1. **No mention of the kinship with Jesus** — but James's authority rested on his spiritual, not
   physical, relation to Jesus; Acts never mentions the kinship either. Argument from silence.
2. **The good Greek** — semiliterary koine, not literary Greek (Ropes); Hengel's work shows
   Palestine was thoroughly Hellenized; Sevenster judged James of Jerusalem capable of writing it.
3. **The "liberal" view of Torah** — the "torah-fanatic" James of later tradition (Hegesippus) is
   tendentious legend; James's silence on the ritual law reflects the letter's occasional purpose,
   not a stance against it.
4. **The relationship to Paul on justification** — the decisive point for dating. See below.

Moo's positive case for an early date rests largely on the justification material: James shows
awareness of the *slogan* "justification by faith alone" but does not engage Paul's actual
meaning — "like ships passing in the night." This *misunderstood Paulinism* is most intelligible
**before** James and Paul hammered out a consensus at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). A later
writer would have known better. The letter also shows no awareness of the Gentile-Torah
controversy that erupted c. AD 47–48. Moo also notes a **decisive objection to the pseudepigraphal
view**: ancient pseudepigraphal letters were not accepted as authentic and authoritative when known
to be forged ("I do not know of a single example"), so the canonical reception of James presumes
its readers believed James wrote it. The "transparent literary device" theory is a
"have-your-cake-and-eat-it-too" position. See [[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]].

**Recipients**: Jewish Christians (they meet in a "synagogue," 2:2; assume monotheism, 2:19, and
the centrality of the law) who have been "dispersed" — probably driven from Palestine by the
persecution of Acts 11:19 — and who face economic oppression at the hands of wealthy landowners
(2:6–7; 5:1–6). Moo reads "diaspora" and "twelve tribes" more literally than figuratively here,
given the Jewish atmosphere and early date — though the phrases also carry the eschatological sense
of the reconstituted people of God of the last days.

## Genre

- **Letter**, but a "literary"/circular one (closest NT parallel: 1 John) — lacking personal
  greetings, travel plans, named individuals.
- **Paraenesis** (Dibelius's influential classification): exhortation, general situation,
  traditional material, loose organization. Moo notes the genre's existence is now doubted, and
  Dibelius over-atomized the letter.
- **Wisdom** (the current favorite): proverbial style, practical admonitions, thematic overlap with
  Proverbs/Sirach. But Moo cautions that "wisdom" is mentioned only twice (1:5; 3:13–18) and is not
  the letter's real topic; only a very broad definition would classify the whole as wisdom. See
  [[wisdom-literature|Wisdom Literature]].
- Best label, for Moo: a **sermon/homily** put into letter form.

A defining feature is its dependence on **the teaching of Jesus** (more than any other NT author —
woven in, not quoted, with the closest parallels in Matthew, esp. the Sermon on the Mount) and on
**Hellenistic Jewish moral tradition** (Sirach, Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, Philo).

## Structure and Theme (Moo)

Moo resists both Dibelius's "chaotic collection" reading and the over-structured
literary/rhetorical schemes (Frankemölle, Davids). He proposes a body (1:2–5:11) in five loose
sections, framed by recurring motifs (testing, poverty/wealth, speech, faith, the law). He titles
the sections around **spiritual wholeness**:

- **1:1** — Address and greeting
- **1:2–18** — The opportunity afforded by trials (joy in testing; wisdom by asking in faith; poor
  and rich; the crown of life; God tests but never tempts; the new birth by the word of truth)
- **1:19–2:26** — The evidence of wholeness: obedience to the word (hearers vs. doers; true
  religion; no partiality / the "royal law"; **saving faith reveals itself in works**)
- **3:1–4:3** — The community dimension: pure speech and peace (the tongue; wisdom from above vs.
  earthly/demonic wisdom; the source of quarrels)
- **4:4–10** — A summons to wholeness (the emotional climax: "you adulteresses")
- **4:11–12** — Critical speech as a violation of the law
- **4:13–5:11** — Understanding time and eternity (arrogant planning; doom of the rich; patient
  endurance)
- **5:12–20** — Concluding exhortations (oaths; prayer and healing; restoring the wanderer)

**Central concern**: not a single "theme" but *spiritual wholeness / integrity* — undivided
loyalty to God. Its opposite is *dipsychia*, "double-mindedness" (1:8; 4:8), and "friendship with
the world" (4:4). The emotional climax (4:4–10), where James abruptly calls his readers
"adulteresses," is the heart of the letter. See [[double-mindedness|Double-Mindedness and Spiritual
Wholeness]].

## Theology (Moo)

James is far more theological than often credited — not christologically (Jesus is named only
twice, 1:1; 2:1) but in its doctrine of **God**, on which all the exhortations are grounded
(Frankemölle). Three divine attributes dominate:
- **Oneness** — God is "single" in purpose (1:5, 17); his undivided constancy is the antidote to
  human double-mindedness. Moo finds an *incipient trinitarianism*: James applies titles and
  functions properly God's (Lord; eschatological Judge) to Jesus (2:1; 5:7–9) without modifying his
  monotheism.
- **Jealousy** — God demands exclusive allegiance (4:5, on Moo's reading; see cruxes).
- **Grace** — "he gives us more grace" (4:6).

Other contributions: **eschatology** (dominant future orientation — the Judge "is standing at the
door," 5:9 — within an inaugurated "already/not yet" frame, 1:18; 2:5; 5:3); **the law** ("the
perfect law of liberty," 1:25; "the royal law," 2:8 — the OT law as fulfilled in and mediated
through Jesus' teaching, internalized per the new-covenant prophecy of Jer. 31:33); **wisdom**
(from above, 3:17 — functioning, per Davids, somewhat as the Spirit does in Paul);
**poverty and wealth** (the OT-Jewish *anawim* tradition; James is a favorite of liberation
theologians, but Moo argues economic and spiritual status do not exactly correlate — there are
believing rich, 1:10; 4:13–17); and **the Christian life** (faith tested in trials; doing not just
hearing; controlled speech; prayer).

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. Faith, works, and justification (2:14–26)** — the central crux. James insists that "faith
without works is dead" (2:17, 26) and "useless" (2:20), and that Abraham (offering Isaac, Gen. 22)
and Rahab (sheltering the spies) were "justified by works." Moo's resolution with Paul: the two
use *dikaioō* ("justify") differently. **Paul**: God's initial declaration of the sinner's
innocence at conversion (forensic, monergistic). **James**: God's ultimate *vindication in the
final judgment*, which takes works into account. "If a sinner can get into relationship with God
only by faith (Paul), the ultimate validation of that relationship takes into account the works
that true faith must inevitably produce (James)." Crucially, James's "faith alone" denotes the
*bogus*, word-only, demonic "faith" (2:19) he is attacking — not Paul's dynamic, obedient faith.
"Works" in James = "works of the law" in Paul (Moo rejects restricting either to ceremonial or
boundary-marker works). Moo rejects the popular evangelical escape route that James's "justify"
merely means "demonstrate/show to be righteous" — the soteriological force (linked to "save," v.
14, and the judgment, vv. 12–13) is inescapable. See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on
Justification]] and [[justification|Justification]].

**2. The tongue (3:1–12)** — the most extended NT treatment of speech-ethics; the tongue as a
fire, a "world of evil," "set on fire by hell," untamable; inconsistency (blessing and cursing
from one mouth) as a mark of double-mindedness.

**3. "You adulteresses" and the jealousy of God (4:4–5)** — Moo reads 4:5 (one of the hardest
verses in the NT) as referring to **God's jealousy** ("God yearns jealously over the spirit he
made to dwell in us"), against the NIV's "the spirit he caused to live in us envies intensely"
(human envy). He concedes the lexical data are genuinely split — grammar favors divine jealousy;
the word *phthonos* (always negative elsewhere) favors human envy — and lets the context (the
spiritual-adultery imagery of v. 4) decide for divine jealousy.

**4. Anointing and healing of the sick (5:14–16)** — physical illness (not spiritual weakness);
the elders anoint and pray. Moo argues the anointing is a **physical act symbolizing
consecration** (setting the sick person apart for God's attention), against medicinal, pastoral,
and sacramental (Roman extreme unction) readings. Healing comes through "the prayer offered in
faith" (v. 15), prayed by the *elders*, not the sufferer — which guards against the cruel
"insufficient-faith" theology of some healing movements. The promise is not unconditional in a
mechanical sense. See [[anointing-healing-james-5|Anointing and Healing of the Sick]].

**5. Oaths (5:12)** — "Above all... do not swear" functions partly as a literary marker of the
letter's conclusion (cf. Paul's *to loipon*), but also highlights integrity of speech. Moo (with
the mainstream, against the Anabaptist total prohibition) reads it as forbidding the *voluntary*
oath, not official/legal oaths; the point (as in Jesus, Matt. 5:34–37, which James reproduces) is
that a Christian's plain "yes/no" should need no reinforcement.

## Relation to Other NT Books

- **Paul (Romans, Galatians)** — the famous justification tension; resolvable on Moo's two-senses
  reading. See [[romans|Romans]], [[galatians|Galatians]], [[justification|Justification]].
- **1 Peter** — many shared traditional motifs (suffering, the *anawim*, dispersion); the
  parallels are common stock, not literary dependence.
- **Matthew / Sermon on the Mount** — the deepest substratum of James's ethics.
- **Jude / 2 Peter** — the other General Epistles with which James shares its "catholic" address
  and slow canonical reception.

## Commentators

- [[douglas-moo|Douglas J. Moo]] — PNTC (Eerdmans, 2000; revising his 1985 TNTC); sympathetic,
  integrative evangelical reading; traditional authorship + early date; "spiritual wholeness" as
  the central concern; two-senses resolution of the James/Paul tension
- Martin Dibelius (rev. Greeven; Hermeneia/Fortress, 1976) — the classic form-critical commentary;
  James as paraenesis; "no theology"; Moo's main foil
- Peter H. Davids (NIGTC, 1982) — redactional layering (Aramaic homilies edited into Greek);
  testing/wisdom/poverty as the three structural themes; wisdom ≈ Spirit
- Luke Timothy Johnson (AB/Doubleday, 1995) — James as deeply theological; Leviticus 19 as the
  letter's ethical template; Hellenistic friendship background to 4:4
- Ralph P. Martin (WBC, 1988) — anti-Zealot, renounce-violence reading of the social situation
- Richard Bauckham (*James*, Routledge, 1999) — James as wisdom teacher / disciple of Jesus the
  sage; declines between Epiphanian and Helvidian views of the kinship
- J. B. Mayor (1913); J. H. Ropes (ICC, 1916); F. Mussner (Herder) — classic critical commentaries
- John Calvin — full apostolic authority; harmonization with Paul (the standard later view)
