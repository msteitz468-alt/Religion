The Psalter (Hebrew: *sēfer tehillîm*, "Book of Praises") is a collection of 150 poems — prayers, hymns, laments, wisdom meditations, royal psalms, and liturgical texts — organized into five books. It is the largest book in the Hebrew Bible and the one most used in both synagogue and church across the history of the two traditions.

The psalms were composed across many centuries, by many different authors and communities, and gathered gradually into sub-collections before reaching their final canonical shape. What distinguishes this commentary's approach: the *ordering* of the psalms is not random but deliberate, and that order tells a story.

## Canon and Textual History

**Five-book structure**: The Psalter is divided into five books by editorial doxologies:

| Book | Psalms | Closing doxology |
|---|---|---|
| Book One | 1–41 | 41:13 ("Blessed be the LORD God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting. Amen and amen.") |
| Book Two | 42–72 | 72:18-19 (+ "the prayers of David son of Jesse are ended," 72:20) |
| Book Three | 73–89 | 89:52 |
| Book Four | 90–106 | 106:48 |
| Book Five | 107–150 | 150:6 (the entire final psalm functions as a doxology) |

The five-book division is ancient: attested in the Dead Sea Scrolls (though with different psalm assignments), in the LXX, and in the Midrash Tehillim (which compares it to Moses's five books of Torah). The doxologies were purposefully shaped and added at roughly the same time; the word "amen" appears in the Psalter only in these doxologies.

**150 psalms — but that number is not as fixed as it appears**: William Yarchin's examination of ~400 medieval MT Psalter manuscripts reveals that only 21% divide the text into the BHS-standard 150-psalm configuration. Most manuscripts present anywhere from 143 to 154 poems. The stabilization at 150 is a product of the printing press and the First/Second Rabbinic Bibles (16th century). The commentary honors the traditional 150 while acknowledging this instability.

**Sub-collections**: The Psalter preserves traces of earlier collections:

| Collection | Psalms | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Davidic | 3–41, 51–72, 108–10, 138–45 | "of David" superscriptions |
| Korahite | 42–49, 84–85, 87–88 | Temple musician guild |
| Elohistic | 42–83 | Uses *Elohim* where parallels use YHWH |
| Asaphite | 73–83 | Temple musician guild |
| Enthronement | 47, 93, 95–99 | "YHWH reigns" theme |
| Songs of Ascents | 120–134 | Pilgrimage liturgy |
| Egyptian Hallel | 113–118 | Passover liturgy |
| Hallelujah | 111–118, 146–150 | *Hallelujah* bracketing |

### Narrative Elements in the Psalter

Although the Psalter lacks a linear plot, it incorporates narrative in two key ways:
1. **Historical Superscriptions**: Thirteen psalms of David (Pss. 3, 7, 18, 34, 51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 59, 60, 63, and 142) contain brief narratives tying the psalm to specific events in David's life. Rather than serving as compositional timestamps, these function as "the earliest commentary," anchoring the prayers in a narrative context.
2. **Historical Psalms**: Psalms 78, 105, and 106 narrate Israel's history—including the Exodus, wilderness wanderings, and conquest—to highlight God's covenant fidelity in contrast to Israel's infidelity.

## The Canonical Shape: Five Books, One Story

The central hermeneutical claim of [[declaisse-walford-jacobson-tanner|deClaissé-Walford, Jacobson, and Tanner]]: the Psalter's five books narrate Israel's history, parallel to the history recorded in Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, and Nehemiah. The ordering is not random — it is a deliberate editorial shaping that tells the story of Israel from David to exile to restoration.

### Book One (Psalms 1–41): The Reign of David

Book One is almost entirely Davidic (39 of 41 psalms bear the *leḏāwiḏ* superscription). It is dominated by lament (59% of psalms). The book opens with Psalms 1-2 as a two-part introduction to the entire Psalter (see below) and closes with *ʾašrê* (happy/blessed) in Psalm 41, forming a bookend with the *ʾašrê* that opens Psalm 1.

The story of Book One is the story of David's life — fraught with conflict from the Philistines, from Saul, from his own family — but sustained by David's unshaken trust in YHWH.

### Book Two (Psalms 42–72): Reign of Solomon

Book Two opens with the Korahite psalms (temple singers from the Davidic-Solomonic court, 42-49) and includes the central Davidic collection (Pss. 51-71, fourteen of which are connected to specific life events of David). It closes with Psalm 72, "of/for Solomon" — a coronation hymn for the new king — and the editorial note "The prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended." Books One and Two together chronicle the rise of the Davidic monarchy; the *ʾašrê* in Psalm 72's final verse forms an inclusio with Psalm 1.

### Book Three (Psalms 73–89): The Divided Kingdoms and Destruction

Book Three is dominated by the voices of Asaph and Korah, the temple musicians whose guilds survived into the exile. David is almost absent (only one Davidic psalm, Ps. 86). The psalms move from crisis to lament to communal desperation.

**Psalm 73** opens the book with a wisdom psalm in the mode of Job — the psalmist sees the wicked prospering and almost loses faith, recovering only in the sanctuary. It signals a turning point: individual theological crisis at the dawn of the divided kingdom period.

**Psalm 88** is the darkest psalm in the entire Psalter — the only lament in the Psalter that ends without any expression of trust or praise. It closes with "darkness is my closest friend." It is not a failure of faith but an act of radical honesty — the Psalter includes even this.

**Psalm 89** is the hinge of the Psalter. It begins with magnificent praise of the Davidic covenant (*ḥesed* of YHWH; "I have sworn to David, I will establish your descendants forever," 89:3-4) and then collapses at v. 38:

> "But now you have rejected and spurned your anointed; you have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the land."

Book Three ends here — with the Davidic covenant apparently broken and the community asking: "Why, O Lord, do you hide yourself? Where are your former mercies?" (89:49). This is the theological crisis of the exile. The rest of the Psalter is the response to it.

### Book Four (Psalms 90–106): The Exile — A New Identity

Book Four is the Psalter's theological answer to Psalm 89's crisis. Its primary moves:

**1. Moses, not David**: Book Four opens with the only Mosaic psalm in the Psalter (Psalm 90, "A Prayer of Moses, the Man of God"). Outside Book Four, Moses is mentioned once in the Psalter (77:20); in Book Four, he appears seven times. When the Davidic monarchy is gone, the Psalter reaches back to the pre-monarchic, pre-temple foundation: Moses and the Exodus.

**2. "YHWH reigns"**: The enthronement psalms of Book Four (93, 95-99) proclaim YHWH as king — not a Davidic king, but YHWH himself enthroned over Israel and all nations. "The LORD is king!" (93:1). This is the editorial community's answer: YHWH was sovereign before David; YHWH remains sovereign without David.

deClaissé-Walford, Jacobson, and Tanner (NICOT 2014) stress that the canonical shape is the key: the Psalter tells the story of Israel's faith from monarchy through exile to a new identity centered on YHWH's kingship and Torah (Ps 1+119 framing). Book 5 moves to return and universal praise. The three co-authors bring canonical, poetic, and theological lenses (Jacobson on rhetoric, Tanner on feminist/embodied reading, deClaissé-Walford on shape).

**3. Confession and petition**: Book Four ends with Psalms 105-106 standing in sequence — 105 recounting God's faithfulness throughout Israel's history; 106 confessing Israel's constant unfaithfulness. The book closes: "Deliver us, O LORD our God, and gather us from the nations, so that we may testify to your holy name." The community is ready to return.

### Book Five (Psalms 107–150): Restoration

Book Five celebrates the community's restoration to the land and establishes YHWH's sovereignty as the permanent new constitution of Israel's identity.

**David returns**: Psalms 108-110, 122, 124, 131, and 138-145 are Davidic. David's voice returns — but now he is leading the whole community in praise rather than crying from individual distress.

**Liturgical collections**: The major liturgical collections of post-exilic Judaism are embedded in Book Five:
- Egyptian Hallel (113-118): Passover liturgy
- Psalm 119: The longest psalm — an acrostic meditation on Torah; *ʾašrê* opens Psalm 119 as well
- Songs of Ascents (120-134): Pilgrimage liturgy for the three annual feasts

**The climax**: Psalm 145 (last Davidic psalm) is an acrostic that calls the whole creation to praise YHWH as king. Then Psalms 146-150 form a five-psalm doxological conclusion, each opening and closing with *Hallelujah*. The Psalter ends in unqualified, universal praise.

**The arc**: From the crisis of Psalm 88 (darkness is my closest friend) and Psalm 89 (the broken covenant) to Psalm 150 (let everything that breathes praise the LORD). This is the Psalter's journey through darkness to doxology.

## Psalm 1 and 2: The Gateway

The two-psalm introduction sets the dual governing frameworks of the entire Psalter:

**Psalm 1** (Torah piety): *ʾašrê* (happy/blessed) is the one who meditates on YHWH's instruction (*tôrâ*) day and night — not following the advice of the wicked but delighting in the Torah. The Psalter announces from the start: these poems are *instruction*, not just worship. They are to be studied, meditated on, and inhabited as a way of life.

**Psalm 2** (Royal piety): The nations rage; YHWH has installed his king on Zion; the nations are warned to "kiss the Son, lest he be angry." The Psalter announces: YHWH's sovereignty is exercised through the Davidic king, whose reign extends to all nations. And the psalm ends: *ʾašrê* (happy/blessed) are all who take refuge in him.

Together: Torah piety + acknowledgment of YHWH's royal sovereignty = the entry point to the Psalter. The tension between these two frameworks (How does YHWH reign without a Davidic king?) is what the five-book structure works out.

## The Psalter as Postexilic Survival Document

DeClaissé-Walford's historical frame: the Psalter achieved final canonical shape as a **constitutive document for postexilic identity**. When other ANE nation-states lost their kingdoms, they disappeared from history. Ancient Israel did not — because it found in its scriptures a new structure for existence. The Psalter's editorial arrangement answered: "Who are we without a king? What are we to do?"

Answer: We are a Torah people (Psalm 1); YHWH is our king (Book Four); David leads us in the poetry of suffering and praise (Books 1-2, 5); the story of our lament and doxology is our identity. This is what Brueggemann calls "the shaping of survival."

## The Poetry of the Psalms

### Hebrew Parallelism

Hebrew poetry does not use end-rhyme or regular meter (unlike most Western poetry). Its defining feature is **parallelism** — the juxtaposition of two or more balanced cola (poetic units). Building on Robert Lowth (1753) and refined by Robert Alter, James Kugel, and Adele Berlin:

- **Within a colon**: "your rod and your staff" (23:4) — two parallel concepts within a single phrase
- **Between cola**: "Sing to the LORD a new song; / sing to the LORD, all the earth" (96:1)
- **Between stanzas**: Psalm 107's four parallel stanzas (vv. 4-9, 10-16, 17-22, 23-32)
- **Between psalms**: "Twin psalms" — 103/104, 105/106, 111/112 are deliberately paired

Kugel's formulation: the second colon is never merely synonymous with the first but always adds, qualifies, or intensifies — "A, and what's more, B." The second line is the more emphatic.

### Poetry as Inseparable from Theology

The commentary's strongest methodological claim: "The meaning of the psalms exists *in, with, and under* the poetic language." This is not decoration around a theological kernel — the evocative power of the language *is* the meaning. Metaphor, imagery, hyperbole, and rhythm are not aids to understanding; they are the form in which the understanding exists. This places the Psalms commentary in a different register from the historical-critical commentaries in the wiki.

## Psalm Types (Form Criticism)

Gunkel's form-critical categories, developed by Mowinckel, remain the standard taxonomy:

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Lament (individual)** | Complaint to God from personal distress; elements: invocation, complaint, petition, expression of trust, vow of praise | Pss. 22, 51, 88 |
| **Lament (communal)** | Same structure but on behalf of the community | Pss. 74, 79, 80 |
| **Hymn of praise** | Imperative call to praise + reason | Pss. 100, 113, 146-150 |
| **Thanksgiving (individual)** | Report of deliverance; testifies to community | Pss. 30, 34, 41 |
| **Royal psalm** | King's installation, battle, or intercessory prayer | Pss. 2, 18, 20, 45, 72, 89, 110 |
| **Wisdom psalm** | Instruction; fear of YHWH; Pss. 1, 73, 119 | Pss. 1, 37, 49, 73, 119 |
| **Enthronement psalm** | "YHWH reigns!" | Pss. 93, 95-99 |
| **Songs of Ascent** | Pilgrimage psalms | Pss. 120-134 |

**The lament-to-praise movement**: 59% of Book One psalms are laments; only 23% of Book Five. The editorial shaping moves the community through grief to doxology. This does not suppress lament — Book Four's Moses psalm still cries out, and Psalm 88 endures — but the direction of travel is toward praise.

## Key Theological Themes

### YHWH as Sovereign King

The dominant theological proposal for the Psalter's center (Wilson, Mays, McCann). The enthronement psalms ("YHWH reigns!") are the editorial answer to the exile's theological crisis. The Psalter as a whole moves from asking YHWH to demonstrate his sovereignty (laments) to celebrating that sovereignty unconditionally (doxologies).

### ḥesed as the Governing Divine Attribute

[[hesed|ḥesed]] (covenant loyalty, steadfast love) is the word that holds the Psalter's theology together even when events seem to contradict it. "Your *ḥesed*, O LORD, fills the earth" (33:5). "Show us your *ḥesed*, O LORD" (85:7). Psalm 89's crisis is precisely that *ḥesed* appears to have failed ("where are your former *ḥesed* acts?" 89:49). Book Five's restoration is framed as YHWH's *ḥesed* vindicated.

### Torah Piety

Psalm 1 and Psalm 119 (the acrostic) bracket a strong instruction about the Psalter's function: it is *Torah* — divine instruction to be meditated on daily. This connects the Psalter directly to Deuteronomy's Mosaic theology and to the wisdom tradition's "fear of YHWH."

### Theodicy in the Psalter

The Psalter does not suppress the theodicy question. Psalm 73 (opening Book Three) echoes Job — the prosperity of the wicked, the suffering of the righteous. Psalm 88 ends in unrelieved darkness. The Psalter's "answer" to theodicy is not intellectual but doxological: the community presses through the darkness of lament until it finds, again, the grounds for praise. This is a different approach from Job's lawsuit and from the Yahweh speeches. See [[theodicy|Theodicy]].

### The Imprecatory Psalms

Several psalms contain explicit prayers for the destruction of enemies (Pss. 69, 109, 137:8-9 — "Happy is the one who seizes your infants and dashes them against the rocks"). These are the most theologically troubling texts in the Psalter for Christian readers. They are genuine prayers, not sanitized. The Psalter includes them because the psalms are the full range of human speech to God — and because honest prayer names the full depth of human rage and pain rather than pretending it does not exist. How to appropriate them in Christian prayer is a live hermeneutical controversy. The NT's use of Ps 22 (from the cross) and Ps 110 (Christological) indicates the psalms were read Christologically early; imprecatory psalms resist easy Christological reading.

## NT Connections

The Psalter is the most-cited OT book in the NT. Key connections:

- **Psalm 2 and Psalm 110**: The two most-cited psalms in the NT. Ps 2:7 ("You are my son; today I have begotten you") is applied to Jesus's resurrection (Acts 13:33), baptism (Luke 3:22), and transfiguration. Ps 110:1 ("The LORD said to my Lord: sit at my right hand") is the basis for Jesus's Lordship argument in the synoptics (Mark 12:36) and the Christological exaltation in Hebrews.
- **Psalm 22**: Quoted by Jesus from the cross ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"); multiple elements (the mockery, the casting of lots) are fulfilled in the passion narrative.
- **Psalm 118:22** ("The stone that the builders rejected has become the capstone"): Applied to the resurrection in all four gospels and Acts.
- **Psalm 51**: The penitential psalm par excellence; the model for Christian confession; Paul cites 51:4 in Romans 3:4 to ground the principle of divine justice.
- **Psalm 23**: The "shepherd psalm" — its imagery of divine care pervades the NT (John 10, Rev 7:17).
- **Heb 3-4**: Quotes Psalm 95 ("Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts") as a warning about the "rest" still available to God's people, interpreted eschatologically.
- **Rev 5:9; 15:3-4**: The "new song" (cf. Ps 98:1) sung by the redeemed before the throne echoes the Psalter's doxological climax.
