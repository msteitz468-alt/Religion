# Malachi

The final book of the [[book-of-the-twelve|Book of the Twelve]] (Minor Prophets)
and, in the Christian ordering, the last book of the Old Testament. A short
post-exilic prophetic book (three chapters in the Hebrew Bible; four in the LXX,
Vulgate, and modern English versions, where ch. 4 is Hebrew 3:19–24) structured as
a series of disputation-speeches rebuking the priests and people of Persian-period
Judah for cultic and covenantal laxity.

The wiki's coverage rests on [[pieter-verhoef]], *The Books of Haggai and Malachi*
(NICOT, 1987) — a grammatical-historical, Reformed evangelical commentary. Claims
below are attributed to Verhoef where interpretive.

## Textual Overview

Malachi opens with a superscription (1:1) and proceeds through seven disputation
pericopes plus a concluding appendix. Verhoef's translation of the heading: **"An
oracle: The word of the Lord to Israel through Malachi."** He reads *maśśāʾ*
("oracle/burden," from *nāśāʾ* "to lift/bear") as retaining a "sense of constraint"
and urgency, and construes the syntax appositionally (with NIV) rather than as a
genitive ("oracle of the word," RSV).

**Structure (Verhoef's seven pericopes + heading + appendix):**

| Unit | Reference | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| Heading | 1:1 | Superscription |
| 1 | 1:2–5 | God's love for Israel (Jacob loved, Esau hated; Edom's ruin) |
| 2 | 1:6–14 | God demands pure offerings (blemished sacrifices rebuked) |
| 3 | 2:1–9 | The priesthood in the balance (covenant with Levi) |
| 4 | 2:10–16 | Mixed marriages and divorce |
| 5 | 2:17–3:5 | The Day of Judgment ("where is the God of justice?"; the messenger of the covenant) |
| 6 | 3:6–12 | Robbing God (tithes and offerings; "test me") |
| 7 | 3:13–21 [Eng. 4:1–3] | Antithesis of righteous and wicked (book of remembrance; sun of righteousness) |
| Appendix | 3:22–24 [Eng. 4:4–6] | Remember the law of Moses; the coming of [[elijah|Elijah]] |

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

- **God's covenant love and Israel's election** (1:2–5). Verhoef stresses the theme
  is *not* the judgment of Edom but God's sovereign, unconditional love for
  [[israelites|Israel]], synonymous "in a sense" with election (*bāḥar*) and
  redemption (*yāšaʿ*). He **rejects reading "Jacob I loved, Esau I hated" as
  predestination to eternal life/damnation** (against Calvin), locating the scope in
  the history of salvation; [[edomites|Edom]] remains within the reach of
  redemptive purpose. Paul cites the text at Rom. 9:13.
- **Cultic purity and the honor due to God** (1:6–14). The priests offer blind,
  crippled, and diseased animals on "the table of the Lord" (a phrase unique to
  Malachi = the altar), treating the ritual as effective *ex opere operato*. God is
  Father and Master whose name is despised.
- **The [[covenant]] with Levi** (2:1–9). See [[levites]]. The ideal priest bore
  "reliable instruction (*tôrâ*) in his mouth" and "is a messenger [*malʾaḵ*] of the
  Lord Almighty" (2:7) — for Verhoef "the noblest statement of the function of
  priesthood... in the Old Testament." The contemporary priests have "annulled the
  covenant with Levi."
- **Marriage, mixed marriage, and divorce** (2:10–16). See [[divorce]]. Verhoef reads
  "have we not all one Father?" (2:10) as a reference to **God** (with Van der Woude),
  grounding Israel's unity; condemns marriage to "the daughter of a foreign god" as
  idolatry; and renders 2:16 with God as subject — **"I hate, I am hating"** divorce.
  "Nowhere else in the OT is there such an elevated view of marriage."
- **The Day of the Lord and the messenger of the covenant** (2:17–3:5; 3:13–4:6). See
  [[day-of-the-lord]] and [[messenger-of-the-covenant]]. The book answers the taunt
  "where is the God of justice?" with a coming judgment, preceded by a forerunner,
  that purifies the [[levites|sons of Levi]] like a refiner's fire and discloses two
  groups — the God-fearing "treasured possession" (*segullah*) written in a "book of
  remembrance," and the arrogant burned like stubble. For those who fear God, "the
  sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings" (3:20 [Eng. 4:2]).
- **Tithing** (3:6–12). "Bring the whole tithe into the storehouse... and test me in
  this." Verhoef treats the unchangeableness of God (3:6) as the ground of Israel's
  survival, and dates the tithe-neglect to Nehemiah's absence (Neh. 13:10).

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

Verhoef judges the MT "well preserved" and "preferable to the ancient versions,"
which he holds have been "unduly emended" by the editors of BHK and BHS. He favors a
handful of minor emendations (e.g., 1:5, 2:10, 2:16) but generally prefers the more
difficult MT reading. Survey of the versions:

- **LXX** — an important witness, generally close to the MT but prone to "verbose
  expansion" and interpretive paraphrase; it reorders the final three verses (23, 24,
  22 [Eng. 5, 6, 4]), "most likely... an attempt to end the book on a less
  threatening note" (see the synagogue haphtarah note below).
- **Peshitta** — presupposes the MT, with LXX and Targum influence.
- **Targum Jonathan** — presupposes the MT with significant later-Jewish paraphrase
  (e.g., rendering *malʾāḵî* at 1:1 as "Ezra the scribe").
- **Vulgate** — a dependable MT witness, often supporting the MT against other
  versions; its singular rendering at Haggai 2:7 underlies the "desire of all nations"
  messianic tradition (see [[haggai]]).
- **Qumran** manuscripts "do not cast any direct light on the MT."

**Chapter division:** the four-chapter division of the translations (ch. 4 = Hebrew
3:19–24) is, in Verhoef's view, "arbitrary."

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

- **The disputation / *Streitreden* form (Verhoef, following Boecker, Wallis,
  Pfeiffer et al.).** Malachi's defining stylistic feature: each pericope follows the
  scheme **statement → question (the addressees' objection) → motivation**. E.g.,
  1:2: "I have loved you" / "In what manner have you loved us?" / "Was not Esau
  Jacob's brother?..." Verhoef doubts the audience literally spoke the objections
  (with Baldwin: "Malachi reads the attitudes of his people and intuitively puts
  their thoughts into words"), and compares the form to the public orator, later
  Mishnaic/Talmudic dialogue, and Paul's proclamation. He defends the style's
  originality against Duhm and Sellin.
- **Redemptive-historical / christological telescoping.** Verhoef reads Malachi's
  eschatology as fulfilled "successively" — centrally at Christ's first coming, finally
  at the second, provisionally through church history — and identifies the messenger
  of 3:1 / Elijah of 4:5 with a "pyramid of forerunners" peaking in
  [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] (attributed; confessional voice).
- **Jewish reception.** Malachi is "the seal of the Prophets" (*ḥōṯam hann'ḇîʾîm*);
  the LXX and later tradition made 3:22–24 the basis of Elijah-as-forerunner
  eschatology; Mal. 3:4–24 is the haphtarah for the "great sabbath."
- **Patristic reception.** Tertullian called Malachi "the skirt and boundary of
  Christianity"; the church fathers largely read *malʾāḵî* as a title, "messenger,"
  rather than a name.

## Canon and Reception

Verhoef: "The canonicity of Malachi has never been disputed." It was counted among
the Twelve by Ben Sira (Sirach 49:10; cf. 48:10 with Mal. 3:24). The NT never names
the prophet or book but quotes and echoes it heavily — most importantly Rom. 9:13
(Mal. 1:2–3); Matt. 11:10 / Mark 1:2 / Luke 1:76; 7:27 (Mal. 3:1); and Matt. 11:14;
17:10–12 / Mark 9:11–12 / Luke 1:17 (Mal. 3:23–24, the Elijah promise). In the
Christian canon Malachi stands last, understood traditionally as "the transition link
between the two dispensations." A synagogue note records that Mal. 3:4–24 is the
haphtarah for the "great sabbath," with the next-to-last verse repeated after the
last because of the closing threat of *ḥērem*.

`canon_scope`: canonical for [[judaism|rabbinic Judaism]] and all
major branches of [[christianity|Christianity]] alike.

## Scholarship and Historiography — as positions

- **The name "Malachi" — proper name or title?** *The* signature crux. Positions
  Verhoef reports:
  - *Title / appellative:* *malʾāḵî* = "my messenger," borrowed from 3:1 and prefixed
    to an originally anonymous book (the LXX "his messenger," the Targum's "Ezra,"
    and most church fathers support a non-name reading); many moderns hold the book
    was anonymous, "Malachi" added to round the Twelve out to twelve.
  - *Proper name:* *b'yaḏ* ("by the hand of") normally takes a proper name; the
    ancient versions (Peshitta, Theodotion, Symmachus, Vulgate) and 2 Esdras treat it
    as a name; the yod may be *yod compaginis* for *malḵî-yāhû*, "messenger of the
    Lord."
  - **Verhoef's own view: a proper name** — "in the absence of compelling arguments to
    the contrary it is logical to accept that the prophet was called Malachi" (with
    Baldwin), though nothing certain is known of the man.
- **Dating.** Consensus: post-exilic (temple standing; a Persian *peḥâ*/governor,
  1:8; abuses paralleling Ezra–Nehemiah). *Where* within the period is disputed:
  - *Before Ezra* (the "very generally accepted view," per Adamson): Malachi shows no
    knowledge of Ezra/Nehemiah or their legislation; some tie this to a pre-"P" date.
  - **Verhoef's preferred position: shortly after 433 BCE, between Nehemiah's two
    visits** (with J. M. P. Smith — the prophecy "fits... as snugly as a bone fits its
    socket"). Reasons: 1:8's *peḥâ* cannot be Nehemiah, who waived the governor's
    income (Neh. 5), pointing to his absence in Susa; the abuses match Nehemiah's
    *second* visit (Neh. 13 — mixed marriages, neglected tithes, priestly corruption);
    the tithe-appeal gains force only after the covenant renewal of Neh. 10.
- **Unity and authenticity.** Essential unity is widely granted (Dentan: product of
  "a single mind"). The critical consensus (Kuhl) treats the mixed-marriage dicta
  (2:11–12) and the Moses/Elijah appendix (3:22–24) as later interpolations. **Verhoef
  rebuts both as "arbitrary,"** holding (with Baldwin) that the appendix "could have
  come from Malachi's hand" and sums up the book's twin themes, "the Law and the
  Prophets, the covenant and the coming Day of the Lord." He does not dogmatically
  declare it original but defends its plausible authenticity.

## Key interpretive cruxes

- **1:11** — "from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is great among the
  nations... a pure offering." Verhoef weighs four readings — (a) present
  Gentile/heathen worship [rejected as contrary to Malachi's theocratic
  exclusiveness]; (b) Jews of the diaspora [partly accepted]; (c) proselytes; (d) the
  messianic/eschatological age — and adopts a synthesis stressing "also": the verse's
  fullness reaches the messianic age when the "wall of partition" between Israel and
  the nations, and between the land and all the earth, is broken (line to Pentecost
  and John 4:23–24).
- **2:15–16** — the divorce crux, "the most difficult pericope"; v. 15a is
  "grammatically and syntactically incomplete." Verhoef reads "residue of spirit" as
  intelligence/sound judgment and renders 2:16 with God as subject: "I hate, I am
  hating" (a participle, continuous). See [[divorce]].
- **3:1** — "my messenger" (a human forerunner, identified with Elijah / John the
  Baptist) vs. "the messenger [angel] of the covenant" (identified with *the Lord*,
  not the forerunner). See [[messenger-of-the-covenant]].
- **3:20 [Eng. 4:2]** — the "sun of righteousness with healing in its wings," "an
  exegetical labyrinth"; Verhoef takes "righteousness" as the key word and "sun" as
  its definition, with Wellhausen's winged sun-disk (Shamash) as background imagery
  transformed — "under the wings of Malachi's sun no violence... but healing."
