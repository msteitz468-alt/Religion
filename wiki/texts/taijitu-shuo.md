# Taijitu shuo — Discussion of the Supreme Polarity Diagram

The **Taijitu shuo** (太極圖說, "Discussion of the Supreme Polarity Diagram") is a brief cosmological
text of approximately 250 Chinese characters by [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] (1017–1073), explaining the
visual diagram known as the **Taijitu** (Diagram of the Supreme Polarity). It is the foundational
cosmological text of the [[neo-confucianism|Daoxue/Neo-Confucian]] tradition as organized by
[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]].

## Overview

The text is extraordinarily short — one printed page — but extraordinarily consequential. It traces
a cosmogonic sequence from the undifferentiated ultimate through yin-yang polarity, the Five Phases,
Heaven and Earth, and the generation of the ten thousand things, culminating in the emergence of
human beings with their moral nature. In [[adler-reconstructing-confucian-dao|Adler's analysis]]
(2014), the text is best read as a compressed statement of Zhou Dunyi's cosmological framework for
his longer and more practically-oriented [[tongshu|Tongshu]].

## Structure and Content (Adler's Section Designations)

**Section A (opening paradox):**  
> "Wuji er taiji!" — "**Nonpolar and yet Supreme Polarity!**"

The opening line is deliberately paradoxical: the ultimate is *wuji* (无極, formless, undifferentiated,
without polarity) AND *taiji* (太極, the Supreme Polarity — the principle from which all bipolarity
proceeds). [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] interpretation: *taiji* is *li* (principle), which has no physical
form or location (*wuji*) and yet is the very ordering principle of everything.

**Section B (activity-stillness cycle):**  
> "Taiji in activity generates yang; at the limit of activity it is still. In stillness it generates
> yin; at the limit of stillness it is also active. Activity and stillness alternate; each is the
> basis of the other."

This is the central cosmological statement: yin and yang are not substances but phases generated by
the cycling of the *taiji* between activity and stillness. The phrase "each is the basis of the
other" points toward the **interpenetration** of yin and yang developed in [[tongshu|Tongshu
section 16]].

**Section C–D (Five Phases):**  
The Two Modes (yin and yang) generate the Five Phases (Water, Fire, Wood, Metal, Earth) through
their interactions. The Five Phases are the "five qi" whose "harmonious distribution" constitutes the
four seasons.

**Section E–F (cosmological completion):**  
"The Five Phases are the unitary yin and yang; yin and yang are the unitary Supreme Polarity; the
Supreme Polarity is fundamentally Nonpolar." The sequence is reversible: from the many to the one.

**Section G (humanity and virtue):**  
> "Only human beings receive the finest and most spiritually efficacious [endowment]. With physical
> form they are born, and their spirit produces intelligence. Their fivefold nature is stimulated to
> activity, and good and evil are distinguished, and the ten thousand affairs emerge."

The sage "establishes the Mean of humanity and rightness" and practices **zhu jing** (主靜,
"emphasizing stillness") — a phrase that troubled many Confucians as sounding Buddhist or Daoist.
Zhu Xi re-interpreted *zhu jing* via Cheng Yi's concept of *jing* (reverent composure), arguing it
was not passive quietism but active moral presence.

**Sections H–J (sagehood and Heaven-earth triad):**  
The sage forms a triad with Heaven and Earth; Heaven generates through yang and fulfills through yin;
the Way of the sage is to determine through humanity and rightness. "Is it not right to call [the
*yi* principles of] the sage and the virtues of Heaven-and-earth — **excellent!**"

## The Taiji Diagram

The visual diagram that this text explains is a vertical sequence of five interlocking circles
showing: (top) the Nonpolar/Supreme Polarity circle → the yin-yang division circle → the Five Phases
circle → the generation circle → (bottom) the ten thousand things circle.

**Daoist origins**: Most modern scholars accept that the diagram was received from Daoist sources —
specifically the chain: **Chen Tuan** (d. 989) → **Chong Fang** (965–1015) → **Mu Xiu** (979–1032)
→ Zhou Dunyi's circles. Multiple similar diagrams appear in the Daoist Canon (compiled in the Ming
dynasty). This chain was first recorded by Zhu Zhen (1072–1138) in 1134.

Zhu Xi denied this origin to protect Zhou Dunyi's position in the [[daotong|daotong]] as the authentic
Confucian transmitter. He argued — on the basis of Pan Xingsi's epitaph — that Zhou composed the
diagram himself. Modern scholarship largely rejects this position.

**The popular yin-yang swirl**: The familiar circular yin-yang symbol (*taijitu* in popular usage)
is NOT Zhou Dunyi's diagram. It first appears in a c. 1370 text by Zhao Huiqian — nearly 300 years
after Zhou.

## Zhu Xi's Commentary and Interpretation

Zhu Xi's commentary on the *Taijitu shuo* (written c. 1173–1188) is the standard reading lens through
which the text has been received:

1. He equated *taiji* with *li* (principle) — the most philosophically consequential move.
2. He placed the *Taijitu shuo* at the **head** of Zhou Dunyi's collected works (reversing the
   manuscript order, which had the diagram after the *Tongshu*), arguing the diagram was the
   "essential idea" that the *Tongshu's* words explained.
3. He resolved the apparent contradiction between *wuji* (Nonpolar) and *taiji* (Supreme Polarity)
   by reading *wuji* as describing the formlessness and omnipresence of *li*, not as a separate
   concept.
4. He reinterpreted *zhu jing* (emphasizing stillness) as equivalent to Cheng Yi's *jing* (reverent
   composure), defusing the Buddhist/Daoist resonance.

## Controversies

1. **The wuji problem**: Lu Jiuyuan argued *wuji* was a Daoist term (*Laozi* ch. 28) and should not
   appear in a Confucian cosmology; his exchange with Zhu Xi on this point is the most famous
   Neo-Confucian philosophical dispute.
2. **Zhu jing (emphasizing stillness)**: Is this Daoist quietism or legitimate Confucian cultivation?
   Zhu Xi's re-reading of it through Cheng Yi is an interpretive decision, not a neutral description.
3. **Diagram authorship**: Zhou's own composition vs. Daoist transmission.
4. **Relation to the *Tongshu***: Does the cosmological diagram come first (Zhu Xi's ordering) or was
   the *Tongshu* the primary text and the diagram supplementary?

## Canon and Reception

The *Taijitu shuo* is not "canonical" in the sense of being scripture, but it is **authoritative** for
the Cheng-Zhu school as the cosmological ground of its metaphysics. Zhu Xi incorporated it as the
first item in his *Jinsilu* (Reflections on Things at Hand, compiled 1175 with Lü Zuqian) — the
standard introduction to Daoxue thought. It became the basis for civil service examination content
after Zhu Xi's system was institutionalized (Yuan dynasty, 1313).
