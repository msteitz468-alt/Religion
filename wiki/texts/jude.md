# The Letter of Jude

A single-chapter (25-verse) letter, the "most neglected" book of the New Testament (Rowston), and
one of its most distinctive: an impassioned, rhetorically crafted summons to "contend for the faith
that was once for all delivered to the saints" (v. 3) against intruding false teachers, argued almost
entirely through a chain of Old Testament and **Jewish apocryphal** examples of judgment. Jude is
famous — and was, in antiquity, controversial — for **quoting 1 Enoch** (1.9, in vv. 14–15) and
alluding to the **Assumption (Testament) of Moses** (the dispute of Michael and the devil over Moses'
body, v. 9). [[gene-green|Gene L. Green]]'s BECNT commentary (2008; fidelity re-ingest [[green-jude-2peter-becnt|source page]], 2026-07-18) defends its authenticity, dates
it to the late 50s–early 60s, locates it in **Palestinian Jewish Christianity**, and reads its
opponents as first-century antinomians, not second-century gnostics. Line loci below refer to the
ebook conversion of Green used for that re-ingest.

## Textual Overview

Twenty-five verses in a single chapter. Form: Hellenistic letter opening (author, recipients, greeting, vv. 1–2) plus a Christian closing **doxology** (vv. 24–25) instead of standard Greco-Roman/Semitic closings. Green classifies the whole as a genuine **letter of exhortation**, not a synagogue homily retrofitted with epistolary ends (against reducing it to pure deliberative/forensic oratory à la Watson). Body structure (Ellis/Bauckham midrash model Green adopts): series of authoritative “texts” (OT, Pseudepigrapha, apostolic prophecy) plus “these are” comments (vv. 8, 10, 12, 16, 19), bracketed by “remember” (vv. 5, 17).

## Authorship

The author identifies himself as "Jude, a slave of Jesus Christ and brother of James" (v. 1). Green
identifies him as [[jude-brother-of-jesus|Jude, the brother of James and of Jesus]] — one of the
"brothers of the Lord" (Matt. 13:55; Mark 6:3), self-identifying by reference to the prominent
[[james-the-just|James]] (shared family honor) rather than claiming apostleship or the kinship to
Jesus directly (L993–L999). The early church largely accepted this identification, though some confused him with
Jude the apostle, with Thaddaeus, or even with Thomas (Syrian tradition; L1001–L1003). On the kinship question Green prefers the **Helvidian** reading (half-brothers of Jesus) as most accessible; Epiphanian is “possible but not probable” and “distinctly theological” (L1011).

**Authenticity (Green's defense):** The contemporary objections — the polished Greek (could a
Galilean write it?), the apparent backward look at "the apostles" (v. 17), and the identity of the
heretics — are answerable. The Greek is explicable by **secretarial assistance** (L1029–L1031); v. 18 shows the readers themselves *heard* the apostles, so the apostolic era is
not distant (L1033); and the heresy fits first-century Christianity — “no hint… of later gnostic cosmology… Jude’s perspective is apocalyptic and not gnostic” (L1035). Ancient doubts about Jude attached *only*
to its use of apocryphal sources, never to its authorship (L1015–L1017). Date: *terminus a quo* late 40s; *terminus ad quem* mid-60s → **“latter part of the 50s or the first half of the 60s”** (L1081–L1083).

## Provenance and Audience

Green argues for **Palestinian Jewish Christianity** (against Syria and against Gunther's Alexandria),
on the strength of: the unqualified reference to James; the association of Jesus' relatives with Palestine (Hegesippus/Eusebius on Domitian and Jude’s grandsons); Jude's **midrashic/pesher exegesis** (Bauckham via Green: most elaborate NT passage of Qumran-pesher-style formal exegesis); and assumption that readers know the Enochic and Mosaic traditions (L1057–L1059). Syria falls hard on Jude’s exclusion from the Syrian canon until Philoxenus (L1045–L1047); Alexandria lacks a compelling non-Carpocratian case once gnostic ID is rejected (L1049–L1055). Audience likely included Hellenistic Jews and Gentiles within the Palestinian sphere (hence Greek; L1061–L1069).

## The Opponents

Intruders who "crept in unnoticed" (v. 4), recognized as disciples (they share the church's
**love-feasts**, v. 12), but whose teaching and conduct deny "our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ":
- **Antinomian**: they pervert the doctrine of **grace** into a license for immorality (v. 4) — a
  misappropriation of Pauline teaching (cf. Rom. 6:1, 15), so the denial is *practical*, not (as in 2
  Peter) doctrinal-Christological.
- **Libertine/sexual**: a recurring emphasis (vv. 4, 7–8, 16, 18).
- **Reject authority and "the glories" (angels)** (v. 8) — possibly because angels were understood as
  mediators of the divine law they spurn.
- **Claim dream-revelations** (v. 8) over against received apostolic tradition.
Green stresses these are *not* gnostics: there is no spirit/matter dualism, no salvation-by-*gnōsis*;
Jude's frame is **apocalyptic**, not gnostic.

## Structure (Green)

- **vv. 1–2** — Epistolary greeting
- **vv. 3–23** — Body: an exhortation to contend for the faith
  - vv. 3–4 — Disclosure of purpose (the letter Jude did *not* intend to write: he meant to write of
    "our common salvation" but must instead urge defense of the faith)
  - vv. 5–19 — A call to remember: types and prophecies of judgment on the intruders
    - vv. 5–7 — three OT examples (unbelieving Israel; the fallen angels of Gen. 6 / 1 Enoch; Sodom)
    - vv. 8–10 — the dispute over Moses' body (Michael vs. the devil; Assumption of Moses)
    - v. 11 — the "way of Cain… Balaam… Korah" (three archetypal rebels)
    - vv. 12–13 — vivid images of the intruders (waterless clouds, fruitless trees, wild waves,
      wandering stars)
    - vv. 14–15 — the prophecy of **Enoch** (1 Enoch 1.9)
    - vv. 16–19 — the apostles' prediction of last-days scoffers, fulfilled in the intruders
  - vv. 20–23 — Exhortations to the beloved: build yourselves up, pray, keep yourselves in God's love;
    show mercy to the wavering; "snatch others from the fire"
- **vv. 24–25** — Closing doxology ("Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling…") — one of
  the best-known passages in the NT

## Rhetorical and Narrative Typologies

Jude employs a highly sophisticated **"Text and Comment" midrashic structure**, presenting ancient narratives of rebellion and judgment ("Text") and immediately applying them typologically to the intruding false teachers ("Comment"). 

His argument relies on several key narrative sequences:
1. **The Three Rebellions (vv. 5-7)**: Jude cites the **Exodus generation** (saved from Egypt but destroyed for unbelief in Num 14), the **rebellious angels** (the Watchers of Gen 6 / 1 Enoch who abandoned their proper dwelling), and **Sodom and Gomorrah** (Gen 19, destroyed for unnatural sexual vice). These three narratives function typologically to show that initial privilege does not exempt one from catastrophic judgment if followed by apostasy.
2. **The Dispute Over Moses' Body (vv. 8-10)**: Referencing the pseudepigraphal *Assumption of Moses*, Jude contrasts the humility of **[[michael-archangel|Michael the Archangel]]**—who would not pronounce a slanderous judgment even against the devil—with the arrogance of the false teachers who blaspheme celestial powers.
3. **The Woe Triad (v. 11)**: Jude creates a composite archetype of rebellion by linking **[[cain|Cain]]** (murderous jealousy and false religion), **[[balaam|Balaam]]** (prophetic greed and leading Israel into sexual immorality), and **[[korah|Korah]]** (insubordinate rebellion against God's appointed leaders). The intruders are characterized as walking the exact same narrative path toward the same destruction.

## Key Interpretive Crux: The Use of Apocryphal Literature

Jude's **quotation of 1 Enoch 1.9** (vv. 14–15: "Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied…") and his
**allusion to the Assumption/Testament of Moses** (v. 9) are the letter's defining feature and the
sole basis of ancient doubts about its canonicity. On the 2026-07-18 fidelity re-read, Green’s load-bearing claims include:

- Jude does **not** treat the Enoch “text” as less authoritative than prior texts (OT examples, Michael tradition); “Nothing in his language or in the context of the book suggests that he viewed this ‘text’ as anything other than authoritative” (digest R3; cf. intro L1159–L1161).
- Quotation formula (`Προεφήτευσεν`) parallels NT introductions of OT prophets; Green rejects J. Charles’s generalized “prophesied” and the opponents-held-Enoch thesis, and places Jude outside Paul’s incidental classical citations (Acts 17:28; 1 Cor 15:33; Titus 1:12).
- Jude reworks the Greek 1 En. 1.9 (knew it “very well”) with christological “Lord” = Christ’s parousia; Green rejects Bauckham’s pure Aramaic-translation hypothesis while admitting some Aramaic traces.
- Mitigating: judicious use tied to **canonical bedrock** (Gen 6; Deut 33–34; Zech 3); 1 En. 1’s motifs themselves interpret Scripture. Green ends near Augustine’s both/and rather than a closed theory. See [[jude-1-enoch-canon|Jude's Use of 1 Enoch and the Canon Question]].

### Other interpretive cruxes (Green)

- **v. 5 “Jesus” vs “Lord”**: Green favors the harder reading that *Jesus* once saved the people from Egypt (weighty MS evidence; Additional Notes) — a high Christology applied to the exodus.
- **vv. 22–23**: Green prefers the **𝔓72 two-clause** form for the mercy/snatch-from-fire instructions (not a three-way split of audiences); focus is disputers/waverers, not the hardened heretics.
- **Love-feasts (v. 12)**: heretics as “stains” at the community meal — specific situational charge, not mere *vituperatio* commonplace.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

- **Midrash / pesher-style text-and-comment** (Green following Bauckham/Ellis): contemporizing application of authoritative texts to present heretics (“these are”).
- **Apocalyptic** judgment typology (not gnostic dualism).
- **Epistolary + limited rhetorical analysis**: letter of exhortation; Green resists reducing structure to Greco-Roman speech genres alone (L1213–L1227).
- **Sociology of honor / *vituperatio***: “these are” vs “but you” boundary-drawing; conventional vice lists applied with case-specific content.
- Modern critical dialogue partners: Bauckham, Fornberg, Neyrey, Watson, J. Charles, Kelly, M. Green.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

Green works from standard critical editions (UBS4/NA27) with per-unit **Additional Notes**. Load-bearing variants he discusses include Jude 5 (Ἰησοῦς vs κύριος; position of ἅπαξ), v. 15 (addition of λόγων), and vv. 22–23 (𝔓72 two-clause preference). Ancient reception: Muratorian Canon; Clement’s commentary; Origen/Athanasius acceptance; Eusebian *antilegomena*; Syriac Peshitta exclusion until Philoxenus 507/508; Jerome’s “rejected by many… by age and use” (L1015–L1019).

## Scholarship and Historiography

Positions on authorship/date range mid-50s to mid-second century (L1079). Green’s package (authentic Jude; first-century antinomians; Palestinian provenance; used by 2 Peter) yields late 50s–early 60s. Critical alternatives (pseudonymity; Carpocratian/Alexandrian second-century setting; Syrian destination) are recorded as engaged positions, not adjudicated in wiki voice. On pseudepigrapha use, see controversy page.

## Theology and Significance

Jude is a window into **non-Pauline, Palestinian Jewish Christianity** and its **apocalyptic**
hermeneutic. Its theology is structured around divine **judgment** (typological certainty that God
judges the ungodly) balanced by confidence in God's **keeping** power (vv. 1, 24). "The faith once
for all delivered" (v. 3) treats the gospel as a fixed, defensible deposit — sometimes read as a mark
of "early Catholicism," but Green sees it as continuous with Paul's appeals to received tradition (2
Thess. 2:15). The doxology (vv. 24–25) has had an outsized liturgical afterlife.

## Canon and Reception

Accepted early in the West and Alexandria (Muratorian Canon; Clement of Alexandria wrote a commentary
on it; Origen and Athanasius accepted it), but slow in **Syria** (excluded from the Peshitta; added
only in the Philoxenian version, AD 507/508). Eusebius classed it among the *antilegomena* ("disputed
books"). The doubts traced *only* to its apocryphal sources (Jerome: "because… it quotes from the
apocryphal book of Enoch it is rejected by many… nevertheless by age and use it has gained
authority"). It was received into the 27-book canon by the 4th century and is today canonical for
[[catholicism|Catholicism]], [[eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern Orthodoxy]], and [[protestantism|Protestantism]].
See the [[jude-1-enoch-canon|Jude and 1 Enoch controversy]].

## Relation to Other Books

- **2 Peter** — 2 Peter reuses much of Jude (esp. Jude ≈ 2 Pet. 2). Green holds 2 Peter depends on
  Jude (*imitatio*), making Jude the earlier and the more honored. See
  [[2-peter-jude-relationship|The Relationship between 2 Peter and Jude]].
- **James** — its sibling letter in the family-of-Jesus corpus; both authors self-identify as
  "slaves" without trading on the kinship. See [[james|James]], [[james-the-just|James the Just]].
- **1 Enoch / Assumption of Moses** — the apocryphal sources; cf. the use of 1 Enoch behind 1 Pet.
  3:18–22 (the fallen-angel tradition). See [[spirits-in-prison-1-peter-3|Christ and the Spirits in
  Prison]] and [[apocalyptic-literature|Apocalyptic Literature]].
