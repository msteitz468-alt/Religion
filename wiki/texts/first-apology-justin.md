# First Apology of Justin Martyr

A formal petition addressed "To the Emperor Titus Aelius Adrianus Antoninus Pius Augustus Caesar, and to his son Verissimus the Philosopher, and to Lucius the Philosopher... and to the sacred Senate, with the whole People of the Romans" (Ch. I). Written c. 155–160 CE by [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr]] (c. 100–165 CE) in Greek, it is the earliest surviving sustained Christian apology addressed to the Roman imperial court. The translation used here is Philip Schaff's (*Apostolic Fathers with Justin Martyr and Irenaeus*, Eerdmans, repr. 2001).

## Canon and Reception

The First Apology is not canonical scripture in any tradition. As a patristic theological text it is treated as authoritative by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant traditions for understanding early Christian life, doctrine, and worship — but not as inspired scripture. Its importance is as a primary witness to 2nd-century Christian practice.

## Overview and Structure

The work is organized in 68 chapters across four functional movements:

### 1. The Apologetic Case (Chs I–XX)
Justin opens by demanding judicial fairness: Christians are condemned for their name alone, not for proven crimes (Chs I–VII). He then addresses the two major charges:

**Atheism** (Chs V–VI): Christians are called atheists for refusing to worship the Roman gods. Justin concedes this in a limited sense — "we confess that we are atheists, so far as gods of this sort are concerned" — but argues Christians worship "the most true God, the Father of righteousness and temperance and the other virtues, who is free from all impurity." They also worship "the Son (who came forth from Him and taught us these things), and the host of the other good angels who follow and are made like to Him, and the prophetic Spirit" (Ch. VI) — an early triadic formula.

**Immorality** (Chs XXVII–XXIX): The charges of incest and cannibalism are denied by appeal to Christian sexual ethics (chastity, avoidance of prostitution, condemnation of child exposure) and the rationality of Christian worship.

Justin argues that Christians are ideal subjects: they pay taxes, obey civil authorities (Ch. XVII, citing "Render to Caesar..."), and avoid wickedness because they believe in divine judgment (Chs XI–XII).

### 2. Christological Analogies to Paganism (Chs XXI–XXVI)
Justin argues that Christian claims about Jesus are analogous to pagan claims about sons of Zeus — Mercury the interpreting word, Asclepius the healer, Bacchus torn limb from limb, Hercules who committed himself to flames, Perseus born of a virgin, Bellerophon who ascended to heaven (Ch. XXI). If pagans accept these without scandal, they should accept the claims about Jesus. Justin's point is rhetorical parity, not theological equivalence.

He names two active heretical figures as evidence that the demons continue to mislead:
- **Simon of Samaria** (Ch. XXVI): a native of Gitta, who performed magic in Rome under Claudius, was honoured with a statue inscribed "Simoni Deo Sancto" (To Simon the Holy God), and whose associate **Helena** is described as "the first idea generated by him." Simon's disciple **Menander of Capparetaea** also performed magic in Antioch. See [[simon-magus|Simon Magus]].
- **Marcion of Pontus** (Ch. XXVI): "even at this day alive, and teaching his disciples to believe in some other god greater than the Creator." See [[marcion|Marcion]].

### 3. Argument from Fulfilled Prophecy (Chs XXX–LIII)
The longest section, and Justin's main positive argument. He contends that the Hebrew prophets foretold everything about Jesus centuries in advance, which proves Christianity is true and that prior pagan imitations of these prophecies were demonic anticipation:

- **The Shiloh prophecy** (Gen 49:10–11) — sceptre not departing from Judah until He comes, binding foal to vine, washing robe in blood of grape: Justin reads this as predicting the Triumphal Entry and the Crucifixion (Ch. XXXII).
- **Virgin birth** (Isa 7:14, Ch. XXXIII): "Behold, a virgin shall conceive" — Justin argues the power of God overshadowed her "not by intercourse, but by power." He identifies the Holy Spirit (= the prophetic Spirit) with the Logos, "the first-born of God."
- **Bethlehem** (Mic 5:2, Ch. XXXIV): verifiable, Justin says, from Roman census records under Cyrenius.
- **Passion prophecies** (Chs XXXV, XXXVIII, L): including Ps 22:16–18 ("They pierced my hands and my feet"), Isa 53 (the Suffering Servant), and Isa 65:2 ("I have spread out my hands to a disobedient and gainsaying people").
- **Crucifixion past tense** (Ch. XLII): Justin explains why prophets use past tense for future events — God foreknows what He will certainly bring to pass, and speaks from that certainty.
- **Free will vs. fate** (Chs XLIII–XLIV): Fulfilled prophecy does not imply fatalism. God foreknows; humans still choose freely. Without free will, there would be no ground for reward or punishment.
- **Logos as pre-existent Word and Angel** (Ch. LXIII): The Angel who spoke to Moses from the burning bush was the Son, not the Father. "Jesus the Christ is the Son of God and His Apostle, being of old the Word, and appearing sometimes in the form of fire, and sometimes in the likeness of angels."

### 4. Demonic Mimicry of Christianity (Chs LIV–LXIV)
Justin argues that the demons, having heard the Hebrew prophecies in advance, constructed pagan myths as pre-emptive counterfeits:
- Bacchus (from the Shiloh prophecy's reference to vine and wine) → demonic anticipation of the Eucharist vine symbolism
- Bellerophon and Perseus → demonic anticipation of the virgin birth and heavenly ascent
- Hercules and Asclepius → demonic anticipations of the miracles

Plato's ideas also derived from Moses (Ch. LIX): "God, having altered matter which was shapeless, made the world" = Genesis 1. The Platonic "second power placed crosswise in the universe" (*Timaeus*) derived from Moses' bronze serpent (Num 21) — which Plato misread as a "placing crosswise" rather than as a cross symbol (Ch. LX). Plato's "third" (around the third) derives from "the Spirit of God moved over the waters" (Gen 1:2).

### 5. Sacramental Descriptions (Chs LXI–LXVII)

**Baptism (Ch. LXI)**: Candidates pray and fast for remission of past sins; the community prays and fasts with them. They are brought to water and washed "in the name of God, the Father and Lord of the universe, and of our Saviour Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit." The rite is called "illumination" (*phōtismos*) because the baptized are "illuminated in their understandings." Theological rationale: "Since at our birth we were born without our own knowledge or choice... in order that we may not remain the children of necessity and of ignorance, but may become the children of choice and knowledge." The name of the ineffable Father is not uttered; the baptizer says "in the name of" without pronouncing a personal name of God.

**Eucharist (Chs LXV–LXVI)**: The newly baptized is brought to the assembly. Prayers in common; the "kiss of peace." Bread and a cup of wine mixed with water are brought to the "president of the brethren" (*proestos*). He offers prayers and thanks "at considerable length" (*epi polú*); all say "Amen" (explained as the Hebrew equivalent of "so be it"/ *genoito*). Deacons distribute to those present and carry portions to the absent. The food is called **Eucharistia**: "not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word, and from which our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh." Justin then cites the institution narrative from "the memoirs composed by the apostles, which are called Gospels" (implying he knows the term "Gospel" for the texts): "This is my body" / "This is my blood." The Mithraic bread-and-cup rite is cited as demonic imitation (Ch. LXVI). See [[eucharist|Eucharist]].

**Sunday Worship (Ch. LXVII)**: The most detailed early account of a full Christian Sunday assembly. "On the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts to the imitation of these good things. Then we all rise together and pray, and... bread and wine and water are brought, and the president in like manner offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability, and the people assent, saying Amen." Collection is taken; the president distributes to orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners, strangers. Sunday is chosen because it is the day of creation (Gen 1:1) and of the Resurrection. The "memoirs of the apostles" (*apomnēmoneumata tōn apostolōn*) are read alongside "the writings of the prophets" at the same liturgical level — a key witness to the functional scriptural status of the Gospels in mid-2nd century Rome.

### Appended Imperial Documents
Three letters are appended, of unequal authenticity:
- **Epistle of Hadrian to Minucius Fundanus** (genuine; c. 124 CE): requires legal procedure before Christians can be punished; calumnious accusers to face heavier penalties.
- **Epistle of Antoninus to the Common Assembly of Asia** (authenticity disputed): scolding Asia for persecuting Christians who remain calm during earthquakes; God not threatened by those who refuse worship.
- **Letter of Marcus Aurelius to the Senate** (widely considered spurious): claims Christian soldiers' prayer brought rain during a military crisis, recommending clemency.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Logos theology**: Justin's most distinctive contribution. The Logos is "the first-birth of God," numerically distinct from the Father, through whom God made all things. "Those who lived reasonably are Christians, even though they have been thought atheists; as, among the Greeks, Socrates and Heraclitus, and men like them; and among the barbarians, Abraham, and Ananias, and Azarias, and Misael, and Elias" (Ch. XLVI). This identification of Christ with the Logos implanted as a "seed" in every human (*logos spermatikos*) is fully developed here. See [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]].

**Triadic formulae**: Justin consistently pairs Father, Son/Logos, and prophetic Spirit as objects of Christian worship and theological reference (Chs VI, XIII, LXI, LXV), though without systematizing their relationship. His subordinationism (the Son "in the second place, and the prophetic Spirit in the third," Ch. XIII) anticipates later trinitarian debates.

**Hermeneutical method**: Justin reads the OT exclusively as predictive prophecy fulfilled in Christ — a typological-prophetic hermeneutic (*derash* in the broad sense; fulfillment/allegorical in Christian terms). He explicitly addresses different voices in prophetic speech: the Father, the Son, the people, the Spirit (Ch. XXXVI). Prophecy in past tense is read as divine foreknowledge expressed with certainty (Ch. XLII).

**The "memoirs of the apostles"**: Justin uses *apomnēmoneumata* for the Gospels (Chs LXVI, LXVII), a term drawn from Xenophon's *Memorabilia* of Socrates, presenting the Gospels as respectable literary equivalents of the best pagan philosophical memoirs. His citations across both Apologies and the *Dialogue with Trypho* indicate knowledge of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and possibly John.

## Hermeneutical Framework

**Prophetic-typological** (Christian fulfillment reading): The entire Hebrew Bible is read as forward-pointing prophecy. Key interpretive moves:
- Prophecy spoken in the past tense predicts future certainties (Ch. XLII)
- Prophetic speakers alternate among Father, Son, Spirit, and people — the reader must discern who is speaking (Ch. XXXVI)
- Pagan analogies are demonic pre-emptions of prophecy, not independent parallels

**Apologetic rationalism**: Justin appeals consistently to reason (*logos*), justice, and philosophical analogy. He presents Christianity as the true philosophy, fulfilling and surpassing Greek wisdom.
