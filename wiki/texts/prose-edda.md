The **Prose Edda** (or Younger Edda) is a handbook of Norse mythology and poetics written c. 1220 by the
Icelandic chieftain, historian, and poet **Snorri Sturluson** — the most **systematic single account** of
Norse myth, and the indispensable companion to the [[poetic-edda|Poetic Edda]].

## Content

- **Prologue** — Snorri (a Christian) frames the gods euhemeristically, as deified human migrants from Troy.
- **Gylfaginning** ("The Beguiling of Gylfi") — the heart of the book: a king questions three disguised gods
  and is told the **whole mythology in order** — the [[ymir|creation from Ymir]], [[yggdrasil|Yggdrasil]] and
  the Nine Worlds, the deeds of [[odin|Odin]], [[thor|Thor]], and [[loki|Loki]], the **death of
  [[baldr|Baldr]]**, and **[[ragnarok|Ragnarök]]** and the world's rebirth.
- **Skáldskaparmál** ("The Language of Poetry") — myths explaining the **kennings** (poetic metaphors), incl.
  the origin of the mead of poetry and Thor's duel with the giant Hrungnir.
- **Háttatal** — a catalogue of verse-forms.

## Significance

Because Snorri **arranged the scattered myths into a connected narrative**, the Prose Edda is the single
clearest window onto [[norse-religion|Norse religion]] — most popular retellings descend from it. But its
**Christian author, late date, and the prologue's euhemerism** make it a *secondary, interpreted* source to
be read alongside the older [[poetic-edda|Poetic Edda]], not behind it. Snorri also has a [[snorri-sturluson|
commentator page]] for his role as the great medieval systematizer of the myths.
