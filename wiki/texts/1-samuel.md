The First Book of Samuel narrates the transition from charismatic tribal leadership to hereditary monarchy in ancient Israel — the most politically consequential transformation in the entire Former Prophets. Three figures dominate: Samuel (prophet, priest, judge), Saul (Israel's first king, ultimately rejected), and David (YHWH's chosen king, whose line will become permanent). The book's theological center is the sovereignty of YHWH over human kingship: no king may rule except as YHWH's vice-regent, subject to the prophetic word.

## Canonical Position

In the Hebrew Bible, 1–2 Samuel form a single unit within the Former Prophets (Joshua–2 Kings). The Greek Septuagint divides the whole into four "Books of Kingdoms" (1–2 Samuel = 1–2 Kingdoms; 1–2 Kings = 3–4 Kingdoms). In the Vulgate, 1–2 Samuel are labeled 1–2 Kings. The Latin title that became standard in most modern Bibles is a later convention.

The name "Samuel" refers not to authorship (Samuel dies in 1 Sam 25:1) but to Samuel's role as kingmaker — the one who anointed both Saul and David. Ancient sources (1 Chr 29:29) refer to written records of "Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet, and Gad the seer" as sources drawn on by later historians.

## Literary Structure

[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] proposes four major literary blocks:

| Section | Chapters | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Story of Samuel (with embedded Ark Narrative) | 1:1–7:17 | Hannah; Samuel's call; Ark of God among the Philistines; Samuel's judgeship |
| Transition to Monarchy | 8:1–22 | The people's demand for a king; YHWH's response |
| Story of Saul | 9:1–15:35 | Saul's anointing, reign, and rejection |
| Story of Saul and David | 16:1–31:13 | David's anointing; rise to prominence; Saul's pursuit; Saul's death |

The overarching structure of 1–2 Samuel is framed by **poetic inclusions**: Hannah's Song (1 Sam 2:1-10) at the beginning, David's Song of Thanks (2 Sam 22) and Last Words (2 Sam 23) at the end. These poems function as theological brackets for the entire narrative.

The **Ark Narrative** (1 Sam 4:1–7:1) is embedded within the Story of Samuel. It is one of the oldest compositional units in the book, possibly from the pre-Davidic era. It narrates YHWH's humiliation and triumph over Philistine Dagon — establishing that YHWH's power is not contingent on Israelite military victory.

## Hannah's Song as Thematic Introduction

The **Song of Hannah** (1 Sam 2:1-10) functions as a thematic introduction to all of 1–2 Samuel. [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] identifies three themes it announces:

1. **YHWH's holy sovereignty** — YHWH is the unique holy one, the God of true knowledge, who "judges the ends of the earth" (2:3, 10). He is king by nature, not by conquest — unlike Marduk or Baal.
2. **Reversal of human fortunes** — "The bows of the mighty are broken, but those who stumbled are girded with strength" (2:4). This pattern recurs in Hannah → Samuel → Saul → David: every elevation comes through divine reversal of human expectation.
3. **The kingship theme** — "He will give strength to his king and exalt the horn of his anointed" (2:10b). The reference to "his anointed" (*mĕšîḥô*) is anticipatory — there is no king in Israel yet. It points forward to the entire Saul-David narrative and, beyond it, to the Messianic king.

The song became the direct prototype of Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55).

## Theological Themes

### YHWH as Divine King
Human kingship in Israel is derivative. When the people ask for a king (1 Sam 8), YHWH tells Samuel "they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them" (8:7). The king's proper title is *nāgîd* ("prince/designated leader") — YHWH's earthly vice-regent, not an autonomous sovereign. Saul is anointed "over his inheritance" (*naḥălāh*) — the king rules YHWH's estate as his deputy.

### Obedience to the Prophetic Word
The organizing principle of 1 Samuel's purpose (per Tsumura): **the king in Israel is subject to the prophet** — i.e., to the word of God conveyed through the prophetic office. Both of Saul's rejections (1 Sam 13; 15) are explicit failures of this principle. The climax: "Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has rejected you from being king" (15:23). Obedience is better than sacrifice; attentiveness than the fat of rams (15:22).

### The Reversal Pattern
Divine reversal of fortune (Hannah's theme) plays out in every major character:
- Hannah: barren → mother of Samuel
- Eli's house: honored → destroyed
- Samuel: anonymous boy → national prophet
- Saul: "from the smallest tribe" → first king → rejected
- David: youngest brother → king-in-waiting

### Providence through the Ordinary
God's providential guidance operates through mundane events: Saul's donkey-search leads to Samuel (ch. 9); David's errand to his brothers enables his meeting with Goliath (ch. 17). YHWH is Lord of history, guiding "the when and where" of his chosen ones without bypassing ordinary human life. [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] cites Romans 8:28 as the NT summary of 1 Samuel's theological claim.

### The Conditional and Unconditional Covenants
Saul's kingship is explicitly conditional — subject to obedience (12:14-15, 25). The rejection of Saul and the election of David sets the stage for the Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7), which is unconditional: "Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me." The movement from Saul to David is the movement from a conditional experiment to an unconditional commitment rooted in YHWH's own purpose.

## Key Episodes and Interpretive Cruxes

### The Monarchy Request (1 Sam 8)
See [[monarchy-request|The Monarchy Request (1 Sam 8)]]. The chapter is the most contested in the book: is the request for a king a sinful rejection of YHWH (anti-monarchic reading) or a providential transition (pro-monarchic reading)? The text holds both in tension. The sin is in the people's motive ("like all the nations"; 8:5, 20) — wanting a military king rather than trusting YHWH. The grant of a king is YHWH's accommodation, not his approval of the motive.

### Saul's Two Rejections
**First rejection (1 Sam 13:7b-15)**: Saul offers sacrifice without waiting for Samuel. Samuel's verdict: his dynasty will not continue ("a man after his own heart" has been sought — referring to David, though unnamed).

**Second rejection (1 Sam 15)**: Saul spares Agag and the best livestock in violation of the *ḥerem* command. Samuel completes the *ḥerem* by executing Agag (15:32-33). The verdict is decisive: "Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has rejected you from being king over Israel" (15:26). Saul's second "confession" (15:30) reveals his real concern — not his sin before God, but his reputation before the elders. The contrast with David's Psalm 51 is implicit throughout.

### God's "Regret" — An Interpretive Tension
1 Samuel 15 presents a genuine theological crux: YHWH "regrets" (*nḥm*) making Saul king (15:11, 35), yet Samuel declares "the Splendor of Israel does not change his mind (*nḥm*), for he is not a man that he should change his mind" (15:29). [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] resolves this by distinguishing two uses of *nḥm*: (a) *emotive* — God's genuine inner grief at Saul's failure (vv. 11, 35); (b) *relational* — in 15:29, paired with "to deceive," the verb means God will not reverse his judicial decision. The two senses are contextually determined, not contradictory.

### David's Anointing and the Reversal Pattern
The anointing of David (16:1-13) is the pivot of the book: "Man looks at the outward appearance, but YHWH looks at the heart" (16:7). The seven elder brothers are rejected; the youngest, overlooked shepherd-boy is chosen. The reversal motif from Hannah's Song is enacted in miniature.

### "Is Saul Also Among the Prophets?" (10:10-12; 19:24)
The proverb appears twice in the book with different valences. At 10:11, it is apparently neutral — surprise at the transformation of Kish's son. At 19:24, in the context of Saul's compulsive prophesying while pursuing David, it carries a negative resonance. [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] argues the proverb does not denigrate either Saul or the prophets in its original context; the nuance shifted with repeated use.

## Text-Critical Notes

The Hebrew text of 1-2 Samuel is in "extremely poor condition" by the standard of MT manuscripts — widely considered the most difficult textual situation in the Hebrew Bible. Three Qumran Samuel scrolls (4QSam^a, ^b, ^c) diverge widely from the MT, often aligning with the LXX. Conventional practice (McCarter, Tov) uses these versions to reconstruct an "eclectic" text.

[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] argues against this practice on principle. Many apparent MT corruptions are:
- **Phonetic spellings** — recording actual pronunciation, not copyist error
- **Brachylogy** — idiomatic ellipsis of standard elements
- **AXB literary patterns** — deliberate rhetorical insertions misread as displaced text

He proposes early composition of the major blocks (10th century BCE), pointing to archaic vocabulary (*ḥămôr leḥem* = an Akkadian calque obsolete by the LXX's era; *ṣemed* as the Akkadian land-area measure *ṣimdu*) that a postexilic Deuteronomistic editor would not have preserved.

Tsumura's discourse grammar analysis (wayqtl for narrative mainline vs. weqtl for predictive discourse; background vs. foreground clauses) resolves apparent chronological issues and supports the literary integrity of the final form. His purpose statement for 1 Samuel: the king in Israel is to be subject to the prophet through whom God conveys his word. Both of Saul's rejections are structured by this principle. He is maximalist on historicity ("the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence") and skeptical of the Deuteronomistic History hypothesis (circularity of labeling expressions "Deuteronomistic," archaic vocabulary preserved only in early composition). ANE comparative material (Ugaritic, Akkadian, Mari, Assyrian inscriptions) is used to illuminate rather than relativize the text.

## Relationship to the Former Prophets Sequence

1 Samuel follows [[judges|Judges]] (ending in tribal anarchy, civil war, and the question "there was no king in Israel") and [[book-of-ruth|Ruth]] (a counternarrative of *[[hesed|ḥesed]]* operating at the micro level during the judges era). 1 Samuel resolves the kingship question that Judges raised but could not answer: the king comes, but he must be YHWH's vice-regent, subject to the prophetic word — and no amount of military capability or popular approval can substitute for that obedience.

## NT Connections

- **Hannah's Song → Magnificat** (Luke 1:46-55): Mary's prayer is structured on Hannah's; the same reversal-of-fortune theology underlies both
- **David → the Davidic Covenant** (2 Sam 7) → Messianic expectation → Matt 1:1 ("Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham")
- **Samuel, David, Jonathan** in Hebrews 11:32 — listed among the heroes of faith
- **Saul and David** as types: Tsumura: "In God's dealings with Saul and David, one might see God's 'justice' and his 'mercy,' respectively; both aspects will be fulfilled, according to the NT, in the person of Jesus Christ, who died on the cross"
- **The Spirit of the LORD "rushing upon"** Saul (10:6, 10; 11:6) and David (16:13) — the *rûaḥ YHWH* as the empowerment of YHWH's anointed; the Spirit's departure from Saul (16:14) as YHWH's enacted rejection

## The Literary Reading (Alter)

[[robert-alter|Robert Alter]]'s [[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]] (1999) reads 1
Samuel as the opening movement of a single, artfully unified narrative running to 1 Kings 2,
against the form-critical division into independent sources (the Ark Narrative, a Saul cycle,
a History of the Rise of David). Its method is the
[[literary-reading-of-the-bible|literary reading of the Bible]]: close attention to
word-motifs, the [[saul|Saul]]/[[david|David]] antithesis on the theme of **knowledge**, and
the writer's use of **paired and tripled episodes** to mirror Saul's tragic ambivalence
(three coronations; two "Saul among the prophets"; two sparings of Saul by David). Alter reads
[[samuel|Samuel]] as a genuinely God-confirmed but "implacable, irascible" prophet whose story
"enormously complicates" rather than promotes prophetic ideology. This synchronic literary
approach is held alongside Tsumura's evangelical-canonical reading and the source- and
redaction-critical tradition per the Contradiction Protocol.
