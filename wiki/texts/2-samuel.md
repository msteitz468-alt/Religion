The Second Book of Samuel continues the narrative of 1 Samuel to its theological climax: the [[davidic-covenant|Davidic Covenant]] (2 Sam 7), in which YHWH promises David an eternal dynasty — the most consequential single divine promise in the entire Former Prophets, and the OT's deepest grounding for Messianic expectation. The book narrates the fullness of David's reign: his ascension over all Israel, the transfer of the ark to Jerusalem, the Davidic covenant, his grave sins (adultery, murder, census), and the catastrophic revolt of his son Absalom. The theological arc moves from YHWH's unconditional faithfulness through human failure to the vindication of divine purpose.

## Canonical Position and Relationship to 1 Samuel

1–2 Samuel form a continuous narrative in the Hebrew Bible (Former Prophets). [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] characterizes 1–2 Samuel as a unified work whose literary structure runs from the Song of Hannah (1 Sam 2:1-10) to the Song of David and Last Words (2 Sam 22-23), with the Epilogues (2 Sam 21-24) forming a concentric closure. 2 Samuel is not a separate work but the second half of the same narrative project — its direct continuation and theological culmination.

**Genre**: Tsumura characterizes 1–2 Samuel as occupying an **intermediate position between epic style (Judges) and annalistic style (Kings)**. Judges uses round numbers for judgeships and handles events in an epic register; Kings records kings' ages, regnal years, and source references in annalistic style. Samuel combines both — narrative epic in the treatment of character and drama, annalistic precision in military lists and administrative catalogues (e.g., 8:15-18; 20:23-26).

## Literary Structure

The structure of 2 Samuel, per [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]], consists of two major blocks plus an epilogue:

### V. "Story of King David" (1:1–20:26)

**i. Story of David's Reign (1:1–12:31)**

| Section | Chapters | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Death of Saul | 1:1-27 | Amalekite's report; David's elegy for Saul and Jonathan |
| King David | 2:1–5:5 | David king of Judah (Hebron); civil war; deaths of Abner and Ish-bosheth; David king of all Israel |
| Jerusalem, the City of David | 5:6-25 | Conquest of Jerusalem; palace; family; Philistine victories |
| Zion, the Place of Worship | 6:1-23 | Ark transferred to Jerusalem; Michal episode |
| "Davidic Covenant": Eternal Throne | 7:1-29 | Nathan's oracle; David's prayer — the theological center |
| Military Catalogue | 8:1-18 | Summary of David's conquests; officials list |
| Mephibosheth | 9:1-13 | David's *ḥesed* to Jonathan's surviving son |
| Israel-Ammon War | 10:1–12:31 | War narrative (A) — David and Bathsheba (X) — war conclusion (B) |

The **David and Bathsheba narrative (11:1–12:25) is structured as an AXB insertion** into the Ammonite war: chs. 10 and 12:26-31 bracket the domestic catastrophe, Tsumura's discourse analysis making explicit that this is a deliberate literary device — the narrator "interrupts" the military narrative with the court drama, then returns to it.

**ii. Story of Absalom's Revolt (13:1–20:26)**

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Amnon and Tamar (13:1-22) | Amnon's rape of Tamar; Absalom's hatred |
| Absalom's vengeance and escape (13:23-39) | Amnon murdered; Absalom flees to Geshur |
| Return of Absalom (14:1-33) | Wise woman of Tekoa; Joab's mediation; partial reconciliation |
| Absalom's conspiracy (15:1-12) | Absalom steals the hearts of Israel |
| David's escape from Jerusalem (15:13–16:14) | Flight across Kidron; Ittai, Zadok, Hushai, Ziba, Shimei |
| Ahithophel and Hushai (16:15–17:23) | Rival counsel; Hushai's victory; Ahithophel's suicide |
| Absalom's death (18:1–19:8) | Joab kills Absalom; David's grief |
| David's return to Jerusalem (19:9-43) | Shimei, Mephibosheth, Barzillai; tribal tensions |
| Sheba's revolt (20:1-22) | Second rebellion; Joab kills Amasa; Sheba's death |

### VI. Epilogues (21:1–24:25)

The epilogues form a **concentric structure (A-B-X-X'-B'-A')** — an integral literary unit, not a secondary appendix (against treating them as mere SN appendices):

| Position | Section |
|---|---|
| A | Famine and execution of Saul's sons (21:1-14) |
| B | Philistine war heroes (21:15-22) |
| X | **Song of David** (22:1-51 = Psalm 18) |
| X' | **Last Words of David** (23:1-7) |
| B' | David's heroes (23:8-39) |
| A' | Census and the Lord's anger (24:1-25) |

[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] emphasizes discourse structure throughout 2 Samuel (supra-sentential grammar distinguishing mainline narrative, background, and predictive elements) and the unity of 1–2 Samuel as a single work framed by Hannah's Song (1 Sam 2) and David's Song + Last Words (2 Sam 22–23). The Bathsheba narrative (11:1–12:25) is an AXB insertion into the Ammonite war. The Davidic covenant (ch. 7) is the theological center; the epilogues provide concentric closure. Purpose of 2 Samuel (Intro §VII): show how God led David's life **despite grave sins** in order to keep the promise of heirs and an eternal dynasty; ch. 24 points to the temple site.

The final episode (ch. 24) — census, plague, Araunah's threshing floor — **points forward to the temple**. Tsumura (Intro §VII): as monarchy sidetracked the people from the true King, Solomon's Temple would lead them to look at the physical temple and away from the real presence of God.

## Narrative Summary

### From Saul's death to kingship over all Israel (chs. 1–5)

The book opens with an [[amalekites|Amalekite]] arriving at Ziklag from the battlefield, claiming he himself finished off the dying [[saul|Saul]] and bringing the crown and armlet to [[david|David]] (1:1-10). David has the man executed for lifting his hand against YHWH's anointed (1:13-16) and sings a lament over Saul and [[jonathan|Jonathan]] — "How the mighty have fallen" (1:17-27). David goes up to [[hebron|Hebron]] and is anointed king over Judah (2:1-4), while Abner son of Ner installs Saul's son Ish-bosheth over the rest of [[israelites|Israel]] (2:8-10). At the pool of [[gibeon|Gibeon]] twelve champions from each side kill one another simultaneously (2:12-16), and the resulting battle turns into a long war: Asahel, brother of [[joab|Joab]], chases [[abner|Abner]] and is run through with the butt of Abner's spear (2:18-23).

Abner, quarrelling with Ish-bosheth over Saul's concubine Rizpah, defects to David (3:6-21). David's price is the return of [[michal|Michal]], taken from her husband Paltiel, who follows her weeping as far as Bahurim before being ordered back (3:14-16). Joab, avenging Asahel and protecting his own position, calls Abner aside at the gate of Hebron and stabs him in the belly (3:26-27); David curses Joab's house and walks behind the bier (3:28-39). Two Benjaminite captains then murder Ish-bosheth in his bed and bring his head to David, who has them killed and their hands and feet cut off (4:5-12). The elders of Israel come to Hebron and anoint David over all Israel (5:1-5). He takes the Jebusite stronghold of [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]], which becomes the City of David (5:6-10), receives cedar and craftsmen from Hiram of Tyre (5:11), takes more concubines and wives, and twice routs the [[philistines|Philistines]] in the Valley of Rephaim (5:17-25).

### The ark, the covenant, and the wars (chs. 6–10)

David brings the ark from Baale-judah on a new cart; when the oxen stumble, Uzzah steadies it and is struck dead, and David leaves the ark three months with Obed-edom (6:1-11). The second attempt succeeds with sacrifices every six paces, David dancing before the ark in a linen ephod; Michal despises him from a window, rebukes him for exposing himself before the slave girls, and the narrative closes with the note that she had no child to the day of her death (6:12-23). David proposes to build a temple; the prophet [[nathan|Nathan]] first approves, then delivers the night oracle reversing the plan — David will not build YHWH a house; YHWH will build David a house, an offspring whose throne is established forever, chastened with the rod of men but never stripped of *ḥesed* as Saul was (7:1-17). David sits before YHWH and prays (7:18-29).

A military summary follows: victories over the Philistines, the [[moabites|Moabites]] (measured with a cord, two lengths put to death and one spared, 8:2), Hadadezer of Zobah, and the [[arameans|Arameans]] of [[damascus|Damascus]], with garrisons in [[edomites|Edom]] and a list of officials (8:1-18). David seeks out Mephibosheth, Jonathan's lame son, restores Saul's land to him and seats him at the royal table (9:1-13). When David's condolence envoys to the new [[ammonites|Ammonite]] king Hanun have half their beards shaved and their garments cut off at the buttocks (10:1-5), war follows; Joab and Abishai defeat the Ammonite-Aramean coalition, and David crushes the Arameans at Helam (10:6-19).

### Bathsheba, Uriah, and Nathan's judgment (chs. 11–12)

In the spring, when kings go out to war, David stays in Jerusalem. From the roof he sees a woman bathing, sends and takes [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]], wife of [[uriah|Uriah]] the Hittite, and lies with her; she conceives (11:1-5). David recalls Uriah from the siege of Rabbah and repeatedly presses him to go home to his wife; Uriah sleeps at the palace door, refusing comfort while the ark and the army are in the field, and does so again after David makes him drunk (11:6-13). David then sends Uriah back carrying his own death warrant: Joab is to station him where the fighting is fiercest and withdraw. Uriah is killed with others at the wall, and Joab's messenger reports it (11:14-25). Bathsheba mourns, David takes her as wife, and she bears a son — "but the thing that David had done displeased the LORD" (11:26-27).

Nathan tells the parable of the rich man who takes the poor man's one ewe lamb; David condemns the man to death, and Nathan answers, "You are the man" (12:1-7). The sentence: the sword will never depart from David's house, and his wives will be taken and lain with in broad daylight by one close to him (12:10-12). David says, "I have sinned against the LORD"; the child sickens and dies after seven days, David rises from the ground and eats, and Bathsheba bears [[solomon|Solomon]] (12:13-25). David then takes Rabbah, seizes its king's crown, and puts the population to forced labor with saws, iron picks, and axes (12:26-31).

### Amnon, Tamar, and Absalom's revolt (chs. 13–20)

[[amnon|Amnon]], David's firstborn, feigns illness on the advice of his cousin Jonadab and rapes his half-sister [[tamar|Tamar]] in his chamber; his hatred afterward is greater than his love, and he has her thrown out and bolted behind (13:1-19). David is furious but does nothing. Two years later [[absalom|Absalom]] has Amnon killed at a sheepshearing feast and flees to Talmai of Geshur for three years (13:23-39). Joab sends the wise woman of Tekoa with a fictitious case to move David, and Absalom is brought back to Jerusalem, though two more years pass before David will see him — Absalom forces the meeting by setting Joab's barley field on fire (14:1-33).

Absalom then stands at the gate intercepting litigants and "steals the hearts" of Israel, and after four years has himself proclaimed king at Hebron (15:1-12). David flees Jerusalem barefoot up the Mount of Olives, sending Zadok and Abiathar back with the ark and planting Hushai as a counter-counselor against Ahithophel (15:13-37); Ziba slanders Mephibosheth, and Shimei of Saul's clan curses David and throws stones and dirt at him (16:1-13). On Hushai's public advice, Absalom pitches a tent on the palace roof and goes in to his father's ten concubines in the sight of all Israel (16:20-22), fulfilling Nathan's word. Ahithophel urges an immediate strike with twelve thousand men; Hushai's slower plan is adopted, and Ahithophel goes home, sets his house in order, and hangs himself (17:1-23).

The armies meet in the forest of Ephraim, where the forest devours more than the sword (18:6-8). Absalom's head catches in a terebinth; Joab, told of it and told also of David's order to deal gently with the young man, drives three javelins into his heart while he hangs alive, and his ten armor-bearers finish him; he is thrown into a pit under a heap of stones (18:9-17). David's cry at the gate — "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would I had died instead of you" — turns the victory into mourning until Joab bluntly warns him that he is shaming the army (18:33–19:8). The return north exposes tribal jealousy between Judah and Israel (19:9-43); Sheba son of Bichri raises the cry "We have no portion in David," and Joab, meeting the newly appointed Amasa on the road, takes him by the beard as if to kiss him and disembowels him with a sword in his left hand (20:1-13). The revolt ends when a wise woman of Abel of Beth-maacah negotiates Sheba's head over the wall (20:14-22).

### Epilogue (chs. 21–24)

A three-year famine is traced to Saul's slaughter of the Gibeonites; David hands over seven of Saul's descendants, who are impaled before YHWH at Gibeah, and Rizpah guards the bodies on sackcloth from harvest until rain (21:1-14). Philistine giant-killings follow (21:15-22), then David's Song (ch. 22, largely paralleling Psalm 18), his Last Words on the everlasting covenant (23:1-7), and the roster of the Thirty, which ends with Uriah the Hittite (23:8-39). Finally David orders a census; Joab objects and complies, and after the count David's heart strikes him. Given three choices by the seer Gad, he chooses pestilence; seventy thousand die, and the angel halts at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite, which David buys for fifty shekels of silver and where he builds an altar (24:1-25). [[1-2-chronicles|1 Chronicles]] retells much of this material with notable differences — it omits the Bathsheba and Absalom narratives entirely, attributes the incitement of the census to Satan rather than to YHWH's anger (1 Chr 21:1), and prices Ornan's site at six hundred shekels of gold (1 Chr 21:25); the accounts are recorded here side by side without harmonization.

## The Davidic Covenant (2 Sam 7) — Theological Center

See [[davidic-covenant|Davidic Covenant]] for full analysis. The oracle of Nathan (7:1-17) and David's responsive prayer (7:18-29) together constitute the theological pivot of 1–2 Samuel and arguably of the entire OT. [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] calls it "the central theme of 2 Samuel, even of the entire Old Testament."

**The promise structure (per Tsumura's discourse analysis)**:
- vv. 8b-9a: PAST narrative — "I took you from the pasture… was with you… cut off your enemies"
- vv. 9b-11aβ: PAST procedural — "I have made your name great… provided a place for Israel… given you rest"
- v. 11b: PRESENT performative declaration — "the LORD tells you: the LORD will make a house for you"
- vv. 12-16: FUTURE promise — offspring, throne, eternal dynasty, divine adoption

The reversal embedded in the promise: David wants to build YHWH a *house* (temple); YHWH promises to build David a *house* (dynasty). The wordplay on *bayit* is the literary and theological hinge of the chapter.

David's prayer (vv. 18-29): Tsumura analyzes it as **poetic prose**, structured in three movements — YHWH's promise and David's humility (vv. 18-21); David's confession of YHWH's incomparability and Israel's uniqueness (vv. 22-24); David's supplication for eternal confirmation (vv. 25-29). The prayer returns four times to *leʿôlām* ("forever"). It is the most theologically concentrated prayer in Samuel.

## David and Bathsheba — Nathan's Indictment

The parable of the ewe-lamb (12:1-6) and Nathan's indictment "You are the man!" (12:7) constitute the narrative's most devastating confrontation of power with prophetic word. The same prophet who delivered the Davidic covenant (ch. 7) now pronounces judgment. Tsumura:

- David's sin is named as "despising the word of the LORD" (12:9) and "despising me" (12:10) — the same theological category as Saul's failure (1 Sam 15:23)
- Nathan's oracle is structured as an AXB tricolon (12:9): the murder (A) and the wife-taking (X) and the murder via Ammonites (B) — the X-line (wife-taking) is the theological center, not the murder per se
- David's "taking" of Bathsheba mirrors the rich man's "taking" of the poor man's ewe-lamb
- David's repentance ("I have sinned against the LORD," 12:13) is genuine and immediate — unlike Saul's concerned-with-face confession (1 Sam 15:30). The atonement: YHWH "takes away" David's sin; the child bears the consequence instead of David
- Psalm 51 as David's repentance psalm: Tsumura defends the title's historical authenticity against dismissal as postexilic. He analyzes Ps 51 as literary unity — personal confession (vv. 1-17) followed by royal prayer for Zion's recovery (vv. 18-19), an A...Z / A'Z' pattern

**The contrast with Saul is explicit and structural**: David sins equally gravely (even more so — adultery, murder, conspiracy). But David receives prophetic confrontation, confesses immediately, and is granted forgiveness. Saul never received a second chance after ch. 15. Tsumura: YHWH's "justice" is seen in Saul; YHWH's "mercy" is seen in David — and both converge in Jesus Christ.

## Theme: Human Desires and Divine Plans

[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] identifies this as the governing theme running through all 1-2 Samuel:

| Character | Human desire | Divine plan |
|---|---|---|
| Hannah | A son | Mother of the king-maker |
| Saul | To please the people | YHWH's conditional experiment — rejected |
| David | To build a temple | YHWH builds David's dynasty instead |

The pattern establishes that "God's plan of salvation continues despite the various phases of human desires. God is the one who proceeds with his plan and purpose by his holy sovereignty and by his grace."

## Absalom's Revolt — Narrative Complexity

The Absalom narrative (2 Sam 13-20) is the biblical text most often analyzed for narrative complexity. [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] engages here with literary critics but criticizes **postmodern intertextuality** (Lyke's Bakhtinian reading of the wise woman of Tekoa, 2 Sam 14) as losing control of meaning through unanchored associations.

His approach: characters matter primarily in their relationship to the major dramatis personae (David). The story of Amnon and Tamar (ch. 13) is intentionally placed within the Story of Absalom's Revolt — Absalom is the major figure; the Amnon-Tamar episode is his narrative motivation. Joab's role throughout (killing Abner, Absalom, Amasa) is tracked in relation to David's authority. The narrative is not biography; it is **salvation history through the Davidic house**.

The Absalom episode enacts Nathan's judgment: "I will raise up evil against you from your house" (12:11). The private sin of David and Bathsheba becomes the public catastrophe of his household — his own son publically humiliating his father's concubines. The narrative's providence is dark: YHWH's word is fulfilled precisely through the most painful consequences of David's failure.

## The Census and Temple Site (2 Sam 24)

The final chapter narrates David's sin in taking a census (24:1-10) and the subsequent plague (24:15-17). David's repentance leads to the purchase of Araunah's threshing floor (24:18-25). Tsumura: David's census is a failure of trust — he counts the military rather than trusting YHWH's provision. The consequence (70,000 die) is reversed by David's intercessory prayer and the altar on Araunah's threshing floor.

The theological logic: David's greatest failure directly leads to the temple site. God uses "even the sins of his servants" — not endorsing them, but incorporating them into his purposes. The threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite becomes Solomon's temple mount (2 Chr 3:1).

## NT Connections

- **Matt 1:1**: "Jesus Christ, the son of David" — the Davidic covenant is explicitly named as the midpoint of salvation history
- **Luke 1:32-33**: Gabriel's announcement draws on 2 Sam 7:12-13, 16 nearly verbatim — the "throne of his father David" and the "kingdom without end"
- **Luke 1:69**: "a horn of salvation... in the house of his servant David" — Zechariah's Benedictus
- **Acts 2:29-36**: Peter's Pentecost sermon — the resurrection of Jesus as the fulfillment of the Davidic covenant promise that one of David's descendants would sit on his throne
- **Heb 1:5**: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Sam 7:14 quoted) — applied to the eternal Son
- **Rev 5:5; 22:16**: "the Root of David" — Christ's Davidic identity in Revelation's climax
- **2 Cor 6:18**: "I will be a father to you and you shall be sons and daughters to me" — echoes 2 Sam 7:14's adoption language, applied now to the church

## The Literary Reading (Alter)

[[robert-alter|Robert Alter]]'s [[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]] (1999) treats 2
Samuel as the heart of "probably the greatest single narrative representation in antiquity of
a human life evolving by slow stages through time." For Alter the true center of the second
half is not dynastic [[succession-narrative|succession]] but **[[david|David's]] sin and the
unfolding of [[nathan|Nathan's]] curse on his house** — a theme he finds closer to Greek
tragedy (and to Faulkner's *Absalom, Absalom!*) than to political apologetic. He stresses the
narrative's **reticence** (David must send to discover who [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] is), its
deliberate **analogy** between David's adultery and [[amnon|Amnon's]] rape of Tamar (both
"prohibited sexual acts" that "lead to murder and political disarray"), and the
**"freighted imagining" of unnecessary detail** — the weeping Paltiel following
[[michal|Michal]] to Bahurim; David's "horrendous stutter of grief" over
[[absalom|Absalom]]. He reads the pious frame of David's deathbed charge (1 Kings 2) as a
light [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]] overlay on an otherwise Machiavellian
testament. Recorded alongside Tsumura's canonical reading and the source-critical tradition
per the Contradiction Protocol.

## Scholarship and Historiography

1. **Tsumura (NICOT 2019):** Final edit ≤ late 10th c. (with 1 Sam 27:6); eternal dynasty not postexilic (ANE parallels); genre intermediate epic/annalistic; not David's biography but salvation history.
2. **Rost / SN-HDR critics:** Court History / Succession Narrative as independent units — Tsumura prefers Story of King David (1–20) + integral Epilogues.
3. **Alter literary:** David Story as character-driven tragedy of sin and curse.
