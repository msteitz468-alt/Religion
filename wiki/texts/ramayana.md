# Rāmāyaṇa

The **Rāmāyaṇa** ("Journey of Rāma") is one of the two great Sanskrit **itihāsas** (epics) of Hinduism,
attributed to the sage **Valmiki** and composed in the anuṣṭubh-derived **shloka** meter — a meter that,
according to the text itself, Valmiki *invented* on the spot when he saw a hunter kill a crane mid-coitus
(compassion at the bird's death crystallized into verse). The text is approximately **20,000 verses** in
its critical edition, making it roughly one-third the length of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]].

It is conventionally set in the **Treta Yuga** (the second age), when moral order was still relatively
intact — a framing that contrasts it with the Mahābhārata's darker Dvāpara/Kali cusp.

## Narrative Summary

**King Daśaratha** of Ayodhyā has four sons by three queens. His eldest and most virtuous son,
**[[rama|Rāma]]**, an avatar of Viṣṇu, is about to be crowned when the youngest queen **Kaikeyī** uses
two old boons to have Rāma exiled for fourteen years and her own son Bharata placed on the throne. Rāma
goes into exile with his wife **[[sita|Sītā]]** and loyal half-brother Lakṣmaṇa.

In the forest, the **ogress Śūrpaṇakhā** attempts to seduce Rāma; he mocks her cruelly and Lakṣmaṇa
cuts off her nose and ears. She informs her brother **Rāvaṇa** (king of the ogres, Lord of Laṅkā) about
Sītā. Rāvaṇa abducts Sītā using the trick of a golden deer. The vulture Jaṭāyu attempts to stop him and
is killed. Rāma allies with the monkey king Sugrīva and his general **Hanumān**, who crosses to Laṅkā
and finds Sītā. A great war follows; Rāvaṇa is killed (a form of Brahminicide, since Rāvaṇa is partly
Brahmin-born) and Sītā is recovered.

Sītā must undergo **fire ordeal** to prove her chastity before Rāma accepts her back. They return to
Ayodhyā, and the idealized reign of Rāma — **Rāma-rāj** — begins. But rumors revive; Rāma banishes
the pregnant Sītā to Valmiki's hermitage, where she gives birth to twin sons Kuśa and Lava. At Rāma's
**horse sacrifice** years later, the twins recite the Rāmāyaṇa (creating a recursive loop — the poem is
within itself). Rāma asks for Sītā's return; she swears her fidelity and **descends into the earth**
(her mother), refusing to return. Rāma eventually returns to heaven as Viṣṇu.

## Major Themes

**The perfect man and his flaws.** The Rāmāyaṇa opens with the question "Is there any man alive who has
all the virtues?" — and answers it with Rāma. Yet its plot repeatedly exposes Rāma's imperfections:
suspicion of Sītā, violence against the monkey Vālin (shot in the back), banishment of a pregnant wife
to appease public opinion.

**Sītā's protest.** Sītā is not the passive ideal she has become in popular reception. Doniger reads her
two fire ordeals as "supreme, defiant protest" and notes that "Sītā is no doormat." Her final vanishing
is an act of abandonment and implicit blame. The text also shows a passionate, willful Sītā who scolds
Rāma, resists his authority, and ultimately outmaneuvers him by choosing her own exit from the world.

**Ogresses as shadow-women.** Kaikeyī, Śūrpaṇakhā, Ṭāṭakā, and Ayomukhī represent the "shadow side"
of Sītā — the sexually voracious woman that men fear lurks inside every "good" woman. Brahmin editorial
tradition suppressed this shadow and made Sītā the ideal of wifely subservience; other traditions preserved it.

**Monkeys as the human unconscious.** The parallel monkey plot (Sugrīva/Vālin mirroring Bharata/Rāma)
functions as dream-work: the poet projects onto animals the anger, resentment, and desire that the
humans cannot express. Vālin, the "usurped" monkey who took his brother's wife and throne, is the mirror
Rāma cannot look into directly.

**Symbolic ogres and the three gunas.** Rāvaṇa (ego/rajas), Vibhīṣaṇa (conscience/sattva), and
Kumbhakarṇa (bestial sleep/tamas) form a triad that maps onto the [[gunas|three gunas]].

**Language and compassion.** The poem's origin myth (Valmiki's shloka born from grief at a dying crane)
connects language, compassion, and the ethical gap between humans and animals. Poetry is "the characteristic
human act" that language makes possible after hunting has "killed sex."

**The horse sacrifice as frame.** The text is bracketed by two horse sacrifices: Daśaratha's at the
beginning (the sacrifice that produces Rāma), and Rāma's/Yudhiṣṭhira's at the end (the sacrifice
that triggers Sītā's final vanishing). Horse sacrifice as political annexation and as expiation is
developed here in parallel with the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]].

## Interpretive Cruxes

- **Why does Rāma banish Sītā (twice)?** The text gives both political (public pressure) and psychological
  (fear of being like his father Daśaratha, who was destroyed by his lust) explanations. Doniger reads
  the banishment as Rāma's politics overriding his love — mirroring the inverse error of Daśaratha
  (whose love overrode politics).
- **Rāma's killing of Vālin.** Ethically unjustifiable even within the text's own terms; Vālin is the
  *legal* parallel to Rāma, not the usurper. The dying Vālin upbraids Rāma for dharmic violation.
- **Śambuka the Śūdra.** Rāma beheads a Śūdra ascetic (Śambuka) for performing tapas above his station
  — killing him revives a dead Brahmin child. The caste system enforced by the very avatar of Viṣṇu.
- **Sītā's goddess status.** Sītā is an "ex-goddess on the way down" while Rāma is a "god in the making
  on the way up" — the two meet in passing like people on adjacent escalators. Her divine nature requires
  her to be mistreated (per the equine-goddess myth pattern: Saraṇyū, Urvaśī).

## Canon and Reception

The Rāmāyaṇa is **smriti** (remembered tradition) rather than **shruti** (heard revelation), but
functions with near-canonical authority across most Hindu communities. It is "the first poem" (*ādi
kāvya*); a thousand retellings exist in Sanskrit and vernacular languages across South and Southeast Asia.
Doniger notes that "Sītā and Rāma continue to shape attitudes to women and to political conflict in
India to this day."

## Hermeneutical Notes

Doniger reads the Rāmāyaṇa using **historical-critical** and **psychoanalytic** methods (dream-work,
displacement, shadow): the text is not an allegory for historical conquest (its ogres do not represent
any real human group) but a mythic working-out of social anxieties about gender, caste, and violence.
The animals function not as symbols of particular human groups but as structural projections of moral
forces the human characters cannot express directly.

## The Rāmāyaṇa in Modern Indian Politics (20th–21st c.)

Doniger (Ch. 24) traces how the Rāmāyaṇa became a live political weapon in modern India:

**Television and political furor.** The 1987–1988 broadcast of the Rāmāyaṇa (78 episodes, national
Indian television) was "a major factor leading to the destruction of Babur's Mosque in 1992." Millions
of Hindus who had known the story only through regional folk performance now knew one single,
standardized, Sanskritized version — closing off the diversity that the scholarly tradition had opened.

**Babri Masjid / Ayodhyā.** Hindu organizations claimed Babur's Mosque (built c. 1527) stood on Rāma's
birthplace. On December 6, 1992, a BJP-led mob of 200,000 destroyed the mosque; over 1,000 died in
subsequent riots. Doniger: "Rama left no archaeological or inscriptional record. There is no evidence
that anyone named Rama did or did not live in Ayodhya." She coins the phrase: "**There is no second
Troy here for a Schliemann to discover. Or, rather, there is a second, and a third, and a nineteenth
Troy for anyone to discover**." See [[babri-masjid-ayodhya|Babri Masjid controversy]].

**Ram Setu (Sethusamudram canal, 2007).** A proposed shipping canal through shoals between India and
Sri Lanka was opposed as destroying "Rāma's Bridge" built by the monkey army (Rāmāyaṇa 4.63.17). The
Archaeological Survey of India and the Indian Supreme Court both determined the formation is natural;
the Indian government nevertheless withdrew its own scientific finding after BJP political pressure.

**Three Hundred Rāmāyaṇas (2008).** Delhi University's BA (Honours) program assigned A. K. Ramanujan's
essay "Three Hundred Rāmāyaṇas: Five Examples and Three Thoughts on Translation" (1976). Hindu groups
objected to Ramanujan's presentation of tribal, Dalit, and folk versions, some of which cast Rāvaṇa or
Lakṣmaṇa differently. On February 25, 2008, an ABVP (RSS student wing) mob ransacked the history
department at Delhi University; threats were issued; the essay was eventually removed from the
curriculum. The protesters: "every deviant telling, mostly tribal and Dalit, be erased." Ramanujan,
who had received the Padma Sri (1976, India's highest honor), was described by one columnist as
"a scholar who did more for Indian culture than all of the ABVP put together."

**Subversive Rāmāyaṇas.** The same tradition that the Hindu right tries to purify has always harbored
inversions: Michael Madhusudan Datta (1824–1873) made Rāvaṇa the hero and equated Rāma with the
British; Tamil separatists cast Rāvaṇa as a noble Tamil king slain by the evil Aryan Rāma; Dalit
retellings show Sītā defending the ogres; a Tribal (Rajnengi Pardhan) version reverses the fire
ordeal — Lakṣmaṇa, not Sītā, undergoes the fire to prove chastity from Rāma's jealousy.

**Doniger's verdict**: "Placing the Rāmāyaṇa in its historical contexts demonstrates that it is a work
of **fiction**, created by human authors who lived at various times, and shows how the human imagination
transformed the actual circumstance of the historical period into something far more beautiful, terrible,
challenging, and elevating than the circumstances themselves."
