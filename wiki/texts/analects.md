The **Analects** (*Lunyu*) is the foundational text of [[confucianism|Confucianism]] — a collection of
the sayings, dialogues, and acts of **[[confucius|Confucius]]** compiled by his disciples. Terse and
unsystematic, it is the primary record of his thought and the first of the **Four Books**.

## Content and Form

Twenty short "books" of aphorisms and exchanges: "The Master said…" Its themes are the cultivation of
character and the right ordering of family and state:

- **[[ren|Ren]]** (humaneness) as the supreme virtue, and **[[li-confucian|li]]** (ritual propriety) as
  its outward discipline.
- The **junzi** (the exemplary person / "gentleman") vs. the petty person.
- The **rectification of names** (names and roles must match reality), **filial piety**, learning,
  and the famous negative Golden Rule: "Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire" (15.23).
- Government by **virtue and example** rather than by law and punishment (2.3).

## Significance

As the most direct window onto Confucius, the Analects is the moral charter of the entire East Asian
Confucian world. Its gnomic wisdom-saying form invites comparison with other gnomic literatures —
[[proverbs|Proverbs]], the [[dhammapada|Dhammapada]] — a candidate comparison page. Its ethic of
deliberate **cultivation** (*li*, learning) stands in sharp, deliberate contrast to Daoist
**[[ziran|ziran]]** and [[wu-wei|wu-wei]].
