# The Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path to Enlightenment

> *Byang chub lam rim chen mo* ("Great [Treatise on the] Stages of the Path to Enlightenment"),
> by **[[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa Blo-bzang-grags-pa]]** (1357–1419), composed 1402. The founding
> systematic treatise of the **[[gelug|Gelug]]** school of [[tibetan-buddhism|Tibetan Buddhism]],
> structuring the whole Mahayana path as a graded sequence (*lam rim*) for practitioners of three
> capacities.

**Ingested:** All three volumes of the Lamrim Chenmo Translation Committee edition (Snow Lion,
2000/2002/2004). **Volume One**: the foundation plus the small- and medium-capacity stages.
**Volume Two**: the **path of the person of great capacity** — the generation of
[[bodhicitta|bodhicitta]] and training in the **[[six-perfections|six perfections]]**. **Volume
Three**: the last two perfections — **meditative serenity** ([[samatha-vipassana|śamatha]]) and
**insight** ([[samatha-vipassana|vipaśyanā]]) — the latter being Tsong-kha-pa's great
**[[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]]** treatise on **[[sunyata|emptiness]]** in its
[[svatantrika-prasangika-distinction|Prāsaṅgika]] form.

## The Lamrim Genre and Lineage

The *lam rim* ("stages of the path") genre descends from **[[atisha|Atiśa]]**'s *Lamp for the Path
to Enlightenment* (*Bodhipathapradīpa*) and the **Kadam** tradition: the whole of the Buddhist path
is arranged as a graded sequence for practitioners of three capacities (small, medium, great).
Tsong-kha-pa's voice throughout is that of a **synthesizing scholastic** — he establishes a point,
then strings together corroborating verses from the Indian authorities (above all
**[[shantideva|Śāntideva]]**, Asaṅga's *Bodhisattvabhūmi*, Maitreya's *Ornament for the Mahāyāna
Sūtras* and *Ornament for Clear Knowledge*, Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti, and **Kamalaśīla**'s *Stages of
Meditation*), adds reasoned analysis, and closes with applied anecdotes from the Kadam masters
(Drom-dön-ba, Bo-do-wa, Sha-ra-wa, Lang-ri-tang-ba). Vehicles, he insists citing Nāgārjuna's
*Precious Garland*, are "differentiate[d] not by philosophical view but by deeds."

## Structure of Volume Three

The meditation and wisdom portion — the *śamatha* (serenity) and *vipaśyanā* (insight) sections
deferred at the end of Volume Two. Published first (Snow Lion, 2002) "because there is a great
interest in its subject matter." Two parts:

**Part One — Meditative Serenity (*śamatha*, *zhi gnas*):**
1. Serenity and Insight (their nature, why both are needed, why in that order)
2. Preparing for Meditative Serenity (prerequisites, posture)
3. Focusing Your Mind (the object, the nine mental abidings, the eight applications/five faults)
4. Dealing with Laxity and Excitement (*bying ba* / *rgod pa*; mindfulness and introspection)
5. Attaining Serenity (the marks of accomplishment; mental and physical pliancy)
6. Serenity as Part of the Path (mundane vs. supramundane paths; serenity alone is insufficient)

**Part Two — Insight (*vipaśyanā*, *lhag mthong*) — the great Madhyamaka treatise:**
7. Why Insight Is Needed · 8. Relying on Definitive Sources (*neyārtha*/*nītārtha*) · 9. The
Stages of Entry into Reality · 10. Misidentifying the Object to Be Negated · 11. Dependent-Arising
and Emptiness · 12. Rational Analysis · 13. Valid Establishment · 14. Conventional Existence ·
15. Production Is Not Refuted · 16. Not Negating Enough · 17. The Actual Object to Be Negated ·
18–21. The Svātantrika/Prāsaṅgika Distinction (misinterpretations, refutation, Tsong-kha-pa's own
interpretation, and that his critique does not damage his own arguments) · 22. Analyzing a Chariot ·
23. The Person Lacks Intrinsic Nature · 24. Objects Lack Intrinsic Nature · 25. Insight Requires
Analysis · 26. Uniting Insight and Serenity · 27. Summary and Conclusion.

## Structure of Volume One

Volume One lays the foundation and the **small-** and **medium-capacity** stages. After a prologue
adopting the Vikramaśīla method (greatness of author, greatness of teaching, how to listen/explain),
it covers: a life of **[[atisha|Atiśa]]** (Ch. 1); the four greatnesses of the *Lamp for the Path*
(Ch. 2); how to listen and teach (Ch. 3); **reliance on the spiritual teacher** (Ch. 4); the
meditation session and the defense of **analytical meditation** against the Hva-shang quietism (Chs.
5–6); the **[[precious-human-life|precious human life]]** of leisure and opportunity (Ch. 7); the
**[[three-types-of-persons|three types of persons]]** (Ch. 8) — the architecture of the whole work;
then the *small-capacity* path: **[[mindfulness-of-death|mindfulness of death]]** (Ch. 9), the lower
realms (Ch. 10), **[[refuge|going for refuge]]** and its precepts (Chs. 11–12), and
**[[karma|karma]]** and its cultivation (Chs. 13–16); and the *medium-capacity* path: the truth of
suffering (Chs. 17–19), its **origin** in the afflictions (Ch. 20), the **twelve factors of
[[dependent-origination|dependent-arising]]** (Ch. 21), the **[[renunciation|determination to be free]]**
(Ch. 22), and the path of the **[[three-trainings|three trainings]]** (Chs. 23–24).

The structure embodies, per editor Joshua Cutler, the blending of the **three capacities** (small,
medium, great) with the **three principal aspects of the path** — the determination to be free
(*nges 'byung*), the spirit of enlightenment (*[[bodhicitta|byang chub kyi sems]]*), and correct view
(*yang dag pa'i lta ba*) — a feature traditionally attributed to Mañjuśrī's instruction to
Tsong-kha-pa.

## Lineage, Antecedents, and Influence

Per D. Seyfort Ruegg's introduction to the edition: Tsong-kha-pa completed the Treatise in 1402 at
**Rva-sgreng (Reting)** monastery, an old Kadam center. He presents it as inspired by Atiśa's
*Bodhipathapradīpa* (itself partly based on Atiśa's teacher [[suvarnadvipa-serlingba|Ser-ling-ba]]),
but it is "no mere commentary … rather, an independent summa." It inaugurated a distinct genre while
having many antecedents: Indian (Śāntideva, Kamalaśīla's *Bhāvanākramas*) and Tibetan Kadam
*bstan rim* / *lam rim* works (rNgog Blo-ldan-shes-rab and Gro-lung-pa; sGam-po-pa's *Jewel Ornament*;
Po-to-ba; the *be'u bum* mind-training literature). Ruegg notes parallel graded-path works in the
**Sakya, Jonang, Nyingma, and Bonpo** traditions — the genre is cross-school — and that Buddhaghosa's
Theravāda *Visuddhimagga* is "the closest parallel" but was unavailable to Tsong-kha-pa in Tibetan.
Its **influence** runs through a lineage of Gelug *khrid yig* guides by Tsong-kha-pa's disciples and
the Dalai and Panchen Lamas (e.g. the Third Dalai Lama's *Lam rim gser zhun ma*, the First Panchen's
*bDe lam*, the Fifth Dalai Lama's *'Jam dpal zhal lung*), and the standard "four interwoven
annotations" (*mchan bzhi sbrags ma*) edition. Tsong-kha-pa also wrote a *Shorter* graded path (1415)
and the parallel *Great Treatise on the Stages of Mantra* (*sNgags rim chen mo*, 1405).

## Major Themes (Volume Two)

**Bodhicitta as the sole entrance to the Mahāyāna.** Membership in the Great Vehicle hangs entirely
on the [[bodhicitta|spirit of enlightenment]]: "Once you have generated this spirit … you are
recognized as a Mahāyāna practitioner even though you may not have generated any other good quality.
When you separate from this spirit, no matter what other good qualities you may have—such as the
knowledge of emptiness … you lapse from the Mahāyāna." Without it, "offering a universe … filled
with jewels will not be considered a bodhisattva deed"; with it, "giving a tiny morsel of food to a
crow" is one.

**Two methods for generating bodhicitta.** Tsong-kha-pa presents two complementary lineages: (1) the
**seven cause-and-effect personal instructions** descending from [[atisha|Atiśa]] — recognizing all
beings as one's mother → recollecting their kindness → wishing to repay it → love → compassion →
wholehearted resolve → bodhicitta, all founded on a preliminary of **impartiality**; and (2)
**[[exchange-of-self-and-other|exchanging self and other]]** from [[shantideva|Śāntideva]]. Great
compassion is the root, "important at the beginning, the middle, and the end."

**The ritual and maintenance of the vow.** He distinguishes **aspirational** from **engaged**
bodhicitta, gives the ritual for adopting the vow before a qualified master, and the precepts that
keep it from degenerating — including the **four dark and four light dharmas**. (He corrects "earlier
Tibetan scholars" who restricted the seven branches of worship to the Nāgārjuna lineage, citing
Atiśa that both lineages use all seven.)

**Method and wisdom; the Hva-shang polemic.** The **[[six-perfections|six perfections]]** unite the
"factor of method" (the collection of merit) and the "factor of wisdom" (the collection of sublime
wisdom); neither alone yields the non-abiding nirvāṇa. On this basis Tsong-kha-pa stages an extended
refutation of the position he assigns to the **Chinese Hva-shang (Ha-shang)** — that since any
thought binds, resting in thoughtless absorption is the whole path and the perfections are for the
dull. This is one of the most pointed sections of the volume; he sides explicitly with **Kamalaśīla**.

**The perfections in sequence.** Definite in number (six) and order, each defined as a *state of
mind* perfected, not an external outcome: generosity ("a state of mind"), ethical discipline (the
attitude of abstention / ten paths of action), patience (the three kinds, with Śāntideva's arsenal
of arguments against anger), joyous perseverance ("delight in virtue," its four powers), and an
**overview** of meditative stabilization and wisdom (the full treatment deferred to Volume Three).
The volume closes with the **four ways to gather disciples** (saṃgrahavastu).

## Major Themes (Volume Three)

**Serenity and insight must be united, and insight requires analysis.** The two are distinguished
not by their objects but by their mode — serenity (*zhi gnas*) is non-analytical one-pointed
stabilization; insight (*lhag mthong*) is "analysis with discerning wisdom." Both knowing-emptiness
serenity and not-knowing-emptiness insight are possible: "there is meditative serenity that knows
emptiness and there is insight which does not know emptiness." Serenity must be developed first
because discerning analysis becomes *actual* insight only once it can itself generate **pliancy**
(*śin sbyangs*); fixing on a single object (even emptiness) yields only serenity. The whole volume
is framed against the position Tsong-kha-pa assigns to the Chinese **[[heshang-moheyan|Hva-shang]]**
(see [[samye-debate|the Samyé debate]]): that since all conceptuality binds, thoughtless absorption
is the path. His countering thesis — that genuine *vipaśyanā* is produced by active discerning
analysis (*vicāra*), not by stopping thought — recurs from the serenity section to the conclusion.

**Serenity alone cannot liberate.** Concentration is shared with non-Buddhists; it suppresses
manifest afflictions (the mundane "calmness/coarseness" path, up to the Peak of Cyclic Existence)
but cannot uproot their seeds. Only the wisdom realizing the **two selflessnesses** (of persons and
of phenomena) severs the root of *saṃsāra*. He sorts authoritative sources by the
[[neyartha-nitartha|definitive/provisional]] (*nītārtha*/*neyārtha*) criterion — texts teaching the
ultimate are definitive — and declares his allegiance: he follows **[[buddhapalita|Buddhapālita]]**
and **[[candrakirti|Candrakīrti]]** in determining the intent of **[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]**, i.e.
the [[svatantrika-prasangika-distinction|Prāsaṅgika]] reading.

**Emptiness is dependent-arising; the [[object-of-negation|object of negation]] must be precisely
identified.** The signature contribution: before negating, one must know exactly what emptiness
denies — *intrinsic existence* (*svabhāva*), defined as a thing's "existing by way of its own
character, not posited through the force of an awareness." **Over-negation** (negating mere
existence and cause-and-effect) falls into nihilism; **under-negation** (refuting only a coarse
constructed self) leaves innate ignorance untouched. The "distinguishing feature of Madhyamaka" is
"the admissibility of all the teachings about cyclic existence and nirvana… in the absence of even a
particle of intrinsic nature." Reason's failure to *find* a thing under ultimate analysis does not
*refute* it; the three criteria for **[[two-truths|conventional existence]]** keep the conventional
world intact.

**The Prāsaṅgika method and the delineation of selflessness.** Chapters 18–21 give Tsong-kha-pa's
influential reading of the [[svatantrika-prasangika-distinction|Svātantrika/Prāsaṅgika split]]: the
Svātantrikas ([[bhavaviveka|Bhāvaviveka]]) accept that things exist "by way of their own character"
conventionally and so may use autonomous (*svatantra*) syllogisms; the Prāsaṅgikas refute intrinsic
character even conventionally and argue only by consequence (*prasaṅga*). The selflessness of the
**person** is delineated by Candrakīrti's **sevenfold chariot analysis** (ch. 22) and the
same/different reasoning (ch. 23); the selflessness of **phenomena** by refuting the four kinds of
production (ch. 24). Insight and serenity are finally united (*yuganaddha*) when analysis itself
induces pliancy (ch. 26). The work closes (ch. 27) with a recap of the whole three-capacities path
and a turn toward the Vajrayāna, dedicated to **Mañjughoṣa** and composed at Reting.

## Canon and Reception

The *Lamrim Chenmo* is not scripture (*buddhavacana*) but an **authoritative treatise** (*śāstra*):
the founding curricular text of the [[gelug|Gelug]] school and a touchstone of [[tibetan-buddhism|Tibetan
Buddhist]] practice across schools through the *lam rim* genre. Within it Tsong-kha-pa repeatedly
adjudicates disputed points *in propria persona* (e.g. what "destroying roots of virtue" by anger
means; whether using mentally-given goods is theft), departing from the wiki's usual descriptive
neutrality — and stages the **Hva-shang controversy** as settled doctrine (see
[[exchange-of-self-and-other]] and [[bodhicitta]]).
