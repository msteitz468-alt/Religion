# Janam-sakhis

The **janam-sākhīs** ("birth evidences") are the hagiographic life-narratives of **[[guru-nanak|Guru
Nanak]]** — the principal traditional source for his biography, and McLeod's central case study in
distinguishing the *historical* Nanak from the Nanak of community memory. They lie **outside the
bounds of canonical scripture** (with the [[rahit-maryada|rahit-nāmās]] and *gur-bilās* works), and
McLeod's verdict is that they "contain virtually no verifiable historical fact" yet are "invaluable as
evidence for the community's self-image and for the early Nanak-panth's practice" (agent 1 ~L474–478,
L735–750).

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Chs 1, 8.)*

## The Collections

- **Bālā** tradition — popular and miracle-heavy.
- **Purātan** tradition — more ordered; the manuscript was recovered in 1872.
- The earliest extant collection is dated **1658 CE**; Santokh Singh's *Nānak Prakāsh* (1823) is the
  standard modern synthesis, drawn from 19th-century oral tradition (agent 1 ~L499–550, L1283–1285).

## Character and Use

Built largely of *sākhīs* (anecdotes) whose motifs are often borrowed and migratory — the protecting
cobra's hood (shared with Buddhist tradition), the Multan jasmine-petal (from Sufi sources about
'Abd al-Qadir Jilani), the Lalo-and-Bhago story championing the low-caste poor, and the death-scene in
which only flowers are found beneath the shroud, divided between Hindu and Muslim devotees (agent 1
~L602–698). The famous maxim *na ko Hindu hai na ko'i musalmān* ("There is no Hindu; there is no
Muslim") is a janam-sākhī attribution; McLeod judges that "the words may not actually be those of the
historic Guru but the interpretation… is consonant with his teachings."
