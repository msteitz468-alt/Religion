# The Didache

## Textual Overview

*The Didache* ("The Teaching") — full title: *The Teaching of the Lord to the Gentiles by the Twelve Apostles* — is one of the most perplexing and important early Christian documents to survive. Although known by name to ancient writers (some of whom apparently used it as scripture), no copy was known to exist until 1873, when Philotheos Bryennios discovered Codex Hierosolymitanus in Constantinople and published the full text in 1883. Since then it has attracted scholarly attention out of proportion to its modest length (ca. 3,000 words).

**Nature**: A composite community document — not the work of a single author, but a compilation of earlier materials (possibly written and oral) from a Jewish-Christian community in Syria or Egypt, brought to its present form perhaps ca. 90–110 CE, though it may preserve materials from as early as 50 CE.

**Title note**: The shorter title (*The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*) omits "to the Gentiles" — a significant distinction, since the full title frames the document as missionary instruction for Gentile converts rather than a document addressed within an existing community.

## Structure

Three sections are evident:

**(a) Two Ways (1.1–6.2)**: "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two." Moral instruction in the form of dos and don'ts:
- **Way of Life (1.2–4.14)**: Begins with the love command and Golden Rule; the Decalogue in abbreviated form; community ethics; care for teachers and the poor; not being double-minded
- **Way of Death (5.1–2)**: A description of the vices and persons characteristic of the road to death

**(b) Church order (6.3–15.4)**: Instructions on food, baptism, fasting, prayer, eucharist, and leadership:
- **Food (6.3)**: If unable to bear the full yoke of the Lord, do what you can; avoid idol meat if possible
- **Baptism (7.1–4)**: Preference for immersion in running water; permission for standing water if necessary; and pouring (*katachein*) as a last resort "in the name of the Father and Son and Holy Spirit"
- **Fasting (8.1)**: Fast on Wednesday and Friday, not Monday and Thursday (as "the hypocrites" do — probably referring to Jewish fasting practice)
- **Prayer (8.2–3)**: The Lord's Prayer three times daily — the *Didache*'s version includes the doxology ("for yours is the power and glory forever") not found in Matthew's oldest manuscripts
- **Eucharist (9.1–10.7)**: The oldest surviving Christian eucharistic prayers. Notably: the Eucharist is described as a *thanksgiving* (*eucharistia*), not a sacrifice; bread before cup; the cup prayer over "the holy vine of your servant David"; the bread prayer over "the life and knowledge which you made known to us through your servant Jesus"; only the baptized may eat
- **Leadership (11.1–15.4)**: Traveling apostles and prophets (charismatic itinerants) vs. resident bishops and deacons (institutional officers). Apostles should stay no more than two days; a true prophet can be recognized by his conduct. Resident "bishops and deacons" are honorable and should not be despised (15.1–2) — suggesting they were being undervalued compared to charismatic prophets

**(c) Apocalyptic (16.1–8)**: Brief eschatological warning — be watchful; the last days will bring false prophets, Antichrist, and cosmic signs; then the resurrection of the saints and the Lord's coming.

## Major Themes

**The Two Ways tradition**: This framework is a **Jewish form of moral instruction** (cf. Ps. 1; Qumran Community Rule 3–4; Epistle of Barnabas 18–21) that the *Didache* has Christianized by inserting collections of gospel sayings (1.3–2.1: "Bless those who curse you"; "If someone gives you a blow on the right cheek..."). The relationship between the *Didache*'s Two Ways section and the *Epistle of Barnabas* (chapters 18–21) is complex; both probably draw on a common antecedent source ("evolved literature") rather than one being directly dependent on the other.

**Jewish-Christian milieu**: The document consistently differentiates Christian practice from Jewish practice ("hypocrites" fasting on other days; Lord's Prayer vs. other prayers) while simultaneously drawing on Jewish ethical and liturgical traditions. It represents a community still defining itself *over against* Judaism rather than having fully separated from it.

**Transitional governance**: The *Didache* captures the church at a transitional moment — itinerant charismatic apostles and prophets are honored but must be tested; resident bishops and deacons are legitimate but undervalued. The transition from the first model to the second is in progress. The *Didache*'s community is between [[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius]]'s world (fully formed monepiscopate) and the earliest Pauline communities (no resident episcopal structure).

**Earliest eucharistic prayers**: The prayers of *Didache* 9–10 are the oldest extant Christian eucharistic texts. They are notably non-sacrificial (the eucharist is pure *thanksgiving*) and structurally distinct from later liturgical forms. The cup-before-bread order in ch. 9 (vs. bread-before-cup in 1 Cor 11) may reflect an earlier tradition or a different setting (a community meal rather than the formal eucharist of ch. 10).

**Baptismal theology**: The preference for running water with permission for standing water and pouring represents the oldest surviving explicit baptismal rubric. The three-fold name ("Father, Son, Holy Spirit") is required — the earliest liturgical use of the Trinitarian formula outside Matt. 28:19.

## The Didache and the New Testament

The relationship to Matthew is the most-debated question in *Didache* scholarship:
- The *Didache* three times refers to "the gospel" (*euangelion*) but never quotes a written document as "the gospel"
- Its parallels with Matthew's Sermon on the Mount (and the Lord's Prayer) may reflect: (a) direct dependence on Matthew; (b) dependence on Matthew's sources (Q-like oral tradition); or (c) complete independence from Matthew (they share common oral tradition)
- Several scholars (Garrow, Milavec) have argued for *Didache* independence from Matthew or even *Matthew's dependence on the Didache* — positions that remain minority views but have forced reassessment of automatic dependence assumptions

There is no meaningful connection with Mark, John, Acts, or any Pauline letter. The *Didache* may represent a strand of early Christianity that developed largely independently of the Pauline mission.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**Date**: The wide range (before AD 50 to the 3rd century) reflects genuine uncertainty. The composition of the document is separated from its final editing; the materials it preserves may be older than its present form. The reference to "mountains" (9.4) suggests a Palestinian or Syrian provenance for at least some materials. Final editing is probably late 1st century.

**The Didache's Judaism**: To what degree does the community still observe Jewish practices? The text differentiates from "the hypocrites" (probably Pharisaic practice) but maintains a Jewish ethical framework. The relationship to the Birkat ha-Minim ("separation from the synagogue") is unclear.

**The "Prophets" in ch. 11–13**: True prophets who "speak in the Spirit" cannot be tested (11.7); yet their conduct can reveal them as false (11.8–12). This tension between charismatic immediacy and community accountability is unresolved and reflects real conflict in the community.

**Eucharist and the unbaptized**: *Didache* 9.5 — "Let no one eat or drink from your thanksgiving unless they have been baptized in the name of the Lord" — is the earliest explicit exclusion of the unbaptized from the eucharist. Its relationship to later eucharistic practice is significant.

**The "apostles" of ch. 11**: Are these the twelve? Traveling missionaries? The term is used broadly — anyone commissioned for missionary work. The term is not yet fixed to "the twelve" alone.

## Canon and Reception

The Didache hovered on the canon boundary: **cited as scripture by some Fathers** (echoes in Clement
of Alexandria; used by the *Didascalia* and Apostolic Constitutions) and grouped among the *disputed*
or *spurious* books in the canon-lists of Eusebius and Athanasius. It was **ultimately excluded** from
the New Testament of [[catholicism|Catholicism]], [[eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern Orthodoxy]], and
[[protestantism|Protestantism]], surviving in a single complete Greek manuscript (Codex
Hierosolymitanus, 1056). See [[nt-canon-formation|NT canon formation]].
