**Cicero's *On the Nature of the Gods*** (*De Natura Deorum*, 45 BCE) is the most important surviving
**ancient philosophical discussion of the gods** — a dialogue in which spokesmen for the three great
Hellenistic schools debate whether the gods exist, what they are, and whether they govern the world.

## The Three Positions

- **Epicurean** (Velleius) — the gods exist but are **remote and indifferent**, taking no part in the world
  or human affairs (so religion's fears are needless; cf. Lucretius).
- **Stoic** (Balbus) — the cosmos is **rational and providential**, pervaded by divine reason (*logos*); the
  traditional gods are allegories of natural forces, and the world itself is divine. (The fullest ancient
  statement of providence.)
- **Academic/Skeptic** (Cotta) — **critiques both**, arguing the philosophers cannot prove their claims;
  Cicero leaves the question artfully open while inclining to maintain traditional [[roman-religion|cult]] for
  the good of the state.

## Significance

The key text for **ancient theology as argument** — divine providence, the problem of the gods' nature, and
the relation of philosophy to civic religion. It deeply influenced Christian theologians (who borrowed its
Stoic providence and its critiques of polytheism), making it a bridge between pagan and Christian thought.
