# Mūlamadhyamakakārikā

The **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** (MMK, "Root Verses on the Middle Way") by **[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]**
(c. 150 CE) is the foundational text of the **[[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]]** ("Middle Way") school of
Indian [[mahayana|Mahayana]] Buddhist philosophy — arguably the single most influential
philosophical work in the Buddhist tradition. It is a *śāstra* (treatise), not *buddhavacana*
(the Buddha's word): a defense, not a scripture. It consists of **449 verses (*kārikā*) in 27
chapters**, each examining and refuting a particular doctrine or concept.

## Genre and the Need for Commentary

A *kārikā* is a terse mnemonic verse; the arguments are "presented in highly compressed form and so
are extremely difficult to comprehend without a commentary." Text-plus-commentary together do the
philosophical work. Four classical Indian commentaries survive — the anonymous *Akutobhayā*,
Buddhapālita's *Madhyamakavṛtti*, **Bhāviveka**'s *Prajñāpradīpa*, and **Candrakīrti**'s
*Prasannapadā* — and their divergences seeded the later **Svātantrika** (Bhāviveka) vs.
**Prāsaṅgika** (Candrakīrti) split within [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]].

## Central Thesis: All Things Are Empty

Nāgārjuna's aim is to establish the chief Madhyamaka tenet: **all things are empty
([[sunyata|śūnya]]) — devoid of intrinsic nature ([[svabhava|svabhāva]])**. The claim first appears
(undefended) in the early [[mahayana|Mahayana]] *Prajñāpāramitā* sūtras; MMK supplies its
philosophical defense. Earlier Buddhism taught only that the *person* is empty (no enduring self);
Nāgārjuna extends emptiness from the person to **everything, including the *dharmas*** themselves —
the supposedly ultimate, impartite reals of [[abhidharma|Abhidharma]] metaphysics. To say all things
are empty is to deny that *anything* has intrinsic nature.

## The Target: Abhidharma Metaphysics

The audience is fellow Buddhists who accept the Abhidharma framework: (1) **[[two-truths|two
truths]]** — conventional (*saṃvṛti*) and ultimate (*paramārtha*); (2) only **dharmas** (simple,
impartite reals) are ultimately real, while wholes like chariots and persons are *conceptual
fictions*; (3) dharmas arise by **[[dependent-origination|dependent origination]]
(*pratītyasamutpāda*)**; (4) dharmas have **intrinsic nature ([[svabhava|svabhāva]])**; (5)
suffering ends by realizing the ultimate truth. Nāgārjuna accepts that *if* there were ultimate
reals they would be dharmas with *svabhāva* — but argues there can be no such things. The dharmas
go the way of the chariot.

## Method: No Master Argument, Only Reductio

Because the claim is that nothing has intrinsic nature, there can be **no single positive "master
argument"** for it (such an argument would itself assert an ultimate truth and so smuggle in
*svabhāva*). Instead Nāgārjuna examines, one by one, the theses of those who posit ultimate reals
and shows each to be incoherent. Recurring argument patterns (catalogued by the translators):

- **Infinite regress** — H, to explain P, requires H′, H″…; a good explanation must end somewhere.
- **Neither identical nor distinct** — a relation R between x and y fails whether x and y are one or
  two (e.g., a cause cannot be the cause of itself, nor act on something wholly separate).
- **The three times** — x cannot have property P in past, future, *or* present (there being no
  durationless present).
- **Irreflexivity** — an entity cannot operate on itself (the knife that cannot cut itself, the
  fingertip that cannot touch itself).
- **Nonreciprocity** — two things cannot stand in mutual, symmetric dependence.

The point is liberative: once enough of one's theses collapse, one abandons the search for ultimate
truth — ending **hypostatization (*prapañca*, "thing-ifying")**, whose auspicious cessation *is*
nirvāṇa.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

- **Dedicatory verse — the eight negations**: dependent origination means "neither cessation nor
  origination, neither annihilation nor the eternal, neither one nor many, neither coming nor
  going" — the middle path between eternalism and annihilationism, aimed at the cessation of
  *prapañca*.
- **24.18–19 (the most celebrated verse)**: *"Dependent origination we declare to be emptiness; it
  is a dependent concept; just that is the middle path."* On Candrakīrti's reading, whatever is
  dependently originated lacks *svabhāva* and so is empty; and **emptiness is itself a "dependent
  concept" — hence itself empty** ([[sunyata#emptiness-of-emptiness|emptiness of emptiness]], cf.
  18.11). Emptiness is no reified Absolute but "a useful way of conceptualizing experience," the
  middle path avoiding both being and nonbeing.
- **Chapter 24 — the two truths objection**: an opponent charges that if all is empty, the Four
  Noble Truths, the Sangha, and the path are destroyed. Nāgārjuna replies that the opponent has
  confused the [[two-truths|two truths]]: **"All is possible when emptiness is possible; nothing is
  possible when emptiness is impossible"** (24.14) — only because things are empty (dependently
  originated) can suffering arise and cease at all. Ultimate truth cannot even be taught "apart from
  customary ways of talking" (24.10); conventional truth is the cup the thirsty must use.
- **The snake-handling warning (24.11)**: misgrasped emptiness destroys the slow-witted — taken
  either as the nihilist denial of everything or as a really-existing void with a real locus; both
  errors stem from missing the two truths.
- **25.19–20 — saṃsāra and nirvāṇa**: *"There is no distinction whatsoever between nirvāṇa and
  saṃsāra… the limit of the one is the limit of the other."* Ultimately neither state can be
  reified; conventionally they remain utterly different (the one to be sought, the other stopped).
- **18.6–12 — the graded self/nonself teaching**: even the **[[anatta|nonself (anatman)]]** teaching
  is a *skillful means*, not the ultimate truth; the Buddha taught "self," "nonself," and "neither
  self nor nonself" as graded teachings for differently situated hearers.

## Hermeneutics

MMK is the great Buddhist instance of **philosophical rationality enlisted for soteriology** —
argument as a tool to dismantle conceptual proliferation. It also rests on the
**[[neyartha-nitartha|interpretable vs. definitive (neyārtha/nītārtha)]]** ranking: teachings like
dependent origination and even nonself are conventionally/provisionally true, while emptiness states
(the absence of) the ultimate.
