Joel is the second book in the MT order of the Minor Prophets (between Hosea and Amos). It contains the most important single OT passage for the founding of the church: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (2:28-32), quoted by Peter on Pentecost (Acts 2:17-21) as the explanation for the disciples' speaking in tongues. The book also contains the OT's most sustained theological exploration of the "Day of Yahweh" concept, presenting three distinct scenarios of how this divine intervention will unfold.

Methodologically, Joel is the most distinctive of the Minor Prophets: it is increasingly recognized as **scribal prophecy** — a postexilic literary compilation that draws extensively on earlier biblical texts (Exodus, Hosea, Amos, Obadiah, Ezekiel, Zephaniah, Malachi, Isaiah/Micah) and that was designed from the outset for its canonical position between Hosea and Amos in the Book of the Twelve.

## Date and Setting

Joel provides no biographical data beyond the prophet's name and father's name (1:1). Six factors point to a postexilic, Persian-period date (probably early 4th century BCE):

1. No mention of a king (consistent with Persian-period Yehud, where political leaders were "elders")
2. References to "elders" as civil leadership (1:2, 14)
3. Presupposition of standing structures — city walls, houses with windows, granaries, functioning temple (all consistent with post-Nehemiah period; walls not rebuilt until 445 BCE)
4. Functioning Second Temple and its personnel (1:9, 13-14; 2:17) — temple rebuilt 520-515 BCE
5. Extensive awareness and citation of other biblical texts, some postexilic themselves
6. Political constellation in 3:4-8 (Tyre, Sidon, Philistines cooperating) fits the late Persian period before 343 BCE (Sidon's destruction)

## Scribal Prophecy and the Book of the Twelve

Nogalski's most important contribution: Joel should be understood as **scribal prophecy** — a sophisticated literary compilation by a temple-associated scribe who combined preexisting poetic units, added transitional passages, and wove together an elaborate network of citations from across the Hebrew Bible. The "prophet Joel" may be this scribal compiler.

**Joel as "literary anchor" of the Twelve**: Every recurring theme identified across the twelve-prophet canonical collection is present in Joel:
- Fertility/infertility of the land
- The Day of Yahweh (all its variety)
- The fate of Judah and Jerusalem
- The long arc of history from past to eschatological future
- Theodicy

This is not accidental. Joel's placement between Hosea and Amos creates lexical bridges in both directions: Joel 3:16 and 3:18 quote Amos 1:2 and 9:13; Joel 2:21-24 reverses the judgment of Hosea 9:1-2. The book "foreshadows the themes of the Twelve" — it functions as a programmatic introduction to what will unfold across all twelve prophets.

## Structure: Three Day of Yahweh Scenarios

The book presents three distinct, chronologically progressive pictures of the Day of Yahweh:

| Section | Chapters | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| Current Threats → Call to Repent | 1:1-2:17 | Multiple disasters as harbingers of impending Day; call to lament and repent |
| Near-Term Promises | 2:18-27 | Systematic reversal of every threat in ch. 1 |
| Long-Term Transformation | 2:28-3:21 | Spirit outpouring + Day of Yahweh against nations |

**The structural key**: Every threat catalogued in 1:2-2:17 is systematically reversed in 2:18-27. The locusts that devastated crops (1:4) are reversed by the promised harvest (2:25). The drought (1:10-18) is reversed by rain (2:23-24). The military threat (1:6-7) is removed (2:20). By 2:27, all threats have been answered — but the book continues with two additional promise sequences for the more distant future.

## The Locust and the Day of Yahweh (Chs. 1-2)

Chapter 1 presents an accumulating series of disasters: four waves of locusts (1:4), drought (1:10-18), and the threat of wildfire (1:19-20). These are not random calamities; they are covenant curses (Deuteronomy's blessing/curse framework) signaling that the people have broken covenant.

The prophet never specifies Israel's sin — he presumes they already know. What matters is the theological interpretation: these disasters are **harbingers of the Day of Yahweh** (1:15). And that Day is imminent (2:1-11): Yahweh himself is leading an army against Jerusalem, like no army the world has ever seen.

The call to repentance (2:12-17) is the pivot of the book:
> "Return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; and rend your hearts and not your garments. Return to the LORD your God, for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love (*ḥesed*), and he relents over disaster."

The theological basis for hope: the character of God as revealed in Exodus 34:6-7 (the divine self-declaration after the golden calf). YHWH's own nature — grace, mercy, *ḥesed* — provides the only possible ground for appeal.

## The Spirit Outpouring (2:28-32) — The Pentecost Passage

The most important passage in Joel for NT theology:

> "It will come about after this that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; and your sons and daughters will prophesy, your old men will dream dreams, your young men will see visions. Even on the male and female servants I will pour out my Spirit in those days. I will display wonders in the sky and on the earth, blood, fire, and columns of smoke. The sun will be turned to darkness and the moon to blood before the great and terrible day of the LORD comes. And it will come about that everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved; for on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there will be those who escape, as the LORD has said, even among the survivors whom the LORD calls." (2:28-32)

Four characteristics of this promise:
1. **"All flesh"**: The Spirit is poured out universally — not reserved for an elite few (kings, priests, prophets) but on sons and daughters, old and young, male and female, slave and free
2. **Prophetic manifestation**: Everyone becomes a prophetic recipient — visions, dreams, prophesying; the specialization of the prophetic office ends
3. **Cosmic signs**: Wonders in the heavens and earth signal the approaching Day
4. **Universal salvation formula**: "Everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved" — the broadest salvation promise in the OT

**NT citation**: Peter quotes Joel 2:28-32 on Pentecost (Acts 2:17-21) as the explanation for the disciples' speaking in tongues. This is the founding charter of the church: what the disciples experienced is the fulfillment of Joel's promise. The eschatological Spirit has been poured out; the "last days" have begun.

**Paul quotes**: "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Joel 2:32) in Romans 10:13 — the universal reach of the gospel.

## The Day of Yahweh Against the Nations (Ch. 3)

Chapter 3 (MT ch. 4) describes the third and final Day of Yahweh scenario: YHWH gathers all the nations to the "valley of Jehoshaphat" (meaning "Yahweh judges") for judgment because of what they have done to Judah and Jerusalem:
- Scattered the people among the nations
- Divided the land
- Sold boys for prostitutes and girls for wine
- Sold Judeans to the Greeks (3:4-8)

The weapons reversal (3:10): "Beat your plowshares into swords and your pruning hooks into spears" — the deliberate inversion of Isaiah 2:4 / Micah 4:3's vision of universal peace. The nations are called to *war*, because this is the Day when YHWH will fight against them.

**The conclusion** (3:17-21): Jerusalem becomes the holy dwelling of YHWH; the land of Judah overflows with wine and milk; Egypt becomes a desolation; Edom a wilderness; "Judah will be inhabited forever and Jerusalem from generation to generation... for YHWH dwells in Zion."

## Theological Themes

**Repentance as the hinge**: Joel's theology is fundamentally structured around repentance. It is the pivot between threat and promise. Whether the disasters become portents of YHWH's permanent judgment or occasions for his gracious intervention depends on Israel's response. YHWH's gracious character makes repentance possible and makes the outcome genuinely open.

**YHWH's graciousness**: The formula from Exodus 34:6-7 ("gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love") appears in Joel 2:13 — as in Jonah 4:2 — as the theological foundation for hope. YHWH is not mechanically locked into punishing his people. His character tends toward grace.

**The land as covenant indicator**: The fertility/infertility of the land signals the state of the covenant relationship. This Deuteronomic theology runs through the entire book and connects Joel to the wider Twelve.

## Obadiah and Jonah

The Nogalski commentary also covers Obadiah and Jonah. Brief notes:

**Obadiah** (the shortest book in the Hebrew Bible; 21 verses) announces judgment on Edom for its participation in Jerusalem's destruction. It is entirely focused on the Day of Yahweh as judgment on Israel's neighboring enemy. Nogalski: Obadiah is intricately connected to Jeremiah 49:7-22 (shared material), and its conclusion (vv. 17-21) is directly cited by Joel 2:32.

**Jonah** is the most theologically surprising of the Minor Prophets — a narrative satire about a reluctant prophet sent to the Assyrian capital, whose preaching produces genuine repentance... which infuriates the prophet. The book is about YHWH's universal mercy extending even to Israel's most feared enemy, and the theological scandal that this represents for narrow nationalists. Joel 2:13's formula ("gracious and merciful...") reappears in Jonah 4:2 — Jonah knows it, which is why he didn't want to go.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

The consonantal Hebrew text of Joel is highly stable. Three fragmentary Judean Desert manuscripts survive — **4Q78** (first half of the 1st c. BCE), **4Q82** (second half of the 1st c. BCE), and **Mur 88 / MurXII** (Wadi Murabbaʿat, 1st c. CE); among them they preserve most of the book, though none contains 1:1–9 or 2:14–25 (deterioration, not absence). [Nogalski] judges the LXX to reflect a Hebrew *Vorlage* very close to the MT: its variations are matters of morphology (*matres lectionis*), attempts to clarify obscure words, and vocalization differences, not major pluses or minuses. Two showcase cruxes: **1:8**, where the MT points *ʾly* as a feminine imperative ("wail") while the Greek reads a preposition-plus-suffix ("to me"); and **1:17**, where the MT's "the seeds shrivel beneath their husks" (two hapax legomena) diverges sharply from the Old Greek ("the heifers leap over their stalls") and Theodotion — [Nogalski] holds none of the versions offers a fully compelling rationale and prefers the MT sense ("seeds become moldy, but cows do not"). Vowel pointing is Masoretic work of ca. 600–800 CE; obscurity reflects "our own lack of knowledge," not corruption.

English versification diverges from the Hebrew after 2:27: ET 2:28–32 = MT 3:1–5; ET 3:1–21 = MT 4:1–21.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

[Nogalski] reads Joel as **scribal prophecy** — the governing framework of his NICOT commentary. On this reading, Joel 1:1–2:17 is "a theological treatise from a scribal prophet named Joel," constructed by combining preexisting poetic units and weaving in an elaborate network of citations, and Joel's disasters "look like the (lack of) blessings and (the multiplicity of) curses of Deut 28–30" — Joel functions "as the mirror image of the covenant curses" ("In Deuteronomy, the curse is proleptic; in Joel, it is happening"). The second controlling frame is the **Book-of-the-Twelve reading**: [Nogalski] treats Joel as the "literary anchor" of the collection and, at points, argues its final form may never have existed apart from its canonical location between Hosea and Amos. A distinctive move: the antecedent-less feminine pronouns "this"/"it" (1:2–3) may point *backward* to the end of Hosea (Hos 14:1–8), making Joel 1:2–3 a hinge between Hosea's call to repent (addressed to Israel/Ephraim) and Joel's parallel call (addressed to Judah/Jerusalem). [Nogalski] explicitly resists reading Joel as a "theodicy of innocent suffering," holding that the covenant-curse rhetoric and contrition-signs assume the people's guilt even though "the prophet never delineates the precise nature of the disobedience."

The **day of Yahweh**, in [Nogalski]'s frame, is "a time of Yahweh's direct intervention in history," not "a final judgment scene at the end of time" — and Joel presents three distinct days (imminent judgment on Judah, 1:2–2:11; deliverance for a purified people, 2:28–32; judgment on the nations, 3:1–21), each drawing on other day-of-Yahweh texts in the Twelve. He assesses each occurrence by **time, target, mode/tools, and provocation**.

## Scholarship and Historiography

[Nogalski] situates his reading against a century of debate over Joel's unity and date, recorded on his page as positions:

- **Composition/unity.** **Bernhard Duhm** split the book into a liturgical 1:1–2:17 and an "apocalyptic" 2:28–3:21 (which Duhm dated to the Maccabean period — "most scholars correctly rejected" the late date). **Jörg Jeremias** isolates four features setting 2:28–32 apart; [Nogalski] rebuts several (2:31 is "essentially a self-quote of Joel 2:10"; the Spirit-gifting is corporate, not individualizing). **John Barton** reads 1:1–2:27 as unified but 2:28–3:21 as "an anthology of ten distinct, originally unrelated pericopes." **Wilhelm Rudolph** and **Hans Walter Wolff** defend a largely unified composition; **Siegfried Bergler** models Joel as a "scribal exegete (*Schriftinterpret*)" bound together by citation and Exodus typology (a model [Nogalski] finds "helpful"); **Jakob Wöhrle** proposes a five-stage "rolling corpus," which [Nogalski] critiques for "editorial myopia" and for discounting citation technique. [Nogalski]'s own synthesis: Joel's unity is a **conceptual unity** in which preexisting texts were combined to serve "a theological paradigm of history," to be read sequentially from beginning to end.
- **Date and setting.** [Nogalski] holds Joel to be one of the latest books in the Nevi'im, arguing from six textual observations (no king; "elders" as Persian-period civil leadership; presupposed physical structures including a wall not rebuilt until ca. 445 BCE; a functioning Second Temple; dense citation of other, some postexilic, texts; the Tyre–Sidon–Philistia constellation of 3:4–8) that "Joel, as a book, does not reach its final form until early in the fourth century." The prophet's only biographical datum is his father's name, **Pethuel** (Greek "Bathuel"), which [Nogalski] notes "might also reflect the name of the scribal interpreter."
