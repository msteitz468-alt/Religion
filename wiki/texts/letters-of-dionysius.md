# Letters of Dionysius the Areopagite

Ten letters attributed to [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]], plus an
eleventh (Letter XI to Apollophanes). The letters address named correspondents — Gaius
(four letters), Dorotheus, Sopatros, Polycarp, Demophilus, Titus, John the Theologian
(on Patmos), and the philosopher Apollophanes — and treat themes including the Divine
Darkness, the nature of divine love, the Incarnation, hierarchical order and misconduct,
and the conversion from paganism.

## Individual Letters

### Letter I — To Gaius Therapeutes: On the Divine Darkness

**Addressee**: Gaius Therapeutes (a Therapeutes — one of the lower liturgical orders in
Dionysius's hierarchy).

**Topic**: The divine Agnosia — the "super-knowledge" that knows God by not knowing him.

**Argument**: Dionysius opens with a paradox: "Darkness becomes invisible by light, and
specially by much light. Varied knowledge, and especially much varied knowledge, makes the
Agnosia to vanish." God's "pre-eminent darkness is both concealed by every light, and is
hidden from every knowledge." Any positive vision of God reaches only creatures: "if any one,
having seen God, understood what he saw, he did not see Him, but some of His creatures that
are existing and known." God, "highly established above mind, and above essence, by the very
fact of His being wholly unknown, and not being, both is super-essentially, and is known above
mind." The paradoxical conclusion: "the all-perfect Agnosia, in its superior sense, is a
knowledge of Him, Who is above all known things." This is an early, compact statement of the
[[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]] developed at length in [[mystical-theology|Mystical
Theology]] and [[on-divine-names|On the Divine Names]].

### Letter II — To Gaius: On the Good

**Addressee**: Gaius Therapeutes (same as Letter I).

**Topic**: God as beyond the very source of Deity and Goodness.

**Argument**: How can God, Who is beyond all, be "above source of Divinity and above source of
Good"? Dionysius answers by distinguishing the *gift* of deification from its *Giver*: Deity
and Goodness are "the very Actuality of the Good-making and God-making gift, and the inimitable
imitation of the super-divine and super-good," through which creatures "are deified and made
good." But God himself is the "super-source of every source, even of the so-called Deity and
Goodness" — he is "beyond source of Divinity and source of Goodness, in so far as He is
inimitable, and not to be retained" and therefore "excels the imitations and retentions, and the
things which are imitated and those participating." The divine Good is thus not merely the
highest in a series but wholly other than any category it grounds.

### Letter III — To Gaius: On the Prayer of Jesus

**Addressee**: Gaius Therapeutes (same as Letters I–II).

**Topic**: The hiddenness of Christ's mystery, even in and after the Incarnation.

**Argument**: Dionysius opens with a definition of the "Sudden" (*exaiphnes*): that which is
"contrary to expectation, and out of the, as yet, unmanifest, is brought into the manifest."
He applies this to the Incarnation: "the Word of God suggests even this, that the Superessential
proceeded forth out of the hidden, into the manifestation amongst us, by having taken substance
as man." But the Incarnation does not dissolve the hiddenness: "He is hidden, even after the
manifestation, or to speak more divinely, even in the manifestation." The mystery of Jesus "has
been reached by no word nor mind, but even when spoken, remains unsaid, and when conceived
unknown." This establishes a Dionysian principle: the apophatic transcendence of God is not
suspended by the Incarnation but is preserved and even concentrated within it.

### Letter IV — To Gaius: On the Incarnation; Jesus as Super-Physical

**Addressee**: Gaius Therapeutes (same as Letters I–III).

**Topic**: How Jesus can be ranked "essentially with all men" while remaining beyond all.

**Argument**: Dionysius insists Jesus is "not as Author of men… here called man, but as being
in absolute whole essence truly man." He refuses both a purely human and a purely divine
categorization: "He is not man only, (neither superessential nor man only), but truly man, He
Who is pre-eminently a lover of man, the Super-essential, taking substance, above men and after
men, from the substance of men." The paradox is that the Superessential, "super-full of
super-essentiality, disregards the excess of this, and having come truly into substance, took
substance above substance." Dionysius cites miracle-evidence: the virgin birth, Jesus walking
on water ("unstable water, holding up weight of material and earthly feet, and not giving way,
by a supernatural power"). The interpretive key is "a certain new God-incarnate energy of God
having become man" — Dionysius's distinctive formula that Christ's deeds were neither purely
divine as God nor purely human as man, but a third mode proper to the God-man.

### Letter V — To Dorotheus: On the Divine Darkness

**Addressee**: Dorotheus, a Leitourgos (a liturgical minister, middle order in the
ecclesiastical hierarchy).

**Topic**: The "Divine gloom" as unapproachable light; knowledge of God as a form of
unknowing.

**Argument**: Dionysius begins with a redefinition: "The Divine gloom is the unapproachable
light in which God is said to dwell." The apparent paradox — God dwelling in darkness yet being
light — is resolved by excess: the gloom is "invisible indeed, on account of the surpassing
brightness, and unapproachable on account of the excess of the superessential stream of light."
Entry into God is achieved "by the very fact of neither seeing nor knowing, really entering in
Him, Who is above vision and knowledge." Dionysius cites Psalm 139 ("Thy knowledge was regarded
as wonderful by me; It was confirmed; I can by no means attain unto it") and Paul's language
about God: "His ways are past finding out and His Judgements inscrutable," his gifts
"indescribable," his peace surpassing every mind — Paul's knowledge of God consisted precisely
in "having known Him as being above all conception and knowledge." The letter thus positions
Letter V as a condensed parallel to [[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]] but addressed to an
intermediate rather than senior figure in the hierarchy.

### Letter VI — To Sopatros the Priest

**Addressee**: Sopatros, a priest.

**Topic**: Practical guidance on theological debate — speak for truth rather than against error.

**Argument**: Dionysius counsels against the adversarial mode of disputation: "Do not imagine
this a victory, holy Sopatros, to have denounced a devotion, or an opinion, which apparently is
not good. For neither — even if you should have convicted it accurately — are the (teachings) of
Sopatros consequently good." One may refute the false without possessing the true, since error
does not determine its opposite: "neither, if anything is not red, is it therefore white, nor if
something is not a horse, is it necessarily a man." The positive directive: "cease indeed to
speak against others, but will so speak on behalf of truth, that every thing said is altogether
unquestionable." This brief letter articulates the methodological principle expanded in Letter
VII — establish the true positively; its stability will refute all rivals automatically.

### Letter VII — To Polycarp the Hierarch

**Addressee**: Polycarp, a hierarch (bishop-level figure in Dionysius's ecclesiastical order).

**Topic**: Three sections — (1) theological method; (2) defense against Apollophanes's charge
of parricide and the eclipse at Heliopolis; (3) directive to Polycarp to press this argument
with Apollophanes.

**Section I — Method**: Dionysius states his practice of demonstrating truth positively rather
than refuting rivals serially: "when the true statement itself has been correctly laid down, and
has remained unrefuted by all the rest, every thing which is not so in every respect is cast
down of itself, by the impregnable stability of the really true." Successive refutation merely
generates successive contention; truth, once established, makes rivals collapse without direct
attack.

**Section II — The Eclipse at Heliopolis**: The Sophist Apollophanes has called Dionysius a
"parricide" for using Greek writings against the Greeks. Dionysius defends natural philosophy
as a proper ascent to the Creator — "the true philosophers ought to have been elevated to the
Cause of things created and of the knowledge of them." He then cites a cosmological event
witnessed jointly with Apollophanes at Heliopolis: an eclipse at the time of the Crucifixion
displaying three anomalies: (a) it occurred outside the appointed time for conjunction;
(b) the moon approached from the *east* (opposite to its normal western approach), covered the
sun completely, then receded back toward the east; (c) the contact and re-clearing did not take
place from the same point but "from that diametrically opposite." Dionysius describes these as
"supernatural things of that appointed time, and possible to Christ alone, the Cause of all."
At that moment Apollophanes said to Dionysius: "these things, O excellent Dionysius, are
requitals of Divine deeds."

**Section III — Directive**: Dionysius urges Polycarp to relay this argument to Apollophanes,
a man "wise in many things, and who perhaps will not disdain to meekly learn the truth, which
is above wisdom." (Note: Letter XI is addressed to Apollophanes directly, and reports his
eventual conversion.)

### Letter VIII — To Demophilus: On Minding One's Own Business and Kindness

**Addressee**: Demophilus, a Therapeutes (lowest liturgical order, below the Leitourgoi and
priests).

**Topic**: Hierarchical order; the sin of exceeding one's rank; divine clemency as the model
for hierarchical conduct. The longest letter — six sections.

**Background**: Demophilus has written to Dionysius reporting that he ejected a penitent sinner
from before a priest who was showing mercy, then "burst, contrary to permission, into the
sanctuary," drove out the priest, and wrote to Dionysius claiming to have "providentially
preserved the things sacred."

**Argument (Sections I–III)**: Dionysius grounds the rebuke in Scripture's portrait of divine
clemency: Moses's meekness, David's generosity to enemies, Job's freedom from injury, Joseph's
forgiveness of his brothers. The model is Christ himself: "He bears with those who heedlessly
reproach Him, and Himself makes excuse for them, and further promises to serve them, and runs
towards and meets even those who hold themselves aloof." Demophilus's violence against the
penitent is contrasted with this pattern of pursuit and restoration.

**Hierarchical order (Section I–II)**: "It is not lawful that a priest should be corrected by
the Leitourgoi, who are above thee, or by the Therapeutae, who are of the same rank with thee."
Each rank receives correction through the appropriate intermediary; the structure of sanctuary
architecture itself — the Holy of holies, the priests, the Leitourgoi, the Therapeutae at the
gates — symbolizes the inviolability of hierarchical mediation. Biblical examples of
unauthorized intrusion: Ozias (struck leprous for offering incense), Saul (punished for
sacrificing), the sons of Sceva (attacked by demons), those who prophesied without being sent.

**Vision of Carpus (Section VI)**: A holy man in Crete, named Carpus, who never undertook
the celebration of the mysteries without a preparatory divine vision, once prayed that God
would "by some stroke of lightning, suddenly, without mercy, cut short the lives" of two
ungodly men. Carpus was shown a vision: the two men standing at the mouth of a dark chasm,
trembling, serpents winding around their feet to drag them down, other figures co-operating in
pulling them down. Below, Carpus felt glad. Above, he saw "Jesus, with innumerable angels, in
the form of men, standing around Him" — Christ standing up from his throne, descending, and
stretching out a helping hand. Jesus spoke to Carpus: "Strike against Me in future, for I am
ready, even again, to suffer for the salvation of men; and this is pleasing to Me, provided
that other men do not commit sin." The vision's conclusion: "see, whether it is well for thee
to exchange the dwelling in the chasm, and with serpents, for that with God, and the good and
philanthropic angels." This vision is the culminating illustration of the letter's case for
clemency. (A biographical note on Titus — the addressee of Letter IX — is inserted here
between the letters at lines 558–559, recording his Cretan origin, education in Homer and
philosophy, his vision calling him to read the Hebrew scriptures, his witness of Christ's
miracles, and his eventual appointment as Bishop of Crete by the Apostles.)

### Letter IX — To Titus the Hierarch: The House of Wisdom

**Addressee**: Titus, a hierarch (the same Titus to whom Paul addressed his epistle; see
biographical note at lines 558–559).

**Topic**: Symbolic interpretation of Proverbs 9 (Wisdom's house, bowl, solid meats and
drinks) as a key to understanding divine symbolic theology as a whole. The longest theological
letter — six sections.

**Symbolic theology and its two-fold method (Sections I–II)**: Dionysius explains why Scripture
uses monstrous symbols for God (bodily generation, trees, branches, fountains, meats, drunkenness,
sleep, anger, oaths). These forms are not ends in themselves but shields for "the science
unutterable and invisible to the multitude": "we must strip them, and view them by themselves
in their naked purity." He distinguishes two modes of theological teaching: (a) "secret and
mystical," "symbolical and initiative"; (b) "open and better known," "philosophic and
demonstrative." This two-fold character also reflects the two parts of the soul: the
passionless part receives "simple, and most inward visions of the most godlike images"; the
passionate part is served by "the pre-arranged representations of the typical symbols." Paul
and "the infallible Word" confirm that visible things set forth invisible things of God.

**The Bowl (Section III)**: The "mystical bowl" of Wisdom (Proverbs 9) is decoded: "the bowl,
being spherical and open, let it be a symbol of the Providence over the whole, which at once
expands Itself and encircles all, without beginning and without end." Yet even while "going
forth to all, It remains in Itself, and stands fixed in unmoved sameness" — the bowl therefore
"also itself stands fixedly and unmovably." Wisdom builds a house in which to set the bowl:
this house signifies that God, as Author of all being and well-being, "is both an all-perfect
providence, and advances to all, and comes into being in everything, and embraces them all; and
on the other hand, He, the same, in the same, par excellence, is nothing in anything at all,
but overtops the whole."

**Solid food and liquid (Section IV)**: The "solid food" = "the intellectual and abiding
perfection and sameness" in which divine things are participated as "stable, and strong, and
unifying, and indivisible knowledge." The "liquid" = "the stream, at once flowing through and
to all" that conducts those nourished "through things variegated and many and divided, to the
simple and invariable knowledge of God." The divine Oracles are therefore likened to dew,
water, milk, wine, and honey on account of their differing nourishing properties.

**God's drunkenness and sleep (Sections V–VI)**: God is described as "inebriated" by the
"super-full, and beyond conception, and ineffable, immeasurableness, of the good cheer" —
in God, drunkenness signifies not immoderate repletion but "the super-full immeasurableness of
all good things pre-existing in Him as Cause." His "sleep" = "the superiority of Almighty God,
and His incommunicability with the objects of His Providence"; his "awakening" = "attention to
His Providential cares." The eschatological feast of the Kingdom (the King who "will come and
make them recline") is interpreted as cessation of labor, painless life, and repleteness with
"every sort of blessed goods" — Jesus himself both making them recline and ministering to them.
Dionysius notes that the lost work *Symbolic Theology* was being sent to Titus, and contains
"together with the house of wisdom, also the seven pillars investigated, and its solid food
divided into sacrifices and breads."

### Letter X — To John the Theologian on Patmos

**Addressee**: John the Theologian, Apostle and Evangelist, described as "imprisoned in the
Isle of Patmos."

**Topic**: Encouragement during John's exile; the spiritual logic of his banishment; prophecy
of his return.

**Argument**: Dionysius opens with warm greeting ("O truly beloved! And to the truly Loveable
and Desired, very beloved!") and situates John's exile within a providential pattern: "things
seen are manifest images of things unseen." The unjust banishers are "the accursed severing
themselves, and departing from the holy" — they bring their own separation upon themselves;
they are not truly imprisoning John but exposing their own alienation from God. Dionysius
refuses to attribute suffering to John himself: "I would never be so crazy as to imagine that
you feel any suffering; but I am persuaded that you are sensible of the bodily sufferings
merely to appraise them." He expresses confidence in a prophetic foreknowledge he has drawn
from John's writings: "you will both be liberated from your imprisonment in Patmos, and will
return to the Asiatic coast, and will perform there imitations of the good God, and will
transmit them to those after you."

### Letter XI — To Apollophanes the Philosopher

**Addressee**: Apollophanes, described as "Philosopher" — the same Sophist named in Letter VII
who called Dionysius a "parricide" and resisted Christianity.

**Topic**: Congratulations on Apollophanes's conversion; a retrospective account of Dionysius's
earlier testimony to him and the decisive eclipse at Heliopolis.

**Argument**: Dionysius recalls a long history of failed persuasion: he had set before
Apollophanes "with great precision" Moses, the Flood, the Exodus miracles, the prophets'
prediction of the Virgin birth; Apollophanes had replied that "he did not know whether these
things were true, and that he was entirely ignorant, even who that Moses was, and whether he
was white or black." He had also "rejected with scorn the Gospel of Jesus Christ" and called
Paul "the globe trotter, and a scatterer of words," reproaching Dionysius as a turncoat from
Greek religion.

**The decisive event**: At Heliopolis, when both were "about twenty-five," on "a certain sixth
day, and about the sixth hour," the sun became obscured by the moon outside its scheduled
eclipse time. Consulting "the table of Philip Aridaeus," they confirmed no eclipse could then
occur. Three anomalies: (a) the moon approached from the *east* rather than the west
(contrary to its normal motion); (b) it covered the whole disk; (c) after full coverage it
"went back towards the east, although that was a time which called neither for the presence of
the moon, nor for the conjunction of the sun." At this moment Apollophanes himself said, with
"inspired lips rather than with speech of human voice": "These are, O excellent Dionysius,
changes of things divine." Dionysius connected this to what Paul had told him — "I gave my
hand to the truth, and extricated my feet from the meshes of error" — and now reports that
Apollophanes has at last yielded: "thou yieldest to life when thou renounced death." He urges
Apollophanes to use against those who led him to the false the same obstinacy he previously
directed at Dionysius. The letter closes: "in the Lord Jesus, Whose Presence is my being and
my life, will henceforth die joyful, since thou also livest in Him."

(Closing doxology: "End of Dionysius the Areopagite. May his prayer be with us!")

## Key Theological Themes

### The Divine Darkness and Agnosia
Letters I and V are the corpus's most concentrated treatments of the central apophatic theme.
God is "unapproachable light" that appears as darkness by its own excess; knowledge of him
consists in "neither seeing nor knowing." The Agnosia of Letter I ("the all-perfect Agnosia,
in its superior sense, is a knowledge of Him, Who is above all known things") is the epistolary
parallel to the ascent described in [[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]].

### Incarnation and the Super-Physical
Letters III and IV develop a distinctively Dionysian Christology: the Incarnation does not
dissolve divine hiddenness (Letter III: "He is hidden, even in the manifestation") but enacts
"a certain new God-incarnate energy of God having become man" (Letter IV) — a tertium quid
beyond both divine and human categories. Letter IV's formula anticipates later Monophysite and
Miaphysite debates; the Dionysian phrase "God-incarnate energy" (*theandrike energeia*) was
cited by Monophysite theologians in Christological controversy.

### Hierarchical Order and Clemency
Letter VIII is the corpus's primary ethical-hierarchical document: a Therapeutes may not
correct a priest; correction flows through the hierarchy from above, not from below. But
hierarchical order serves the pattern of divine clemency — the Carpus vision shows that
withholding mercy from the fallen is spiritually dangerous and contrary to Christ's own example.

### Symbolic Theology
Letter IX is effectively a treatise on how Scripture's "monstrous" symbols for God function
pedagogically. The two-fold teaching (mystical/symbolic and philosophic/demonstrative) and the
two-fold nourishment (solid food and liquid) map onto the two parts of the soul and the two
levels of theological ascent. The letter also witnesses to a lost Dionysian work (*Symbolic
Theology*), which treated all the Scripture symbols systematically.

### The Heliopolis Eclipse
Letters VII and XI both narrate a shared observation of an anomalous solar eclipse at Heliopolis
at the time of the Crucifixion — three impossible features witnessed by Dionysius and
Apollophanes together. Parker treats this eclipse narrative as internal evidence for the
apostolic-era date of the corpus. The modern consensus regards it as a literary device of
pseudonymous authorship that anchors the fictional persona in the first-century apostolic
world.

## Sharpe's Translation of Letters I, II, V (1910)

A. B. Sharpe's *[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True Nature and Value]]* (1910) translates the
three letters that bear directly on the [[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]]'s doctrine of the
Divine Darkness (Sharpe titles Letter I's recipient "Caius the Monk," the same correspondent
Parker calls Gaius Therapeutes):

- **Letter I (to Caius)** — the discursive intellect not only fails to know God but *precludes*
  such knowledge: "the knowledge of Him who is above all that can be known is for the most part
  ignorance." Sharpe's footnote insists the Greek *hyperechikōs* means **transcendence, not
  privation** — the ignorance "is more, not less, than natural knowledge."
- **Letter II (to the Same)** — God is "above the very principle of divinity and of goodness,"
  i.e. above the participated gift "whereby we also are made good and divine"; being "inimitable
  and incomprehensible, He is above imitation and comprehension." Sharpe glosses: human goodness
  resembles God's "only analogically, not absolutely."
- **Letter V (to Dorotheus)** — "the divine darkness is the inaccessible light in which God is
  said to dwell"; whoever is counted worthy to know God does so "by the very fact that he neither
  sees nor knows Him," perceiving that "God is beyond all things both sensible and intelligible."
  Sharpe's note on *meta panta* corrects renderings as "in" or "with": it means "**after**" all
  things — "in which the natural comes before and is nearer than the supernatural."

These three letters, Sharpe notes, "depend on no references to persons, places or events" — which
is why he treats them (with the *Mystical Theology*) as the most securely valuable part of the
corpus regardless of authorship. See [[mysticism]] and [[apophatic-theology]].
