# Genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:1-17)

## Textual Overview

The opening pericope of [[matthew|Matthew's Gospel]] is a genealogy running from
[[abraham|Abraham]] through [[david|David]] to Jesus, arranged by Matthew himself (1:17) into
three sets of fourteen generations. Its first two words are, as [[rt-france|R. T. France]]
notes, "literally 'book of genesis'" — *biblos geneseōs*.

France suggests these opening words hint at a new creation, "comparable in effect to John's 'In
the beginning,'" but immediately concedes the limit of the suggestion: "that particular concept
of fulfillment is not clearly developed elsewhere in the gospel." He rejects reading "book of
origin" as a heading for the whole gospel or for the whole of 1:1–4:11, since LXX Gen 5:1 and 2:4
show the phrase covers only contiguous material — "but this is to put too much weight on the term
'book' rather than on the combined phrase 'book of origin.'"

France sets the pericope within a larger frame: he treats 1:1–4:11 as "a lengthy preamble to
introducing this Messiah," with the main story of the Messiah's revelation to Israel beginning
only at 4:17 with the Galilean proclamation (see [[galilee|Galilee]]). He holds that the
narrative elements of these chapters "are presented not simply as biographical information, but
as pointers, in the light of Scripture, to the theological significance of the story," and that
the section as a whole builds toward a portrayal of Jesus "as not only Son of David but also Son
of God (an issue which will be raised again explicitly in 22:41-46)."

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

**Davidic descent and throne succession.** France argues the list is royal: "Matthew thus signals
that this is a royal list, with the probable implication that the throne succession has continued
while the actual monarchy has been in eclipse" until it reached the Messiah. He reads the
tripartite division as highlighting "the two turning points," David and the exile, the middle
section running from the founding of the monarchy to its dissolution. On the titles, France
insists that for Matthew "Christ" is not a surname: "the colorless translation 'Jesus Christ' …
does not do justice to the excitement in Matthew's introduction." He sees no reason to doubt
popular first-century use of "Son of [[david|David]]" as Matthew indicates, though noting that
the earliest surviving attestation of the phrase in this sense is *Pss. Sol.* 17:21.

**Universalism through Abraham.** France suggests "son of [[abraham|Abraham]]" may carry a
universalist hint, since "Abraham was not merely the ancestor of Israel but also the ancestor of
'a multitude of nations'" and the one through whom all the families of the earth are blessed.

**The three-times-fourteen scheme.** France argues the scheme is theological reflection rather
than statistical observation, precisely because the actual history fits it only with difficulty:
"for the author this is not so much a statistical observation as a theological reflection on the
working out of God's purpose." He shows the three periods are chronologically very unequal —
Abraham to David perhaps 700–800 years, David to the exile about 400, exile to Jesus about 600 —
so the "generations" are improbably long: "Matthew's list, like some other biblical genealogies,
is selective." For the exile-to-Jesus period, Matthew has thirteen names where Luke has
twenty-two.

**Legal rather than biological paternity.** France notes that in Matthew, unlike Luke, "it will be
[[joseph|Joseph]], as the legal father, who is the lead player" in 1:18–2:23, and that
introducing him as "husband of [[mary|Mary]]" rather than as father of Jesus prepares for
1:18-25. [[mary|Mary's]] "significance in Matthew's story is purely as Jesus' mother"; no family
background is given and her experience is not related for its own sake as in Luke. France reads
"who is called the Messiah" (*legomenos*) as not pejorative here, unlike Pilate's usage at 27:17,
but as "the ordinary way of distinguishing a person with a common name" — "Jesus," the Greek form
of Joshua, being "among the commonest Jewish names in the first century."

**The main aim.** Despite the unresolved puzzles, France states the aim plainly: "its main aim is
clear enough: to locate Jesus within the story of God's people, as its intended climax," with a
special focus on the Davidic monarchy.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

The ingested source supplies no textual-critical apparatus for this pericope; the conversion of
the commentary used for this ingest dropped all footnote bodies, where such discussion would
normally sit. This section is therefore left thin rather than filled from elsewhere.

What France does treat is Matthew's *sources*. He holds that Matthew followed the summary
tradition of 1 Chr 2:1-15 and Ruth 4:18-22 for the Abraham-to-David span, and 1 Chr 3:10-17 for
the monarchy: "the names after Jacob are conveniently brought together in 1 Chr 2:1-15 and Ruth
4:18-22, and Matthew has followed that summary tradition." For most post-Zerubbabel names,
however, Matthew has no biblical source, and though patristic writers assume he had access to
preserved records, "we cannot now trace it." France reports Julius Africanus, via Eusebius
(*Hist. eccl.* 1.7.13), that Herod destroyed Jewish family archives to prevent challenges to his
own mixed pedigree.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

France's own approach here is historical-critical in method but sympathetic to Matthew's design:
he reads the genealogy as deliberate theological composition, repeatedly distinguishing what
Matthew *does* from what a chronicler would do. Three interpretive moves are characteristic:

1. **Numerical patterning read as theology, not arithmetic** — the 3×14 scheme as "theological
   reflection on the working out of God's purpose."
2. **Restraint about conjecture.** France repeatedly marks the limits of what can be shown. On
   gematria (DWD = 4+6+4 = 14) he reports the proposal but judges it conjectural, "since Matthew
   makes no explicit link between letters and numerical value as gematria usually does": "it can
   be at best a matter of conjecture whether he intended or would have recognized it." On
   fourteen as twice seven (six sevens pointing to a climactic seventh) he calls the inference
   doubtful, since "he divides Israel's history into three fourteens rather than six sevens."
3. **Preference for the mundane explanation.** France's own preferred account of the number is
   deflationary: "perhaps it is more likely that his focus on the number 'fourteen' derives from
   his observation that there were in fact fourteen names" from Abraham to David in the OT, after
   which he adjusted the king list to produce symmetry. The theological focus, on this reading, is
   the royal dimension, not the number.

See also [[typology|typology]] and [[fulfillment|fulfillment]] for the wider interpretive
apparatus of Matthew's opening chapters.

## Canon and Reception

Matthew 1:1-17 is canonical within the Gospel of Matthew for the Christian traditions holding the
New Testament canon, and no communal dispute over its standing is recorded in the ingested
source. France's remarks touch reception only obliquely: he notes that patristic writers assumed
Matthew had access to preserved genealogical records; and he observes that the popular
harmonization "that Luke's is in fact the genealogy of [[mary|Mary]]" — a widely received
Christian solution — "runs aground on Luke's explicit wording" and on Jewish patrilineal
practice, since both genealogies are explicitly Joseph's. No further reception history is
supplied by the ingest.

## Scholarship and Historiography

Positions are recorded as positions; no adjudication is offered here.

- **The four women.** France observes that mentioning mothers is unprecedented in this kind of
  list (fourteen appear in 1 Chr 2 alone), and that Tamar and the wife of Uriah "came to Matthew
  already in the Chronicles lists"; "only Rahab, as the mother of Boaz, does not derive directly
  from Matthew's OT sources." On Rahab he flags a chronological difficulty: Rahab of Jericho is at
  least a century too early to be Boaz's mother, but since Matthew names her without
  identification, "it is generally assumed (and it can be no more than an assumption) that despite
  the chronological problem he had Rahab of Jericho in mind." France states that "probably all
  four of them were non-Israelite": Tamar and Rahab [[canaanites|Canaanites]], Ruth a Moabite,
  Bathsheba the wife of a Hittite.

  He then weighs two explanations. The customary "disreputable mothers prepare the reader for an
  irregular pregnancy" theory he weakens, since "embarrassment over their sexual activities is
  primarily a modern phenomenon" — in Jewish tradition Tamar, Rahab and Ruth were heroines, and it
  was David rather than Bathsheba who was stigmatized. He judges "more appropriate to Matthew's
  own context" the view that the four foreign women prepare for non-Israelites following Israel's
  Messiah, foreshadowed in the [[magi|magi]] and climaxing at 28:19 (see [[gentiles|Gentiles]]) —
  but he does not close the question: "he gives us no overt indication of it, so that, as with the
  significance of 'fourteen,' we cannot go beyond conjecture."

- **The omitted kings — an internal discrepancy.** France counts five kings omitted between David
  and Jehoiachin: "Matthew has had to omit five of the actual kings recorded in the OT history" —
  Ahaziah, Joash, Amaziah (plus Athaliah), and the brothers Jehoahaz and Jehoiakim. Elsewhere in
  the same discussion he refers instead to "the omission of three kings between Joram and Uzziah."
  **[DISCREPANCY]** The two counts are recorded here as they stand in the commentary rather than
  harmonized; the digest of this ingest flags the tension explicitly and the wiki does not resolve
  it.

  France rejects explaining the omissions purely as confusion of similar Greek names ("two happy
  accidents"), since even on that account "the three groups are not equally balanced": the third
  group has only twelve names after Jehoiachin.

- **Matthew versus Luke.** France notes the two genealogies coincide only from Abraham to David
  plus Shealtiel and Zerubbabel. He rejects the view that either evangelist simply invented names,
  on the ground that "genealogy was too valued a pursuit in the Jewish world to make such a
  cavalier attitude plausible," and that evidence suggests genealogies were preserved and
  consultable. He floats — and immediately disowns — the hypothesis that Luke gives a biological
  family tree (through Nathan) while Matthew traces throne succession: "it is of course totally
  incapable of proof."

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **The origin of "fourteen."** Gematria on DWD; twice-seven / six-sevens; France's own
   observation-plus-adjustment account. France judges the first conjectural, the second doubtful,
   and prefers the third.
2. **Why four foreign women?** The "irregular unions" reading versus the Gentile-foreshadowing
   reading. France leans to the second but explicitly declines to move beyond conjecture.
3. **The relation to Luke's genealogy.** France rejects the Marian-line harmonization and the
   invention hypothesis, and calls his own dynastic-versus-biological suggestion unprovable.
4. **The count of omitted kings.** Five, or three between Joram and Uzziah — see the discrepancy
   flagged above.
5. **How far does "book of origin" reach?** France restricts it to the genealogy against those
   who read it as a title for the gospel or for 1:1–4:11.
