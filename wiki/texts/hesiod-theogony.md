**Hesiod's *Theogony*** ("birth of the gods," c. 700 BCE) is the foundational text of
[[greek-religion|Greek]] theology — the nearest thing the Greeks had to a **systematic account of the
origin and genealogy of the gods**. With Homer, Hesiod "gave the Greeks their gods" (Herodotus).

## The Genealogy of the Cosmos

The *Theogony* narrates the generation of the divine world from the beginning:

- First **[[chaos|Chaos]]** (the Void), then **[[gaia|Gaia]]** (Earth), **Tartarus**, and **[[eros|Eros]]**.
- Gaia bears **[[uranus|Uranus]]** (Sky), the mountains, and the sea; from Gaia and Uranus come the
  **[[cronus|Titans]]**, the Cyclopes, and the Hundred-Handers.
- **[[cronus|Cronus]] castrates Uranus** and rules; he swallows his children until **[[zeus|Zeus]]**
  overthrows him in the **Titanomachy**, establishing the reign of the **[[zeus|Olympians]]**.

It also catalogues the personified powers — Night and her children (Death, Sleep, the [[fate-moirai|Fates]],
Nemesis, Strife), the rivers, and the divine order Zeus imposes.

## Works and Days

Hesiod's companion poem, **Works and Days**, gives the myth of the **Five Ages** (Golden to Iron), the
**Pandora** story, and a farmer's almanac of piety and justice (*dikē*) under Zeus.

## Significance

The closest Greek approach to a **doctrinal theology** — a structured cosmogony that organized the
otherwise unsystematic body of myth, and the reference point for every later Greek and Roman handling of the
gods' origins.

## The Succession Myth and the Titanomachy (in detail)

The spine of the *Theogony* is a **violent succession of divine kings**, each son overthrowing his father —
a structure with close Near Eastern parallels (the Hurrian-Hittite *Kumarbi* cycle).

**Uranus and the castration.** [[uranus|Uranus]] (Sky) lay each night upon [[gaia|Gaia]] (Earth) and fathered
the [[cronus|Titans]], the Cyclopes, and the Hundred-Handers — but, hating his children, he **stuffed them
back inside Gaia**, who groaned in pain. She fashioned a great flint **sickle** and begged her sons to act;
only the youngest, **[[cronus|Cronus]]**, dared. Lying in wait, he **castrated his father** and flung the
genitals into the sea — from the bloody drops on the earth sprang the Furies, the Giants, and the Meliae;
from the sea-foam rose **[[aphrodite|Aphrodite]]**. Sky was forever separated from Earth, and Cronus reigned.

**Cronus and the swallowed children.** Warned by Gaia and Uranus that his own son would overthrow him, Cronus
**swallowed each child** [[hestia|Hestia]], [[demeter|Demeter]], [[hera|Hera]], [[hades|Hades]], and
[[poseidon|Poseidon]] at birth. When [[zeus|Zeus]] was due, Rhea hid him on Crete and gave Cronus a **stone in
swaddling-clothes** to swallow instead. Grown in secret, Zeus forced Cronus to **disgorge** his siblings
(and the stone), and freed the imprisoned Cyclopes, who in gratitude forged the **thunderbolt**.

**The Titanomachy.** There followed a **ten-year war** between the Olympians (on Mount Olympus) and the
Titans (on Mount Othrys). The tide turned when Zeus released the **Hundred-Handers** from Tartarus, who hurled
three hundred boulders at a time; amid earthquakes and the blaze of Zeus's bolts, the Titans were crushed and
**cast down into Tartarus**, sealed behind bronze gates and guarded by the Hundred-Handers.

**Typhon, the last challenge.** Gaia, in fury at her defeated Titan children, bore the monstrous
**[[zeus|Typhon]]** — a hundred dragon-heads belching fire. In a final, cosmos-shaking duel Zeus blasted him
with the thunderbolt and pinned him beneath **Mount Etna**, whose eruptions are his struggles. With order
secured, the gods pressed Zeus to be king, and he apportioned honors among them.
