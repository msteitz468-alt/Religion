# The Letter to the Colossians

A short letter addressed to the church in Colossae, a city in the Lycus Valley in the Roman province of Asia (modern Turkey), near Laodicea and Hierapolis. Colossae was a second-tier city, smaller and less significant than its neighbors. Paul had not visited Colossae personally — the church was founded by Epaphras (1:7; 4:12), apparently one of Paul's coworkers who came to faith during Paul's Ephesian ministry. The letter is written jointly by Paul and Timothy (1:1).

Colossians is marked by an anti-heretical purpose absent from Ephesians: it confronts a "philosophy" (2:8) — involving Torah observance (food laws, Sabbath, calendar), ascetic bodily practices, and mystical claims to visionary revelation — whose proponents apparently argued that Christ was insufficient for full spiritual access. Paul's response is a Christological escalation: Christ is not one heavenly power among many but the one in whom "the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (2:9), through whom all things were created (1:16), and through whose cross the powers were disarmed (2:15).

## Authorship

Colossians is generally accepted as Pauline even among scholars who reject Ephesians' Pauline authorship. Some (Käsemann, Lohse, Pokorný) argue for pseudonymity on stylistic and theological grounds, but this is a minority position. The dominant critical position: most scholars accept Colossians as Pauline or deutero-Pauline.

[[frank-thielman|Thielman]] treats Colossians as Pauline (c. AD 62), composed during the Roman imprisonment described in Acts 28, shortly before Ephesians. [[scot-mcknight|McKnight]] also accepts Pauline authorship but from a distinctive methodological angle: he attacks the "known-to-unknown" comparative method underlying pseudo-Pauline hypotheses, arguing there is no fixed baseline of "pure Paul" since every Pauline letter was co-authored and situationally varied. Colossians is "Pauline as much as Galatians." McKnight favors an Ephesian imprisonment origin (c. AD 52–57) based on the distance argument for Onesimus, Aristarchus's arrest in Ephesus (Acts 19:29), and Philemon 22's implied proximity.

See [[colossians-authorship|Colossians: Pauline or Deutero-Pauline?]] for the full debate.

## The Colossian Opponents

The opponents are identified through their practices described in 2:8–23: dietary regulations (2:16), festival observance, new moons, and Sabbath (2:16); "worship of angels" (2:18); ecstatic visions (2:18); and ascetic bodily disciplines ("do not handle, do not taste, do not touch," 2:21). Paul calls their teaching "the elemental spiritual forces (*stoicheia*) of this world" (2:8, 20).

The identity of the opponents is one of the most contested questions in Colossians scholarship. The leading positions: (1) Jewish-Christian halakic mystics who combine Torah-observance with asceticism aimed at heavenly ascent and angelic-liturgy participation (McKnight: "halakic charismatics"; Francis, Sappington, Bandstra); (2) Phrygian Jewish syncretism combining halakic Torah practice with local angel veneration (Arnold); (3) broadly Jewish apocalyptic-ascetic piety without a mystical dimension (Dunn). Most scholars accept the Jewish character of the opposition; earlier proto-Gnostic and purely pagan readings have been largely abandoned. See [[colossian-opponents|The Colossian Opponents ("Colossian Philosophy")]].

## Relationship to Ephesians

The verbal relationship between Colossians and Ephesians is the most extensively discussed feature of both letters. Mitton's calculation: 26.5% of Colossians' vocabulary appears in Ephesians. The relationship is complex:

- **Not direct copying**: Apart from the Tychicus commendation (Col 4:7–9 // Eph 6:21–22, sharing 32 words with two long stretches of consecutive agreement), extended verbatim correspondence is rare.
- **"Mind saturated"**: Mitton's judgment — that the author of Ephesians had "his mind saturated with the contents of Colossians" — describes the relationship better than copying.
- **Conceptual divergences**: *Mystērion* in Colossians = consistently Christ himself (1:26–27; 2:2); in Ephesians it varies. *Oikonomia* in Colossians = Paul's "assignment"; in Ephesians = God's "providential dispensation." Col 2:19: Christ as head of the *cosmos*; Eph 4:15–16 uses the same vocabulary with Christ as head of the *church*.

The dominant scholarly view: Colossians was written first; Ephesians was written later (either by Paul or a Pauline disciple) with Colossians' themes in mind. See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

## Narrative Context: The Return of Onesimus

Colossians is structurally and narratively tethered to the **[[philemon|Letter to Philemon]]**. Paul explicitly names **[[onesimus|Onesimus]]** as "one of you" (Col 4:9), confirming that the runaway slave is a Colossian returning home alongside the envoy **[[tychicus|Tychicus]]**. The two letters were delivered together, making the public theological claims of Colossians—such as the command for masters to treat their slaves with equality and justice (Col 4:1)—the communal backdrop against which Philemon would have to receive Onesimus back. This grounds the high, cosmic theology of Colossians in the immediate social crisis of slavery and reconciliation.

## Structure

**McKnight's Outline:**

**I. Introduction (1:1–2:5)**
- Salutation (1:1–2)
- Thanksgiving (1:3–8)
- Intercession (1:9–23):
  - The Prayer (1:9–12)
  - Digression on the Messiah's Work (1:13–23): Redemption (1:13–14) → Preeminence (1:15–20) → Reconciliation (1:21–23)
- Authorizing Biographical Disclosure (1:24–2:5)

**II. Doctrinal Correction (2:6–3:4)**
- The Essential Exhortation (2:6–7)
- Correction of False Religion (2:8–19): Philosophical Problems (2:8–15) + Practical Manifestations (2:16–19)
- Exhortation to True Religion (2:20–3:4): in light of corporate death (2:20–23) + corporate resurrection (3:1–4)

**III. Practical Exhortation (3:5–4:6)**
- Old and New Existence (3:5–17)
- Household Regulations (3:18–4:1) — with Excursus on the Haustafeln
- Ecclesial Instructions (4:2–6)

**IV. Conclusion (4:7–18)**

## Major Themes

### 1. The Supremacy of Christ — The Christological Hymn (1:15–20)

The theological center of the letter. Two strophes, each opening with a title and followed by a causal clause:

**Strophe 1: Supreme in Creation (1:15–17)**
- "The Son is the image (*eikōn*) of the invisible God, the firstborn (*prōtotokos*) over all creation" (1:15)
- Reason: "in him all things were created... through him and *for* him" (1:16)
- Recapitulation: "He is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (1:17)

**Strophe 2: Supreme in Redemption (1:18–20)**
- Anticipation: "He is the head of the body, the church" (1:18a)
- "The beginning and the firstborn from among the dead, so that in everything he might have supremacy" (1:18b)
- Reason: "God was pleased to have all his fullness (*plērōma*) dwell in him, and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether things on earth or things in heaven, by making peace through his blood, shed on the cross" (1:19–20)

McKnight's reading of *eikōn* (1:15): not so much "Adamic Christology" (Jesus = Second Adam) but *christological anthropology* — Christ is the prior Eikōn-template; Adam was modeled *on* Christ (not vice versa). The one "through whom all things were created and for whom they exist" is the prior referent; Adam is a secondary instance. Cf. Philo and the Wisdom tradition's identification of the divine Word/Wisdom as the archetypal image according to which humanity was made.

McKnight on *prōtotokos*: designates ontological superiority (preeminence, not temporal birth-sequence; cf. Ps 89:27 of the Davidic king). Since v. 16 makes Christ the Creator of *all* things, the genitive "of all creation" is objective/comparative: "firstborn *over* all creation," not "first *among* the created."

The hymn's claim: Christ is simultaneously the *source* (in him), *means* (through him), and *telos* (for him) of all creation — and simultaneously the beginning of the new creation, the head of the reconciled cosmos. See [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]] for comparison with Johannine and Philonic parallels.

### 2. The Defeat of the Powers (2:15)

"And having disarmed (*apekdysamenos*) the rulers and authorities, he made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross."

This is the most explicit statement of *Christus Victor* atonement theology in Paul. The cross, which appeared to be the powers' triumph over Christ, was actually their disarmament and public humiliation. The Greek *apekdysamenos* uses the same root as 2:11 ("the body of the flesh stripped off") — Christ stripped the powers of their weapons (their claim to spiritual sufficiency, their dominion over the cosmos) as he died.

McKnight's reading: the powers in Col 1:16 are identified as part of *all things* created by and for Christ. In 2:15, those same powers are *disarmed* at the cross — the event toward which all creation was headed (*for him*, 1:16). The cross thus executes the christocratic purpose for which creation was made. The powers are not merely spiritual beings but, in McKnight's "polluted structures" reading, the *systemic* forms of cosmic rebellion — earthly, institutional manifestations of angelic opposition. The cross defeats the system, not just the beings. See [[principalities-powers|Principalities and Powers]].

### 3. The Mystery Disclosed (1:26–27; 2:2–3)

"The mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed to his saints. To them God chose to make known how great among the Gentiles are the riches of the glory of this mystery, which is *Christ in you, the hope of glory*." (1:26–27)

In Colossians, the mystery's primary content is *Christ himself* — specifically the indwelling Christ among the Gentiles. "God's mystery, which is Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (2:2–3). McKnight: the mystery's Christological content (Christ *in* you) is the mission-grounding claim — because God indwells the Son fully and bodily (1:19; 2:9), and because the mission of that indwelt Son is cosmic reconciliation, the mystery of Paul's mission is to incorporate the Gentiles into the one family of God under the ascended Lord Jesus. See [[mysterion|The Mystery (*Mystērion*) in Paul]].

### 4. The Colossian "Philosophy" and Paul's Response (2:6–23)

Paul's strategy against the halakic mystics is not primarily argumentative but Christological:
- The *plērōma* (fullness of deity) dwells *sōmatikōs* (bodily) in Christ (2:9) — no supplementary spiritual access route to divine fullness is needed or available
- Believers are already "circumcised" in Christ (2:11), "buried and raised" in baptism (2:12), "made alive" through forgiveness (2:13)
- The Torah practices (food laws, calendar, Sabbath) are "shadows" of the reality; the "body" (substance) is Christ (2:17) — a salvation-historical hermeneutic, not a Platonic denigration of law
- The ascetic program fails to accomplish what it promises: "they lack any value in restraining sensual indulgence" (2:23) — the moral victory they seek is available only in co-death and co-resurrection with Christ

The opponents are not simply wrong; they are making the Colossians submit to *stoicheia* they have already died to in baptism. McKnight: "The death of Christ spelled the end of all such systems; his death and resurrection provided the key insight into the reality of the world."

### 5. The New Life in Christ (3:1–17)

The positive counterpart to the anti-heretical section. Co-resurrection with Christ grounds the "seek the things above" imperative (3:1–4). The "put off / put on" pattern (3:5–17) describes the life of one who has died and been raised:
- Put off: sexual immorality, impurity, lust, greed, anger, malice, slander, filthy language, lying (3:5, 8–9)
- Put on: compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, patience, forgiveness, love (3:12–14)
- The inner transformation: "the new self (*neon anthrōpon*), which is being renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator" (3:10) — Christoformity as ongoing renewal in the image of God

McKnight on 3:10–11: the renewal is *christo-anthropological* — the image of the Creator is the image that Christ bears (1:15), so renewal in that image is renewal toward Christlikeness. The theological elimination of ethnic/social barriers at 3:11 ("there is no Greek and Jew... slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all") is the ecclesiological form of what the hymn claimed cosmologically.

### 6. The Household Code (3:18–4:1)

The Colossian household code is the *earliest* NT example of the Haustafel tradition, addressing three household pairs: wives/husbands (3:18–19), children/fathers (3:20–21), slaves/masters (3:22–4:1). The Colossian code is briefer than Ephesians' parallel (Eph 5:22–6:9) and lacks Ephesians' Christological elaboration of the husband-wife relationship (the Christ-church typology of Eph 5:25–33 is absent). The mutual submission framing of Eph 5:21 has no direct parallel in Colossians; instead, the Colossian code is unified by the repeated phrase "in the Lord" (3:18, 20, 23, 24).

**McKnight's reading**: The code does not establish hierarchy; it subverts the Roman honor-shame structure through Christoformity. The wife's submission is grounded in "what is fitting in the Lord" (3:18) — i.e., Christoform living — not in the husband's authority or in any creation-order ontology. The husband is never instructed to *lead*; he is instructed to *love* (3:19) and not to be harsh. McKnight explicitly charges complementarian readings with being "more Aristotelian than Christian." The critical equality claim is 4:1 — masters are to provide what is "right and fair (*isotēta*)" because they have a Master in heaven — which McKnight reads as a justice principle that subverts the master-slave power relationship.

**Thielman's reading (Ephesians)**: complementarian; the husband has authority (*kephalē* = authority not source); the wife's submission is grounded in the creation order; the mutual submission of Eph 5:21 introduces but does not equalize the asymmetry.

This evangelical-vs-evangelical disagreement is one of the significant live hermeneutical disputes in Pauline household code studies. See [[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]] for the full debate.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. The Christological Hymn: Pre-Pauline or Pauline?**
Is 1:15–20 a pre-Pauline hymn that Paul incorporated (and possibly modified, e.g., adding "of the church" in 1:18a)? Or is it Pauline composition throughout? Most scholars accept some pre-formed tradition lies behind the passage, but the degree of redaction is debated. McKnight acknowledges the tradition but does not emphasize its pre-Pauline character.

**2. *Plērōma* (1:19; 2:9)**
The "fullness" that dwells in Christ. Against the Gnostic use of *plērōma* for the totality of divine emanations, Paul insists the fullness dwells "bodily" (*sōmatikōs*, 2:9) — in the incarnate and risen Christ, not distributed across a spiritual hierarchy. This is the direct counter-claim to the halakic mystics' implied view that divine fullness must be accessed through additional spiritual practices.

**3. "Firstborn of All Creation" (1:15)**
The Arian controversy centered here: does *prōtotokos pases ktiseōs* mean Christ was the first being created? The orthodox response: *prōtotokos* designates preeminence and priority, not temporal sequence (cf. Ps 89:27). The argument from 1:16 clinches this: the one through whom *all things were created* cannot himself be among the things created.

**4. *Apekdysamenos* in 2:15**
"Having disarmed/stripped" — subject is ambiguous: God stripped the powers (Lohse), or Christ stripped himself of his flesh (Lightfoot's older reading). The majority view: God or Christ stripped the powers of their weapons. The resonance with 2:11's stripping off of "the body of flesh" ties the language to the cross as a stripping/disarming event.

**5. "Worship of Angels" (2:18)**
Three interpretations: (a) objective genitive — worshiping angels; (b) subjective genitive — angels' worship, i.e., joining angelic liturgy through mystical ascent (Francis, McKnight); (c) descriptive genitive — angel-like worship. McKnight: the context (combined with 2:23's lack of any mention of angels in the restatement) favors the third option — ascetic practices designed to achieve a kind of angel-like purity and worship-experience.
