# Institutes of the Christian Religion

*Institutio Christianae Religionis* (French: *Institution de la religion chrétienne*) — [[john-calvin|John
Calvin]]'s ordered summary of Christian doctrine, the single most influential systematic work of the
Reformed Reformation. As [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Bruce Gordon's biography of the
book]] stresses, it was **not one book but a twenty-year project**: Calvin revised and vastly
expanded it across successive Latin and French editions from 1536 until the definitive 1559 Latin
text, each edition "an instantiation" mapping the development of his thought.

## Purpose and genre

Calvin's stated aim (1559 letter to the reader): "to prepare and instruct candidates in sacred
theology for the reading of the divine Word… I believe I have so embraced the sum of religion in all
its parts, and have arranged it in such an order, that if anyone rightly grasps it, it will not be
difficult for him to determine what he ought especially to seek in Scripture." The *Institutes* was
designed to be read **side by side with Calvin's biblical commentaries**: the commentaries follow the
grain of the text and explain the words; the *Institutes* gathers the *loci* (topics, doctrine) found
in scripture into their right order. Calvin disliked the word "theology" for its medieval resonance.

The book's purpose shifted by audience: from 1539 the **Latin** editions were written to educate
pastors; the **French** editions (and later English, Dutch, German) were for educated lay readers,
with classical and patristic references excised but the doctrine undiluted.

The work is **rhetorical in the ancient sense** — like a sermon, it aims to move the whole person to
transformation and union with Christ, not merely to secure intellectual assent. Gordon calls it "an
extended hymn of praise… to a saving God," "a spiritual and intellectual autobiography," and warns
that "the gravest misreadings arise from the belief that with the *Institutes* in hand one is holding
a textbook."

- **1536 (Basel)**: printed by Thomas Platter; six chapters modelled on Luther's 1529 Catechism (Law,
  Apostles' Creed, Lord's Prayer, sacraments), plus the five rejected "sacraments," Christian freedom,
  and spiritual/temporal authority. Opens: "the whole of sacred doctrine consists of two parts,
  knowledge of God and of ourselves." Prefaced by the **dedicatory letter to King Francis I** (kept in
  all later editions), pleading that French evangelicals were loyal subjects, not seditious Anabaptists.
- **1539 (Strasbourg)**: doubled in size; predestination, providence, and union with Christ emerge
  with clarity, shaped by Calvin's pastoral work in Strasbourg, his 1540 Romans commentary, and
  [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Melanchthon's *Loci communes*]]. The **French translation of
  1541** made Calvin the most widely read Protestant author in France; the Paris parlement banned it
  in 1542 (which boosted sales; colporteurs smuggled pocket editions into France).
- **1559 (Geneva)**: written by a man who believed he was dying; headed "Now first arranged in four
  books… so that it can almost be regarded as a new book." Vastly expanded and **rearranged**, but
  Calvin insisted the argument and doctrine were essentially unchanged.

Models Calvin drew on: the title *Institutes* from **Lactantius**; Melanchthon's *Loci communes*;
Peter Lombard's *Sentences*; and a deep admiration for Bernard of Clairvaux and (as his "spiritual
and intellectual mentor") [[augustine|Augustine]].

## Structure of the 1559 edition (four books)

A **Trinitarian** arrangement (not original to Calvin, shared with his contemporaries), following the
order of topics derived from Paul's Letter to the Romans and the articles of the Apostles' Creed:

1. **Book 1 — God the Creator.** Knowledge of God and of self (the "twofold knowledge"); scripture as
   "spectacles" that correct our fallen blindness; God's *accommodatio* (he speaks as a nurse lisps to
   an infant).
2. **Book 2 — God the Redeemer.** The Fall and conscience; Christ the **Mediator** in his threefold
   office of **prophet, king, and priest** (*munus triplex*) — distinctively, Christ receives the
   faithful "as his companions" in the priestly office.
3. **Book 3 — The Holy Spirit and the reception of grace.** The "fruits" of the Spirit in order: faith
   and repentance; [[justification|justification]] and sanctification; prayer; and the doctrine of
   election/**predestination** as the ground of **assurance**. Contains Calvin's treatment of the
   Christian life ("We are not our own… we are God's") and of **union with Christ** (faith as "an empty
   vessel"; no mystical annihilation of the person).
4. **Book 4 — The Church.** "The external means or aids" by which God brings and keeps believers in
   Christ: the church (a "mixed body" of elect and reprobate — Augustine's wheat and tares), the
   ministry of Word and sacrament, the Lord's Supper as the supreme moment of unity, and church
   discipline.

## Key interpretive cruxes

- **Knowledge (*cognitio*).** Not finite information but "a continuous state of awakening" — an active,
  lifelong, God-initiated progression from self-knowledge to knowledge of God. The opening "two parts,
  knowledge of God and of ourselves" survives verbatim from 1536.
- **[[predestination|Double predestination]].** From 1539 Calvin emphatically taught that God
  predestined the **elect to life and the reprobate to death** before creation, apart from any human
  merit — separating him from his friends [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Melanchthon and
  Bullinger]], who accepted election but rejected the reprobation half. Gordon insists predestination
  is **not** the "cornerstone" of the *Institutes* (a later caricature), but a **pastoral** doctrine of
  assurance, yet it remained the teaching most associated with Calvin. Opponents charged it made God
  "the author of sin," which Calvin angrily and repeatedly denied, grounding everything in the divine
  will as "the highest rule of righteousness."
- **[[divine-providence|Providence]].** Distinct from but linked to predestination: all things, good
  and bad, are ordained to the believer's benefit and to be received in gratitude and patience.
- **Atonement and election.** Christ's death "fully atoned for the sins of the world," but its benefits
  extend only to the elect.

## Canon and Reception

The *Institutes* is **not scripture** in any tradition — Calvin subordinated it entirely to the Bible.
It functions as the foundational **confessional/authoritative standard** of the Reformed tradition
([[calvinism|Calvinism]] / Reformed / Presbyterian churches), "sitting in the library of any educated
Protestant cleric." It was **condemned by the
Catholic Council of Trent** (which regarded Calvin as the arch-heretic among Protestants), hated by
most Lutherans, and even publicly burned in Switzerland by Calvin's *Protestant* opponents over double
predestination. Its long, contested afterlife — across the Enlightenment, America, apartheid South
Africa, and China — is traced in [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Gordon's biography]] (Scopes
B–C pending).
