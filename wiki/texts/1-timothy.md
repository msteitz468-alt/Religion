# First Letter to Timothy

## Overview

First Timothy is the longest of the three Pastoral Epistles, addressed to Paul's trusted coworker Timothy, who has been left at Ephesus to address doctrinal and organizational problems there (1:3). Its occasion is twofold: the presence of false teachers promoting "myths and endless genealogies" (1:4), probably a form of proto-gnostic Jewish speculation, and the need for stable church structures (leadership, worship practices, care of widows) in the face of Paul's continued absence.

The letter addresses three primary concerns: false teaching and its refutation (chs. 1, 4, 6); church order — worship (ch. 2) and leadership qualifications (ch. 3); and pastoral care for specific groups — widows, elders, slaves (chs. 5–6).

The authorship of 1 Timothy is the central question in scholarship. It presents itself as Pauline (1:1) and is accepted as such by patristic sources from Polycarp onward, but since the 19th century a significant majority of Western scholars has argued for pseudonymity. See [[pastoral-epistles-authorship|Pastoral Epistles: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

## Historical Situation

Ephesus was one of the most significant cities in the Pauline mission — Acts records a lengthy ministry there (Acts 19–20). Paul had earlier charged the Ephesian elders to "watch out for yourselves and for all the flock" against "savage wolves" who would arise after his departure (Acts 20:28–30), a warning that 1 Timothy may be seen as fulfilling in writing.

The false teachers in the letter (1:4, 6–7; 4:1–3; 6:3–5, 20–21) are difficult to identify with precision. Prominent features include: attention to "myths and genealogies" (possibly Jewish-gnostic speculation); ascetic prohibitions on marriage and foods (4:3); "what is falsely called knowledge" (*gnōsis*, 6:20); focus on disputes, controversies, and "empty talk." Yarbrough resists over-specification of the false teaching and notes that Porter's study shows close structural parallels between 1 Timothy's setting and 1 Corinthians'.

## Structure

1. **Greeting** (1:1–2) — Paul-to-Timothy framing; "grace, mercy, and peace"
2. **Charge Regarding False Teaching** (1:3–20) — commissioning of Timothy; Paul's autobiographical doxology (1:12–17); "Christ came to save sinners, foremost of whom I am"
3. **Instructions for the Church** (2:1–3:16) — worship, prayer, gender roles, overseer and deacon qualifications; creedal fragment "the mystery of godliness" (3:16)
4. **The Good Minister** (4:1–16) — apostasy prediction; warning on ascetic false teaching; Timothy's own formation and example
5. **Care for Specific Groups** (5:1–6:2a) — widows (extensive); elders; slaves
6. **Final Exhortation and Warning** (6:2b–21) — false teachers and love of money; "fight the good fight"; final charge to Timothy

## Major Themes

### 1. The Primacy of God

Yarbrough argues that the PE are *primarily about God*, not primarily about church organization. Words for divinity constitute ~3.6% of total words in 1 Timothy. The letter opens with theology (*God our Savior*, *Christ Jesus our hope*) and climaxes in a doxology to God as "King of ages, immortal, invisible, the only God" (1:17). God's universal saving will (2:4: "wants all people to be saved") governs the structure of the letter.

### 2. Sound Doctrine (*Hygiainōn Didaskalia*)

A distinctive PE vocabulary: nine references across the three letters to "sound" or "healthy" teaching, doctrine, or faith. In 1 Timothy these are at 1:10 and 6:3. The medical metaphor describes the antidote to what the false teachers are spreading — spiritually pathological instruction that weakens, sickens, and destroys. For the concept, see [[sound-doctrine|Sound Doctrine (*Hygiainōn Didaskalia*)]].

### 3. Godliness (*Eusebeia*)

A second distinctive PE term, occurring eight times in 1 Timothy. *Eusebeia* is not borrowed wholesale from Hellenistic moralism (contra Dibelius) but represents "the new existence in Christ that combines belief in God and a consequent manner of life" (Marshall/Towner). Its source is "the mystery from which true godliness springs" (3:16). It pairs naturally with the PE's *sōtēr* emphasis: God's saving initiative generates the human response of godly living.

### 4. The Mystery of Godliness (3:16)

The creedal fragment in 3:16 is often regarded as a hymn or confession:

> *He appeared in a body, was vindicated by the Spirit, was seen by angels, was preached among the nations, was believed on in the world, was taken up in glory.*

The six-line chiasm covers incarnation, vindication, angelic witness, universal proclamation, worldwide faith, and exaltation — a compressed Christological summary. Yarbrough reads this as the foundation for the "mystery" (*mystērion*) that the church is to embody.

### 5. Pastoral Office and Its Requirements (3:1–13)

The overseer (*episkopos*) must be "above reproach, faithful to his wife" (*mias gynaikōs andra*, "a one-woman man"), temperate, respectable, hospitable, able to teach. Yarbrough reads "faithful to his wife" not as requiring marriage but as demanding exclusive marital fidelity when married. The overseer is explicitly male (*andra*); N. T. Wright's claim that "throughout as a man" is just Greek grammar's generic is false on Yarbrough's reading (*andra* = man/male, not generic person).

### 6. Women, Worship, and the Teaching Office (2:9–15)

The most contested passage in the PE. Paul's instructions for women in worship (modesty, quiet learning, the prohibition of teaching/authority over men) are grounded in creation order and the fall narrative. For the full controversy, see [[1-tim-2-12-women-teaching|Women, Teaching, and Authority in 1 Timothy 2:11–15]].

## Interpretive Cruxes

1. **Authorship**: Is 1 Timothy Pauline or pseudonymous? See [[pastoral-epistles-authorship|Pastoral Epistles: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

2. **1 Tim 2:12** — *Authenteō*: does it mean "usurp authority" (pejorative) or "exercise authority" (neutral/positive)? The answer determines whether the prohibition is about the *abuse* of teaching authority or the *office* of congregational oversight. See [[1-tim-2-12-women-teaching|Women, Teaching, and Authority in 1 Timothy 2:11–15]].

3. **1 Tim 2:15 — "Saved through childbearing"**: The most puzzling verse in the PE. Options: (a) reference to the incarnation (Christ born of woman); (b) women preserved through childbirth (physically); (c) salvation through faithfully fulfilling the role of godly womanhood, with *childbearing* as synecdoche — correcting the false teachers who forbade marriage (4:3). Yarbrough prefers (c), with an appeal to Gen 3:16 as the contextual background.

4. **1 Tim 3:16 — The creedal fragment**: Was this an existing hymn? Why six passives? What is the structure? How does the incarnation/vindication/appearance sequence map to the known Christological tradition? The fragment lacks explicit manuscript evidence of being quoted from an existing source but is linguistically marked as liturgical.

5. **1 Tim 6:20 — "What is falsely called knowledge" (*gnōsis*)**: Does this imply a fully developed Gnosticism (which would push the date post-Paul) or proto-gnostic Jewish-philosophical speculation well within the Pauline era? The pseudonymity argument depends heavily on this; Yarbrough argues the usage is consistent with the 60s.
