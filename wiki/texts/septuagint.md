# The Septuagint (LXX)

The ancient **Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures**, begun in Alexandria in the 3rd century BCE
for the [[hellenized-diaspora-jews|Greek-speaking Jewish diaspora]] and adopted as the Old Testament of
the early Church. Its name and the siglum **LXX** ("seventy") derive from the tradition of seventy (or
seventy-two) translators. The Church's apostles quote it; the Eastern Church still treats it as its
authoritative Old Testament text, and its longer canon underlies the Catholic deuterocanon.

## The Translation Legend and Its Authority (City of God XVIII)

[[augustine|Augustine]], in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XVIII (chs. 42–44), gives a classic
statement of the LXX's **inspired authority**. He recounts the legend (from the *Letter of Aristeas*
tradition): **Ptolemy Philadelphus** commissioned the translation; **seventy-two translators**, six
from each of the twelve tribes, worked separately yet produced identical versions —

> "they differed from each other in no word … as if the translators had been one, because in very deed
> the one Spirit had been in them all."

From this Augustine draws his distinctive position: the **same Spirit** who inspired the prophets
inspired the seventy, so that where the Greek **diverges** from the Hebrew it is **not error but a
second prophecy**:

> "whatever is in the Septuagint and not in the Hebrew copies, the same Spirit chose rather to say
> through the latter … both were prophets."

His worked examples: **Jonah's "forty days"** (Hebrew) versus **"three days"** (LXX) over Nineveh — both
true, one of the post-resurrection appearances, one of the Resurrection itself; and **Haggai 2:7**,
where the LXX's "the things that are chosen … from all nations" (the elect/Church) and the Hebrew's
"the desired One shall come" (Christ) are both prophetic, suited to body and Head respectively.

## Relation to Jerome and the Hebrew

Augustine acknowledges **Jerome**'s new Latin translation **from the Hebrew** to be faithful and
learned, and grants that where a copyist's error is involved one should prefer "that language out of
which the translation was made." But he insists the Church "judge that no one should be preferred to the
authority of so many men" — refusing to subordinate the LXX to the *hebraica veritas*. The apostles, he
notes, quoted **both**. This is a preserved tension: the confessional authority of the Greek against
the philological priority of the Hebrew.

## Canon and Reception

The LXX is a **translation**, not a separate canon, but it carries a **wider table of contents** than
the Hebrew Bible — including Wisdom, Sirach, the Maccabees, and additions — and this is the structural
source of the later Christian **deuterocanon**. Augustine elsewhere in Book XVIII notes that the
**Maccabees** are "held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church," and that **Wisdom** and
**Sirach**, though ascribed to Solomon and received "especially [by] the Western [Church]," are judged
by the learned not to be his — and "cannot be quoted against [Jewish] contradictions with so great
validity." Thus:

- **Eastern Orthodoxy** treats the LXX as its canonical Old Testament.
- **Catholicism** receives its deuterocanonical books (via the LXX/Vulgate) as deuterocanonical.
- **[[hellenized-diaspora-jews|Hellenistic Judaism]]** produced and used it as authoritative Scripture
  in Greek, though [[rabbinic-judaism|Rabbinic Judaism]] later fixed the shorter Hebrew canon.
