The **Upanishads** are the philosophical capstone of the Vedas — *Vedanta*, "the end of the Veda" — and
the fountainhead of [[hinduism|Hindu]] metaphysics. As **shruti** (eternal, authorless revelation) they
are the highest scriptural authority, and the first of the **prasthana-trayi** (the three foundations of
Vedanta). The tradition counts 108; the **principal** ones (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Katha, Isha,
Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Kena, Prashna, Svetashvatara, Maitri) are the philosophically
central core.

## Teaching

The Upanishads turn from Vedic ritual to inward knowledge, asking: what is the ground of reality, and
what is the self? Their answers found the whole later tradition:

- **[[brahman|Brahman]]** — the one ultimate reality behind all things.
- **[[atman|Atman]]** — the innermost self.
- The **mahavakyas** ("great sayings") that relate them: *tat tvam asi* ("that thou art," Chandogya),
  *aham brahmasmi* ("I am Brahman"), *ayam atma brahma* ("this self is Brahman"), *prajnanam brahma*
  ("consciousness is Brahman").
- **Samsara** (rebirth), **karma**, and **[[moksha|moksha]]** (liberation through knowledge).

## One Canon, Opposite Readings

The Upanishads are the supreme example of the wiki's "shared canon, divergent interpretation" pattern:
all three Vedanta schools treat them as `canonical` (hence `canonical_for: [hinduism]`), yet
[[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] reads *tat tvam asi* as **literal identity**,
[[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]] as **inseparable dependence**, and [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]]
as **likeness/difference** — see [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the controversy triad]]. (Listed
`authoritative_for: [buddhism]` only as the philosophical foil the Buddha's [[anatta|non-self]]
rejects, not as Buddhist scripture.)

## Dasgupta's Reading (Vol. I, Ch. III)

[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] devotes Ch. III to the *earlier* Upaniṣads — the
~13 oldest of the 108 in the Muktikā list, those commented on by Śaṅkara (Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya,
Aitareya, Taittirīya, Īśa, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya). His account adds several things the
wiki should track:

- **Not a system but a "melting pot."** Dasgupta insists the Upaniṣads are *not* a systematic treatise
  but "a repository of diverse currents of thought... in which all later philosophic ideas were still in a
  state of fusion." He identifies **three currents** left unreconciled: (1) the dominant **monism** —
  Ātman/Brahman the sole reality, all else unreal; (2) a **pantheism** identifying the universe *with*
  Brahman; (3) a **theism** treating Brahman as a Lord (*Īśa*) controlling the world (the two-birds image
  of Śvetāśvatara IV.6). Because these sat side by side, Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, and Madhva could each later
  quarrel over the texts to extract a consistent system — and the seed of [[maya|māyā]] doctrine is
  "slightly hinted at once in Bṛhadāraṇyaka and thrice in Śvetāśvatara." A modern interpreter, he urges,
  should "turn a deaf ear" to every school's absolute claim and read the texts independently.

- **From sacrifice to meditation to knowledge.** The Brāhmaṇa → Āraṇyaka → Upaniṣad sequence is a
  movement of *transferred value*: the actual sacrifice gives way to **symbolic meditation** on it (the
  visible universe meditated upon as the sacrificial horse, Bṛh. I.1), and finally to **knowledge of the
  self**. Dasgupta argues the "magic power" of sacrifice, bound up with **[[rita|ṛta]]**, was gradually
  *abstracted* from the rite and reconceived as the supreme power **Brahman** — the path of knowledge
  (*jñāna-mārga*) superseding the path of works (*karma-mārga*).

- **The Kṣatriya-origin debate.** Against **Garbe** (and partly Winternitz), who argued from stories like
  Ajātaśatru instructing Bālāki, or Pravāhaṇa Jaibali instructing Śvetaketu, that the *Brahman = All*
  doctrine "took its rise in the ranks of the warrior caste," Dasgupta pushes back: the texts show
  Kṣatriyas (and women — Maitreyī, Gārgī) shared keenly in the quest, but he finds "no definite evidence"
  that the doctrine *originated* outside Brahmin circles rather than growing out of the Brāhmaṇas and
  Āraṇyakas. (Recorded as a live scholarly dispute, not adjudicated.)

- **The word itself.** *Upaniṣad* < *upa-ni-ṣad* (to sit near): **Max Müller** — "session" of pupils
  round a teacher; **Deussen** — "secret / secret instruction" (Dasgupta sides with Deussen); **Śaṅkara**
  — from *sad* "to destroy," i.e. that which destroys ignorance.

- **Negative method and the equation Ātman = Brahman.** The quest first *fails* through positive
  identifications (prāṇa, ākāśa, sun, manas meditated upon as Brahman) and resolves into the **negative
  method** — Yājñavalkya's *neti neti* ("not this, not this"), "the Ātman is silence." The "sum and
  substance" of the teaching is then the equation **Ātman = Brahman**, unfolded through the five sheaths
  ([[pancha-kosha|pañca-kośa]]) to the *ānandamaya* self, and dramatized in **Prajāpati's instruction of
  Indra and Virocana** (Chā. VIII) and the **Naciketas–Yama** dialogue (Kaṭha). On *tat tvam asi*
  ("that art thou, O Śvetaketu," Chā. VI.10) Dasgupta reads the dominant Upaniṣadic current as plain
  identity — the ground Śaṅkara builds on and Madhva must reread (see [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the
  triad]]).

- **Transmigration and emancipation.** See [[samsara|saṃsāra]] and [[moksha|mokṣa]]: Dasgupta stresses
  that in the *earlier* Upaniṣads it is **desire (kāma), not karma**, that is named as the cause of
  rebirth — karma being only the link between desire and its fruit — and that **emancipation is not a new
  acquisition** but the realization of a freedom that always already is.

- **Reception.** The Upaniṣads reached Europe via Dārā Shukōh's Persian translation (1657), rendered into
  Latin by Anquetil Duperron (1801–02), which **Schopenhauer** read with rapture ("the solace of my life…
  of my death"); **Rām Mohan Roy** founded the Brāhma Samāj on Upaniṣadic doctrine.
