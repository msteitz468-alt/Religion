# Inferno (*Divine Comedy*, Cantica 1)

The first cantica of [[divine-comedy|the *Commedia*]] (34 cantos): the descent of
the character [[dante-alighieri|Dante]], guided by [[virgil|Virgil]], through the
graded circles of [[hell|Hell]], from the dark wood to the frozen centre of the
earth where [[satan|Satan]] is imprisoned. The journey spans Good Friday evening
to before dawn on Easter Sunday. Each circle embodies the [[contrapasso|contrapasso]] —
a punishment that mirrors the sin. Read here in Robin Kirkpatrick's translation
with his per-canto notes; verse line-loci below follow that edition.

## The Plan of Hell

Kirkpatrick stresses (Canto 11) that the classification is **not** the Seven
Capital Vices (reserved for [[purgatorio|Purgatorio]]) but an **Aristotelian–
Ciceronian** schema graded by how deliberately each sin corrupts the distinctively
human gift of reason: *intemperance* (least culpable) → *violence* → *fraud* →
*treachery* (worst). Deceit is "specifically a human wrong" and so "displeases God
the more" (11:25–26); treachery is worst because it violates "the love particular
that trust creates" (11:63).

| Circle | Sin | Guardian | Contrapasso |
|--------|-----|----------|-------------|
| Vestibule | Apathy / the neutrals (incl. the angels who "existed for themselves alone," 3:39) | — | Chase a whirling banner, stung by wasps; names denied them |
| 1 | [[limbo|Limbo]]: unbaptized, virtuous pagans | — | Hopeless desire, no torment — "sighs" not weeping (4:26) |
| 2 | Lust | Minos | Blown forever on a violent wind |
| 3 | Gluttony | Cerberus | Drowned in foul, freezing rain and filth |
| 4 | Avarice & Prodigality | Plutus | Roll boulders against each other; identities effaced |
| 5 | Wrath (surface) & Sullenness (submerged) | Phlegyas | Mutual violence in the Styx; the sullen gurgle beneath |
| 6 | Heresy (Epicurean denial of the soul) | Fallen angels (City of Dis) | Burning tombs, sealed forever at the Last Judgment |
| 7 | Violence (3 rings: vs. neighbours / self / God–Nature–Art) | Minotaur, Centaurs | Boiling-blood river (Phlegethon); wood of suicides; burning sand |

## Selected Cantos and Figures

- **Cantos 1–2 — The Dark Wood.** Three beasts (leopard, lion, she-wolf — from
  Jeremiah 5:6, glossed by Kirkpatrick as pleasure, pride, avarice) drive Dante
  back; Virgil appears, sent through a chain of intercession ([[beatrice|Beatrice]]
  ← St Lucy ← the Virgin Mary). The enigmatic *veltro* ("hound") prophecy
  (1:100–110) Kirkpatrick deliberately refuses to identify.
- **Canto 3 — The Gate.** "Justice inspired my exalted Creator… Primal Love"
  (3:4–6): Hell as an expression of divine justice, wisdom, and love. The neutrals,
  including "the one who made, from cowardice, the great denial" (Celestine V).
  Charon ferries the damned, whose fear God's justice "becomes that sharp desire"
  (3:124–25).
- **Canto 4 — Limbo.** Virgil's home; the Harrowing of Christ (unnamed) who led
  out Adam, Abel, Noah, Moses, Abraham, David, Rachel. The "noble fort" of
  virtuous pagans: Homer, Horace, Ovid, Lucan (Dante enrolls as "sixth"),
  Aristotle ("the master of all those who think and know," 4:131), Plato, Saladin,
  Avicenna, Averroes ("who made the Commentary").
- **Canto 5 — The Lustful.** Minos judges with his tail; the wind-blown souls
  (Semiramis, Dido, Cleopatra, Helen, Achilles). [[francesca-da-rimini|Francesca
  da Rimini]] and Paolo: the Lancelot book, the "Amor" anaphora; Kirkpatrick's
  crux — her deeper sin is "moral apathy and failed intelligence," not lust alone.
- **Canto 6 — Gluttony.** Cerberus; Ciacco ("Hoggo") delivers the first prophecy
  of Florentine Black–White [[guelphs-and-ghibellines|Guelf]] strife and Dante's exile.
- **Canto 7 — Avarice/Prodigality; Wrath.** Plutus's gibberish; the lecture on
  Fortune as a providential, angelic power (modifying Boethius).
- **Cantos 8–9 — The City of Dis.** Filippo Argenti (Dante's righteous anger,
  blessed by Virgil); Virgil barred by fallen angels, the Furies summon Medusa,
  and a Heavenly Messenger opens the gate "simply with his rod" — dramatizing the
  limits of reason before grace.
- **Canto 10 — Heresy.** [[farinata-degli-uberti|Farinata degli Uberti]], proud
  Ghibelline patriot, rises "as though he held all Hell in high disdain"; Cavalcante
  collapses at Dante's past-tense verb implying his son Guido's death. The damned
  see the future but not the present (10:100–108).
- **Cantos 12–14 — Violence.** The Minotaur and Centaurs (Chiron, Nessus, Pholus)
  guard the boiling-blood river of tyrants (Alexander, Dionysius, Attila, Ezzelino,
  Guy de Montfort). The wood of suicides, where [[pier-della-vigna|Pier della
  Vigna]] is a bleeding thorn-tree; the squanderers hunted by hounds. The
  blasphemers on burning sand: Capaneus, still defying Jove; the **Old Man of
  Crete** (from Daniel 2), source of Hell's rivers.
- **Cantos 15–16 — Sodomites.** [[brunetto-latini|Brunetto Latini]], whom Dante
  honours even in damnation ("you taught me how man makes himself eternal,"
  15:85); the Guelf nobles Guido Guerra, Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, Iacopo Rusticucci.
- **Canto 17 — Usurers; Geryon.** Usurers known only by their family coats of arms;
  descent to the *Malebolge* on the back of Geryon, "that filthy effigy of fraud."
- **Cantos 18–20 — Malebolge (panders to diviners).** Panders and seducers
  (Venedico Caccianemico; Jason); flatterers (Alessio Interminei; Thais);
  simoniacs planted head-down with burning soles — Dante rebukes **Pope Nicholas
  III**, who prophesies Boniface VIII and Clement V (Canto 19, with the Donation
  of Constantine condemned); the diviners with heads twisted backward (Amphiaraus,
  Tiresias, Manto, Michael Scott), Virgil dissociating his own providential
  prophecy from sinful soothsaying.
- **Cantos 21–23 — Barrators; Hypocrites.** The mock-epic of the *Malebranche*
  demons (Malacoda; the troop "made a trumpet of his arse," 21:139) and the
  trickster Ciampolo of Navarre; the hypocrites in gilded leaden cloaks; Caiaphas
  crucified on the ground, "who counselled… one man should die for the people"
  (cf. John 11).
- **Cantos 24–25 — Thieves.** Endless serpent-transformations and reconstitution;
  Vanni Fucci; Dante claims to outdo Ovid and Lucan in depicting metamorphosis.
- **Canto 26 — [[ulysses-dante|Ulysses]].** Wrapped in flame with Diomedes; his
  speech on the last voyage past the Pillars of Hercules — "You were not made to
  live as mindless brutes, / but go in search of virtue and true knowledge"
  (26:118–120) — and shipwreck at the mountain of Purgatory. Kirkpatrick: the
  speech has no known source precedent.
- **Canto 27 — [[guido-da-montefeltro|Guido da Montefeltro]].** The false
  counsel to Boniface VIII and the "devil's syllogism": absolution cannot precede
  an unrepented sin, for "willing a sin and not willing it cannot be simultaneous."
- **Canto 28 — Schismatics.** Sowers of discord hacked apart and re-wounded:
  [[muhammad|Muhammad]] and Ali (placed here under the medieval — and historically
  false — construal of Muhammad as a Christian schismatic; recorded without
  endorsement), Mosca de' Lamberti, and **Bertran de Born** carrying his own head
  like a lantern — who names the *contrapasso* ("counter-suffering," 28:142), its
  single explicit use in the poem.
- **Cantos 29–30 — Falsifiers.** Alchemists (Griffolino, Capocchio), impersonators
  (Gianni Schicchi, Myrrha), and counterfeiters (Master Adam) diseased and raving;
  Adam and Sinon brawl, prompting Virgil's sharpest rebuke of Dante.
- **Canto 31 — The Giants.** Nimrod (babbling, builder of Babel), Ephialtes,
  Antaeus, who lowers the poets to the pit: "when the powers of working intellect /
  are wed to strength and absolute illwill, / then humans cannot find a place to
  hide."
- **Cantos 32–34 — Treachery (Cocytus).** The frozen lake's four rings: Caïna
  (kin), Antenora (country — Bocca degli Abati), Ptolomea (guests — Fra Alberigo,
  whose soul falls here before his body dies), Judecca (lords). [[ugolino|Count
  Ugolino]] gnaws Archbishop Ruggieri ("hunger proved a greater power than grief").
  At the centre, **Satan**: a three-faced parody of the [[trinity|Trinity]],
  weeping, chewing Judas, Brutus, and Cassius — impotent, the embodiment of
  [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation]]. The poets climb down his body, pass the
  earth's centre, and emerge "once more [to] see the stars."

## Theological and Literary Notes

- **Evil as anti-climax.** Satan is deliberately unthreatening; Hell is "banal
  monotony." See [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation]].
- **Apocalyptic register.** From Canto 17 onward (Geryon), Kirkpatrick notes, the
  imagery increasingly draws on Revelation (the Whore of Babylon at Canto 19).
- **Political theology.** The condemnation of Brutus and Cassius beside Judas
  reflects Dante's imperial sympathies — Rome as providential — a standard he does
  not apply consistently (Cato, their fellow Caesar-opponent, guards Purgatory).
