# First Letter to the Thessalonians

## Textual Overview

First Thessalonians is Paul's earliest surviving letter (on standard scholarly dating) and one of the earliest documents in the entire New Testament, written from Corinth during his eighteen-month stay there after the founding of the Thessalonian church. The cosenders are Paul, Silvanus (Silas), and Timothy (1:1). The congregation at Thessalonica was predominantly Gentile — Paul explicitly notes that his readers had "turned to God from idols" (1:9) — though some converts from the local synagogue are present (Acts 17:4). The letter is universally accepted as authentic Pauline; no significant challenge to its authenticity exists in the scholarly literature.

[[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]] (BECNT, 2014; [[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|source page]]) treats the letter as a real epistolary communication shaped by Greco-Roman letter conventions, not a free-standing treatise. On his reading the opening (1:1) is formally unembellished because the relationship with the church is good and Paul's status is not under dispute; Silvanus and Timothy are named as cosenders for weight and unified leadership expectation, not because they are co-authors. Weima argues that first-person singulars (2:18; 3:1–5; 5:27) show Paul as the real author, with plurals taken literarily rather than as a group-composition model. Silvanus is identified with Acts' Silas (senior associate after Barnabas); Timothy is the junior associate whose Corinth report occasions the letter.

### Historical situation (mission, expulsion, letter occasion)

Paul founded the Thessalonian church during his second missionary journey (Acts 17:1–9), arriving from Philippi via the Via Egnatia with Silvanus and Timothy. Weima argues that "on three Sabbaths" (Acts 17:2) describes only the synagogue phase: a longer post-synagogue stay is "demanded" by the Gentile majority (1:9), multiple Philippian gifts (Phil 4:15–16), trade modeling (1 Thess 2:9; 2 Thess 3:7–9), imperfect-tense repeated teaching, and appointment of leaders (5:12–13)—yet the stay was still brief and forced ("driven out," 2:15; "orphaned," 2:17; faith still "lacking," 3:10).

The post-synagogue Gentile mission, on Weima's reconstruction (following Hock and Malherbe), centered in a leatherworking/tentmaking workshop—possibly Jason's shop—where Paul "worked night and day" and "preached… the gospel of God" (2:9) simultaneously. Workshop evangelism supplied self-support, a model against idleness, and a conversion site; one-on-one fatherly exhortation (2:11) continued the same pastoral mode.

The mission was cut short when Jewish and pagan opposition orchestrated a riot. Weima coordinates Acts 17:5–9 with 1 Thess 2:14–15: charges before the *politarchs* included (1) disturbing the peace / "world-troublers" and (2) acting "contrary to the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king—Jesus." He prefers E. A. Judge's reading of charge 2: imperial bans on predicting a ruler's death or change of rule (Augustus AD 11; Tiberius AD 16 as *dogma*), which Paul's eschatological Lord/kingdom preaching (2:12) could trigger. The city officials took a security deposit (bail) from Jason and the other converts, which Weima reads as guaranteeing the peace and legally preventing Paul and Silas from returning—one historical mechanism that may lie behind "Satan hindered us" (2:18). Forced to depart at night, the missionary trio fled to Berea.

Jewish antagonism followed them to Berea, driving Paul south by sea to Athens while Silas and Timothy temporarily remained. The three briefly reunited in Athens before Paul sent Timothy back to Thessalonica (3:1–5)—Timothy was eligible because, unlike Paul and Silas, he was not implicated in the legal judgment. Weima takes the "we" of 3:1 as a literary/epistolary plural (Paul left alone in Athens) and reconstructs: Athens reunion → Timothy to Thessalonica (Silas likely to Macedonia/Philippi) → both rejoin Paul in Corinth (Acts 18:5) → Timothy's report prompts 1 Thessalonians.

From Corinth, Timothy's positive report about the Thessalonians' faith and love (3:6), together with news of "things that are lacking" in their faith (3:10), occasions the letter in late AD 50 or early 51. Weima dates this via Timothy's return ≈ Acts 18:5 and Gallio's proconsulship (Acts 18:12–17), fixed by the Delphic Inscription of Claudius to the start of a one-year term in July 51 or 52. Concerns Timothy brought back (as Weima maps them):
- ongoing affliction from fellow citizens (3:3–4; social harassment/ostracism rather than primarily martyrdom)
- need for holiness in sexual conduct (4:3–8)
- *philadelphia* / work with hands / idle freeloading (4:9–12; 5:14)
- the fate of believers who die before Christ's return (4:13–18)
- anxiety about the day of the Lord for the living (5:1–11)
- church leadership and congregational order, including *ataktoi* and prophecy (5:12–22)

The letter is warm throughout and extensively defends Paul's apostolic integrity against apparently circulating charges of impropriety (2:1–16), then rebuilds trust via the "apostolic parousia" of 2:17–3:10 so that readers will accept the paraenesis of 4:1–5:22.

### Thessalonica: The City

Weima's commentary provides exceptional detail on the city's historical significance:

**Location and scale**: Founded by Cassander (316–315 BC) by synoecism of twenty-six villages on Therme; named after his wife. On the Via Egnatia (~260 miles west to Dyrrachium; ~430 miles east toward Byzantium) and the Thermaic Gulf (deep harbor protected from southeast winds). Population estimates c. 65,000–100,000 put it among the top ten largest cities in the Roman Empire; ancient sources call it "metropolis of Macedonia."

**Political status**: Provincial capital after 146 BC; free city (*civitas libera*) since 42 BC (Marc Antony reward for loyalty: local autonomy, minting rights, no garrison inside walls, tax concessions). Free-city status preserved Greek civic structure: citizen assembly (*dēmos*—the intended venue of Acts 17:5), council (*boulē*), and *politarchs*—the distinctive city officials of Acts 17:6, 8. Weima notes the politarch office was once thought unattested outside Acts; it is now known from ~70 nonliterary references (Horsley, Riesner), 28 from Thessalonica itself; the office is pre-Roman and the number of politarchs varied (five in the late 1st c. BC; three–seven later). They were chief administrative/judicial officers with peace responsibility—explaining their "disturbed" reaction (Acts 17:8). Macedonia's status flipped senatorial/imperial under Augustus–Tiberius–Claudius (restored senatorial AD 44); Weima (with Riesner/De Vos) links status anxiety to elite sensitivity about anti-Roman charges six years before Paul's visit.

**Religious pluralism**: Over twenty-five gods, heroes, and personifications worshiped. Most frequent: Dionysus (from the city's founding; tribal name, coinage, state cult); Egyptian gods (Serapis, Isis—Weima reports the city as second center of the cult after Delos outside Egypt); and **Cabirus**—from the Flavian period at latest the chief tutelary deity (Edson; Witt's "Kabeiric monotheism"; singular Cabirus, not uncritically the twin Cabiri of Samothrace). Imperial cult of Roma and Roman benefactors, temple/priesthood of Caesar, and divine honors on coinage were politically and religiously significant; Weima records Hendrix's benefaction-expansion thesis but sides with Green/Ferguson that temple, priesthoods, and divine titles point to genuine religious sentiment beyond mere politics. A synagogue community also existed; Weima defends first-century Jewish presence against dismissal of Acts: 1 Thess 2:14–15; Philo/Agrippa on Jews in Macedonia; later epigraphic evidence (Jacob/Anna sarcophagus fine "to the synagogues"); Riesner: of twenty-five Acts details, nineteen are directly or indirectly confirmed by 1 Thessalonians.

**Imperial ideology and cost of conversion**: Thessalonica's close relationship with Rome—political calculation, free-city status, cult of Roma and benefactors—means, for Weima, that proclamation of another *kyrios*, another *parousia*, another king challenged the political-religious fabric of the city. The phrase "peace and security" (*eirēnē kai asphaleia*, 5:3) is read by Weima as Roman imperial propaganda (coins with Pax/Securitas; Pompey inscription at Ilium pairing *eirēnē* and *asphaleia*; Res gestae; Plutarch *Ant.* 40.4), not primarily an OT "Peace, peace" echo (Jer 6:14 etc.—which lacks "security" and would be anomalous in this Gentile correspondence). Converts' exclusivity invited charges of "atheism" (Barclay) and threatened the free city's favored status; affliction from *symphyletai* ("fellow citizens," 2:14) is primarily social harassment for refusing civic/cultic participation.

## Structure (Weima's five-part epistolary outline)

[[jeffrey-weima|Weima]] positions his commentary as **literary-epistolary** rather than wholesale Greco-Roman rhetorical analysis: the most important source for understanding Paul's letters is letter-writing practice of his day; rhetorical handbooks are not applied "direct and wholesale," though everyday devices (e.g. *paraleipsis* at 4:9; 5:1) are recognized. Each unit is introduced with literary analysis of form, function, boundaries, and internal structure.

Weima's macrostructure of 1 Thessalonians:

1. **Letter Opening** (1:1) — sender (Paul + Silvanus + Timothy), recipient (*ekklēsia* "in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ"), greeting ("Grace… and peace"; shorter form original per Weima/Metzger)
2. **Thanksgiving** (1:2–10) — Weima delimits the thanksgiving to **1:2–10 only** (against Schubert's mega-thanksgiving 1:2–3:13); four main clauses; foreshadows integrity defense, persecution, moral service, and parousia; pastoral, exhortative, and "preview" functions
3. **Defense of Apostolic Actions and Absence** (2:1–3:13)
   - **Past visit** (2:1–16): integrity of preaching and conduct (2:1–12: bold preaching; God-examined speech; "infants" / *nēpioi*; nursing mother; self-sufficient labor; father); Thessalonians' response (2:13–16: word of God; imitation of Judean churches)
   - **Present absence** (2:17–3:10): "apostolic parousia" (presence-device) — concern over absence (2:17–20, Paul as *orphaned* child); Timothy's mission under persecution (3:1–5); both concerns answered by Timothy's good news (3:6–10)
   - **Transitional prayers** (3:11–13): discrete body benedictions summarizing first half and foreshadowing holiness, love, and parousia
4. **Exhortations** (4:1–5:22) — fulfill desire to "complete the things that are lacking" (3:10)
   - Sexual holiness (4:1–8)
   - *Philadelphia* / quiet work with hands (4:9–12)
   - **Comfort concerning deceased believers at the parousia (4:13–18)**
   - Comfort concerning living believers at the day of the Lord (5:1–11)
   - Congregational life and worship (5:12–22: leaders; *ataktoi*/fainthearted/weak; worship triad; test prophecy)
5. **Letter Closing** (5:23–28) — expanded peace benediction (complete sanctification of spirit/soul/body at the *parousia*); prayer request; holy kiss to "**all** the brothers"; solemn charge to read the letter aloud; grace — chiastic inclusio with 1:1

Internal substructure notes Weima stresses: 2:1–12 six main clauses of integrity; 2:13–16 belongs with past *eisodos* (not a separate thanksgiving unit); 4:13–18 and 5:1–11 are independent literary units (*peri de* + vocative) despite formal parallels; 5:12–22 is four-unit situation-specific paraenesis, not shotgun generic lists.

## Major Themes / Narrative Summary

### 1. The Parousia and Eschatological Hope

Both sections of 4:13–5:11 address anxieties about Christ's return. The first (4:13–18) addresses grief over believers who have died before the parousia; the second (5:1–11) addresses anxiety of the living about the day of the Lord—not mere curiosity about timing but fear whether they would avoid wrath and obtain salvation/life with Christ (Weima). Throughout, eschatological hope grounds ethical exhortations and comfort against ongoing affliction. Election/call language (1:4; 2:12; 3:3; 4:7; 5:9, 24) underwrites confidence that destiny rests in God, not fallible human ability.

### 2. The *Parousia* as Royal Visit

The term *parousia* (2:19; 3:13; 4:15; 5:23) evokes the Hellenistic image of a visiting king or emperor's formal arrival; Weima holds that a predominantly Gentile church would naturally have heard it in imperial-visit terms (and may help explain Acts 17:7 "another king—Jesus"), even while OT day-of-the-Lord roots are present. Weima argues Paul's use of *apantēsis* (formal reception) in 4:17 extends this metaphor: believers do not fly up to a heavenly destination but rather go out to meet the descending Christ and escort him the final distance to earth — just as civic delegations escorted visiting dignitaries into their city (Peterson 1930; Green; Gundry vs Cosby). See [[parousia-1-thess-4|Parousia and the Fate of the Deceased (1 Thess 4:13–18)]].

### 3. Anti-Imperial Polemic ("peace and security")

"Peace and security" (*eirēnē kai asphaleia*, 5:3) is, for Weima, a standard Roman political slogan disseminated through official media (coins, monuments, inscriptions—not OT false-"peace" alone). Paul's reframing — "sudden destruction will come upon them… they will certainly not escape" — challenges that claim. Weima notes fellow citizens (2:14) may have countered Christian "day of the Lord" / "coming wrath" by appealing to Rome as protector of present peace and prosperity. The proclamation of a rival *kyrios* with his own *parousia* would have been heard in Thessalonica as politically as well as religiously provocative. Fee/Shogren (reported in apparatus notes) treat transferring OT "day of the LORD" to Jesus's coming as high Christology already in this earliest letter; Weima rejects Malherbe's "false prophets inside" reading of 5:3—the contrast is with outsiders.

### 4. Apostolic Defense and Integrity

An unusually long section (2:1–16) defends Paul's character against apparent accusations that he exploited his converts (flattery, greed, honor-seeking) and fled at the first sign of trouble. Weima holds that opponents are best identified as *symphyletai* outside the church (Timothy's good report 3:6; affectionate tone; "hope and joy and crown" preclude internal enemies as primary target); mirror-reading of antitheses (esp. 2:1–7b) yields defense of integrity/motives, not abandonment of mirror method (contra Lyons). Paul responds with parental metaphors — infants/*nēpioi* (2:7), nursing mother (2:7–8), father (2:11) — and points to manual labor (2:9) as evidence he was no wandering charlatan. Plural "apostles of Christ" (2:7a) includes Silvanus and Timothy on Weima's evolutionary reading of the title. The defense unit prepares trust for later paraenesis (4:1–5:22).

### 5. Affliction and Imitation

The Thessalonians receive the gospel "in much affliction" (1:6) and are compared to Judean churches who suffered from their own people (2:14). Affliction is reframed as *mimēsis* — imitation of Paul, of the Lord, of other churches — and as divine destiny ("we are destined for this," 3:3–4). Weima frames 2:17–3:10 as answering twin concerns: doubt over Paul's love due to absence, and threat to faith under persecution (same Satanic power at 2:18 and 3:5 "the Tempter"). Timothy's "good news" of faith and love (3:6) answers both.

### 6. Workshop Evangelism

Weima argues that Paul's evangelistic ministry in Thessalonica operated primarily through his leatherworking workshop (likely in Jason's home), not public venues. "Working night and day… we preached to you the gospel of God" (2:9) refers to simultaneous activity: preaching while working. This is consistent with ancient evidence that workshops served as settings for intellectual discourse (cf. Celsus on Christians converting in cobblers' shops) and models the work ethic later demanded of *ataktoi*.

### 7. Congregational order: leaders, *ataktoi*, Spirit and prophecy

Weima treats 5:12–22 as deliberately situation-specific. Leaders are to be respected for hard work, ruling "in the Lord," and admonishing (5:12–13)—authority functions, not pure egalitarianism. The whole church (not leaders alone) must admonish the *ataktoi* ("rebellious idlers"—laziness *plus* refusal to obey leaders/Paul; illuminated by 4:11–12 and 2 Thess 3:6–15), encourage the fainthearted (*oligopsychoi*—best linked to grief over the dead, 4:13–18), and be devoted to the weak (*astheneis*—hypothesis: excessive anxiety about status at judgment, 5:1–11). Peace among yourselves (5:13b) may relate to tension around the *ataktoi*. Worship triad (5:16–18: rejoice, pray, give thanks *in* everything) and balanced testing of prophecy (5:19–22: do not quench/despise; test; hold good; abstain from evil) close the body. The closing charge under oath that the letter be read to **all** the brothers (5:27)—likely Paul's own hand—reflects fear that *ataktoi* would defy written as they defied oral instruction.

### Narrative arc (summary)

Mission-founding under opposition → forced departure and legal hindrance → Timothy's strengthening mission and mixed report → letter of apology + comfort + paraenesis from Corinth → ethical holiness, equal participation of dead and living at the Lord's royal arrival, and ordered congregational life under persecution. Companion letter [[2-thessalonians|2 Thessalonians]] (same cosenders, short interval on Weima's dating) escalates day-of-the-Lord crisis and idle rebellion.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

1 Thessalonians is transmitted as part of the Pauline corpus with early external attestation: Marcion's canon, the Muratorian Fragment, early versions; Irenaeus is the first to quote it by name (Weima on 5:23). No floating or relocatable placement of major units is comparable to Rom 16:25–27 or 1 Cor 14:34–35.

**Key text-critical loci discussed by Weima (BECNT Additional Notes / Excursus 2):**

- **2:7 *nēpioi* ("infants") vs *ēpioi* ("gentle")**: Weima's Excursus 2 argues external evidence is decisive for *nēpioi*—oldest Greek witnesses (𝔓65 3rd c.; א* B 4th; C* D* I 5th); Old Latin, Sahidic, Bohairic; Clement/Origen/Ambrosiaster. Oldest *ēpioi* is A (5th c., ~200 years later). Even advocates of "gentle" concede external strength for *nēpioi* (Fee). Internal arguments against *nēpioi* (dittography; pejorative *nēpios*; mixed metaphor infants→nurse) fail to override: haplography equally possible; Paul is not always pejorative with *nēpios* (1 Cor 14:20 *nēpiazō* positive); rapid metaphor shifts are Pauline (Gal 4:19; mother/father/orphan within 1 Thess 2). Weima ties *nēpioi* to the inverted orphan metaphor of 2:17 (*aporphanizō* always = children orphaned from parents in extant Greek). NA/UBS shifted toward infants; many modern translations still prefer "gentle."
- **1:1 greeting**: shorter "Grace to you and peace" original; longer "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" is secondary assimilation (Metzger; Alexandrian + Western support for short reading).
- **3:2 Timothy's title**: Weima prefers "coworker of God" (*synergon tou theou*) as the most difficult reading that best explains five variants—stressing Timothy as authoritative emissary, not mere mailman.
- **2:12 *kalountos***: present strongly supported (aorist under Gal 1:6 influence).
- **2:16**: *ephthasen* (aorist) preferred over minority perfect *ephthaken*; *tou theou* with wrath late/few—not original though ET "of God" may be warranted.
- **2:15 *idious* prophets**: secondary (strengthens OT-prophet reading).
- **3:13 Amen**: slightly stronger evidence for inclusion.
- **5:27**: singular adjuration amid plural letter formulas points to autograph (pattern explicit at 2 Thess 3:17).

On 2:13–16 as a whole: **universal textual support in every extant manuscript**—no MS evidence that the unit is secondary (central Weima point against interpolation theories).

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

**In this wiki's primary source ([[jeffrey-weima|Weima]], BECNT):**
- **Epistolary-literary criticism** as primary method: letter opening, thanksgiving (Jervis five-unit form preferred over Schubert types), body conventions (autobiography / apologetic defense; apostolic parousia as loose topos; paraenesis; body benedictions), and letter closing as carefully adapted recapitulation of body themes (Weima's form-critical work on closings).
- **Historical-situational reading**: coordinates 1 Thessalonians with Acts 17–18 and epigraphy (politarchs; free city; imperial cult; Cabirus) without Knox-school discounting of Acts; Riesner/Bruce-style confidence in the Acts framework where it agrees with the letters.
- **Social-historical reconstruction**: workshop ministry (Hock, Malherbe); wandering-preacher antitheses; imperial propaganda for 5:3; civic *apantēsis* custom for 4:17.
- **Evangelical confessional frame** (BECNT series: trustworthiness/essential unity of Scripture; creeds/Reformation as legitimate framework) with heavy engagement of critical scholarship.
- Everyday **rhetoric** conceded (paraleipsis, antithesis, disclosure formulas, inclusio) without wholesale speech-rhetoric outlines.

**How communities have read 1 Thessalonians (reception sketches; not exhaustive):**
- **Patristic / traditional**: early canonical use; Chrysostom already deploys royal-entry analogy for 4:17; peace-benediction trichotomy sometimes over-read as load-bearing anthropology (Weima resists).
- **Reformation / confessional Protestant**: Pauline authenticity; election (1:4) and sanctification themes; often harmonized with broader Pauline systems.
- **Historical-critical / modern**: authenticity of the letter as a whole undisputed since the nineteenth century (except fringe Crüsemann 2010); main live dispute is 2:13–16 interpolation (Pearson et al.) and relationship to [[2-thessalonians|2 Thessalonians]] (see [[2-thessalonians-authorship]]); social-scientific (Malherbe) and rhetorical (Wanamaker) schools; anti-imperial readings of 5:3 and *parousia* language.
- **Dispensational / popular evangelical**: "rapture" reading of 4:17 (*harpazō* / meeting in the air as removal to heaven)—Weima and most critical commentators reject this destination as foreign to *apantēsis* formal-reception imagery. See [[parousia-1-thess-4]].
- **Jewish–Christian / post-Shoah ethics of reading**: 2:14–16's harsh language about "the Jews" is a live pastoral and ethical crux; Weima's authenticity defense coexists with his note that the target may be a limited group or geographical "Judeans," and that *eis telos* ("until the end") may leave room (with Rom 11) for future hope—positions, not adjudication.

## Canon and Reception

**Canon status:** Canonical for the major historic Christian traditions. Frontmatter `canon_scope` lists [[catholicism]], [[eastern-orthodoxy]], and [[protestantism]] under `canonical_for`. No significant community within those streams rejects the letter.

**Early reception (Weima's summary):** Marcionite canon, Muratorian Fragment, early versions; Irenaeus first to quote by name. External attestation for 1 Thessalonians is solid; Weima notes that for [[2-thessalonians|2 Thessalonians]] external evidence is "both earlier and more extensive," with no early-church doubt about either letter's Pauline claim until modern criticism (Schmidt 1801 onward for 2 Thess).

**Intra-corpus standing:** Universally treated as authentic Pauline and as among the earliest Christian documents; often used as a control for reconstructing early eschatology, co-sender practice, and Paul's relationship with a predominantly Gentile urban congregation. Companion reception of [[2-thessalonians]] as either genuine twin or pseudonymous sequel remains the larger open controversy (see [[2-thessalonians-authorship]]).

**Liturgical and pastoral use:** Closing elements (holy kiss; public reading charge 5:27; grace/peace benedictions) reflect and shaped early congregational practice of reading apostolic letters aloud. Eschatological units (4:13–18; 5:1–11) remain central in funeral, advent, and "end times" preaching across traditions—with sharply divergent destinations assigned to the *apantēsis* scene.

## Scholarship and Historiography

### Authorship of 1 Thessalonians as a whole

Universally accepted as Pauline in current scholarship. Weima cites Jewett: "No one in the current scholarly debate doubts its authenticity"; Kümmel (1977: 185): "there can be no justifiable doubt that all of 1 Thess is of Pauline origin." Internal evidence (1:1; 2:18 personal interjection; style) and external canons/citations underwrite the consensus. Fee (reported via Weima's notes): denial faces "enormous historical difficulties—so much so that one wonders, 'Why bother?'" Over a century has passed without significant challenge since the refutation of the older Schrader/Baur line; Crüsemann (2010) is noted as an exception without traction.

### The 1 Thess 2:13–16 interpolation debate (positions)

The one internal dispute of substance is whether 2:13–16 — containing the harshest language about Jews in Paul's Letters ("the Jews… who drove us out… wrath has come upon them…") — is a post-Pauline interpolation inserted after the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 (Pearson 1971; also Boers; Richard; Koester; earlier Baur 1875). Proponents combine theological, historical, form-critical, and (Schmidt) linguistic arguments.

[[jeffrey-weima|Weima]] evaluates the four arguments and finds each unpersuasive; he treats the unit as authentic and structurally integral to 2:1–16 (missionaries' conduct → Thessalonians' response, pattern already in 1:5–10):

1. **Theological:** Tension with Romans 9–11 is overstated; Rom 9–11 also has severe judgments on Jewish unbelief; 2:14–16 may target a limited group or geographical "Judeans"; *eis telos* as "until the end" (temporal, parallel *pantote*) may leave room for future salvation (Rom 11:25–32); 1 Cor 2:8 "rulers" need not contradict Jewish involvement in the crucifixion charge.
2. **Historical:** Aorist *ephthasen* is past but need not mean AD 70—pre-50 calamities (Claudius AD 49 expulsion preferred by many near the letter date; Jerusalem massacre AD 49; famine; Theudas; Agrippa I; Zealot pressure of the late 40s/early 50s; general rejection of the gospel) or gospel-rejection as wrath are available; Judean Christian suffering is attested pre-70 (Paul's own career; Agrippa I). Weima rejects prophetic-aorist future for *phthanō* here ("arrive/reach"). T. Levi 6.11 near-parallel does not fix the referent.
3. **Form-critical:** A second thanksgiving is not anomalous (cf. 2 Thess 2:13–14); 2:13 may not be a formal thanksgiving; Paul adapts forms; *kai* + anaphoric *dia touto* link to 2:1–12; no transitional formula forcing a break at 2:13; emphatic "we" at 2:17 works better with 2:13–16 present; eschatological climax of the unit is at 2:16, not 2:12.
4. **Linguistic (Schmidt):** Pauline variation and possible traditional material; parallels for *kai*/embedding/word order; "and because of this" also in 2 Thess 2:11.
5. **Textual (decisive for Weima):** Universal MS attestation; no floating location.

**In-range exegetical notes (Weima on the unit as authentic):** 2:13 *paralambanō* + *dechomai* = technical reception of tradition + internal appropriation; word of God still "at work" (*energeitai*) in believers; 2:14 Judea = broader Palestine; same things from own *symphyletai* as Judean churches from *Ioudaioi*; aorist participle in 2:15 (Malherbe: supports discrete expulsion like Acts 17:5 unless one combines extreme Acts-skepticism with interpolation theory).

See [[2-thessalonians-authorship|2 Thessalonians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]] for the parallel (larger) debate about the second letter. Weima's four-argument rebuttal of 2 Thess pseudonymity (tone; 3:17 autograph; eschatology; literary dependence) uses 1 Thess 5 as a control: alleged "thief without warning" vs "preceding signs" misreads 1 Thess 5—unbelievers are surprised; believers "know well," are sons of light, and are not overtaken.

### Dating and Acts coordination

Traditional late AD 50 / early 51 from Corinth remains Weima's date. Gallio inscription anchors the Corinthian stay. Literary plural "we" in 3:1–5 helps reconcile the Acts itinerary (Donfried, via Weima). Absolute methodological priority of the letters over Acts is rejected: autobiographical accounts may have gaps or bias; politarchs and other Acts 17 details are epigraphically and letter-confirmed (Riesner; Bruce).

- **Interpolation debate:** Pearson 1971; Schmidt linguistic; Baur 1875 line

## Key interpretive cruxes

1. **1 Thess 4:13–18 — Rapture or formal reception?** *Apantēsis* in 4:17 is a technical term for the Hellenistic formal reception of a visiting dignitary. On Weima's reading (with Peterson, Green, most commentators; Cosby objections answerable per Gundry), the movement is believers meeting the descending Christ "in the air" and escorting him **to earth**—not ascending to a heavenly destination. Chrysostom already used the royal-entry analogy. Weima's preferred grief hypothesis: fear that the dead are at a **disadvantage** relative to the living at the parousia (not Gnostic "resurrection already," not total absence of prior resurrection teaching, not collapsed confidence in the second coming). Supports: emphatic "will certainly not precede" (15b); dead rise "first" then living (16–17); *hama syn autois* early placement; intertestamental parallels that "those who are left are more blessed." "Word of the Lord" (4:15) grounds the sequence (Pauline summary 15b + logion 16–17a + climax 17b). Full debate: [[parousia-1-thess-4|Parousia and the Fate of the Deceased (1 Thess 4:13–18)]].

2. **1 Thess 2:13–16 — Authentic or interpolated?** Anti-Jewish polemic and aorist *ephthasen* in 2:16 are the crux. Weima defends authenticity with four-argument rebuttal + universal MS support (see Scholarship above). Pearson (1971) and others argue post-70 interpolation.

3. **2:7 — *nēpioi* or *ēpioi*?** Textual crux with pastoral payoff: "we became **infants** among you" (Weima, NA/UBS trajectory, oldest witnesses) vs traditional "gentle." Ties to orphan metaphor at 2:17 and the innocence claim of 2:5–7b. See Textual and Manuscript Tradition.

4. **"Word of the Lord" (4:15) — Jesus tradition or prophetic word?** Paul appeals to an authoritative *logos kyriou* to ground equal participation of living and dead. Scholars debate a direct dominical saying unattested in the Gospels, a teaching inferred from resurrection faith, or a prophetic utterance in Christ's name. Weima structures 15–17 as summary + logion + Pauline climax without flattening the options into one wiki-voice solution.

5. **"Day of the Lord comes like a thief" (5:2) — Imminent expectation?** Paul says the Thessalonians "accurately know" the day comes unexpectedly (thief metaphor from Jesus tradition: Matt 24:43; Luke 12:39; also 2 Pet 3:10; Rev 3:3; 16:15—"in the night" unique to Paul among thief texts). For Weima this unpredictability applies to **unbelievers**; believers are "sons of light/day" who are not surprised (5:4–5). The pastoral problem is anxiety-filled fixation, not indifference—hence "ready" (*grēgoreō*) and "steady" (*nēphō*). Whether Paul expected an imminent parousia or teaches *unknowability* of timing remains debated; 5:10 contemplates both awake (alive) and asleep (dead) at Christ's coming.

6. **Paul's "we" in 4:15, 17 — Self-inclusion among those alive at the parousia?** "We who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord" does **not**, for Weima, prove Paul expected to live until the parousia: the "we" identifies the class of living believers; participles qualify; both states are possible at 5:10; life-threatening career already. Compare 1 Cor 15:51; Phil 1:21–24.

7. **5:3 "peace and security" — Imperial slogan or OT echo?** Weima prefers Roman imperial propaganda over traditional Jer/Ezek "Peace, peace" (anomaly of OT citation; Gentile audience; intro "whenever they say"; OT lacks "security"). Malherbe's internal false-prophet reading is rejected.

8. **5:14 *ataktoi* — Who and how serious?** Weima: "rebellious idlers," not merely the "disorderly"; lexical and thematic links to 4:11–12 and the extended command of 2 Thess 3:6–15 (Russell: 2 Thess 3 as "commentary" on 1 Thess 4:11–12 and 5:14—usable if 2 Thess is authentic, as Weima holds). Explains peace command, "all the brothers" holy kiss, and solemn public-reading charge.
