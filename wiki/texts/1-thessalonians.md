# First Letter to the Thessalonians

## Overview

First Thessalonians is Paul's earliest surviving letter (on standard scholarly dating) and one of the earliest documents in the entire New Testament, written from Corinth during his eighteen-month stay there after the founding of the Thessalonian church. The cosenders are Paul, Silvanus (Silas), and Timothy (1:1). The congregation at Thessalonica was predominantly Gentile — Paul explicitly notes that his readers had "turned to God from idols" (1:9) — though some converts from the local synagogue are present (Acts 17:4). The letter is universally accepted as authentic Pauline; no significant challenge to its authenticity exists in the scholarly literature.

## Historical Situation

Paul founded the Thessalonian church during his second missionary journey (Acts 17:1–9), arriving from Philippi via the Via Egnatia. He was accompanied by Silvanus (Silas) and Timothy. His synagogue ministry lasted at minimum three Sabbaths, after which a post-synagogue ministry to Gentiles followed, apparently centered in a workshop (see "Workshop ministry," below). This longer stay allowed Paul to receive financial aid from Philippi and establish his tentmaking trade.

The mission was cut short when Jewish and pagan opposition orchestrated a riot. The city officials took a security deposit (bail) from Jason and the other converts, which guaranteed the peace and legally prevented Paul and Silas from returning to the city—explaining Paul's claim that "Satan hindered us" (2:18). Forced to depart at night, the missionary trio fled to Berea.

Jewish antagonism followed them to Berea, driving Paul to travel south by sea to Athens to escape their jurisdiction, while Silas and Timothy temporarily remained in Berea. The three briefly reunited in Athens before Paul sent Timothy back to Thessalonica to strengthen the infant congregation (1 Thess 3:1–5)—Timothy was chosen because, unlike Paul and Silas, he was not implicated in the legal judgment of the Thessalonian authorities.

From Athens, Paul traveled to Corinth. There, Silas and Timothy rejoined him, bringing financial aid from Macedonia that allowed Paul to minister full-time. Timothy's positive return report about the Thessalonians' faith and love (3:6) occasions the first letter, written from Corinth in late AD 50 or early 51. The letter addresses several concerns Timothy brought back:
- ongoing affliction from fellow citizens (3:4)
- need for holiness in sexual conduct (4:3–8)
- the fate of believers who die before Christ's return (4:13–18)
- questions about "times and dates" of the day of the Lord (5:1–11)
- church leadership and congregational order (5:12–22)

The letter is warm throughout and extensively defends Paul's apostolic integrity against apparently circulating charges of impropriety (2:1–16).

## Thessalonica: The City

Weima's commentary provides exceptional detail on the city's historical significance:

**Location**: On the Via Egnatia (major Roman east-west highway) and the Thermaic Gulf (best harbor in the Aegean). Population c. 65,000–100,000; among the top ten largest cities in the Roman Empire.

**Political status**: Free city (*civitas libera*) since 42 BC, provincial capital of Macedonia. Governed by citizen assembly (*dēmos*), council (*boulē*), and *politarchs* — the distinctive city officials mentioned in Acts 17:6, 8. The politarch office is attested by ~70 inscriptions, 28 from Thessalonica itself, vindicating Acts' historical accuracy.

**Religious pluralism**: 25+ gods worshiped including Dionysus, Egyptian gods (Serapis, Isis), and **Cabirus** — the patron deity of Thessalonica, whose ubiquity on coinage led one scholar to speak of "Kabeiric monotheism." The imperial cult was politically significant: temples, priesthoods, coinage, and inscriptions all honored Roma and the deified emperors. A synagogue community also existed; Paul's own words in 1 Thess 2:14–15 confirm Jews in the city.

**Imperial ideology**: Thessalonica's close relationship with Rome — its history of political calculation, its free-city status, its cult of Roma and Roman benefactors — means Paul's proclamation of another *kyrios*, another *parousia*, another king constituted a direct challenge to the political-religious fabric of the city. The phrase "peace and security" in 5:3 almost certainly echoes Roman imperial propaganda disseminated through official media.

## Authorship

Universally accepted as Pauline. Even Kümmel (1977: 185) concluded: "there can be no justifiable doubt that all of 1 Thess is of Pauline origin."

### The 1 Thess 2:13–16 Interpolation Debate

The one internal dispute is whether 2:13–16 — containing the harshest language about Jews in Paul's Letters ("the Jews...who drove us out") — is a post-Pauline interpolation inserted after the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 (Pearson 1971; Boers; Richard; Koester). Weima evaluates four arguments for interpolation (theological, historical, form-critical, linguistic) and finds each unpersuasive:
- The theological tension with Romans 9–11 is overstated; both texts hold Jewish condemnation and future hope in tension.
- The aorist *ephthasen* need not refer to AD 70; Jewish suffering predating the letter is well documented (Zealot movement, Herod Agrippa's persecution).
- A double thanksgiving is not structurally anomalous; 2:13–16 connects organically with 2:1–12.
- The linguistic features Schmidt cites as un-Pauline have parallels throughout the undisputed letters.
- The passage has universal textual attestation — no manuscript evidence for it being secondary.

See [[2-thessalonians-authorship|2 Thessalonians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]] for the parallel debate about the second letter.

## Structure (Weima's Epistolary Analysis)

Weima employs an epistolary framework emphasizing formal letter conventions:

1. **Letter Opening** (1:1) — sender, recipient, greeting
2. **Thanksgiving** (1:2–10) — election; the missionaries' conduct; the Thessalonians' conversion and exemplary standing in Macedonia and Achaia
3. **Defense of Apostolic Actions and Absence** (2:1–3:13)
   - Defense of past visit — integrity of preaching and conduct (2:1–12); Thessalonians' reception of the word (2:13–16)
   - Defense of present absence — Satan's hindrance (2:17–20); Timothy's mission and return (3:1–10); transitional prayer (3:11–13)
4. **Exhortations** (4:1–5:22)
   - Sexual holiness (4:1–8)
   - *Philadelphia* / mutual love (4:9–12)
   - **Comfort concerning deceased believers at the parousia (4:13–18)**
   - Comfort concerning living believers at the day of the Lord (5:1–11)
   - Congregational life and worship (5:12–22)
5. **Letter Closing** (5:23–28) — peace benediction; prayer request; holy kiss; charge to read aloud

## Major Themes

### 1. The Parousia and Eschatological Hope

Both sections of 4:13–5:11 address anxieties about Christ's return. The first (4:13–18) addresses grief over believers who have died before the parousia; the second (5:1–11) addresses anxiety about the timing of the day of the Lord. Throughout, the eschatological hope grounds the ethical exhortations and provides comfort against ongoing affliction.

### 2. The *Parousia* as Royal Visit

The term *parousia* (2:19; 3:13; 4:15; 5:23) evokes the Hellenistic image of a visiting king or emperor's formal arrival. Weima argues Paul's use of *apantēsis* (formal reception) in 4:17 extends this metaphor: believers do not fly up to a heavenly destination but rather go out to meet the descending Christ and escort him the final distance to earth — just as civic delegations escorted visiting dignitaries into their city. See [[parousia-1-thess-4|Parousia and the Fate of the Deceased (1 Thess 4:13–18)]].

### 3. Anti-Imperial Polemic

"Peace and security" (*eirēnē kai asphaleia*, 5:3) echoes a standard Roman political slogan, asserting that Pax Romana guarantees prosperity and stability. Paul's reframing — "sudden destruction will come upon them" — directly challenges this claim. The proclamation of a rival *kyrios* with his own *parousia* would have been heard in Thessalonica as politically as well as religiously provocative.

### 4. Apostolic Defense and Integrity

An unusually long section (2:1–16) defends Paul's character against apparent accusations that he exploited his converts (flattery, greed, honor-seeking) and fled at the first sign of trouble. Paul responds with parental metaphors — nursing mother (2:7–8), father (2:11) — and points to his manual labor (2:9) as evidence that he was no wandering charlatan of the type common in antiquity.

### 5. Affliction and Imitation

The Thessalonians receive the gospel "in much affliction" (1:6) and are explicitly compared to the Judean churches who suffered from their fellow citizens (2:14). Their affliction is reframed as *mimēsis* — imitation of Paul, of the Lord, of other churches — and connected to the eschatological vindication that awaits them.

### 6. Workshop Evangelism

Weima argues that Paul's evangelistic ministry in Thessalonica operated primarily through his leatherworking workshop (likely in Jason's home), not in public venues. "Working night and day... we preached to you the gospel of God" (2:9) likely refers to simultaneous activity: preaching while working. This is consistent with ancient evidence that workshops served as settings for intellectual discourse (cf. Celsus's complaint about Christians converting in cobblers' shops).

## Interpretive Cruxes

1. **1 Thess 4:13–18 — Rapture or formal reception?** The word *apantēsis* in 4:17 is a technical term for the Hellenistic formal reception of a visiting dignitary. The movement implied is believers meeting the descending Christ in the air and escorting him to earth — not believers ascending to heaven. Dispensationalist "secret rapture" readings require importing a different destination than the text implies. See [[parousia-1-thess-4|Parousia and the Fate of the Deceased (1 Thess 4:13–18)]].

2. **1 Thess 2:13–16 — Authentic or interpolated?** The anti-Jewish polemic and the aorist *ephthasen* in 2:16 are the crux. Weima defends authenticity; Pearson (1971) and others argue for post-70 interpolation. See entry above.

3. **"Word of the Lord" (4:15) — Jesus tradition or prophetic word?** Paul appeals to an authoritative *logia kyriou* in 4:15 to ground the assertion that living believers will not precede deceased ones. Scholars debate whether this is a direct dominical saying unattested in the Gospels, a teaching inferred from the resurrection faith, or a prophetic utterance delivered in Christ's name.

4. **"Day of the Lord comes like a thief" (5:2) — Imminent expectation?** Paul says the Thessalonians "know well" that the day of the Lord comes unexpectedly, but this is for unbelievers; believers are "sons of light" who are not surprised (5:4–5). Whether this reflects an expectation of an imminent parousia or simply the *unknowability* of timing is debated.

5. **Paul's "we" in 4:15, 17 — Self-inclusion among those alive at the parousia?** Paul writes "we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord." Does this mean he expected to outlive the parousia? Weima argues no: the "we" identifies the class (living believers) to which Paul and his readers currently belong; Paul elsewhere contemplates both possibilities (1 Cor 15:51; Phil 1:21–24).
