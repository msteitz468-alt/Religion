# The Second Letter to the Corinthians

Paul's most emotionally intense and personally revealing letter. Second Corinthians is simultaneously a defense of apostolic authority, a theology of the cross, and a pastoral appeal to a church that has rejected the apostle who founded it. It contains some of the most difficult Greek in the New Testament and some of its most theologically rich passages — including the ministry of the new covenant (ch. 3), the earthen vessel (ch. 4), the reconciliation passage (5:11–21), and the "fool's speech" and its climax in the thorn in the flesh (11:22–12:10).

## Authorship, Date, and Setting

Pauline authorship is not disputed. Second Corinthians was written from Macedonia (7:5–6) after Paul had received Titus's report that the Corinthians had been reconciled to the apostle, at least in part, following the "tearful letter" Titus had carried to them.

**The Corinthian correspondence as a whole**: At least four letters passed between Paul and Corinth, only two of which survive:
1. **The "Previous Letter"** (lost) — warned against association with immoral persons (cf. 1 Cor 5:9); possibly echoed in 2 Cor 6:14–7:1
2. **First Corinthians** — written from Ephesus, c. AD 54–55
3. **The "Tearful Letter"** (lost) — written "out of much affliction and anguish of heart, through many tears" (2:4) after Paul's painful emergency visit to Corinth from Ephesus; carried by Titus
4. **Second Corinthians** — written from Macedonia, c. AD 55–56, after Titus's return with the news of partial reconciliation

Paul's emergency visit (his second to Corinth) was triggered by deteriorating relationships; he "came in grief" (2:1) and was publicly affronted by a leading member of the church. The church, by its silence, tacitly supported the offender. Paul withdrew, sent the Tearful Letter via Titus, and eventually received news of Corinthian sorrow and renewed zeal. Second Corinthians is written to build on this fragile reconciliation — and to address the new threat posed by rival apostolic claimants who have arrived in the church.

## Structure

The letter divides into three major sections organized around Paul's relationship with the Corinthians and his defense of apostolic authority:

**I. The Letter Opening: The Call to Fellowship with the Apostle (1:1–2:17)**
Prescript and apostolic benediction; Paul's explanation of why he failed to visit as promised; the theology of comfort in affliction; the "odor of Christ" — from death to life (2:14–17)

**II. The Body: The Apostolic Mission and the Apostle in Mission (3:1–7:16)**
- The new covenant ministry and Moses (3:1–18): Letter vs. Spirit; the veil; the glory that surpasses
- The earthen vessel and the word of faith (4:1–15)
- The eternal weight of glory (4:16–18)
- The heavenly dwelling / the Spirit as down payment (5:1–10)
- The ministry of reconciliation (5:11–21): new creation; the great exchange (5:21)
- The appeal to receive Paul's apostleship (6:1–7:16), including the call to separation (6:14–7:1) and Titus's report (7:5–16)

**III. The Letter Closing: The Call to Simplicity (8:1–13:14)**
- The collection as the confession of the Gospel (8:1–9:15)
- The legitimation of Paul as apostle: bold appeal and fool's speech (10:1–13:14)
  - Paul's lowly appeal and bold assertion of authority (10:1–18)
  - The fool's speech: Paul's sufferings as his apostolic credentials (11:1–12:10)
  - The thorn in the flesh; power in weakness (12:1–10)
  - Closing exhortation and grace-pronouncement (13:11–14)

## Occasion and Purpose: Seifrid's Reading

[[mark-seifrid|Seifrid]] (PNTC, 2014; fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18, [[seifrid-2-corinthians-pntc|source page]]) argues that the singular purpose of 2 Corinthians is the **legitimation of Paul's apostolic authority** in Corinth. Every section — the collection, the new covenant exposition, the fool's speech, the call to separation — serves this single aim. The question driving the letter is: **Does the risen Lord speak in Paul, the weakling?** (13:3). The Corinthians, drawn to outward marks of power and charisma, have found Paul's weak presence scandalous and preferred rival apostolic claimants. Opponents appear as **intruders** into the apostle–church bond, not as the letter's primary drama.

The fundamental issue is not Paul's person but the Gospel. To judge the apostle by outward power and rhetorical success is to misjudge the Gospel itself. Seifrid: the Corinthians recognize that “the life and existence of an apostle is merely **Christian existence written large**” — already the issue in 1 Corinthians, and the focal issue in 2 Corinthians. (Prior wiki summary had paraphrased this as “large-screen display”; Seifrid’s wording is “written large.”) To accept the weak apostle is to accept that their own lives take the same form — through affliction toward comfort (1:3–7), through dying toward life (4:10–12).

## Major Themes

**1. The Theology of the Cross (Theologia Crucis)**
God's saving work operates *sub contrario* — under its opposite. Power is present in weakness (12:9–10). Life is given in death (4:10–12). Wealth is found in poverty (8:9). Wisdom comes through foolishness. The Gospel does not deliver believers from suffering into visible triumph; it delivers them through suffering, so that they might be freed from themselves and given Christ. See [[theology-of-the-cross|Theology of the Cross]].

**2. Apostolic Existence as the Pattern of Christian Life**
"As you are partakers of sufferings, so also you are partakers of salvation" (1:7). The apostle bears "the deadness of Jesus" in his body so that the life of the risen Jesus may be manifest in him (4:10). This apostolic pattern — death → life, weakness → power — is not exceptional but is the normative shape of Christian existence (“believing existence written large,” Seifrid on 4:8–9). The Corinthian preference for powerful apostles who display rhetorical skill and miraculous power amounts to a practical denial of the Gospel.

**3. The New Covenant and the Veil of Moses (Ch. 3)**
Paul describes his apostolic ministry in contrast to the ministry of Moses: letter vs. Spirit; old covenant vs. new; glory that is *abrogated* (*katargeō*, not merely “fading”) vs. surpassing glory. Seifrid's distinctive reading: Paul is **not** addressing a second Judaizing front but using Moses to expose a **universal human blindness** of which Israel’s story is paradigmatic, including the Corinthians. The veil remains “until today” on the public reading of the old covenant; it is removed only “in Christ” (3:14–16). Glory is revealed *sub contrario*; the “mirror” of 3:18 is the crucified Christ — “presently we see only through our ears.” See [[new-covenant|New Covenant]].

**4. The Ministry of Reconciliation and the New Creation (5:11–21)**
Three declarations that together constitute Paul's Gospel in miniature (Seifrid densified 2026-07-18):
- **"If anyone is in Christ, [there is] a new creation"** (5:17) — localized *in Christ*; personal renewal that necessarily implies cosmic re-creation; new creation intersecting the fallen world, not a general new historical era. See [[new-creation]].
- **"God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them"** (5:19) — Seifrid: *katallassō* primarily overcomes **human** enmity against God; reconciliation is a forensic event (final judgment entered present); universal scope ≠ universal salvation (“interruption” remains). Ambassadors’ “Be reconciled to God!” is the message to the world (5:20); explicit address to the Corinthians turns at 6:1. See [[reconciliation]].
- **"He made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God"** (5:21) — Seifrid: irreducibly **ontological** exchange (exclusive + inclusive place-taking; Christ the “sinless sinner”); forensic and extrinsic *in Christ*, not bare “money in the bank” imputation nor infusion; “We remain Christ’s sin… Christ remains our righteousness.” See [[justification]] · [[theology-of-the-cross]].

**5. The Earthen Vessel**
"We have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (4:7). The apostle's fragility and weakness are not incidental to his mission; they are essential to it. God displays his power precisely in the weakness of his messenger. This is Paul's counter to the Corinthian preference for impressive apostles.

**6. The Eternal Weight of Glory**
"This light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (4:17). The present sufferings are real but not ultimate; the invisible is more real than the visible; the eternal outweighs the temporary. This eschatological reserve grounds Christian endurance.

**7. The Collection as Apostolic Bond and *Haplotēs***
Chapters 8–9 on the collection for Jerusalem are not a digression but part of the letter’s closing turn (apostolic *parousia*). The collection binds the Corinthians to the wider churches and to Paul; Titus returns to complete it (8:16–17). Seifrid defends the unity of chapters 8–9 against partition theories. Load-bearing translation: *haplotēs* is **“simplicity”** (open receiving-and-giving), not English “generosity” (ESV/NIV/NRSV) — a letter-theme linking 1:12, 8:2; 9:11, 13, and 11:3 (“simplicity toward Christ”). At 8:9 the grace of the Lord Jesus (rich/poor exchange) is *theologia crucis* in economic form: Christ’s self-giving counters Corinthian upward striving.

**8. The Fool's Speech and Power in Weakness (11:22–12:10)**
Paul's ultimate credential is his catalog of sufferings (11:23–29). The climax is the "thorn in the flesh" (12:7) — Seifrid: *skolops* as pointed object/thorn (not impalement); apposed as “angel/messenger of Satan”; **identity deliberately open** (not reduced to a single medical diagnosis); threefold plea like Gethsemane; answer: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness” (12:9) — christologically grounded absolute, not a general worldly principle. At **13:4** Seifrid reads Christ crucified ***ex astheneias*** (“**out of** weakness”), not major English “**in** weakness”: weakness had become inherent to Christ; God’s power operates only within weakness and overcomes it without removing it. This is the theological center of the letter.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. The Identity of Paul's Opponents (Chs. 10–13)**
The rivals who have arrived in Corinth are the most disputed question in 2 Corinthians scholarship. See [[pauls-opponents-2-corinthians|Paul's Opponents in 2 Corinthians]].

**2. The Integrity of the Letter**
Partition theories propose up to five original fragments (2:14–7:4; 6:14–7:1; 8; 9; 10–13). Seifrid defends the unity of the letter throughout. The coherent theme of apostolic legitimacy runs from 1:1 to 13:14.

**3. The "Thorn in the Flesh" (12:7)**
Over a dozen interpretations have been proposed: chronic illness (ophthalmia, malaria, epilepsy), a spiritual temptation, a person (a persistent opponent). Seifrid: *skolops* = pointed object/thorn; divine passive “given”; apposed as “angel of Satan” who buffets Paul so he not exalt himself over surpassing revelations; **identity left open** (not reduced to one diagnosis); God uses even Satan for Paul’s good; occasion of the letter’s theological climax (12:9).

**4. "Being Unequally Yoked" (6:14–7:1)**
Many scholars regard this passage as a non-Pauline interpolation (Qumran-like language; apparent disruption of 6:11–13 / 7:2). Seifrid **defends originality** and reads it as directed at the **opponents** (not generic pagan social contact), likely echoing the previous letter’s separation warning, now applied to false apostolic claimants; temple / composite Scripture; cleanse and perfect holiness (7:1).

**5. "He Was Made Sin" (5:21)**
"God made him who knew no sin to be sin for us." Options: (a) sin offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*); (b) treated as if sinner (bare forensic imputation); (c) identification with sinful condition without sinning. Seifrid: irreducibly **ontological** exclusive + inclusive place-taking (Christ the “sinless sinner”); forensic event + extrinsic location *in Christ* + new creation — not bare “money in the bank” imputation nor infusion; “We remain Christ’s sin… Christ remains our righteousness.”

**6. The Scope of "New Creation" (5:17)**
Individual / corporate / cosmic? Seifrid: localized *in Christ*; personal re-creation necessarily implies cosmic re-creation; not a general new era within history but new creation intersecting the fallen world.

**7. Asia Crisis (1:8–11)**
Illness vs judicial/violent threat? Seifrid **rejects** a primary illness reading for Asia; the “thorn” is older; Acts’ Ephesus tumult and possible legal aftermath more plausible. The **trouble with the Corinthians**, not Asia, backgrounds the letter’s message.

**8. The Offender (2:5–11)**
Seifrid: not identified with the incestuous man of 1 Cor 5; a distinct affront to Paul on the emergency visit; the many have rebuked him; Paul urges forgiveness and comfort — *charizomai* as gift.

## Textual and Manuscript Tradition

Pauline authorship undisputed. The letter is preserved in the standard NT manuscript tradition (𝔓⁴⁶, major uncials). Seifrid works from the Greek text with ESV as base English; he does not re-litigate major textual variants as a central project. Critical debate concentrates on **literary integrity** (partition into multiple letters) rather than verse-level MS problems — see cruxes above. For reception history of partition theories, Seifrid’s introduction and closing notes (Titus aorists; omission of the church-chosen brother) argue that such data do **not** force a later-letter partition of 10–13.

## Scholarship and Historiography

Major positions in the commentary tradition Seifrid engages: Bultmann (KEK — close exegetical partner, demythologizing rejected); Furnish (AB — historical reconstruction); Georgi (divine-man opponents — rejected as over-determined); Käsemann (legitimacy of the apostle in 10–13 — expanded by Seifrid to the whole letter); Harris (NIGTC — technical Greek); Hafemann (suffering and Spirit). Dating first Corinth visit via Gallio/Delphi to A.D. 50–51 is Seifrid’s chronological anchor; 2 Cor from Macedonia after Titus’s return, c. mid-50s.
