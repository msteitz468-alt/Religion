# The Second Letter to the Corinthians

Paul's most emotionally intense and personally revealing letter. Second Corinthians is simultaneously a defense of apostolic authority, a theology of the cross, and a pastoral appeal to a church that has rejected the apostle who founded it. It contains some of the most difficult Greek in the New Testament and some of its most theologically rich passages — including the ministry of the new covenant (ch. 3), the earthen vessel (ch. 4), the reconciliation passage (5:11–21), and the "fool's speech" and its climax in the thorn in the flesh (11:22–12:10).

## Authorship, Date, and Setting

Pauline authorship is not disputed. Second Corinthians was written from Macedonia (7:5–6) after Paul had received Titus's report that the Corinthians had been reconciled to the apostle, at least in part, following the "tearful letter" Titus had carried to them.

**The Corinthian correspondence as a whole**: At least four letters passed between Paul and Corinth, only two of which survive:
1. **The "Previous Letter"** (lost) — warned against association with immoral persons (cf. 1 Cor 5:9); possibly echoed in 2 Cor 6:14–7:1
2. **First Corinthians** — written from Ephesus, c. AD 54–55
3. **The "Tearful Letter"** (lost) — written "out of much affliction and anguish of heart, through many tears" (2:4) after Paul's painful emergency visit to Corinth from Ephesus; carried by Titus
4. **Second Corinthians** — written from Macedonia, c. AD 55–56, after Titus's return with the news of partial reconciliation

Paul's emergency visit (his second to Corinth) was triggered by deteriorating relationships; he "came in grief" (2:1) and was publicly affronted by a leading member of the church. The church, by its silence, tacitly supported the offender. Paul withdrew, sent the Tearful Letter via Titus, and eventually received news of Corinthian sorrow and renewed zeal. Second Corinthians is written to build on this fragile reconciliation — and to address the new threat posed by rival apostolic claimants who have arrived in the church.

## Structure

The letter divides into three major sections organized around Paul's relationship with the Corinthians and his defense of apostolic authority:

**I. The Letter Opening: The Call to Fellowship with the Apostle (1:1–2:17)**
Prescript and apostolic benediction; Paul's explanation of why he failed to visit as promised; the theology of comfort in affliction; the "odor of Christ" — from death to life (2:14–17)

**II. The Body: The Apostolic Mission and the Apostle in Mission (3:1–7:16)**
- The new covenant ministry and Moses (3:1–18): Letter vs. Spirit; the veil; the glory that surpasses
- The earthen vessel and the word of faith (4:1–15)
- The eternal weight of glory (4:16–18)
- The heavenly dwelling / the Spirit as down payment (5:1–10)
- The ministry of reconciliation (5:11–21): new creation; the great exchange (5:21)
- The appeal to receive Paul's apostleship (6:1–7:16), including the call to separation (6:14–7:1) and Titus's report (7:5–16)

**III. The Letter Closing: The Call to Simplicity (8:1–13:14)**
- The collection as the confession of the Gospel (8:1–9:15)
- The legitimation of Paul as apostle: bold appeal and fool's speech (10:1–13:14)
  - Paul's lowly appeal and bold assertion of authority (10:1–18)
  - The fool's speech: Paul's sufferings as his apostolic credentials (11:1–12:10)
  - The thorn in the flesh; power in weakness (12:1–10)
  - Closing exhortation and grace-pronouncement (13:11–14)

## Occasion and Purpose: Seifrid's Reading

[[mark-seifrid|Seifrid]] (PNTC, 2014) argues that the singular purpose of 2 Corinthians is the legitimation of Paul's apostolic authority in Corinth. Every section — the collection, the new covenant exposition, the fool's speech, the call to separation — serves this single aim. The question driving the letter is: **Does the risen Lord speak in Paul, the weakling?** (13:3). The Corinthians, drawn to outward marks of power and charisma, have found Paul's weak presence scandalous and preferred rival apostolic claimants.

The fundamental issue, however, is not Paul's person. It is the Gospel. To judge the apostle by the standards of outward power and rhetorical success is to misjudge the Gospel itself. "The Corinthians imagine that salvation means that they will be made like God. They do not realize that in salvation God has been made like them and has come to them in their foolishness, sin, and weakness." The form of apostolic life is nothing other than the form of Christian life — the apostle is "Christian existence in large-screen display" (1:7). This is the reason the Corinthians find Paul's weakness intolerable: they know, intuitively, that to accept the apostle is to accept his form of life as their own.

## Major Themes

**1. The Theology of the Cross (Theologia Crucis)**
God's saving work operates *sub contrario* — under its opposite. Power is present in weakness (12:9–10). Life is given in death (4:10–12). Wealth is found in poverty (8:9). Wisdom comes through foolishness. The Gospel does not deliver believers from suffering into visible triumph; it delivers them through suffering, so that they might be freed from themselves and given Christ. See [[theology-of-the-cross|Theology of the Cross]].

**2. Apostolic Existence as the Pattern of Christian Life**
"As you are partakers of sufferings, so also you are partakers of salvation" (1:7). The apostle bears "the deadness of Jesus" in his body so that the life of the risen Jesus may be manifest in him (4:10). This apostolic pattern — death → life, weakness → power — is not exceptional but is the normative shape of Christian existence. The Corinthian preference for powerful apostles who display rhetorical skill and miraculous power amounts to a practical denial of the Gospel.

**3. The New Covenant and the Veil of Moses (Ch. 3)**
Paul describes his apostolic ministry in contrast to the ministry of Moses: letter vs. Spirit; old covenant vs. new; fading glory vs. surpassing glory. Seifrid's distinctive reading: Paul is not addressing Judaizing opponents but using Moses to expose a universal human blindness. The veil on the hearts of "the sons of Israel" — which prevents them from seeing the glory of the Lord in Moses' face — is the veil on all human hearts. It is removed only "in Christ" (3:14). The Corinthian inability to see Christ's presence in the weak apostle is a specific instance of this universal blindness.

**4. The Ministry of Reconciliation and the New Creation (5:11–21)**
Three declarations that together constitute Paul's Gospel in miniature:
- **"If anyone is in Christ, [there is] a new creation"** (5:17) — the eschaton has broken into the world; the old has passed away; the new has come. This is not merely individual renewal but implies the re-creation of all things.
- **"God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them"** (5:19) — God is the initiator and agent; the apostle is a bearer and ambassador of this reconciliation.
- **"He made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God"** (5:21) — the "great exchange": Christ bears sin; believers receive righteousness. See [[theology-of-the-cross|Theology of the Cross]].

**5. The Earthen Vessel**
"We have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (4:7). The apostle's fragility and weakness are not incidental to his mission; they are essential to it. God displays his power precisely in the weakness of his messenger. This is Paul's counter to the Corinthian preference for impressive apostles.

**6. The Eternal Weight of Glory**
"This light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (4:17). The present sufferings are real but not ultimate; the invisible is more real than the visible; the eternal outweighs the temporary. This eschatological reserve grounds Christian endurance.

**7. The Collection as Apostolic Bond**
Chapters 8–9 on the collection for Jerusalem are not a digression. The collection serves Paul's aim of binding the Corinthians to the wider network of churches and to his own apostolic authority. Titus — the very emissary through whom the reconciliation was achieved — returns to Corinth to complete the collection project (8:16–17). Seifrid defends the unity of chapters 8–9 against partition theories.

**8. The Fool's Speech and Power in Weakness (11:22–12:10)**
Paul's ultimate credential is his catalog of sufferings (11:23–29): imprisonments, beatings, shipwrecks, dangers of all kinds. The climax is the "thorn in the flesh" (12:7) and the divine response: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (12:9). Paul's apostolic authority rests not in miracles or rhetoric but in his participation in the dying and rising of Jesus. This is the theological center of the letter.

## Key Interpretive Cruxes

**1. The Identity of Paul's Opponents (Chs. 10–13)**
The rivals who have arrived in Corinth are the most disputed question in 2 Corinthians scholarship. See [[pauls-opponents-2-corinthians|Paul's Opponents in 2 Corinthians]].

**2. The Integrity of the Letter**
Partition theories propose up to five original fragments (2:14–7:4; 6:14–7:1; 8; 9; 10–13). Seifrid defends the unity of the letter throughout. The coherent theme of apostolic legitimacy runs from 1:1 to 13:14.

**3. The "Thorn in the Flesh" (12:7)**
Over a dozen interpretations have been proposed: chronic illness (ophthalmia, malaria, epilepsy), a spiritual temptation, a person (a persistent opponent). Seifrid notes Paul already describes the thorn as pre-existing his crisis in Asia and relates it to his apostolic existence; whatever it is, it is the occasion for the letter's theological climax.

**4. "Being Unequally Yoked" (6:14–7:1)**
Many scholars regard this passage as a non-Pauline interpolation, given its Qumran-like language and its apparent disruption of the appeal in 6:11–13 and 7:2. Seifrid defends it as Pauline — likely an echo of the previous letter (1 Cor 5:9) applied now to the rival apostles who have entered the church.

**5. "He Was Made Sin" (5:21)**
"God made him who knew no sin to be sin for us." Is this (a) Christ became a sin offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt* = sin offering in the LXX); (b) God treated the sinless Christ as if he were a sinner (the traditional Protestant substitution reading); or (c) Christ identified with the human condition of sinfulness without himself sinning? Seifrid reads this as the most compressed and densest expression of substitutionary atonement in the NT — the "great exchange" foundational to justification.

**6. The Scope of "New Creation" (5:17)**
Is "new creation" in 5:17 individual (the individual believer's renewal in Christ), corporate (the new covenant community), or cosmic (the eschatological renewal of all creation)? Seifrid: the re-creation of the human being necessarily implies the re-creation of all things — the personal and the cosmic are inseparable.
