# Temple Scroll (Megillat ha-Miqdash)
**Sigla**: 11QT (11Q19 = Temple Scroll A, the main copy); also 11Q20 (Temple Scroll B), 4Q365a

The longest of the Dead Sea Scrolls (approximately 8.6 meters; 66 columns in the main copy). A systematic, idealized Torah for the Temple, Jerusalem, and the life of Israel — written as if spoken directly by God to Moses in the first person. It effectively presents itself as a **supplement to and rewriting of Deuteronomy** and is sometimes called the "Deuteronomy of Qumran."

## Canon and Reception
The Temple Scroll's status within Qumran is contested. Some scholars (Yadin) argue it was treated as canonical Scripture at Qumran; Vermes is more cautious — it is authoritative but probably not placed on the same level as the Torah. Its rewriting of Scripture in the first person divine voice (*lex divina*) style represents a claim of equal authority. Not canonical in any other tradition; unknown before the Qumran discovery.

## Structure and Contents

### Temple Section (Cols. II–XLVII)
The central section describes an idealized Temple:
- **Dimensions**: The Temple courts are vastly larger than the historical Temple. The outer court is a massive square (1600 cubits per side by one reconstruction — about 800m), with twelve gates named after the twelve tribes
- **The Holy of Holies**: Strict regulations for the inner sanctuary's purity; no one may enter except the High Priest
- **Furnishings**: Detailed specifications for the altar, layers, and courts — paralleling Exodus 25-27 but with significant expansions
- **500-cubit inner court**: An intermediate court not found in the historical Temple, providing an additional buffer of purity
- **Festival Calendar**: A comprehensive calendar of offerings for every day of the year, organized according to the 364-day solar calendar

### Festival Laws (Cols. XIII–XXIX)
Extensive regulations for:
- Passover, Unleavened Bread
- Pentecost (Shavuot)
- **New Wine Festival** (50 days after Shavuot): A new festival not in the biblical Torah — the offering of first fruits of wine
- **New Oil Festival** (50 days after New Wine): First fruits of oil
- **Wood Offering Festival**: Twelve staggered days across the year when each tribe brings wood for the altar (also attested in the Reworked Pentateuch 4Q365, which expands the Song of Miriam and includes Wood Offering legislation)
- Day of Atonement
- Feast of Booths (Sukkot)

The calendar is the **Qumran solar calendar** (364-day year), making all festivals fall on fixed days of the week.

### Purity Laws (Cols. XLVIII–LI)
Rigorous expansions of biblical purity law:
- Extended impurity periods for corpse contact, skin disease, discharge
- Strict rules about who may enter Jerusalem and the Temple precincts
- Jerusalem itself is treated as equivalent to the Temple precincts in terms of purity requirements — sexual intercourse and defecation are forbidden within the city ("because Jerusalem is the Temple city")
- Latrine facilities must be located 3000 cubits northwest of the city

### The King's Law (Cols. LVI–LIX)
Based on Deuteronomy 17:14-20 ("the law of the king"), expanded significantly:
- The king must not multiply horses, gold, silver, or wives beyond specified limits
- He must have a Council of twelve priests, twelve Levites, and twelve men from the people — 36 councillors
- He may not make war without consulting the Council and the High Priest
- The **bodyguard** of the king is specified: 12,000 men divided into 12 units of 1,000

### The Treason/Crucifixion Penalty (Col. LXIV)
One of the most historically significant passages:
> "If a man slanders his people and delivers his people to a foreign nation and does evil to his people, you shall hang him on a tree and he shall die."

The Hebrew *talah* ("hang") is applied to the **traitor** who betrays Israel to foreigners. This is the Qumran interpretation of Deuteronomy 21:22-23 ("if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, you shall hang him on a tree"). In the biblical context, hanging follows execution; the Temple Scroll makes hanging itself the means of execution for treason — i.e., **crucifixion as capital punishment**.

The Nahum Commentary (4Q169) connects this explicitly to Alexander Jannaeus's mass crucifixion of Pharisees: the "furious young lion" who "hangs men alive" (*talah anashim ḥayyim*). Paul cites Deut. 21:23 ("cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree") in Galatians 3:13 in connection with Christ's crucifixion. The Temple Scroll demonstrates that this passage was already associated with execution by crucifixion in Second Temple Jewish interpretation.

## Hermeneutical Significance
The Temple Scroll adopts a **divine-first-person** voice throughout, transforming the Mosaic narrative into direct divine speech ("I command you" rather than "God commanded Moses"). This makes it either:
1. A sectarian composition claiming divine revelation equal to Torah (Yadin's view)
2. A systematized halakhic document collecting and harmonizing Torah texts in an authoritative but subordinate mode (more cautious view)

The rewriting of Torah in God's own voice is a hermeneutical move also seen in Jubilees and later in Samaritan Pentateuch expansions — part of a broader Second Temple tendency to produce expanded, authoritative reworkings of the Mosaic legislation.
