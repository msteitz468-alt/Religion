# Quis Dives Salvetur (Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?)

A homily by [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]] — the only complete surviving example of
his *popular* (as opposed to esoteric) teaching. Cast as a sermon but too long for one oral delivery,
it addresses a question acute in mercantile Alexandria: how to reconcile Christianity with the
possession of wealth. The text survives in the Escurial manuscript "S," with the closing legend
recovered from Eusebius (*H.E.* iii.23) and Maximus Confessor. Translated in the Delphi edition by
G. W. Butterworth.

## Base Text and Method

The homily expounds the pericope of the Rich Young Ruler, **Mark 10:17–31** ("sell whatsoever thou
hast and distribute to the poor"; the camel and the needle's eye; "That which is impossible with men
is possible with God"). Clement's hermeneutic is explicitly **allegorical/spiritual, not literal**:
"we must not understand His words literally, but with due inquiry and intelligence we must search out
and master their hidden meaning" (l. 29899). This is a paradigm case of the Alexandrian preference for
the spiritual over the plain sense (see [[allegorical-reading|allegorical reading]],
[[clement-of-alexandria|Clement (commentator)]]).

## Core Thesis: Renounce the Passions, Not the Property

The command to "sell what belongs to thee" is "not what some hastily take it to be, a command to
fling away the substance that belongs to him... but to banish from the soul its opinions about riches,
its attachment to them, its excessive desire" (l. 29996). "So let a man do away, not with his
possessions, but rather with the passions of his soul" (l. 30072).

Clement argues the literal reading is both insufficient and self-contradictory:
- **Mere poverty is no virtue.** The beggars in the road would otherwise be "most blessed," though
  ignorant of God — absurd. Pagans (Anaxagoras, Democritus, Crates) already renounced property for
  philosophy or vainglory, and some only "intensified" their passions, becoming "supercilious,
  boastful, conceited."
- **Wealth is a morally neutral instrument.** "Wealth too is an instrument... You can use it rightly;
  it ministers to righteousness... For its nature is to minister, not to rule" (l. 30064).
  Responsibility lies in "the mind of man, which has in itself both free judgment and full liberty,"
  not in the thing. See [[use-of-wealth|use of wealth]].
- **Literal divestment would contradict other dominical sayings** — "Make to yourselves friends from
  the mammon of unrighteousness," feeding the hungry and clothing the naked (impossible if all are
  destitute). Christ guests with Zacchaeus, Levi, and Matthew without bidding them give up their
  riches.

Hence two wealths and two poverties: "the man who is truly and nobly rich... is he who is rich in
virtues"; the "spurious rich man" is "rich according to the flesh" (l. 30163). The disciple who holds
goods "as gifts of God," ministers from them, "is not the slave of his possessions," and could lose
them "with a cheerful mind" is the one "called poor in spirit."

## The Program of Salvation for the Rich

"Salvation does not depend upon outward things... but upon the soul's virtue, upon faith, hope, love,
brotherliness, knowledge, gentleness, humility and truth" (l. 30139). Practically: appoint "some man
of God as trainer and pilot" — a spiritual director to obey and be reproved by; give generously and
seek out the needy (the athlete/contest metaphor frames salvation as a trained struggle). The two
great commandments are expounded through the Good Samaritan, read allegorically: **Christ himself** is
the Samaritan who pities humanity "almost done to death by the world-rulers of the darkness," pouring
"the wine, the blood of David's vine" and "the oil... of pity from the Father's heart" (l. 30379).

On [[freedom|free will]]: "force is hateful to God... to save men against their will is an act of
force, but to save them when they choose is an act of grace" (l. 30217).

## Second Repentance

The homily affirms forgiveness for post-baptismal sin: even one who "after the baptismal seal" falls
"into certain sins" should not despair — "to every one who turns to God in truth with his whole heart
the doors are opened" (l. 30566). "Of sins already committed God gives remission, but of those that
are to come each man procures his own remission." See [[second-repentance|second repentance]].

## The Legend of John and the Robber

The homily's celebrated climax (ch. 42): "hear a story that is no mere story, but a true account of
the apostle John." After Patmos, John entrusts a promising youth to a bishop; the youth, neglected,
falls in with bad company and becomes a violent robber chief. Returning, John demands back "the
deposit which Christ and I together entrusted"; told the youth is "dead to God," he rides to the
mountains, lets the robbers capture him, and pursues the fleeing chief: "Why do you fly from me,
child, from your own father, from this old, unarmed man?... Stand; believe; Christ has sent me"
(l. 30680). The robber throws down his weapons, weeps, is "baptized a second time with his tears,"
and John kisses the blood-guilty right hand "as having been purified by his repentance" — "a great
example of sincere repentance... a trophy of a resurrection that can be seen." The homily closes with
a doxology to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The legend is a major source for the figure of the
aged Apostle [[john-apostle|John]] at Ephesus.

## Canon and Reception

A non-scriptural patristic homily, authoritative in Eastern Orthodoxy and Catholicism. Historically it
is one of the earliest sustained Christian treatments of wealth ethics, and Clement's "disposition,
not dispossession" reading became a perennial (and contested) position in Christian social teaching —
set against the literal-renunciation tradition of monasticism and later radical movements. See the
controversy page [[rich-man-renunciation|Must the Rich Man Sell All?]].
