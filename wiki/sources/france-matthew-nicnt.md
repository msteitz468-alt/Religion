## What the source is

R. T. France, *The Gospel of Matthew*, New International Commentary on the New Testament (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2007) — a full-scale exegetical commentary on the Greek text of Matthew, presented in English translation with transliterated Greek, running to roughly 1,200 printed pages. It is France's final and largest work, building on his earlier *Matthew: Evangelist and Teacher* (1989) and *Jesus and the Old Testament* (1971).

The volume comprises three evidence classes that must be kept separate:

1. **France's own translation** of Matthew, printed pericope by pericope.
2. **France's exposition** — the main commentary voice, containing his interpretive and theological claims.
3. **Footnote-level engagement** with secondary literature (Davies–Allison, Luz, Gundry, Hagner, Carson, and others), where France reports and contests positions he does not hold.

Claims from class 3 are attributed to the scholar France is reporting, not to France.

## Hermeneutical frame

France reads Matthew as a coherent literary composition rather than as a seam of redacted sources, and the commentary's method follows from that. Four commitments govern it.

**Structure is reader-imposed, not authorial.** France rejects Bacon's five-book scheme and Kingsbury's three-part "from that time" framework, adopting a six-division geographical outline derived from Mark's — but he presents it as his own sympathetic reading of the narrative's dynamics, explicitly declining to claim recovery of an authorial blueprint.

**Fulfilment is typological pattern-recognition, not predictive verification.** This is the volume's governing theme and its most transferable methodological contribution. France concedes without hedging that the quoted texts are frequently not predictive in their own settings, and defends Matthew's usage as the recognition of transferable patterns instead.

**Source criticism is retained but held loosely.** Markan priority stands; "x-copied-y" models, a unitary Q, and even the vocabulary of "redaction" do not. France prefers fluid cross-fertilisation of oral and written traditions.

**The discourses are anthologies, not transcripts.** Matthew gathered scattered traditions into thematic collections; the argument rests on those traditions appearing dispersed across varied contexts in Mark and Luke.

A limit on citation deserves flagging: France explicitly refuses to write a standard introduction, stating his conclusions on authorship, provenance, date, and sources "baldly without argument" and referring readers to his own *Matthew: Evangelist and Teacher* (1989). **This volume is not a source for France's reasoning on those questions.**

### Defects corrected on [[matthew]]

The 2026-06-05 ingest left three ungrounded claims on the Gospel of Matthew page, now repaired:

1. **A claim contradicting France.** The page stated the six antitheses "are not contradictions of Torah but intensifications." France's actual position is "bypass, not abolish," with a three-mode taxonomy in which only the first two antitheses intensify; the third and fourth declare the regulated action should never have occurred, and the fifth declares a judicial rule inapplicable to personal ethics.
2. **A fabricated citation.** A specific position on Papias was attributed to France at "NICNT, 2007, pp. 14–22." Papias is not named anywhere in France's Introduction, which does not treat the *logia* question.
3. **Unsupported argument detail.** France's pre-70 dating was presented with three supporting temple-passage arguments. France states this conclusion without argument and refers the case out to his 1989 monograph.
