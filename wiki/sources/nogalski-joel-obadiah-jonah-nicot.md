## What the source is

The New International Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT) volume on Joel, Obadiah,
and Jonah by **James D. Nogalski** (Baylor University), Eerdmans, 2023. NICOT is a
critical–evangelical series; Nogalski provides his own translation, extensive Hebrew
text-critical and philological notes, form- and redaction-critical analysis, and a
sustained reading of each book within the **Book of the Twelve**. The volume opens with a
general **Introduction to the Book of the Twelve** shared across Nogalski's contributions
to the series.

## Hermeneutical frame

The commentary is written in a critical–evangelical register from an author who is a leading **redaction critic of the Book of the Twelve**. Its distinctive method is close **inner-biblical citation and allusion analysis**: Nogalski reads each book both discretely and as an editorially shaped member of the Twelve, and his governing category for the three postexilic books is **scribal prophecy** (temple-associated scribes combining and adapting prior scripture). His redaction-critical and dating theses are argued as positions against named interlocutors and are recorded on the wiki in attributed voice, not as bare fact. Three body-text emphases distinguish this work: (1) the **vetitive** grammar of Obadiah 11–14 and its resolution through canonical placement; (2) the **satire/comedy** genre of Jonah, with the disjunctures read as deliberate humor rather than editorial seams; and (3) Joel as the **"literary anchor"** of the Twelve. Nogalski provides his own translation and dense Hebrew text-critical notes throughout, and repeatedly separates the biblical text's claims from his own reconstruction, cautioning against over-literal theological application.

## Volume synthesis

Nogalski's NICOT volume treats three short books that share little on the surface — a locust-and-drought liturgy, a 21-verse anti-Edom oracle, and a comic prophet-tale — and welds them into a single argument about how the postexilic scribes who shaped the **Book of the Twelve** did their work. The unifying claim is that all three are **scribal prophecy**: not transcripts of oral preaching but literary compositions built by quotation, allusion, and canonical positioning. Joel is his showpiece — the "literary anchor" whose every theme (fertility/infertility, the fate of Judah, the day of Yahweh, theodicy) and whose lexical hooks to Hosea (before) and Amos (after) mark it as a programmatic text designed for its slot in the scroll. Obadiah demonstrates the method in miniature: Nogalski shows it reworking Jeremiah 49 while simultaneously mirroring the particles and structure of Amos 9, so that Edom's fate is set in parallel with the Northern Kingdom's. Jonah closes the volume by turning the same intertextual machinery into comedy — its prophet flees precisely *because* he knows Joel's citation of Exodus 34:6, and the book satirizes the theology of exclusion by making pagan sailors and Assyrians the models of repentance.

What the volume adds to the wiki is a rigorous, position-aware account of **how these books were made and where they sit**, upgrading pages that previously stated Nogalski's theses partly in bare wiki voice. It sharpens three arguments in particular: the day of Yahweh as historical intervention (not end-of-time judgment) refracted into distinct scenarios; the **vetitive** reading of Obadiah 11–14, which reframes apparent past indictments as future-oriented warnings resolved by canonical placement; and the **satire** genre of Jonah, which reclaims the book's "disjunctures" as authorial humor rather than redactional damage.

Because Nogalski is himself the redaction-critical school under discussion, his readings sit in productive tension with more author-and-setting-oriented commentators already in the collection (e.g. the Dearman Hosea and Carroll Amos volumes ingested alongside this one): where they foreground an individual prophet's historical ministry, Nogalski foregrounds the scribal compiler and the shaped scroll. The wiki records both as positions. Companion to this volume is Nogalski's [[james-nogalski|Micah]] (NICOT, 2024), already noted on his commentator page.
