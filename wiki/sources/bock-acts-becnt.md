## What the source is

Darrell L. Bock's *Acts* is a volume in the **Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New
Testament** (BECNT), edited by Robert W. Yarbrough and Robert H. Stein (Baker Academic,
2007; ebook 2012). Bock (b. 1953; PhD Aberdeen; Research Professor of NT at Dallas
Theological Seminary) also authored the BECNT *Luke* (2 vols.), and reads Acts as the
second volume of a unified [[luke-acts-unity|Luke-Acts]]. The BECNT format supplies, for
each pericope: an author's translation, an "Additional Notes" section on text-critical and
grammatical questions, a passage-level exegesis and exposition, and a "Summary" drawing
theological threads. This is the largest single-volume Acts commentary in English.

Register discipline for this ingest: the **body text** is Bock's own exegesis and
exposition; the **Additional Notes** carry his text-critical judgments; the **Introduction**
states his positions on genre, authorship, date, historicity, and theology. All
interpretive, theological, and historical-reconstruction claims are attributed to Bock (or
to the scholars he cites), never promoted to bare wiki voice on the strength of this single
source.

## Hermeneutical frame

Bock reads Acts as a **historical monograph** in the ancient sense — theology written by a
careful ancient historian "comparable to Thucydides," with God (not Peter or Paul) as the
central actor. His method is grammatical-historical exegesis attentive to Luke-Acts unity,
narrative characterization, and OT fulfillment, conducted from a self-described "informed
evangelical" stance that defends historicity while engaging moderate-critical scholarship.
The apparatus (Additional Notes) applies reasoned eclectic text-criticism.

## Volume / work synthesis

Bock's *Acts* is the largest single-volume English commentary on the book, and its argument is
unusually unified: Acts is a **theocentric historical monograph**, written by a "careful,
ancient historian" whose central character is not Peter or Paul but God, unfolding a
foreordained plan (*boulē*) through the exalted Jesus and the Spirit. This governs every
level of the reading. Genre (a Jewish-modelled monograph, not a Greco-Roman "acts" of a hero),
authorship (Luke, on external evidence Bock calls "statistically improbable if invented"),
date (his preferred "towards AD 70," before Jerusalem's fall), and the historicity of the
speeches (faithful summaries, "Thucydidean but with considerable restraint") all serve the
same thesis: theology and history are not competing categories, and Luke does both well.

For this wiki, the volume's largest contribution is a dense, verse-anchored, evangelical
counter-weight to the critical (Dibelius–Haenchen–Conzelmann) reading that already sits in the
collection. It supplies a full treatment of the OT-in-Acts hermeneutic (typological-prophetic
reading, *gezerah shewa* catchword-linking, the "this-is-that" pesher form), a high but
non-adoptionist Lukan Christology built from Peter's Pentecost catena (Joel 2, Ps 16, Ps 110),
and a Spirit-centered ecclesiology with "no set pattern" of Spirit-bestowal (the Samaria
delay, Cornelius before baptism, the Ephesian disciples). It fills out the wiki's coverage of
the Gentile-inclusion arc — the Cornelius hinge, the Jerusalem Council decree, the God-fearer
bridge category — and of the network of figures, cities, and speeches that carry the mission
from Jerusalem to Rome.

Its tensions with already-ingested material are mostly with the **critical NT scholarship**
the wiki records as positions rather than with other confessional sources. Bock's dating (late
60s), his defense of the speeches' substantial authenticity, and his minority **Acts 11 = Gal 2**
chronology all stake out evangelical-maximalist ground against Haenchen and, more moderately,
Barrett and Fitzmyer; these are filed on [[acts-paul-tensions|Acts and Paul's Letters]] and the
relevant text/figure pages as attributed positions, not adjudicated. A register caution
surfaced by the endnotes: Bock routes most **early-church reception** claims through Pelikan's
Brazos *Acts* (2005), so those belong to Pelikan, not to Acts itself, and are attributed
accordingly. Two of Bock's own emphases are worth flagging as single-source claims held in
attributed voice pending corroboration: the "vindicating judge" reading of the standing Son of
Man (7:56) and the deliberately **open ending** of Acts as authorial design.
