# Jobes, *1 Peter* (BECNT, 2005)

## What the source is

Karen H. Jobes's *1 Peter* is a volume in the **Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New
Testament** (BECNT), edited by Robert W. Yarbrough and Robert H. Stein (Baker Academic,
2005). At publication Jobes (Ph.D., Westminster Theological Seminary, Philadelphia) was
associate professor of New Testament at Westmont College; she later became Gerald F. Hawthorne
Professor of New Testament Greek and Exegesis at Wheaton College Graduate School. She is a
Septuagint specialist (co-author with Moisés Silva of *Invitation to the Septuagint*) and also
wrote commentaries on Esther (NIVAC) and 1–3 John.

The BECNT format supplies, for each pericope: an author's translation, "Exegesis and
Exposition," a "Summary," and "Additional Notes" on text-critical and grammatical questions.
Jobes states three self-described distinctive contributions: (1) the **Roman colonization
theory** for the recipients; (2) a sustained **LXX-as-matrix** reading; (3) **syntax criticism /
bilingual interference** undercutting the "Greek too good for a fisherman" objection.

Register discipline: the **body** is Jobes's own exegesis and exposition; **Additional Notes**
carry text-critical judgments; the **Introduction** states positions on authorship, date,
recipients, genre, and theology; the **Excursus** ("The Syntax of 1 Peter") is a quantitative
syntax study. All interpretive, theological, and historical-reconstruction claims are
attributed to Jobes (or to scholars she cites), never promoted to bare wiki voice on this
source alone.

## Hermeneutical frame

Jobes reads 1 Peter as **personal correspondence** in the form of a **Diaspora letter**, with the
**Septuagint as primary scriptural matrix**. Method is grammatical-historical exegesis attentive
to LXX context (not proof-texting), sociohistorical reconstruction (colonization theory;
Elliott/Balch/Volf engagement), and syntax criticism (bilingual interference). Stance is
evangelical-critical: she engages Achtemeier, Elliott, Goppelt, Michaels and defends traditional
authorship where she finds the critical case inconclusive.

## Volume / work synthesis

Jobes's BECNT *1 Peter* is a full-scale evangelical grammatical-historical commentary whose
distinctive load is **historical and linguistic rather than merely homiletical**. Across the
Introduction and Units I–V she argues that the letter is unified personal correspondence in
Diaspora-letter form, addressed to Christians in the five northern Asia Minor regions of 1:1 who
suffer unofficial, local social hostility rather than imperial judicial persecution. The
controlling self-understanding she attributes to the letter is that of **visiting foreigners and
resident aliens** (*parepidēmoi* / *paroikoi*), grounded both in a possible historical
displacement and in the spiritual truth that allegiance to the risen Christ makes believers
outsiders wherever social values clash with the gospel.

Three programmatic contributions structure the whole. First, the **Roman colonization theory**:
Jobes proposes that the recipients were converted elsewhere — probably Rome — and relocated to
Asia Minor under Claudian colonization (colonies in all five named regions) and/or the Claudian
expulsion of Jews/Christians (*Chrestus*), which would explain rapid Christian presence without a
named founding evangelist, deep LXX literacy, Peter's association with them, and the literal
force of the foreigner vocabulary. Second, the **LXX-as-matrix** method: Peter does not
proof-text but imports the *contexts* of Greek OT passages (Isa 53 ordered by the passion at
2:21–25; Ps 33 LXX as a running *paroikia* matrix; the stone complex at 2:4–10; Lev 19:2
holiness; Isa 40 seed/word). Third, **syntax criticism**: quantitative comparison (Martin
criteria; Jobes S-number **0.28** with LXX quotes) shows bilingual interference consistent with
a Semitic-speaking L2-Greek author — undercutting the “Greek too good for Peter” premise —
while Jobes explicitly **does not** claim that syntax alone proves Petrine authorship, names an
amanuensis, or identifies the author with that of 1 Thessalonians.

On the major cruxes, Jobes follows the **Dalton–Selwyn** line for 3:18–22 (ascension
proclamation of victory to the disobedient spirits of Noah's day / Watchers tradition; baptism
as *eperōtēma* “pledge” through the resurrection) and **severs 4:6** from 3:19 (no postmortem
second chance; the dead = those who heard while alive). The household code is read through
**Volf's differentiated engagement** (neither Elliott's sectarian resistance nor Balch's
assimilation alone); slaves who suffer unjustly are paradigmatic for all Christians via the Isa
53 Servant; the wives passage, on Jobes's reading, addresses verbal abuse and loss of standing
and does not sanction physical abuse. Closing details prefer Silvanus as **courier** for *dia
Silouanou egrapsa*, “Babylon” as Rome functioning as Diaspora *inclusio* rather than post-70
apocalyptic code, and Mark as John Mark.
