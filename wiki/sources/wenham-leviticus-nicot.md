# The Book of Leviticus (NICOT) — Gordon J. Wenham

## What the source is

Gordon J. Wenham's *The Book of Leviticus* (c. 1979) in the **New International Commentary on the Old Testament** (NICOT; Eerdmans; originally Routledge & Kegan Paul). Wenham (then Queen’s University Belfast; later Trinity College Bristol and University of Gloucestershire) writes a classic-Protestant, final-form commentary that pairs plain-sense historical exegesis with chapter-level NT theological application. Distinctive among evangelical NICOT volumes for sustained engagement with **Mary Douglas** (*Purity and Danger*) and social anthropology on purity and holiness.

## Hermeneutical frame

Wenham names his method the **"classic Protestant approach"** (Calvin; R. North on Lev 25): plain original meaning + abiding theological value for the church. Deliberately de-emphasizes source-critical reconstruction in the commentary body (restricted to Introduction). Tools: ANE comparative ritual; social anthropology (Douglas); new literary criticism (structure, key words, repetition, triadic patterning). Each major unit closes with **Leviticus N and the NT**.

**Load-bearing moves (verified in digests):**
- Holiness as **wholeness/completeness** (Douglas), not mere separateness
- Graded sacrificial models (burnt = general ransom/propitiation; purification = sanctuary cleansing; reparation = debt/trespass; peace = communion; cereal = tribute)
- *Kipper* as cleanse *and/or* ransom (*koper*)
- Food laws: Douglas accepted; hygiene-as-primary rejected
- Azazel: etymology open; ceremony = **sin exterminated from Israel**
- Ch. 17 as **hinge**, not simply H’s first chapter
- Ch. 27 as association-of-ideas **and** book-level **recapitulation** of holiness
- Nadab/Abihu: decisive “not commanded,” not a fully solved ritual mechanics puzzle

## Volume / work synthesis

Wenham’s NICOT *Leviticus* remains the wiki’s load-bearing **evangelical final-form** treatment of the book: classic Protestant plain sense + NT application, unusually open to social anthropology. The controlling theological claim is that holiness is **wholeness**—categorical integrity that unifies sacrifice, purity, priestly blemish rules, sexual ethics, and Jubilee economics under “Be holy, for I am holy.”

The sacrificial system is not one generic sin-rite but a **graded grammar** of relationship: burnt offering ransoms and propitiates; purification cleanses the sanctuary so God may dwell; reparation settles debt against holy things or the divine name (with Isa 53 as early *ʾāšām* reception); peace offerings celebrate *shalom*; cereal offerings pay covenant tribute. Blood is life given so that life may be ransomed (17:11). The Day of Atonement climaxes the purity half; whatever Azazel means philologically, the public goat declares sin gone from Israel.

Literarily, Wenham insists Leviticus is **history with law inside it**: chs. 8–10 complete the tabernacle story; chs. 11–15 prepare ch. 16 as 1–7 prepare 8–10; ch. 17 hinges cult and ethics; ch. 26 closes like other ANE/biblical law corpora; ch. 27 recapitulates the whole under total consecration. On formation he surveys traditional, Wellhausen, and Kaufmann streams and finds postexilic P hard to maintain—without making Mosaic authorship a dogmatic requirement.
