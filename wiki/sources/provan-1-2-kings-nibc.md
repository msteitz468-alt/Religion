# Provan, *1 and 2 Kings* (NIBC, 1995)

## What the source is

Iain W. Provan’s volume in the **New International Biblical Commentary** (OT series; general editors W. Ward Gasque, Robert L. Hubbard Jr., Robert K. Johnston). Evangelical “believing criticism”: literary-canonical reading of Kings as **one book** (not two), with historiographical intent and didactic purpose. NIV base text; section-by-section commentary (§1–§57) plus four **canonical-context excursuses** (Solomon §18; Elijah §34; Elisha §46; Hezekiah and Josiah §57). ## Hermeneutical frame

Attributed to Provan (Introduction):

1. **One book as narrative literature** — regnal formulae, plot, characters; final-form coherent story; resists source/redaction atomization as primary reading strategy.
2. **Historiographical literature** — about Israel’s past; selective and theologically shaped; portrait vs subject; referentiality cannot be dodged.
3. **Didactic literature** — teaches about God and God’s ways; exclusive YHWH; worship/ethics; sin and judgment; grace and promise (Davidic + Abrahamic tension).
4. **Canonical / typological Christian reading** — OT on its own terms *and* total Christian canon; four dedicated excursuses.
5. **Believing criticism** — NIBC series posture (Hubbard/Johnston foreword).

## Volume / work synthesis

Provan’s NIBC Kings is a sustained argument that **1–2 Kings is one book** best read as a carefully crafted narrative that is also about the past and also meant to teach. The Introduction’s three natures (narrative / historiographical / didactic) govern every section: regnal formulae and plot artistry; selective portraiture that still claims real-world referentiality; and a theology in which exclusive loyalty to the LORD, worship inseparable from ethics, delayed judgment, and the paradox of the Davidic promise structure the whole arc from Solomon’s succession to Jehoiachin’s table.

The Solomonic block (§1–18) sets the didactic pattern: law and wisdom as David’s charge; temple prayer as the theological heart (Name without containment; grace underpins law; exile/restoration petition); glory under a cloud (Deut 17 abrogations); deferred, non-final division. The schism and Omride rise (§19–25) install “the sin of Jeroboam” as northern identity and prophecy-fulfillment as history’s engine. The Elijah–Elisha mega-block (§26–46) is Provan’s longest demonstration of **salvation within judgment**—public Carmel, Horeb whisper, Naboth justice, Micaiah’s true word, humble and foreign channels (Naaman), incomplete Jehu, Joash’s survival of Athaliah, bones that still speak. The endgame (§47–57) makes 2 Kgs 17 the Decalogue-rooted indictment, Hezekiah the second David saved by grace, Manasseh the point of no return, Josiah the second Moses who arrives too late, and Jehoiachin the slender thread by which “grace may triumph over law.”

Four canonical excursuses refuse both OT-only isolation and NT-only raiding: Solomon, Elijah, Elisha, and Hezekiah/Josiah are read as types and warnings inside a unified Christian Bible without dissolving their Kings-plot roles. Against classical DtrH atomization Provan does not deny growth history so much as refuse to let reconstructed layers replace the final story; against pure literary insulation he refuses to abandon historiographical intent.

**Tensions recorded (not adjudicated):** vs Noth-style composite conflicting voices; vs minimalist biblical-vs-historical-Israel programs; vs historicist commentaries that supply coherence from outside the narrative; vs “sincere Yahwist calves” readings of Jeroboam; vs purely cynical readings of Jehoiachin’s epilogue. Parallel accounts in Chronicles (Leithart) and Isaiah 36–39 (Oswalt) remain complementary portraits already on the wiki.
