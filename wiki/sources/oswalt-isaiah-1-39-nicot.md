# Oswalt, The Book of Isaiah, Chapters 1–39 (NICOT, 1986)

## What the source is

Volume 1 of John N. Oswalt's two-volume commentary on Isaiah in the New International
Commentary on the Old Testament (Eerdmans, 1986). Evangelical (Wesleyan-Arminian; Asbury
Theological Seminary). Verse-by-verse commentary on his own translation, with a substantial
Introduction (title, background 739–400 BC, unity of composition, date and authorship,
occasion, canonicity, Hebrew text, theology, problems in interpretation, analysis of
contents) and two excursuses (I: the historicity and relation of chs. 36–37 to 2 Kings 18–19;
II: extract from Sargon's royal annals).

## Hermeneutical frame

Confessional-evangelical (Wesleyan-Arminian) grammatical-historical commentary with a
strong final-form commitment. Governing rules, in Oswalt's own words: "the theological and
ideological unity of the book is a primary datum"; "Our mandate is to interpret the text as
it is before us unless there is manuscript evidence to correct that text"; "It is the
present text which holds authority over us." Predictive prophecy is treated as load-bearing
("at least in Isaiah theology and prediction are inextricably intertwined"). ANE
comparanda (Ugaritic Lotan/Leviathan, Yam, *ǵlmt*, the cloud-rider) are used under the rule
that the prophet appropriates "well-known emotive language" without adopting the
mythological worldview. Christological application is explicit and frequent, usually marked
as NT fulfillment rather than original sense.

Evidence classes kept separate: (a) Oswalt's own translation with text-critical notes —
the notes were displaced into the endnote block by conversion and swept in R6; (b) his
exposition; (c) the Introduction's engagement with critical scholarship (Duhm, Radday,
Childs, Hanson, Achtemeier, North, Rowley, Clements, Kaiser, Wildberger, et al.), recorded
as positions on [[isaiah-authorship|the authorship controversy page]].

## Volume synthesis

Oswalt's first volume is best read as a single sustained argument that the question of
Isaiah 1–39 is trust. Chapters 1–5 pose the book-length problem — how sinful Israel can
become what she is called to be — and ch. 6, deliberately placed "six chapters into the book
it inaugurates," answers it in miniature: vision of the Holy One, cleansing, commission.
Chapters 7–39 then run the question through history as a "programmed learning experience":
Ahaz's failed pre-test (7–12), the didactic panorama of the nations (13–35), and Hezekiah's
passed post-test (36–39). The oracles against the nations are read, with Erlandsson, as
addressed to Israel ("don't trust the nations!"), with Babylon and Tyre as symbolic
brackets; chs. 24–27 are the "finale" that generalizes them — eschatological, Oswalt
insists, not apocalyptic; and 34–35 close the panorama with the desert/garden alternative.

The volume's theological center is the [[holy-one-of-israel|Holy One of Israel]]: holiness
as inseparably ontological otherness and moral perfection, with sin as prideful rebellion
and idolatry as its stupid consequence. Its most distinctive exegetical contributions are
the dual-focus reading of 7:14 (ʿalmâ "admirably suited" to a sign rooted in Ahaz's time
yet reaching to the Messiah), the anti-Satan reading of 14:12–15 with the Reformers, the
"whole complex" resolution of the 28:16 cornerstone, and the insistence that 25:8 and
26:19 constitute "the highest conception of resurrection in the OT."

What the volume adds to the wiki beyond the 2026-06-07 bulk pass is chiefly (a) the full
argumentative texture of the unity/authorship case, now recorded as positions on
[[isaiah-authorship]]; (b) chapter-level exegesis of 1–39 with verbatim grounding; (c) the
figure network (Ahaz–Hezekiah–Sennacherib–Isaiah) around the 701 crisis, including Excursus
I's historiographical argument; and (d) text-critical data (1QIsaᵃ adjudications) for the
Textual and Manuscript Tradition section of [[isaiah]]. Tensions with already-ingested
sources: the Hartley direction-of-influence question (Job → Servant) remains open; Oswalt's
1986 rejection of the "Isaiah Apocalypse" genre label sits in recorded tension with the
wiki's broader apocalyptic-literature account; and his defense of the historicity of 37:36
is a position, not wiki voice. This page does not back-project the 40–66 volume, whose own
fidelity re-ingest remains outstanding.
