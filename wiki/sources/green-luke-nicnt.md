# Green, *The Gospel of Luke* (NICNT, Eerdmans 1997)

## What the source is

Joel B. Green, *The Gospel of Luke*, New International Commentary on the New Testament (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1997). Full-scale evangelical commentary on the Third Gospel via NRSV, with sustained attention to how Luke’s narrative “works” as Greco-Roman historiography continuous with Acts. Green (Ph.D. Aberdeen 1985 under I. Howard Marshall; Methodist; then New College Berkeley / ABSW; from Fall 1997 Professor of New Testament Interpretation, Asbury Theological Seminary) replaces Norval Geldenhuys’s 1951 NICNT Luke (series editor Gordon D. Fee’s preface).

1. **NRSV base text** (with Green’s occasional editorial departures noted).

## Hermeneutical frame

Attributed to Green (Author’s Preface + Introduction; digests R0–R1):

1. **Discourse analysis:** correlating narratology (order, co-text, characterization, theme) with culture-critical / social-scientific analysis (honor-shame, patronage, purity, kinship). Named project: “Toward a Unified Hermeneutic: The Application of Discourse Theory and Sociological Analysis to the Gospel of Luke” (CBA 1991–92).
2. **Not form/redaction/source criticism:** Fee’s preface and Green’s method section deliberately leave atomistic Synoptic comparison to other commentaries; read Luke as first readers would — without constant cross-reference to Matthew or Mark.
3. **Genre:** Luke-Acts as Greco-Roman **historiography** (Cadbury consensus on preface; Aune “general history”) undercut as pure *bios* by theocentrism and the two-volume whole; Alexander’s “scientific” preface affinities noted and qualified.
4. **Luke-Acts unity:** two volumes, one story of God’s purpose to bring full salvation to all; Gospel incomplete without Acts (e.g. Gentile light in 2:32 realized in Acts).
5. **Purpose:** primarily **ecclesiological** — strengthen the Christian movement under opposition by ensuring interpretation/experience of God’s redemptive purpose and calling to continued faithfulness and witness.
6. **Theocentric plot:** God as primary actor; δεῖ / “purpose” / Spirit as agent; Jesus as prophet-Messiah-Son-Savior whose identity disciples struggle to correlate with suffering.
7. **Salvation holistic and present:** no dichotomy of social vs spiritual or individual vs communal; release (ἄφεσις), Jubilee, table fellowship, possessions ethics, women as disciples.
8. **NRSV base;** text-criticism selective. **Authorship** identity treated as interpretively secondary (narrator, not physician-exegesis).

Series frame (Fee NICNT preface): thorough exposition “loyal to Scripture as the infallible Word of God”; pastoral recovery; Green as second Methodist in series (with Marshall’s Johannine Epistles).

## Volume synthesis

Green’s NICNT *Luke* remains the wiki’s primary full-scale commentary on the Third Gospel. The 2026-07-18 fidelity re-ingest confirms — with verbatim loci across ~333k body words — the load-bearing profile already sketched in the 2026-06-05 first pass, and supplies the coverage ledger and body density that pass lacked.

**Methodologically**, the commentary is a post-Conzelmann project: after the redaction-critical decades associated with Conzelmann and Fitzmyer, Green joins Tannehill and Johnson on the literary side and Moxnes and Esler on the social-scientific, under the umbrella term *discourse analysis*. Order (καθεξῆς) is a primary control on meaning; isolated pericopes and lectionary atomism will not do. Historiography identification sets expectations of causation, teleology, and persuasive instruction, while the commentary prioritizes *signification* of events over verification of each episode for modern historicist readers. “For him, the narrative is not the basis of proclamation; rather, narration is proclamation.”

**On Luke-Acts**, Green’s most fundamental commitment is that the two books are one story: roughly equal papyrus-roll lengths; Acts 1:1 “began to do and teach”; parallel Jesus/apostles architecture; a single narrative cycle of God’s purpose of full salvation for all people. The Gospel anticipates what only Acts realizes (Gentile mission under the Spirit); reading Luke alone is like reading half a sentence. Canonical placement (Luke among the Four, Acts as bridge to Paul) obscures this unity for modern readers.

**On the Gospel’s program**, the Nazareth sermon (4:16–30) remains the interpretive key: good news to the poor (low-status honor, not only economic destitution); double ἄφεσις (forgiveness, liberation from bondage, Jubilee economics); “year of the Lord’s favor” as eschatological Jubilee; “today” as inaugurated salvation; Elijah/Elisha outsiders as warrant for grace beyond Israel’s insiders. Birth songs (especially the Magnificat) announce the same reversal program; the Travel Narrative (9:51–19:48) is Luke’s distinctive literary-theological construction for discipleship resocialization (hearing-and-doing, prayer, possessions, inclusion of the lost), not a plausible itinerary. Jerusalem is culture-center and destiny: temple reclaim for revelatory teaching, conflict with leadership, weeping over visitation unrecognized, passion under divine necessity (δεῖ), and exaltation that hinges into Acts (witnesses, Spirit promise, temple joy).

**On contested historical questions**, Green does not stake the commentary on traditional Lukan authorship or a precise date: identity is “unimportant to its interpretation,” and dating is secondary to narrative reading. Where he engages historicity (e.g. Jesus as healer), he argues that for Luke and contemporaries the issue was *source and signification* of extraordinary works, not bare occurrence. These are Green’s methodological choices, not wiki-voice adjudications against redaction-critical or traditional-author readings (contrast Bock on Acts).
