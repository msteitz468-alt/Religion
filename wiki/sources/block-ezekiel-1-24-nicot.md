# Block, *The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24* (NICOT, Eerdmans 1997)

## What the source is

Volume 1 of Daniel I. Block's two-volume commentary on Ezekiel in the New International
Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT) series. Evangelical critical commentary: fresh
translation, verse-by-verse philological and rhetorical commentary, and "Theological
Implications" sections per unit. The Introduction covers the entire book (both volumes).
This is a **fidelity re-ingest**: the work was first ingested 2026-06-05 under
pre–Scope-and-Fidelity standards with no source page.

## Hermeneutical frame

Evangelical grammatical-historical exegesis with three distinctives: (1) **rhetorical-
theological method** — genre identification (rîb, disputation speech, riddle/māšāl, parody
qinah, proof saying) subordinated to rhetorical analysis; Ezekiel read as "a preacher, not a
chronicler or a systematic theologian"; apparent contradictions resolved rhetorically.
(2) **Comparative ANE philology** — Akkadian, Ugaritic, Aramaic, Egyptian, Hittite parallels
(pīt pî, Ludlul, Erra Epic, Mari, adoption/treaty formulae), always with the caveat that
borrowed forms are "radically transformed" and demythologized. (3) **Holistic conservatism**
— final-form reading against redaction-critical dissection; MT-based, lectio-difficilior text
criticism; textual roughness attributed to the prophet's emotional state. Register separation:
exegesis proper is historical; each unit closes with an openly confessional "Theological
Implications" section (NT and church application) — the two registers are kept distinct in
wiki attribution. A rare full reception excursus appears at the 20:25 crux and the ch. 9 taw.

## Volume synthesis

Block's Vol. 1 is a sustained argument that Ezekiel's judgment preaching (chs. 1–24) is
neither the raving of a disturbed personality nor a redactional patchwork, but the coherent
rhetorical program of a reluctant priest-prophet systematically demolishing the four pillars
of Jerusalem's official theology — eternal covenant, land, city, and Davidic house — so that
Yahweh's honor, not Israel's merit, becomes the only ground of hope. The Introduction carries
the load-bearing frame: the seven-phase model of prophetic-book genesis with substantial
Ezekielian authorship; the formula statistics and "resumptive exposition" pattern deployed
against stratification; and a via-media eschatology ("restoration oracles predict literal
events, not all his descriptions portray the events literally").

Across the commentary the most distinctive throughline is **radical theocentricity**: Yahweh
acts "for the sake of his name" (ch. 20's thrice-restrained wrath), judgment is covenant
"fine print" (Lev 26/Deut 28) rather than caprice, and the glory's departure — not 586 —
is "the turning point in Israel's history." Block's contrarian exegetical calls give the
volume its edge: the reluctant-prophet thesis; 21:32 as Nebuchadrezzar with Gen 49:10
inverted; the biblical Daniel in 14:14; the sour-grapes proverb as fatalism; 16:60 as
reinstitution, not new covenant; 24:1–14 as a "hitherto unrecognized disputation speech."
He engages the modern critique of chs. 16 and 23 in formal excurses rather than evading it.
