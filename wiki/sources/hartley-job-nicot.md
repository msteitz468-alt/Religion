# Hartley, The Book of Job (NICOT, 1988)

## What the source is

John E. Hartley's commentary on Job in the New International Commentary on the Old Testament
(Eerdmans, 1988). Evangelical Wesleyan (Azusa Pacific University); commissioned by series
editor R. K. Harrison. Verse-by-verse commentary on Hartley's own literal translation, with
a substantial Introduction (§§I–XII) and an "Aim" section closing each speech that states
its contribution to the book as a whole.

## Hermeneutical frame

Hartley states his own method in the preface: "the purpose of this work has been to concentrate on
interpreting the book's message rather than to write a linguistic commentary." Three consequences
shape what this source can and cannot support.

**Message over philology.** He works from his own literal translation and notes emendations, but
subordinates technical analysis to theological synthesis, drawing freely on Dhorme, Pope, Dahood,
Fohrer and Gordis without adopting their reconstructions. His apparatus for this is the **"Aim"**
section closing each speech, which states that speech's contribution to the whole. The Aims are the
most load-bearing prose in the volume and are the basis of most of what this wiki attributes to him.

**Textual conservatism.** He defaults to the MT — "scholarly study has continued to authenticate its
quality, and it offers an objective standard superior to any series of extensive emendations." In the
third cycle, the volume's hardest structural problem, he relocates exactly one block (27:13–23) and
creates nothing, explicitly declining the fuller reconstructions of Gordis and Hölscher/Duhm.

**Three evidence classes, kept separate.** The commentary body, the Introduction's survey of others'
positions, and Hartley's own confessional extensions are not interchangeable. The extensions are
identifiable and were flagged during integration: the NT Mediator analogy at 9:33; dating full
resurrection doctrine to after Christ's resurrection at 14:14; a Sermon on the Mount move at ch. 31.
The control case is 33:23 — he catalogues "the concealed Christ" among six proposed identities for
the mediator and does **not** adopt it, identifying the mediator instead with "the angel of Yahweh."
He is likewise restrained on the Satan, never identifying him with the later or NT Satan.

## Volume synthesis

Hartley's commentary is organized around a single controlling thesis, and nearly everything
distinctive in it follows from that thesis rather than from philological commitments. The thesis is
that the book of Job does **not** overturn the doctrine of retribution but corrects its misapplication:
"the book of Job does not categorically reject the doctrine of retribution, it endeavors ardently to
correct erroneous applications of that doctrine." What is corrected is the inference from suffering to
sin, not the link between fearing God and blessing. This is why he can defend the epilogue as integral
rather than embarrassing — his argument being a *reductio*, that if fearing Yahweh led inevitably to
hardship, "such a faith would be sadistic."

The thesis is held together by the prologue's *ḥinnām*. Hartley tracks the word across the Satan's
charge (1:9), Yahweh's rebuttal (2:3), and Job's own accusation (9:17), and makes the whole book turn
on it: the question is whether disinterested piety is possible. This yields his sharpest and least
obvious move — the comforters, by urging Job to repent in order to regain blessing, "unwittingly" take
the Satan's side, because they would have Job serve God for gain. The friends are not merely poor
pastors but the wager's second instrument. It also yields his reading of 42:6, where Job surrenders
not a sin but his justification, and thereby settles the wager: "Job demonstrates that he serves God
for himself alone… not even his own justification."

Against the wiki's existing coverage, Hartley sits as a conservative counterweight at several points.
Where the Zohar-and-Kabbalah cluster and the combat-myth material press mythological readings, Hartley
grants the mythic vocabulary of Behemoth and Leviathan while denying the combat — Yahweh "never had to
subdue it in mortal combat as the ruling gods Marduk or Baal" — a position now flagged as a live
tension on `the-combat-myth`. Where Christian reception since Jerome has read Job 19:25 as resurrection
and messianic prophecy, Hartley rejects that as "a reading back of the NT into the OT" and argues for
vindication before death at the ash heap, despite being an evangelical writing for an evangelical
series. That combination — confessionally committed but exegetically unwilling to let the New Testament
supply the Old Testament's meaning — is the volume's most useful characteristic for this wiki, and the
reason his explicit confessional extensions are worth having marked as such rather than filtered out.

Two things this source cannot settle. Its dating argument is internally inconsistent, since the
Isaiah-priority claim requires an 8th-century Job while the stated conclusion is 7th-century; both are
recorded. And its treatment of the Elihu interpolation debate is a survey of others' arguments ending
in a deliberate non-answer — "Whether they are the work of the author of Job or of a final redactor is
difficult to ascertain" — so the wiki records Hartley's case for Elihu's *integration* without
attributing to him a position on Elihu's *authorship*.
