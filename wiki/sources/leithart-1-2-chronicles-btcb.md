## What the source is

Peter J. Leithart's *1 & 2 Chronicles* (Grand Rapids: Brazos Press, 2019) in the
**Brazos Theological Commentary on the Bible** (R. R. Reno, general editor). It completes
Leithart's trilogy of Old Testament historical-books commentaries after *A Son to Me* (1–2
Samuel, Canon Press) and *1 & 2 Kings* (Brazos, 2006). The Brazos series is an explicitly
**postcritical, doctrinal** project: commentators are chosen as theologians formed in the
Nicene rule of faith rather than as philologists, on the premise that doctrine *clarifies*
rather than obscures the text ("not anti-critical but post-critical"). See
[[peter-leithart|Leithart's commentator page]] for the series' methodological stance.

## Hermeneutical frame

Leithart reads Chronicles as a **literary-theological, canonical, figural** commentary,
explicitly *not* historical-critical. His avowed method (Introduction): "discern the shape of
the book, to follow thematic threads… pay attention to literary structures and stylistic
features and attempt to tease out theological conclusions," with the aim "to make Chronicles
preachable." Four load-bearing moves recur at every scale:

1. **Whole-OT recapitulation / typology** — Chronicles "begins with the name Adam and ends
   with the decree of Cyrus," secretly retelling the entire OT beneath the surface history of
   kings: genealogies = Genesis, Saul's death = Egyptian bondage, David = Moses (cult-founder),
   Solomon = Joshua (sapiential conquest), divided kingdom = Judges cycles, Cyrus = a new
   (Gentile) Davidic commission.
2. **Chiastic / concentric structure analysis** — nearly every pericope is laid out as a
   chiasm (borrowing from Dorsey, Boda, Williamson, Merrill) to locate theological centers;
   e.g. the genealogy centers on the Levites and (at the center of the center) the singers;
   the personnel list (1 Chr 23–27) centers on music.
3. **New-creation reading** — temple construction as a new creation on the Genesis-1 week
   (*kalah/shalam* echoing Gen 2:1); David's reign mapped as a creation-week; fire from heaven
   at the dedication as a Pentecost.
4. **Ecclesiology as the avowed central interest** — Chronicles as "a manual for church
   reformers," a "vindication of bureaucracy," with the *qahal* (assembly), Levitical offices,
   and music read as figures of the church's structure and worship.

Chief scholarly interlocutor: **William Johnstone** ("whose fingerprints are everywhere here"),
with Japhet, Hahn, Boda, Pratt, Sparks, Williamson, McConville, and James B. Jordan recurrently
cited. These are Leithart's cited authorities, not this wiki's sources. The register throughout:
interpretive/typological claims are **Leithart's** (attributed); only brute textual facts
(verse loci, canonical order, presence/absence of an episode) are stated plainly.

## Volume / work synthesis

Leithart's *1 & 2 Chronicles* is the most methodologically distinctive OT commentary in the
wiki: a postcritical, Nicene, figural reading that treats the Chronicler not as a tendentious
post-exilic redactor but as a canonical theologian who retells the whole prior history of
Israel — from Adam to the decree of Cyrus — as a single typological arc. Its governing thesis is
that Chronicles is structured as a recapitulation (Genesis → Egypt → Moses/David →
Joshua/Solomon → Judges/divided-kingdom → a new "establishment of the monarchy") whose surprise
ending is that the awaited "new David" after the Saul-like Josiah is **Cyrus the Persian** — a
Gentile who inherits the Davidic task of house-building. On this reading the Davidic covenant
does not fail at the exile; it *transfers* into an "ecumenical imperial order" of Gentile
empires that provides the stage for the Messiah. The commentary's second signature contribution
is its theology of **music as priestly-royal-prophetic act** (Levitical song as the fulfilled
form of sacrifice, dominion, and prophecy), grounded in the structural centrality of the singers
in both the genealogy and the temple-personnel list.

Against the wiki's other Chronicles/Kings material, Leithart occupies a deliberately different
register. Where [[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] reads the Deuteronomistic History as one
didactic narrative arc and Isaiah-focused sources ([[oswalt-isaiah-1-39-nicot|Oswalt]]) read
Hezekiah/Ahaz through the theology of trust, Leithart reads the *same* kings through immediate
retribution (*seek → find / forsake → be abandoned*), the *maʿal* (sacrilege) thread, and
liturgical typology. He shares with Provan the "good kings in bad times" reading of Hezekiah and
Josiah, but frames the exile not primarily as covenant discipline toward Jehoiachin's release
but as the land finally receiving its withheld Sabbaths (Lev 26) before a Jubilee proclamation.
The commentary carries no text-critical apparatus and does not engage the Chronicles/Samuel-Kings
synoptic problem as a critical question — a self-imposed limit that is its weakness as a
reference work and the condition of its strength as a sustained figural-theological reading. Its
most speculative moves (the trinitarian "sons make fathers fathers"; the "Spirit"-not-"a spirit"
reading of the deceiving prophets in 2 Chr 18; the numerological/gematria observations) are
flagged as such and recorded here as Leithart's positions, not wiki voice.
