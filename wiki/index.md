### Apostolic Fathers (non-canonical early Christian texts)

*(Holmes, Michael W. [ed./trans.]. *The Apostolic Fathers: Greek Texts and English Translations*, 3rd ed. Baker Academic, 2007 — primary source for all pages below)*

- [[1-clement|First Clement (1 Clement)]] — anonymous; Roman church to Corinth; ca. 96 CE; succession argument; Peter and Paul martyrdom witness; nearly canonical (Codex Alexandrinus); Greek
- [[2-clement|Second Clement (2 Clement)]] — misnomer; oldest complete Christian sermon outside NT; ca. 140 CE; anonymous; Corinth; first NT text cited as *graphē* (2.4 cites Matt 9:13); functional Christological equation (1.1); non-canonical saying paralleled in Gospel of Thomas; Greek
- [[didache|The Didache (Teaching of the Twelve Apostles)]] — late 1st century; Jewish-Christian composite; oldest eucharistic prayers; baptism rubric; Lord's Prayer with doxology; transitional governance (itinerants → resident bishops); sole complete Greek text in Codex Hierosolymitanus; Greek
- [[epistle-of-barnabas|Epistle of Barnabas]] — anonymous; not by Barnabas of Acts; Alexandrian; ca. 70–135 CE; radical allegory; supersessionism (Israel's covenant "shattered"); Two Ways section; in Codex Sinaiticus; Greek
- [[shepherd-of-hermas|Shepherd of Hermas]] — Roman; freedman author; ca. 90–155 CE; composite; postbaptismal repentance; wealth and poverty; minimal Christology; more early copies than many canonical writings; in Codex Sinaiticus; Greek
- [[epistle-to-diognetus|Epistle to Diognetus]] — anonymous; ca. 150–225 CE; Greek apology; "third race" passage (sec. 5); soul-body analogy (sec. 6); sole manuscript destroyed 1870 (Strasbourg); includes Fragment of Quadratus; secs. 11–12 a separate document; Greek
- [[fragments-of-papias|Fragments of Papias]] — Papias of Hierapolis; ca. 100–125 CE; 28 fragments; "living voice" principle; Mark as Peter's interpreter (Frag. 3.15); Matthew's "Hebrew logia" (Frag. 3.16); literal millennialism; Two Johns problem; Greek (preserved in Eusebius and others)
- *Letters of Ignatius* — seven authentic letters (Middle Recension); ca. 107–117 CE; monepiscopate; anti-Docetism; martyrdom theology; documented in [[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius of Antioch]] figure page; Greek
- *Letter of Polycarp to the Philippians* — ca. 107–110 CE; Pauline letters as scripture (Eph cited as *graphē*); anti-Docetism; plural elder governance at Philippi; documented in [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]] figure page; Greek
- *Martyrdom of Polycarp* — ca. 155–160 CE; oldest Christian martyrdom narrative outside NT; typology of Polycarp as *typos Christou*; documented in [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]] figure page; Greek

## Figures

### Patristic and Hellenistic

- [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]] — Hellenistic Jewish philosopher-exegete; Middle Platonist; Logos as intermediary; major bridge to Christian theology (Stead, *Philosophy in Christian Antiquity* Ch. 6)
- [[origen|Origen]] — Alexandrian Christian commentator, theologian, allegorist; head of catechetical school; philosophical exegesis of John, Song of Songs, Torah; influence and controversies (Stead)

### Second-Century Christianity

- [[clement-of-rome|Clement of Rome]] (floruit ca. 90–100 CE) — probable drafter of 1 Clement; leading presbyter in the collegial Roman church; the "Clement" named in Shepherd of Hermas 8.3 as external correspondence officer; traditionally listed as third pope (anachronistic); no monarchical episcopate in Rome attested (Holmes)
- [[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius of Antioch]] (c.35–108 CE) — Third bishop of Antioch; seven letters during martyrdom journey; anti-Docetic Christology; key transitional figure for monepiscopate; first use of "Catholic Church" (Kruger; Holmes primary text)
- [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]] (c.69–155 CE) — Bishop of Smyrna; disciple of John the Apostle; companion of Papias; teacher of Irenaeus; Epistle to the Philippians (earliest explicit scriptural citation formula on a Pauline letter); Martyrdom of Polycarp (Kruger; Holmes primary text)
- [[papias|Papias of Hierapolis]] (floruit ca. 100–130 CE) — Bishop of Hierapolis; companion of Polycarp; hearer of John the Elder; *Expositions of the Sayings of the Lord* (5 books, lost except fragments); "living voice" principle; Mark-Peter and Matthew-logia traditions; literal millennialism; Two Johns problem (Holmes)
- [[irenaeus|Irenaeus of Lyons]] (c.130–202 CE) — Bishop of Lyons; *Against Heresies*; fourfold Gospel; apostolic succession; recapitulation theology; anti-gnostic polemicist; rule of faith formulator (Kruger)
- [[tertullian|Tertullian]] (c.155–220 CE) — Father of Latin theology; Trinitarian vocabulary (*trinitas*, *persona*, *substantia*); *Prescription against Heretics*; *Against Marcion* (5 vols.); later Montanist sympathies (Kruger)
- [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]] (c.150–215 CE) — Head of Alexandrian catechetical school; philosopher-theologian; allegorist; the true Christian *gnostikos*; preserved Valentinian sources; 23/27 NT books as functional canon (Kruger)
- [[marcion|Marcion of Sinope]] (c.85–160 CE) — Founded Marcionite church (c.144 CE); two gods (OT Demiurge / NT Father); Docetic Christology; first closed Christian canon (edited Luke + 10 Pauline letters); primary heretical foil for Irenaeus/Tertullian (Kruger)
- [[valentinus|Valentinus]] (c.100–175 CE) — Most influential gnostic teacher; Valentinian school; Pleroma cosmology; Sophia myth; three-class anthropology; *Gospel of Truth*; school included Ptolemy, Heracleon, Marcus, Theodotus (Kruger)

### Ancient Near Eastern Peoples & Empires (Hebrew Bible — Coogan ingest, 2026-06-07)

*(Peoples, tribes, and empires of the biblical world from the Bronze Age to the Persian period, from [[coogan-oxford-history-biblical-world|Coogan, ed., Oxford History of the Biblical World]].)*

- [[canaanites|Canaanites]] — indigenous Bronze Age population of the land; the cultural matrix from which early Israel emerged (not merely its conquered foe); religion (El, Baal, Asherah) known from Ugarit; inventors of the alphabet. The autochthonous "other" of the conquest narratives.
- [[amorites|Amorites]] — Northwest-Semitic people of the MB "Age of the Amorite kingdoms" (Mari, Babylon/Hammurapi, Yamhad); in the Bible, pre-Israelite people of the land and of Sihon's Transjordan kingdom.
- [[hurrians|Hurrians]] — population of N. Syria/Mesopotamia; the empire of Mitanni (a Late Bronze "great power"); the Nuzi tablets once tied to the patriarchal narratives (parallels now mostly discounted); biblical "Horites."
- [[hyksos|Hyksos]] — Canaanite-origin "rulers of foreign lands" (Egyptian Dynasty 15, c. 1648–1540 BCE); the only attested mass descent–sojourn–exodus of Asiatics in/out of Egypt; key analogue (Manetho/Josephus) for the biblical Exodus.
- [[apiru-habiru|Apiru / Habiru]] — déclassé social stratum (not an ethnic group) across the 2nd-millennium Near East; outlaws, mercenaries, dependents; the "peasants' revolt" model of Israel's origins; debated tie to "Hebrew" (ʿibrî).
- [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] — Aegean/Anatolian coalition (Peleset, Tjeker/Sikils, Sherden, Denyen, Weshesh) whose c. 1200 BCE migrations helped collapse the Late Bronze world; named Sicily/Sardinia/Palestine.
- [[philistines|Philistines]] — Aegean-derived Sea People of the southern-coast pentapolis (Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron, Gaza, Gath); the archetypal Iron Age enemy of Israel and catalyst for kingship; "Palestine" derives from "Philistia."
- [[israelites|Israelites (Emergence and Tribal Israel)]] — first attested on the Merneptah Stela (c. 1209 BCE) as a *people*; emerged as a rural subset of Canaanite culture; the four origin models (conquest / pastoral-nomad / peasants' revolt / ruralization); the "people of Yahweh."
- [[midianites|Midianites]] — Arabian caravaneers; in-laws of Moses (Jethro) and possible source of Yahwism (the Midianite/Kenite hypothesis); later archetypal raiders (Gideon); kin of Israel via Keturah.
- [[edomites|Edomites]] — "sons of Esau"; Transjordan/Negeb brother-enemy of Judah; betrayer at Jerusalem's fall (586; Obadiah, Lam. 4); later Idumea (Herod's homeland).
- [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]] — the seafaring coastal Canaanites (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos); transmitters of the alphabet; Israel's trading partners (Hiram) and a conduit of Baal/Asherah worship (Jezebel); dominant people of the Persian Levant.
- [[assyrians|Assyrians]] — the empire that destroyed Samaria (720) and the "ten lost tribes," besieged Jerusalem (701), and pioneered mass deportation; prophetic "rod of God's anger" and symbol of cruel pride.
- [[babylonians|Babylonians (Neo-Babylonians / Chaldeans)]] — Nebuchadrezzar's empire; destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple (586) and authored the Exile; preserved exiles' ethnic identity (unlike Assyria); apocalyptic "Babylon."
- [[persians|Persians (Achaemenid Empire)]] — ended the Exile and authorized the Second Temple; overlord of Yehud; Cyrus uniquely called "messiah" (Isa. 45.1); "tolerance" as imperial self-interest; matrix of Second Temple Judaism.
- [[samaritans|Samaritans (Samarians)]] — Yahweh-worshiping population of the former northern kingdom; rivals of Jerusalem's restoration; "more derivation than deviation" from Second Temple Judaism (correcting 2 Kings 17); ancestors of the Samaritans.
- [[moabites|Moabites]] — Transjordanian "people of Chemosh" east of the Dead Sea; kin-enemy via Lot (with Ammon); Balak/Balaam, Eglon, Ruth; King Mesha's revolt and the Mesha Stela ("house of David").
- [[ammonites|Ammonites]] — "sons of Ammon" (Bit-Ammon) around Rabbah/Amman; Milcom worshipers; Lot-kin with Moab; Nahash, Jephthah, and David's siege of Rabbah; Tobiah the Ammonite.
- [[arameans|Arameans]] — the Syrian city-states (chiefly Aram-Damascus); patriarchal kin (Laban; "a wandering Aramean"); Israel's chief northern rival (Hazael); the Tel Dan stela; gave Aramaic to the Near East.
- [[kedarites|Kedarites]] — North Arabian Arab confederation (Kedar, son of Ishmael); incense caravaneers; Persian allies (Geshem the Arab); pushed Edom into Idumea; heirs of Midian, forerunners of the Nabateans.
- [[idumeans|Idumeans]] — post-exilic Edomites displaced west into Idumea (south of Judea); forcibly Judaized by John Hyrcanus (128 BCE); homeland of Herod the Great; "who counts as a Jew?"

### Second-Century Heterodox Groups

- [[gnostics|Gnostics]] — Loose family of 2nd-c. movements sharing spirit-matter dualism, demiurgic cosmology, and *gnosis* soteriology; major sub-groups: Valentinianism, Basilideanism, Sethianism, Cerinthus, Ophites; primary sources: Nag Hammadi (1945) + heresiologists; phenomenological/historical synthesis: Rudolph, *Gnosis* (nature as late-antique religion with Pleroma/Sophia/Demiurge myth, gnosis-soteriology, ascent, community/ethics; history through 2nd-c. great systems, Manichaeism, Mandaean relic); primary patristic refutation: Irenaeus *Against Heresies* (Kruger, Stark, Rudolph)
- [[ebionites|Ebionites]] — Jewish-Christian movement (c.100–400 CE); law-observance required; reject Paul; adoptionist Christology (Spirit descended on Jesus at baptism); *ebyōnîm* ("the poor"); concentrated in Transjordan; opposed by Irenaeus, Origen, Tertullian (Kruger)
- [[marcionites|Marcionites]] — Marcion’s rival church (c.144 CE–5th/6th c.); spread from Euphrates to Britain; ascetic practice; two gods; Docetic Christ; first closed canon (edited Luke + 10 Pauline letters); refuted most extensively by Tertullian (Kruger)
- [[montanists|Montanists]] — "New Prophecy" movement (c.165–present in Phrygia); Montanus + Priscilla + Maximilla; ecstatic utterance; claim of ongoing Spirit revelation; excommunicated by Asian synods; Tertullian’s later affiliation; Muratorian Fragment explicitly condemns (Kruger)

### Ancient Near Eastern / Hebrew Bible Sites (Coogan ingest, 2026-06-07)

*(Cities and sites of the biblical world, with historical inhabitants by period + modern identification, from [[coogan-oxford-history-biblical-world|Coogan, ed., Oxford History of the Biblical World]].)*

- [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] — Jebusite city-state (Amarna: Abdi-Hepa) → Davidic/Judahite capital and Temple city → tripled by Assyrian-era refugees (Hezekiah, Siloam Tunnel, Sennacherib's 701 siege) → destroyed by Babylon 586 → tiny Persian-period Yehud capital (Second Temple). Zion; al-Quds. Modern: Jerusalem (City of David / Temple Mount–Haram al-Sharif).
- [[samaria|Samaria]] — Omri's northern-kingdom capital → Assyrian province of Samerina after 720 (the "ten lost tribes") → prosperous Persian-period Samarian (Yahwistic) center → Herod's Sebaste. Modern: Sebastia, West Bank.
- [[babylon|Babylon]] — Hammurapi's city → Neo-Babylonian imperial capital (Nebuchadrezzar) → place of the Judean Exile → fell to Cyrus 539. "Babel"; apocalyptic "Babylon the great." Modern: ruins near Hillah, Iraq.
- [[ugarit|Ugarit]] — Late Bronze N. Syrian port (Ras Shamra); destroyed c. 1185 by the Sea Peoples. Its tablets (Baal Cycle, Kirta, Aqhat) "revolutionized" study of Canaanite religion and the Bible (El/Yahweh; Hebrew poetry). Modern: Tell Ras Shamra, Syria.
- [[hazor|Hazor]] — largest Canaanite city (72 ha); "head of all those kingdoms"; the one major conquest site (with Bethel) that fits Joshua archaeologically; Solomonic gate; destroyed by Assyria 732. Modern: Tel Hazor, Galilee.
- [[megiddo|Megiddo]] — Jezreel-pass stronghold (20+ cities); Thutmose III's battle; Solomonic city; Assyrian Magiddu; death of Josiah (609). Gives its name to **Armageddon** (Rev. 16.16). Modern: Tel Megiddo.
- [[shechem|Shechem]] — central-highlands city (Amarna: Labayu); Joshua 24 covenant assembly; Abimelech (Judg. 9); first northern capital (1 Kings 12); Samaritan Mount Gerizim. Modern: Tell Balata, Nablus.
- [[byblos|Byblos]] — ancient Phoenician port (Gebal); Egypt's chief cedar port; Amarna's Rib-Addi (67 letters); Giblite Temple-builders; name underlies "Bible." Modern: Jbeil, Lebanon.
- [[ashkelon|Ashkelon]] — Canaanite seaport (Merneptah: still Canaanite, c. 1209) → chief Philistine pentapolis port → destroyed by Babylon 604. Modern: Tel Ashkelon, Israel.
- [[ekron|Ekron]] — small Canaanite town → large Philistine pentapolis city (Tel Miqne) → great Assyrian-era olive-oil center; "Baal-zebub of Ekron"; royal inscription; destroyed by Babylon 604/603. Modern: Tel Miqne, Israel.
- [[lachish|Lachish]] — chief Judahite city after Jerusalem; Sennacherib's 701 siege (the Lachish reliefs at Nineveh) and Babylon's destruction (586; the Lachish ostraca). Modern: Tell ed-Duweir, Shephelah.
- [[jericho|Jericho]] — one of the oldest towns on earth (Neolithic tower); Joshua's iconic first conquest, but **largely unoccupied in the 13th c.** — a showcase (with Ai) for the failure of the literal-conquest model. Modern: Tell es-Sultan, near Jericho/Ariha.
- [[nineveh|Nineveh]] — Neo-Assyrian imperial capital (Sennacherib's "palace without rival"; the Lachish reliefs; Ashurbanipal's library); subject of Jonah and Nahum; destroyed 612 BCE. Modern: Kuyunjik/Nebi Yunus, within Mosul, Iraq.
- [[ashdod|Ashdod]] — Philistine pentapolis city; the ark vs. Dagon (1 Sam. 5); Sargon II's conquest (711) and Isaiah's sign-act; "language of Ashdod" (Neh. 13); Roman Azotus. Modern: Tel Ashdod, Israel.
- [[gaza|Gaza]] — southernmost Philistine pentapolis city on the Egypt–Levant road; Samson's blinding and the fall of Dagon's temple (Judg. 16); Alexander's 332 siege. Modern: under modern Gaza City.
- [[mari|Mari]] — middle-Euphrates Bronze Age city (Tell Hariri); Zimri-Lim's palace and ~25,000-tablet archive (Amorite-era diplomacy and prophecy); destroyed by Hammurapi c. 1760. Not in the Bible. Modern: E. Syria.
- [[ebla|Ebla]] — 3rd-millennium north Syrian city (Tell Mardikh) with a great early-Semitic archive; reshaped study of pre-patriarchal Syria; the overheated 1970s "Ebla and the Bible" debate. Modern: near Aleppo, Syria.
- [[shiloh|Shiloh]] — premonarchic central sanctuary of the tribes (ark, Eli, Hannah/Samuel); destroyed by the Philistines c. 1050; Jeremiah's paradigm of a forsaken sanctuary. Modern: Khirbet Seilun, West Bank.
- [[bethel|Bethel]] — Jacob's "house of God" (the ladder); one of the rare conquest-fitting destructions (with Hazor); Jeroboam's southern royal calf-shrine ("the sin of Jeroboam"); Amos vs. Amaziah. Modern: Beitin (disputed).
- [[dan|Dan]] — northern limit ("Dan to Beersheba"); Canaanite Laish taken by the migrating Danites; Jeroboam's northern calf-sanctuary (the bamah); the **Tel Dan stela** ("house of David"). Modern: Tel Dan, Galilee.
- [[gezer|Gezer]] — great Canaanite city-state (Amarna's Milkilu; Merneptah's city-determinative vs. "Israel" as a people); Solomon's Egyptian-dowry rebuild (with Hazor, Megiddo); the Gezer Calendar. Modern: Tel Gezer.
- [[sinai-horeb|Sinai / Horeb]] — the wilderness "mountain of God" of the covenant and lawgiving (and Elijah's "still small voice"); location unknown/disputed (Jebel Musa vs. Midian/Arabia vs. Negeb); Midianite origin of Yahwism.
- [[mount-gerizim|Mount Gerizim]] — the Samaritan holy mountain above Shechem (the "mountain of blessing"); the temple destroyed by John Hyrcanus (128 BCE); the counter-Jerusalem of John 4; living Samaritan Passover site. Modern: Jabal at-Tur, Nablus.
- [[gerar|Gerar]] — western-Negev town of the patriarchal narratives (Abraham & Isaac and "Abimelech king of Gerar"; the wife-sister episodes and well-disputes); the Philistine "anachronism." Modern: usually Tel Haror, Israel.
- [[beersheba|Beersheba]] — the proverbial southern limit ("from Dan to Beersheba"); patriarchal wells, oaths, and altars; an Iron Age Judahite town with a dismantled horned altar (cult suppression?). Modern: Tel Beersheba, near Be'er Sheva.

### Scholarship (Patristic Philosophy)

- [[christopher-stead|Christopher Stead]] (1913–2008) — *Philosophy in Christian Antiquity* (CUP 1994); philosophical analysis of Greek background (Middle Platonists, Philo) and patristic use in Trinity/Christology; cautious on "Christian philosophy" label; semantic precision on ousia/hypostasis; dedicated treatment of Augustine. Primary modern source for the philosophical formation of classical Christian doctrine.
- [[harry-austryn-wolfson|Harry Austryn Wolfson]] (1887–1974) — *The Philosophy of the Church Fathers, Vol. I: Faith, Trinity, Incarnation* (Harvard, 1956; part of "Structure and Growth of Philosophic Systems from Plato to Spinoza" series); hypothetico-deductive reconstruction of the "Christian version of Greek philosophy" created by the Fathers (building on Philo); detailed on faith/reason (single vs. double faith theories), allegorical method (classifications from Philo + NT), Trinity (three-and-one via relative unity of rule or substratum/ousia/hypostasis/homoousios; twofold vs. single stage Logos; Cappadocian vocabulary), Incarnation (two-and-one via physical union analogies, especially "predominance" and perichoresis), and "the Anathematized" heresies (Gnostic systems, Modalism, neo-Ebionism/neo-Docetism as logical outcomes). Foundational for understanding patristic thought as systematic philosophy. Cross-links to Stead and new groups/locations (Cappadocians, Nicaea). See also [[wolfson-the-philosophy-of-the-church-fathers|source summary]] in scholarship/.
- [[mark-julian-edwards|Mark Julian Edwards]] — *Origen against Plato* (Ashgate 2002; reissued Routledge 2018; Ashgate Studies in Philosophy & Theology in Late Antiquity). Challenges over-Platonizing of Origen ("contamination" narrative from ancient heresiologists to moderns like John Paul II); Origen constructed *Christian* philosophy *to preserve* theology from pagan infiltration. Common vocabulary/parallels insufficient for dependence claims. New interpretations of Gnostics and Clement's Trinity. Core: Origen's hermeneutics, eschatology, cosmology, Trinitarian theology tied to his *radically anti-Platonic* understanding of human nature/anthropology. Complements Stead (overview) and Wolfson (systematic reconstruction) as corrective on Origen specifically. Cross-links in Origen figure, logos-christology, and scholarship pages. See [[edwards-origen-against-plato|source summary]] in scholarship/.

- [[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]] (1943–2019) — *Destroyer of the Gods: Early Christian Distinctiveness in the Roman World* (Baylor, 2016); *One God, One Lord* (1988); *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003). Social-historical analysis of early Christianity as a genuinely distinctive and novel movement within its Roman-world context. Five axes of distinctiveness: (1) exclusivist monotheism directed to the Roman "market"; (2) a particular "bookishness" including codex preference and *nomina sacra*; (3) a trans-ethnic identity as a new *tertium quid* (neither Jewish nor Gentile); (4) ethical rigorism, especially on sexual practice; (5) dyadic devotional pattern (God + Jesus jointly venerated). - [[rodney-stark|Rodney Stark]] (b. 1934) — University of Washington / Baylor; *The Rise of Christianity* (Princeton, 1996). See also [[stark-rise-of-christianity|source summary]] in scholarship/.
- [[kurt-rudolph|Kurt Rudolph]] (1929–2020) — German historian of religion; *Gnosis: The Nature and History of Gnosticism* (German 1977/1980; English 1987, ed. R. McL. Wilson). Foundational phenomenological + historical synthesis treating Gnosis as a coherent late-antique religion (*spätantike Religion*) with structured mythology (Pleroma of aeons, Sophia fall, Demiurge as inferior creator often = OT God, gnosis as saving knowledge and ascent, community/cult/ethics); detailed on Nag Hammadi as revolutionary primary sources; early schools, 2nd-c. See [[rudolph-gnosis-the-nature-and-history-of-gnosticism|source summary]] in scholarship/; primary beneficiary pages: [[gnostics|Gnostics]], [[valentinus|Valentinus]], new concepts (gnosis, demiurge, pleroma, sophia-gnostic), [[nag-hammadi-library|Nag Hammadi Library]].

### NT/Early Christianity
- [[dyadic-devotion|Dyadic Devotion]] — Hurtado's term for the early Christian devotional pattern of jointly venerating God (Father) and Jesus as co-recipients of corporate worship; six ritual forms; the Shema reformulation in 1 Cor 8:4-6; no adequate Jewish precedent; the mutation that distinguishes Christianity from Second Temple Judaism

- [[religious-economy|Religious Economy]] — Stark-Bainbridge-Iannaccone framework: religious groups as firms in a market; exclusive vs. nonexclusive firms; free-rider problem solved by sacrifice and stigma; paganism declining before Constantine (supply-side model); compensators; Christianity's organizational superiority (Stark, *Rise of Christianity*, Ch. 9)
- [[network-conversion|Network Conversion (Lofland-Stark Model)]] — Lofland and Stark (1965): conversion follows pre-existing social ties, not ideological persuasion; fieldwork on American Moonies; seven stages; applied to early Christianity in Stark Ch. 1: Christianity grew by maintaining open social networks; household conversion as structural norm; ideology as post-hoc rationalization
- [[rule-of-faith|Rule of Faith (*Regula Fidei*)]] — Early Christian doctrinal summary (*kanōn tēs pisteōs*); seven core affirmations; geographical spread across Syria, Rome, North Africa, Gaul, Egypt; attestation from Ignatius through Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement, Origen; anti-heretical function; narrative structure (creation–fall–redemption–consummation); relationship to NT canon (Kruger)

- [[monepiscopate|Monepiscopate]] — Development of singular bishop office in 2nd century; from plural elders (Didache, 1 Clement, Polycarp's Epistle) through Ignatius' transitional advocacy to Irenaeus' apostolic succession argument; Jerome's explanation; Quartodeciman controversy (Kruger)
