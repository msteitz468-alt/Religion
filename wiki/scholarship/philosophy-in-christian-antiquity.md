# Philosophy in Christian Antiquity — Christopher Stead (1994)

Christopher Stead (Ely Professor of Divinity Emeritus, University of Cambridge) provides a lucid, philosophically precise survey of how Greek philosophical traditions (especially Platonism and its Middle Platonic developments) shaped the intellectual articulation of Christian doctrine from the second century to the Council of Chalcedon (451 CE), with a dedicated closing treatment of Augustine.

The book is structured in three parts:

**Part I: The Philosophical Background** surveys Greek philosophy from the Pre-Socratics through Socrates, Plato (Forms, maturity), Aristotle, Epicurus and the Stoics, the Middle Platonists (including a full chapter on Philo of Alexandria as the key Jewish-Hellenistic bridge), and the philosophy of late antiquity (Neoplatonism).

**Part II: The Use of Philosophy in Christian Theology** is topical rather than author-by-author: the debate about whether Christianity should be considered a "philosophy"; Greek vs. Hebrew conceptions of God; proofs of God's existence; God as simple unchanging Being; how God is described; Logos and Spirit; Unity of substance (ousia); Substance and Persons (hypostasis); Christ as God and Man; Two natures united.

**Part III: Augustine** examines his views on philosophy, faith and knowledge, and freedom and goodness, presenting him as the one patristic figure no student of late antiquity or philosopher can ignore.

## Method and Hermeneutical Stance

Stead writes as a philosopher with theological sensitivity. He insists on accurate logical analysis and careful historical semantics of key terms (ousia, hypostasis, homoousios, Logos). He is cautious about anachronism and over-generous labeling: he argues that what is often called "Christian philosophy" in this period is largely Christian theology that borrows and adapts philosophical doctrines and methods while remaining fundamentally committed to the Bible and ecclesial tradition. Only a few figures (e.g., certain later logicians or Boethius) approach philosophy as an autonomous critical discipline.

He draws on (and sometimes corrects or extends) prior scholarship, including his own earlier *Divine Substance*. The approach is neither purely historical-critical nor confessional; it combines precise conceptual analysis with attention to the concrete debates (Arianism, Nicene settlement, Cappadocian clarification) that forced terminological refinement.

## Key Contributions and Claims

- **The "debate about Christian philosophy" (Ch. 8)**: Educated pagans looked to philosophy (logic, ethics, physics) for a committed way of life and rational worldview. Early Christians presented their faith as a "new" or "barbarian philosophy" and entered debate with the schools. They borrowed heavily (especially from Platonism) for articulating God, creation, evil, and free will, while correcting where necessary and maintaining a sharper communal and scriptural identity. Stead argues most patristic work is theology using philosophical tools rather than philosophy proper; commitment to revelation and tradition left limited room for dispassionate critical autonomy. Justin Martyr is cited as a Christian who taught philosophy but did not match the acumen or breadth of the best contemporary pagans (Plutarch, Galen). Later separation increased as Christian theology rigidified and Neoplatonism turned esoteric and anti-Christian.

- **Philo of Alexandria as bridge (Ch. 6)**: The Middle Platonists (revival associated with Antiochus of Ascalon, claiming substantial agreement between Plato, Aristotle, and Stoicism) produced a theistic, syncretistic framework. Philo stands as the major Jewish representative who applied this philosophical toolkit (allegory, intermediary principles) to the exegesis of Scripture. His Logos doctrine (as mediating "image," "firstborn," or "second God" between the transcendent God and the material world) provided conceptual resources that early Christians adapted — while going decisively further by identifying the Logos with the incarnate Christ.

- **Terminological precision on Trinity and Christology (esp. Chs. 13–17)**: Detailed treatment of how the Fathers used and refined philosophical language. "Logos and Spirit" explores the development of personal and pre-existent categories. "Unity of substance" (Ch. 14) challenges overly Aristotelian readings of ousia as fixed by the Categories before Nicaea; notes that Athanasius and others were initially flexible on "hypostasis" terminology. The Cappadocians (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of Nazianzus) were decisive in stabilizing ousia as common nature/species and hypostasis as individual subsistence, while expressing unity through simplicity, shared activity/energy, and modes of origination. Stead highlights the Latin-Greek confusion (substantia used for both ousia and hypostasis in different traditions) and problems with later formulas such as "numerical identity of substance." The two-natures discussion (Ch. 17) addresses the philosophical difficulties of uniting divine immutability/simplicity with genuine humanity.

- **Augustine (Part III)**: Augustine is treated with respect as a thinker who can "stand up to the toughest criticism." He integrates Neoplatonic insights (illumination theory of knowledge, the soul's turn inward to the divine) but firmly subordinates philosophy to faith and Scripture. His treatment of freedom, goodness, and grace shows both philosophical sophistication and distinctively Christian reorientation (original sin, grace as healing the will).
