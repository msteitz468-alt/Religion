# Destroyer of the Gods: Early Christian Distinctiveness in the Roman World

**Larry W. Hurtado** (Baylor University Press, 2016)

Accessible scholarly monograph addressed to a general academic audience, arguing that early Christianity (first three centuries) was genuinely distinctive — not simply one religious option among many — in the Roman world. The title derives from Roman-era pagan critics who viewed Christians as threatening the traditional gods. Hurtado's dual thesis: (1) early Christianity was demonstrably unusual in its Roman context, and (2) the features that made it unusual have since become Western cultural commonplaces, creating what he calls "cultural amnesia" about their origins.

---

## Source Type and Hermeneutical Method

**Historical-critical / sociological**: Hurtado operates entirely within the academic study of early Christianity, drawing on literary, papyrological, epigraphic, and archaeological evidence. The book is self-consciously neither apologetics nor skeptical polemic — it aims at historical description. He engages the German *religionsgeschichtliche Schule* (history-of-religion school) critically, arguing that its members overemphasized parallels between early Christianity and its environment at the expense of genuine distinctives, partly because they had theological axes to grind.

Hurtado focuses on **"proto-orthodox" Christianity** — the stream that became mainstream — while acknowledging significant early diversity (Valentinian, Marcionite, gnostic varieties). His warrant: proto-orthodox circles were numerically dominant and historically formative, and it is this variety that pagan critics treated as the primary object of concern.

## Chapter Summaries

### Introduction: Historical and Distinctive
Early Christianity emerged c. 30 AD from Jewish circles in Roman Judaea centered on the crucified and resurrected Jesus. By the early second century the term "Christians" (*Christiani*) was in common use. Growth estimates: ~1,000 adherents by 40 AD; ~7,000–10,000 by 100 AD; ~200,000 by 200 AD; ~5–6 million by 300 AD. In light of this remarkable growth, Hurtado argues that early Christianity's distinctiveness (rather than mere resemblance to its environment) best explains its success. Drawing on Rodney Stark's sociology of new religious movements, a successful movement must maintain "medium tension" with its setting — neither fully alien nor fully assimilated.

### Chapter 1: Early Christians in the Eyes of Non-Christians
The pagan record uniformly presents Christianity as strange and objectionable:

- **Jewish opposition**: Paul's own letters testify to his pre-conversion persecution of the early movement, likely provoked by what he saw as inappropriate reverence for Jesus. His "zeal" (Phil 3:6; Gal 1:13-14) places him within the **Phinehas tradition** — justifiable violence against fellow Jews for the sake of collective covenant integrity (Num 25; 1 Macc 2:26; Philo *On the Special Laws*).
- **Tacitus** (*Annals* 15.44): Christianity is a "deadly/dangerous superstition" (*exitiabilis superstitio*); Christians were "hated for their abominations" and executed under Nero for "hatred of the human race."
- **Suetonius** (*Nero* 16.2): Christians are "a class of people given to a new and wicked superstition."
- **Pliny the Younger** (*Epistles* 10.96, c. 112 AD): His investigation of Christians in Bithynia-Pontus — the earliest outsider description of a Christian worship gathering — finds only "a perverse and extravagant superstition." He executes persistent Christians simply for their faith, demands apostasy proved by sacrifice to the gods and cursing of Christ, and reports the spread of Christianity had economically damaged pagan temples.
- **Galen** (129–199 AD): More benign; treats Christianity as a philosophy deficient in rational demonstration but admires Christians for their courage facing death, sexual self-restraint, and "keen pursuit of justice."
- **Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 AD): Dismisses Christian readiness to die as "mere obstinacy"; ordered executions of Christians including Justin Martyr.
- **Lucian of Samosata** (*The Death of Peregrinus*, late 2nd c.): Extended parody mocking Christians as credulous simpletons devoted to "that crucified sophist."
- **Celsus** (*The True Word*, c. 175–180 AD): The most intellectually ambitious pagan attack. Characterizes Christianity as a vulgar philosophy for the ignorant, ridicules the Incarnation and Resurrection, and — crucially — condemns Christians not for their ethics (he sometimes admires their ethics) but for their refusal to worship the traditional gods. Willing to tolerate Christianity if it only submitted to the gods. Origen's later refutation (*Contra Celsum*) preserves ~75% of the text.

The cumulative point: pagan critics consistently identified Christian *exclusivism in worship* — not mere oddity of ritual — as the decisive problem.

### Chapter 2: A New Kind of Faith
Early Christianity represented a genuinely different kind of religion in the Roman environment:

**The Roman religious world**: A "world full of gods" in which piety (*eusebeia*, *religio*) meant readiness to worship all the gods. Deities were linked to households, cities, guilds, legions, and the empire itself. Religious participation was not voluntary but civic and familial obligation. No deity demanded exclusive loyalty; to refuse worship to any god was impious.

**Jewish precedent**: Jews also refused to worship the pagan gods, but this was tolerated as an ethnic peculiarity. Jewish monolatry was ethnically bounded.

**The specifically Christian offense**: Early Christianity took the Jewish exclusivist stance but propagated it *transetnically*. Gentile converts had no ethnic basis for refusing the gods — they simply abandoned worship they had previously performed, which struck fellow pagans as arbitrary, impious, and socially disruptive. The early Christian vocabulary for this refusal — *eidōlon* ("idol"), *eidōlolatria* ("idolatry") — was Jewish in origin but applied on a transethnic scale for the first time.

**Christian beliefs about their God**: The early Christian God was (a) radically transcendent and imageless, (b) yet directly accessible in prayer and worship, and (c) moved by love (*agapē*) for humanity — a triad without real parallel in pagan theology. The notion that the supreme transcendent deity loves the world and seeks human redemption was, in Hurtado's judgment, "preposterous" to most educated pagans.

**The "dyadic" devotional pattern**: Most distinctive of all, early Christianity was characterized by what Hurtado terms **dyadic devotion** — the inclusion of Jesus alongside God as a co-recipient of worship. From the earliest evidence, Jesus was not merely a revered teacher or heavenly intercessor but the co-object of devotion alongside "the Father." This had no precedent even in Second Temple Jewish tradition. The "chief agent" figures in Jewish apocalyptic (Enoch, Michael, Wisdom) never received the kind of corporate cultic devotion that Jesus received in early churches. This is the mutation Hurtado identifies as distinguishing the Jesus-movement even from the Jewish matrix in which it emerged. 1 Corinthians 8:4-6 is his prime exhibit: a reformulation of the Shema that places Jesus as "Lord" within the monotheistic confession.

### Chapter 3: A Different Identity
Early Christianity introduced what Hurtado argues is the first form of **trans-ethnic voluntary religious identity** in Western history:

In the Roman world, religious identity was normally embedded in ethnic/civic identity — your gods came with your birthright. Voluntary religious associations (mystery cults, philosophical schools) were supplementary, not exclusive. Even the mystery cults — Mithras, Isis — made no demand of exclusive allegiance and did not require members to abandon other religious obligations.

Early Christianity was unique in demanding that converts (Jewish and Gentile alike) make their Christian commitment *exclusively* identity-defining — replacing, not supplementing, other religious loyalties. The term *Christianoi* (first attested in Antioch; Acts 11:26) reflects Latin naming conventions for partisan supporters of a named figure (*Caesariani*, *Herodianoi*): Christians were "Christ-partisans." The Epistle to Diognetus (late 2nd c.) is a key text: Christians follow local customs in most matters but refuse to worship the gods and do not expose their infants — markers of a distinct social identity.

Hurtado argues early Christianity also introduced the conceptual and legal separation of religious identity from political loyalty — visible already in Romans 13:1-7 and developed by Tertullian (*To Scapula*) into what may be the first explicit argument for religious liberty in the ancient world.

### Chapter 4: A "Bookish" Religion
Early Christianity was unusually text-centered compared to other Roman-era religious groups:

**Reading**: The practice of reading sacred texts in corporate worship went back to the synagogue, but early churches extended it by treating distinctly Christian writings — Paul's letters, then Gospels — as scripture alongside the OT. Justin Martyr's account of Roman Christian worship (c. 150 AD) describes the reading of "memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets" as a regular element.

**Writing**: The volume, variety, and ambition of early Christian literary output was extraordinary. In the first three centuries: at least 200 identifiable texts — Gospels, letters, apologies, apocalypses, theological treatises, church-order documents, martyr accounts. Paul's letters alone are off the scale for their length in the ancient letter-writing tradition. No other Roman-era religious movement (Mithraism, Isis cult, etc.) left a comparable literary output. Where other cults left shrines and inscriptions, Christianity left books.

**The codex preference**: Early Christians preferred the codex (leaf book) for their scriptural texts at a time when the bookroll was the prestige literary format (95% of non-Christian literary manuscripts were bookrolls; 75%+ of Christian manuscripts were codices). This was strongest for texts treated as scripture — nearly 100% of extant 2nd–3rd century Christian OT and NT manuscripts are codices, vs. ~1/3 codex for other Christian literary texts. Hurtado argues this was a deliberate, countercultural choice that visually distinguished Christian sacred books from the wider book culture.

**Nomina sacra**: Early Christian scribes developed a distinctive abbreviation convention — the *nomina sacra* — writing key words (God, Lord, Jesus, Christ) in contracted form with a horizontal overline. Evidenced already in the earliest surviving fragments of Christian manuscripts (mid-to-late 2nd c.). This is probably the earliest evidence of a specifically Christian visual culture and represents a reverential scribal innovation without exact parallel in Jewish or pagan manuscripts.

### Chapter 5: A New Way to Live
Early Christianity made a collective behavioral commitment central to religious identity in a way unprecedented in the Roman religious environment:

**Roman-era "religion" normally had little to say about everyday ethics** — its focus was ritual correctness (sacrifice, offerings, festival observance). By contrast, early Christian texts are saturated with behavioral exhortation applied to all adherents immediately upon initiation (baptism), not only philosophical elites.

Key behavioral distinctives:

- **Infant exposure**: While common and legally acceptable in the Roman world (including among otherwise caring people, as Hilarion's 1 BC letter shows), early Christians universally condemned it, echoing Jewish practice. Their distinctive language — condemning it as killing the innocent — stood in contrast to philosophical voices (Musonius Rufus) who expressed mild disapproval but mounted no organized opposition.
- **Gladiatorial spectacles and blood-sport**: Early Christians generally refused to attend or participate.
- **Sexual ethics**: Paul's use of *porneia* to condemn male extramarital sex (including with prostitutes, courtesans, and slaves) was a radical departure from Roman double-standard morality. The innovation was bringing men under the same sexual standard applied to honorable women. 1 Timothy's *mias gynaikos andra* ("husband of one wife") required a new Greek phrase because no cultural concept of male marital fidelity to one partner existed in Roman vocabulary.
- **Child sexual abuse**: Early Christians coined new vocabulary — *paidophthorein* ("to corrupt children sexually") — to relabel what the broader culture euphemistically called *paiderastia*. This appears in Didache 2.2 and Epistle of Barnabas 19.4.

Hurtado argues the key distinction from philosophical parallels (Musonius Rufus, Stoics) is social scope: philosophical teaching was for elite male students with leisure for intellectual formation; early Christian behavioral exhortation was addressed to all social levels, to women and slaves as well as free men, delivered collectively in house-church settings, and embedded in a theological framework (divine love, bodily resurrection, the Lord's judgment) rather than merely an appeal to personal honor.

### Conclusion
The distinctives of early Christianity — exclusive worship, trans-ethnic identity, textual culture, behavioral ethics — made it genuinely strange and dangerous in Roman eyes. These features also explain its remarkable numerical growth, which was achieved without state support and against social and legal opposition. After Constantine, Christianity's influence reshaped Western culture such that these originally peculiar features became unexamined assumptions about what "religion" is.

### Appendix: The History of Early Christianity in Scholarly Perspective
Critical review of the *religionsgeschichtliche Schule* (Hatch, Reitzenstein, Bousset). Hurtado's critique: these scholars overemphasized pagan parallels and underemphasized Jewish roots and genuine Christian distinctives, partly under the influence of Liberal Protestant theological programs. Bousset's *Kyrios Christos* (1913) argued that worship of Jesus as divine Lord arose in Gentile circles under pagan influence — a thesis Hurtado has refuted in his technical scholarship (especially *Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003).
