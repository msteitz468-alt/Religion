# Holmes, *The Apostolic Fathers: Greek Texts and English Translations* (3rd ed., 2007)

## Bibliographic Note

Holmes, Michael W., ed. and trans. *The Apostolic Fathers: Greek Texts and English Translations*. 3rd edition. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2007. ISBN 978-0-8010-3468-8.

A revision and expansion of the diglot (Greek + English facing-page) edition originally compiled by J. B. Lightfoot and J. R. Harmer (1891); updated by Holmes in a first edition (1989), second edition (1999), and this third edition (2007). The standard scholarly English diglot for academic use.

---

## Nature of This Source

This is both a **primary text collection** (the Apostolic Fathers themselves, in Greek) and a **scholarly edition** (Holmes' introductions, textual decisions, critical apparatus, and English translation). The two aspects require different handling:

- The **Greek texts** and their English translations are primary sources — the actual writings of the Apostolic Fathers, not scholarship *about* them.
- Holmes' **introductions, textual notes, and bibliographies** are secondary scholarship: his editorial judgment about manuscript traditions, dates, authorship, and interpretive context.

For wiki purposes, individual text pages document the primary documents; this scholarship page records Holmes' editorial framework and the collection as a whole.

## Holmes' General Introduction: The "Apostolic Fathers" as a Category

### Definition and History of the Collection

"Apostolic Fathers" is a modern scholarly label — not a self-designation used by these writers. It names the earliest extant Christian writings outside the New Testament, originating in the period ca. AD 70–150, written by authors who were (or were thought to be) contemporaries of the apostles.

The collection as a formal entity began with Jean-Baptiste Cotelier's edition (1672). Gallandi added *Diognetus* and Papias (1765). The *Didache* was unknown until 1873, when Philotheos Bryennios discovered Codex Hierosolymitanus in Constantinople and published the full text in 1883.

The collection has **no intrinsic unity**: these documents do not belong to a single tradition, genre, or doctrinal school. Their grouping reflects a scholarly judgment that they are the earliest non-canonical Christian writings, not a historical community.

### Historical Setting

The texts arise in the context of:
- **Post-70 CE Judaism**: The temple's destruction forced Pharisaic Judaism to restructure around Torah study; synagogue and church were separating. The *birkat ha-minim* (a synagogue benediction cursing heretics) eventually excluded Jewish Christians.
- **Greco-Roman cultural challenge**: Christian beliefs (monotheism, resurrection, rejection of civic religion) conflicted with Roman social norms. Christians were accused of atheism, cannibalism, incest, and political disloyalty.
- **Internal governance transitions**: The apostles were dying; authority shifted to written texts (gospels, letters) and to resident officers. The transition from charismatic itinerant leadership (prophets, apostles) to resident episcopal leadership is visible across several of these texts.

### Textual History and Scholarship

**The Tübingen School (F. C. Baur, 19th c.)**: Applied Hegelian dialectic to early Christianity; viewed 1 Clement and the Ignatius letters as 2nd-century forgeries. J. B. Lightfoot and Theodor Zahn demolished this, establishing both texts as authentic and providing the "Archimedean point" for early Christian historical reconstruction.

**Walter Bauer's *Orthodoxy and Heresy* (1934)**: Argued heresy preceded orthodoxy; drew heavily on the Apostolic Fathers. Substantially challenged by subsequent scholarship (including Kruger; see [[kruger-christianity-at-the-crossroads]]).

**The Codex Hierosolymitanus (1873)**: The most important single manuscript discovery — contains the full text of 1 Clement, 2 Clement, Didache, and the long form of the Ignatian letters. Before this discovery, 1 Clement was only known through Codex Alexandrinus and a Syriac translation; the full Greek text of the Didache was completely unknown.

## Holmes' Editorial Contribution

### Textual Decisions

Holmes builds on Lightfoot-Harmer with updated critical apparatus reflecting 20th-century papyrus discoveries and manuscript studies. Key decisions:

- **Ignatius**: Accepts the 7-letter Middle Recension (Lightfoot's position); rejects the long recension (13 letters) as a 4th-century interpolation and the Syriac short recension (3 letters) as an abbreviation.
- **Hermas**: Provides both the traditional Visions-Mandates-Similitudes structure and the newer sequential chapter numbering; both reference systems appear in the translation.
- **Papias**: Expands the collection from Lightfoot's edition by adding newly identified fragments (nos. 21–28).
- **Diognetus**: Notes the break between sections 10 and 11 explicitly; treats 11–12 as a distinct document accidentally bound with the apology.

### Hermeneutical Framework (Holmes as Scholar)

Holmes operates within the **historical-critical tradition** — he evaluates authorship, date, occasion, manuscript tradition, and theological context using standard critical tools. He makes no confessional commitment to any position: he notes the open questions honestly, presents competing scholarly views, and avoids adjudication where the evidence is insufficient.

The **thematic analysis** section at the end of the volume identifies cross-cutting themes (Christology, eschatology, church order, ethics, use of scripture) across all ten texts — a comparative analytical contribution.

## Key Takeaways: What This Collection Adds to the Wiki

### 1. First Direct Access to Primary Texts

All prior wiki pages on the Apostolic Fathers (Ignatius, Polycarp) were built from secondary scholarship (Kruger, Stark). Holmes gives us the texts themselves. This shifts the epistemic status of all Apostolic Father content from "scholarship about X says..." to "the text of X says..."

### 2. The Diversity of Early Christianity

The collection reveals a range of social locations, theological emphases, and governance models that should not be flattened:

| Text | Social Location | Governance Model | Key Concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Clement | Educated Roman elite | Hierarchical succession | Order, legitimate authority |
| Shepherd of Hermas | Lower-class Roman freedmen | Charismatic-institutional mix | Postbaptismal sin, wealth |
| Didache | Jewish-Christian Syria/Egypt | Transitional (itinerant → resident) | Liturgy, community ethics |
| Ignatius letters | Asian churches in crisis | Emerging monepiscopate | Anti-Docetism, unity |
| Diognetus | Educated apologetic circles | Not addressed | Christian identity vs. Greco-Roman world |

### 3. Canonical Witnesses

Several of these texts function as key witnesses to NT canon formation:
- **Papias** (ca. 125 CE): traditions on Mark as Peter's interpreter and Matthew's "Hebrew logia"
- **2 Clement** (ca. 140 CE): first instance of a NT passage quoted as "scripture" (Matt 9:13 at 2 Clem. 2.4)
- **1 Clement**: Cited itself as scripture by Clement of Alexandria; present in Codex Alexandrinus right after Revelation
- **Barnabas and Hermas**: In Codex Sinaiticus; cited as scripture by Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Didymus

### 4. The Two-Johns Problem

Papias' famous preface (Frag. 3) mentions two figures named John: John among the apostles and "the Elder John" among the disciples. Eusebius uses this to attribute the Book of Revelation to the Elder rather than the Apostle, freeing the Apostle from millennialist associations. Directly relevant to [[revelation-date-authorship]] and [[john-apostle]].

### 5. The Diognetus Loss

The sole manuscript of the *Epistle to Diognetus* was destroyed in the 1870 Siege of Strasbourg. The text survives only through printed editions and handwritten copies made before the fire. This is the most tenuous manuscript tradition of any text in the wiki.
