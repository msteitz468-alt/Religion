# The Philosophy of the Church Fathers — Harry Austryn Wolfson (1956)

Harry Austryn Wolfson (Nathan Littauer Professor of Hebrew Literature and Philosophy at Harvard) presents in this volume (the third in his series tracing philosophic systems from Plato to Spinoza) a systematic reconstruction of how the Church Fathers created a "Christian version of Greek philosophy." Building directly on his earlier *Philo* (1947), Wolfson argues that the Fathers, like Philo, started from the assumption that Greek philosophers had discovered by reason certain truths revealed in Scripture, and thus recast Christian beliefs (especially the mysteries of the Trinity and Incarnation) into a coherent philosophic system.

The work is the first volume of what was planned as a multi-volume study (only Vol. I published in this form). It uses Wolfson's "hypothetico-deductive method of text study": a general analysis and forecast as working hypothesis, followed by representative texts from the Fathers (not exhaustive, but illustrative, often beginning with earliest appearances and concluding with Augustine or John of Damascus) pieced together to reconstruct the latent processes of reasoning behind scattered terms, formulas, and analogies. The goal is to uncover "unuttered thoughts" and determine the true meaning by tracing how and why certain things were said.

## Structure and Key Arguments

**Part One: Faith and Reason**
- Starts with Paul’s differentiation of "the wisdom of God" and "the wisdom of the world" and his refusal to adorn teachings with persuasive Greek arguments, yet notes the inevitable introduction of philosophy by the Fathers due to historical circumstances and the need to explain and defend the faith to educated audiences.
- Detailed comparison with Philo on (1) limitations of philosophy vs. Scripture, (2) evidence for the truth of Scripture’s teachings, (3) the origin of philosophy itself.
- The Allegorical Method: Background in rabbinic midrash (predictive/historical/eschatological, moral, preexistential) and Philo’s philosophical "allegory" (with terms like "type," "shadow," "parable," "tropical"). NT uses Philonic terms for non-philosophical midrashic senses. Clement of Alexandria combines Philonic philosophical interpretations with NT adventual/eschatological/moral/preexistential ones; his classifications traceable to Philo. Development (with modifications) in Origen (corporeal/psychical/spiritual or mystical; "spiritual" includes preexistential/adventual but wider "ineffable mysteries" and doctrines on God, Son, creatures, etc.), Jerome, Augustine, Cassian; some Antiochians deviate toward more literal.
- Scriptural Presuppositions: Fathers maintain allegory against extreme literalism or denial; use "Reason and the Rule of Faith" as criteria for literal acceptance. The Christian Rule of Faith embodies six of Philo’s eight presuppositions (existence and unity of God, creation, providence, revelation of the Law—including a Christianized Oral Law—and existence of ideas), understood in specific ways by the Fathers.
- Handmaiden of Scripture: Philosophy subordinated to Scripture in both Philo and the Fathers (difference in meaning/extent of subordination). Three views among Fathers on subordination of faith to reason, traceable to differing understandings of "faith" in Scripture.
- Single Faith Theories: Tertullian (criticism of philosophy, self-sufficiency of faith, prohibition of searching for demonstrations, yet his own use and judgment that philosophers arrived at some truths by reason; apparent contradictions reconciled). Origen (superiority of rationalized faith to simple faith, yet simple faith not without merit). These as two types of single-faith theory.
- Double Faith Theory: Aristotelian "faith" (judgment of truth of immediate/derivative knowledge) combined with Stoic "assent." Clement of Alexandria first applies this to religious faith as assent to Scripture teachings (whether immediate/not demonstrated or derivative/demonstrated = "gnosis," "scientific faith," or "exact faith"); contends for equality of simple and demonstrated faith against both opponents of philosophy and depreciators of simple faith. Augustine: permissibility and desirability of philosophy in faith matters; meaning of reason and faith (faith as assent); evidence for equality of simple and rationally demonstrated faith, and that "faith" technically includes both; general characterization of other Fathers.

**Part Two: The Trinity, the Logos, and Platonic Ideas**
- Origin of the Trinitarian Formula: NT unipartite/bipartite/tripartite confessions (baptismal formula, 2 Cor closing salutation, Paul’s admonitory/hortative uses); the tripartite need explanation due to the new significance the Holy Spirit acquired with Paul.
- The Holy Spirit as the Preexistent Christ (Paul’s identification of Spirit with preexistent Christ/wisdom; implications for confessions and the Law’s function passing to the Spirit).
- The Holy Spirit as the Begetter of Jesus (Matt/Luke virgin birth; transformation from Paul’s view; no Jewish precedent for supernatural birth without human paternity; no pre-birth Trinity in Synoptics).
- The Logos as the Preexistent Christ (Philo’s Logos + Wisdom of Solomon’s wisdom identified with John’s Logos; vagueness on "became flesh" and distinctness of Spirit from Logos; no definite pre-birth Trinity in John).
- Identification and then Differentiation of the Logos and the Holy Spirit (in Ignatius, Apologists, Apostolic Fathers the preexistent Christ is both; no pre-birth Trinity early; differentiation appears with Apologists’ twofold stage theory of the Logos).
- Twofold Stage Theory of the Logos (Greek and Latin Fathers express belief that the Logos became a distinct hypostasis/person at some point, e.g., for creation or incarnation; interpretations of John 1:1 "in the beginning"; lingering in some).
- Single Stage Theory (Irenaeus and Origen express it; reasons for Irenaeus’ rejection of twofold; Origen and Plotinus comparison; debate over Clement; "eternal generation" meaning both eternally continuous process and completely generated from eternity—discussed by Alexander, Marius Victorinus, Augustine, etc.; generation "by will" (Philo, Apologists, Origen, Athanasius debate) or "by nature"; Holy Spirit’s origination parallel, with lingering identifications of Spirit with preexistent Christ even after differentiation: application of "God," "wisdom," and functions to both; verbal and causal distinctions).
- The Logos and the Platonic Ideas: Two questions—Fathers’ view of an intelligible world/ideas, and which contemporary interpretation of Plato’s theory (Aristotelian, Albinian, Philonic) they followed. Detailed analysis of Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Augustine; general characterization for others. (Philonic transcendent ideas in the Logos/mind of God often influential.)

**Part Three: The Incarnation (The Mystery of the Generation; The Mystery of the Trinity; The Mystery of the Incarnation)**
- The Mystery of the Generation: Application of "mystery" (Paul) to generation of Logos, Trinity, Incarnation. Transition from God as artisan (Hebrew Scripture) to begetter (Greek mythology influence warned against). Rejection of Gnostic "prolation" or "from things nonexistent." Physical analogies (uttered word, sun beam/light) for generation of Logos and Holy Spirit; aptness questioned; mystery remains despite analogies.
- The Mystery of the Trinity: Problem of reconciling inherited one God with three beings each God (three and one). Rejection of denying reality of preexistent Christ/Spirit. Solutions on premise of real individual species with relative unity: (1) unity of rule; (2) unity of substratum/genus/species (ousia/hypostasis, homoousios). Residual mystery even after philosophic analogies. Detailed in Origen (hypostases for individuals, ousia for common specific genus, homoousios; also unity of rule), Tertullian (personae for individuals, substantia for common substratum; could have used consubstantialis; unity of rule; three pairs of contrasting terms), Basil (fixes vocabulary for reality/individuality and common unity amid vagueness and heresies; Nicene Creed uses ousia/hypostasis synonymously for common unity as substratum, with Son begotten of Father’s ousia and homoousios; expression "homoousios" lends itself to multiple interpretations leading to suspicions like homoiousios).
- The Mystery of the Incarnation: "Two and one" problem. Use of analogies of physical union (soul and body in man) to explain duality of natures with unity of person. Detailed analysis of kinds of union in Aristotle (composition, mixture, "predominance" loosely called mixture/composition) and Stoics (composition, mixture differing, confusion); five kinds total. Orthodox preference for "predominance" (like form-matter or soul-body; used by Tertullian as electrum/mixture in popular sense, Origen, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Cyril of Alexandria, Leontius of Byzantium, John of Damascus, Fifth Ecumenical Council). Perichoresis ("total penetration" or "thorough penetration," Stoic term for mixture) used for interchange of properties (examples in Gregories, pseudo-Cyril, Maximus, John Damascene). Mystery remains due to essential differences from human soul-body union. Unorthodox uses (Apollinaris and later Monophysites apply "predominance" differently, denying full human nature; Nestorius uses "composition"/synapheia for union of two persons/natures). Duality of wills and operations (connected logically/historically to two natures; Monenergist and Monothelete controversies; Sophronius, Sergius, Honorius, Maximus Confessor vs. Pyrrhus; prohibition of "one will" and "two wills" expressions).

**Part Four: The Anathematized**
- Gnosticism: "Verbal Christianizing of Paganism"—pagan syncretisms Christianized into new type of Christianity whose exponents are Gnostics. Detailed reconstruction of systems (Cerinthus: three tiers of supramundane beings, preexistent Christ/Holy Spirit identification in second tier, creator in third identified with OT God; Ebionism of his Christology; Simon Magus: similar but two in second tier; other systems classified by number of supramundane beings as one/two/three/four Gods or natures, prolation, Christology as Ebionitic/Docetic/Sidereal Monophysitism or Polyphysitism reducible to Dyophysitism). Not a true philosophy (Fathers trace elements to Democritus/Epicurus/Plato/Heraclitus/Aristotle/Empedocles but distinguish; conclusion: Gnosticism is not philosophy).
- Heresies: Logical basis in difficulties inherent in catholic beliefs about preexistent Christ (to God) and born Christ (to God), leading to heresies and statutory Catholicism replacing consentaneous catholicism. With regard to preexistent Christ: Modalism (Sabellius et al.; one thing of many persons) and Creationalism (Arius; three generically different ousiai/hypostases without underlying unity). With regard to born Christ: neo-Ebionism and neo-Docetism. Mixed cases (Apollinaris/Eutychians orthodox on Trinity but neo-Docetist on Christology; Theodotus/Nestorius orthodox on Trinity but neo-Ebionite on Christology; Sabellius case). Conclusion on the heresies.

## Relation to Existing Wiki and Additions from This Source

Wolfson’s work is the classic, comprehensive treatment of the "philosophy of the Church Fathers" as the creation of a systematic Christian philosophy parallel to Philo’s Jewish version. It complements and deepens the recent Stead *Philosophy in Christian Antiquity* ingest (more concise overview with strong Augustine focus and semantic precision; Wolfson is more exhaustive on development from Philo/NT, allegorical classifications, single vs. double faith theories, detailed physical union analogies for the Incarnation with "predominance" and perichoresis as key, and the "Anathematized" heresies as arising from the same problems).

Specific contributions:
- Philo as the direct foundation/prolegomenon for patristic philosophy (allegory, scriptural presuppositions, subordination of philosophy, Logos, ideas in the mind/Logos).
- Detailed tracing of allegory’s evolution and classifications (corporeal/psychical/spiritual; terms from Philo).
- Faith and Reason: precise single-faith (Tertullian, Origen) vs. double-faith (Clement’s gnosis/scientific faith as demonstrated assent; Augustine’s equality and permissibility) theories.
- Trinity: NT origins of tripartite formula via Paul’s Spirit = preexistent Christ identification; twofold vs. single stage generation of the Logos; eternal generation (continuous + from eternity; by will or nature debates); fixed Cappadocian (Basil) vocabulary of hypostasis (individual) vs. ousia (common substratum/genus); homoousios interpretations and suspicions.
- Logos and Platonic Ideas: which Plato interpretation the Fathers followed (often Philonic transcendent in Logos).
- Incarnation: "mystery" despite physical analogies; "predominance" (Aristotelian soul-body/form-matter) as the orthodox solution for two natures/one person (detailed use by Tertullian, Origen, Gregories, Augustine, Cyril, John Damascene, Council); perichoresis as Stoic-derived total penetration for property interchange; unorthodox applications; connected wills/operations controversies (Monothelete etc.).
- Heresies: Gnosticism as pagan syncretism (detailed tier systems, not philosophy); Modalism (Sabellius), Arian Creationalism for Trinity; neo-Ebionite (Theodotus, Nestorius) and neo-Docetist (Apollinaris) for Christology; logical link to orthodox solutions.
