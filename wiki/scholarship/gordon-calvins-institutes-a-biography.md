# Bruce Gordon, *John Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion: A Biography*

A short **reception history** of [[institutes-of-the-christian-religion|Calvin's *Institutes*]] — a
"biography" not of the reformer but of *the book*, in the same Princeton series as
[[lawrence-quran-a-biography|Lawrence's *Qur'an: A Biography*]]. Gordon traces the work from its
composition (1536–1559) through four and a half centuries of being claimed, weaponised, ignored, and
reclaimed, across Europe, America, South Africa, and China. It is the wiki's anchor for the
*Institutes* as a text and for the **composition and reception history** of Reformed theology's
foundational compendium.

Gordon's governing thesis: **Calvin is not "Calvinism."** The historical reformer and the diverse
movement bearing his name are routinely conflated, by admirers and detractors alike, and much
popular "Calvin" (predestinarian rigorism, the Protestant work ethic, theocracy, sexual repression)
owes more to the seventeenth-century Synod of Dort and to later interpreters than
to the pulpits of Geneva. "Calvin never saw a tulip in his life." A second thesis: the *Institutes*
was **not one book but a twenty-year project**, evolving through successive Latin and French
editions, each expressing the historical moment that produced it; the famous 1559 Latin edition has
unjustly eclipsed the earlier ones.

## Source's own framing and method

Gordon is explicit that "bean counting of references" is a poor measure of influence: Jonathan
Edwards and the Synod of Dort barely cite Calvin, yet the Westminster Assembly cited
him constantly. He reads the *Institutes* as a **rhetorical and transformative** work in the mould of
a sermon — aiming not at intellectual assent to a few propositions but at the transformation of the
whole person toward union with Christ (a reading congenial to this wiki's hermeneutical-tracking
remit). He repeatedly cautions against treating the book as a "textbook" or a closed "system."
