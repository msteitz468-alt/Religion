# Adler — *Reconstructing the Confucian Dao: Zhu Xi's Appropriation of Zhou Dunyi* (SUNY, 2014)

Joseph Alan Adler's study is the fullest treatment in English of how [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] (1130–1200)
constructed [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] (1017–1073) as the founder of the Cheng-Zhu school of
[[neo-confucianism|Daoxue]], and why. It contains the only complete English translation of the
*Tongshu* with Zhu Xi's published commentary (Ch. 7) and the key editorial postfaces (Ch. 8).

## Central Argument

The conventional account says Zhu Xi elevated Zhou Dunyi because Zhou's *taiji* (Supreme Polarity)
concept provided the cosmological link between *qi* and *li* that the Cheng brothers' philosophy needed.
Adler accepts this but calls it insufficient. The **deeper reason**: Zhou's theory of the
**interpenetration of activity and stillness** — stated most concisely in Tongshu section 16 — provided
the *religious/practical* grounding for Zhu Xi's resolution of his decade-long spiritual crisis of the
1160s. Zhu Xi is better compared to **Benedict of Nursia** (primarily concerned with facilitating
religious practice, building a structure for sagehood-cultivation) than to Thomas Aquinas (the
scholastic synthesizer).

The construction of Zhou Dunyi as the link in the [[daotong|daotong]] ("succession of the Way") was
**nearly entirely Zhu Xi's retrospective invention** — completed approximately 100 years after Zhou
died — not a recognition of existing fame.

## Structure

- **Ch. 1**: Zhou Dunyi's obscurity in his own time; his texts; the Daoist origin of the Taiji Diagram
  (chain: Chen Tuan → Chong Fang → Mu Xiu → Zhou Dunyi's circles); Zhu Xi's denial of this origin.
- **Ch. 2**: Zhu Xi's campaign to elevate Zhou Dunyi into the daotong; the 22 pieces written
  1169–1196; the political implications of challenging imperial-lineage *zheng tong* logic.
- **Ch. 3**: The **intellectual-spiritual biography** of Zhu Xi; his three stages in resolving the
  *zhong/he* (centrality/harmony) problem; the 1169 "ji-chou enlightenment"; how Tongshu section 16
  provided the cosmological verification of his solution.
- **Ch. 4**: Translation and annotation of the *Taijitu shuo* (Discussion of the Supreme Polarity
  Diagram) with Zhu Xi's full published commentary and additional comments from the *Yulei*.
- **Chs. 5–6**: Annotated translation of the complete *Tongshu* (unannotated text, sections 1–40),
  with Zhu Xi's published commentary and additional comments.
- **Ch. 7**: Full annotated Tongshu translation with commentary, Parts I and II.
- **Ch. 8**: Zhu Xi's two postfaces (1169 and 1179) and note on his *Taijitu shuo* commentary.

## Key Findings

### Translation Choices
Adler's most important terminological departure from the secondary literature:

- **Taiji** = "**Supreme Polarity**" (NOT "Supreme Ultimate"). *Ji* (極) is the ridgepole of a roof:
  both the highest point AND the pivot-junction dividing the roof into complementary halves. The term
  names the **principle of yin-yang bipolarity**, not a supreme monad above the polarities.
- **Wuji** = "**Nonpolar**" (NOT "without polarity" in a simple sense). *Wuji er taiji* = "Nonpolar
  and yet Supreme Polarity!" — the paradoxical opening of the *Taijitu shuo*, not a contradiction but
  a statement that the ultimate is undifferentiated yet the very principle of differentiation.
- **Cheng** = "**being authentic**" (NOT "sincerity"). *Shi li* = actualized/real principle; the
  complete actualization of one's moral nature.
- **Daoxue** = "**Learning of the Way**" — the movement's self-designation. "Neo-Confucianism" is
  Adler's preferred Western shorthand but he notes its problems.

### Zhou Dunyi's Texts and Their Origins
- Zhou Dunyi wrote **two texts**: the *Taijitu shuo* (one-page discussion of the diagram) and the
  *Tongshu* (40 short sections on sagehood and moral cultivation).
- The **Taiji Diagram** was almost certainly received from Daoist sources:
  Chen Tuan (d. 989) → Chong Fang (965–1015) → Mu Xiu (979–1032) → Zhou Dunyi's circles.
  Multiple similar diagrams exist in the Daoist Canon (compiled Ming dynasty).
  The familiar modern yin-yang swirl first appears c. 1370 (Zhao Huiqian).
- Zhu Xi **denied this origin** to protect Zhou's position in the daotong succession.
- Zhou had minimal fame in his own lifetime; Cheng Hao's famous characterization —
  "chanting of the wind and fondling of the moon" — suggests an aesthetically oriented person,
  not a revered master.

### Zhu Xi's Three Stages (Zhong/He Problem)
The *weifa/yifa* (unexpressed/expressed phases of mind) problem from *Zhongyong* 1:

1. **1153–1163** (under Li Tong): quiet-sitting; emphasis on the still, unexpressed phase of mind;
   *weifa* = human nature, *yifa* = mind.
2. **1167–1168** (persuaded by Zhang Shi / Hunan school): genuine sagehood requires active engagement;
   perfect stillness is one-sided and verges on Chan Buddhism.
3. **1169 "ji-chou enlightenment"**: **interpenetration** — stillness within activity, activity within
   stillness, joined by *jing* (reverent composure). Zhang Zai's formula: "The mind connects the nature
   and the feelings." The *ti-yong* (substance-function) rubric applied to the two phases.

This is the resolution Zhu Xi then found confirmed in Tongshu section 16: "That which has no activity
in activity, and no stillness in stillness, is spirit."

### Daotong Construction
Full lineage as constructed by Zhu Xi:  
Fuxi → Shennong → Huangdi → Yao/Shun/Yu → King Tang → Kings Wen, Wu, and Duke of Zhou →
Confucius → Yan Hui → Zengzi → Zisi → Mencius → **[1,400-year gap]** → **Zhou Dunyi** →
Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi → (implicitly) Zhu Xi.

Hu Hong (1106–1161) was the first to connect Zhou Dunyi to the Chengs, claiming Zhou "revealed
Mencius' untransmitted learning to the Chengs." Zhu Xi systematized and greatly expanded this claim.
The 1169 and 1179 postfaces are the key documentary evidence.

### The Tongshu
Central concept: **cheng** (being authentic) = the foundation of sagehood = actualized principle.
Key sections: 1–3 (being authentic), 4 (sagehood: authentic/spiritual/incipient), **16** (activity
and stillness — the pivot of Zhu Xi's appropriation), 20 (learning to be a sage: unity / no desire /
vacuous when still / direct in activity), 23 (Master Yan's happiness), 29 (comprehensiveness of the
sage).

Zhu Xi's equation (additional comment on section 20): "Unity" = Supreme Polarity; "vacuous when
still" = stillness of yin; "direct in activity" = activity of yang; "clear and penetrating, impartial
and all-embracing" = Five Phases.

### Buddhism Parallels
- Zhu Xi's three stages parallel the Chan dispute between **Dahui Zonggao** (koan/active engagement)
  and **Hongzhi Zhengjue** (silent illumination / *mo zhao*) — the same controversy that became
  Japanese Rinzai vs. Sōtō Zen.
- Zhu Xi studied Chan under Daoqian (student of Dahui) until age 22, then turned to Confucianism.
- Huayan Buddhist *wu-ai* (non-obstruction) — *li-shi wu-ai* and *shi-shi wu-ai* — is the structural
  parallel to Zhu Xi's *wuji-taiji* (nonpolar-yet-supremely-polar) relationship.

### Canonization Timeline
1227: Emperor Lizong ennobled Zhu Xi  
1241: Five Daoxue figures (incl. Zhou Dunyi) installed in Confucian temple  
1313 (Yuan dynasty): Zhu Xi's Four Books commentaries become basis of civil service exams  
1345: Incorporated into *Song shi* (official Song history)
