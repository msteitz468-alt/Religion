# Bruce Lawrence, *The Qur'an: A Biography*

A short, accessible **reception history** of the [[quran|Qur'an]] — a "biography" not of Muhammad
but of *the book*, traced through fifteen "vignettes," each fixed to a date, a place, and a figure or
monument that received, transmitted, or contested the text. Written for a general series (alongside
biographies of Plato's *Republic*, Darwin, and Marx), it is the wiki's anchor for the Qur'an's
**reception and translation history** and for several figures otherwise thinly covered (Ja'far
al-Sadiq, al-Tabari, Robert of Ketton).

## Organizing Idea: "A Book of Signs"

Lawrence's through-line is that the Qur'an is, in its own self-description, **a Book of Signs**
(*ayat* meaning both "verse" and "sign/miracle"), and above all a **recited, oral** book: "only
fully the Qur'an when it is recited." From this he derives a theology of translation — the written
Arabic is a "second-order" revelation relative to what Muhammad *heard*, and any translation a
"third-order" one. This frames the whole book's interest in how successive communities *re-perform*
rather than merely read the text.

His central interpretive claim is a contrast the wiki should preserve: the Qur'an is read either as
**plurivocal** (multilayered, continuously reinterpreted in history — "*A* Book of Signs") or as
**univocal** by "absolutists" who treat it as the timeless, single-meaning reflection of the
heavenly prototype (*umm al-kitab*, the "Mother of the Book" — "*the* Book of Signs"). Lawrence reads
the militant univocalism of figures like Osama bin Laden as heir to the early **Khawarij**, against
the pluralist invocation of an Imam W. D. Mohammed — both drawing on the same text.

## The Fifteen Vignettes (structure)

Grouped in five triads:

- **Arab Core** — Muhammad as merchant-messenger (619 CE) and as statesman (632 CE); the **Dome of
  the Rock** (692), whose inscriptions preserve the earliest dated Qur'anic text.
- **Early Commentaries** — **[[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]]** (760), the Shi'ite Imam-exegete;
  **[[al-tabari|al-Tabari]]** (913), the Sunni historian-exegete; **[[robert-of-ketton|Robert of
  Ketton]]** (1144), the first Latin translator.
- **Later Interpretations** — **[[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]]** (1235); **Jalal al-Din Rumi** (1270); the
  **Taj Mahal** (1636) as a built Qur'anic vision of paradise.
- **Asian Echoes** — Sir Sayyid **Ahmad Khan** (1884) and **Muhammad Iqbal**, the South Asian
  rationalist-modernist readers reconciling revelation with European science.
- **Global Accents** — Imam **W. D. Mohammed** (African-American Islam), **Osama bin Laden** (militant
  absolutism), and a sick woman who uses Qur'anic words as healing "magic" — popular, lived invocation.

## What It Adds to the Wiki

1. A **reception-history spine** for [[quran|the Qur'an text page]]: orality and recitation, the
   collection under Uthman, the seven canonical *qira'at* (fixed c. 934), and the long afterlife of
   the text in commentary, architecture, poetry, and translation.
2. The wiki's first treatment of **[[robert-of-ketton|Robert of Ketton]]** and the **Latin/Christian
   reception** of the Qur'an (Peter the Venerable, the Cluny/Toledo project, the polemical
   *Lex Mahumet pseudoprophete*) — a "hostile but engaged" translation that nonetheless relied on
   Muslim commentaries (al-Tabari, al-Tabarsi).
3. Material for **[[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]]** and the Shi'ite doctrine of the **Imam as
   authoritative interpreter** (the threefold literal/historical/allegorical reading; "a quarter of
   the Qur'an is about the Imams"; the *light* verses) — see [[shia-islam|Shia Islam]].
4. Material for **[[al-tabari|al-Tabari]]**'s *Jami' al-bayan* as the **Sunni exegetical backstop**:
   *tafsir bi'l-ma'thur*, the equation of *ta'wil* with *tafsir*, and the Clear vs. Ambiguous Signs
   distinction — the Sunni counter to the Shi'ite esoteric reading.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks Tracked

Sunni *tafsir bi'l-ma'thur* (al-Tabari) vs. Shi'ite **Imami *ta'wil*** (Ja'far al-Sadiq) vs. the
*zahir/batin* layering taken up by the Sufis ([[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]], Rumi); and, at the far end,
modern rationalist reading (Ahmad Khan, Iqbal). See [[tafsir|Tafsir]] and
[[allegorical-reading|Allegorical Reading]].
