# Al-Azami, *The History of the Qur'anic Text from Revelation to Compilation*

A **traditionalist counter-history** of the [[quran|Qur'an]]'s text, subtitled *A Comparative Study
with the Old and New Testaments* (2003), by the hadith scholar **[[al-azami|M. M. Al-Azami]]**. It is
the wiki's deliberate **Contradiction-Protocol counterpart** to
[[bell-watt-introduction-to-the-quran|Bell & Watt]]: where the Western historical-critical tradition
reconstructs a gradual, editorially shaped collection, Al-Azami argues the Qur'an was completely
written and memorized in Muhammad's own lifetime and transmitted with unbroken integrity — and that
the orientalist enterprise that says otherwise is methodologically and morally compromised.

## Organizing Structure (Three Parts)

- **Part I — The History of the Qur'anic Text** (chs. 1–14): the traditionalist account — revelation
  to [[muhammad|Muhammad]], the teaching and recording of the Qur'an in his lifetime, the **written
  compilation**, **Uthman's mushaf** as mere *standardization* of an already-fixed text, reading aids
  and Arabic paleography, the **causes of variant readings**, the **"law of witness,"** and a
  rebuttal chapter on the **"so-called mushaf of Ibn Mas'ud."**
- **Part II — The History of the Biblical Scriptures** (chs. 15–18): a polemical survey arguing the
  **Old Testament's "corruption"** and the **New Testament's anonymous, variant-ridden** transmission
  — the *comparative* move that contrasts the Qur'an's transmission favorably with the Bible's. This
  intersects the wiki's biblical text-critical pages: the [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary
  Hypothesis]] and the [[mark-ending|ending of Mark]].
- **Part III — An Appraisal of Orientalist Research** (chs. 19–21): the direct attack — "The
  Orientalist and the Qur'an," "Orientalist Motivations," and an Epilogue.

## Core Arguments

1. **The "law of witness."** Al-Azami's signature explanatory device against the critical reading of
   the collection-traditions: in the Islamic *isnad* system a text has value only when received
   *in person* from teacher to pupil with eyewitness attestation. This, he argues, is why the
   Prophet's written materials could *not* serve as a primary source for Zayd ibn Thabit, and why
   Umar feared the death of the *huffaz* at Yamama — not because the text was unrecorded, but because
   the *certified human chain* back to the Prophet was at risk. Abu Bakr therefore required **two
   witnesses** per verse. This reframes the very data Bell & Watt read as evidence of a late, uncertain
   collection.
2. **Uthman standardized, did not create.** The Uthmanic step fixed orthography and suppressed
   error-prone regional copies of an already-complete text; the variant-codex reports (Ibn Mas'ud,
   etc.) are exaggerated, misreported, or fabricated.
3. **The comparative argument.** The textual instability orientalists "find" in the Qur'an is really
   projected from the demonstrable variant-history of the Bible; the Qur'an's transmission is of a
   categorically more reliable kind.
4. **The critique of Orientalism.** He periodizes the field (Nöldeke, Goldziher, Hurgronje,
   Bergsträsser, Tisdall, Jeffery, Schacht → a post-1948 phase of Rippin, Crone, and above all
   **Wansbrough**, whose two-century "community product" thesis he treats as the most radical), and
   charges it with bias, "illegitimate sources, unsound methodology and even straightforward deceit,"
   and with "transmuting Islam into foreign idioms" (Schacht casting fiqh as Roman law; Wansbrough
   casting the Qur'an in biblical-studies categories). The book was prompted by Toby Lester's 1999
   *Atlantic* article "What Is the Koran?"
