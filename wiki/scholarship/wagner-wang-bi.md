## Source Overview

The second of three volumes in Wagner's career-long Wang Bi study (the first is *The Craft of a
Chinese Commentator: Wang Bi on the Laozi*; the third is *Language, Ontology, and Political
Philosophy in China: Wang Bi's Scholarly Exploration of the Dark*). This volume provides:

1. A critical reconstruction of the Laozi text actually used by Wang Bi (distinct from the later
   "Wang Bi Laozi Receptus")
2. A critical edition of Wang Bi's commentary
3. An **"extrapolative translation"** — a reading that makes explicit what Wang Bi's commentary
   implies for the Laozi text (bracketed supplements showing how Wang Bi construed each phrase)
4. A full translation of Wang Bi's *Laozi weizhi lüeli* (*The Structure of the Laozi's Subtle Pointers*)

## Key Finding: The Wang Bi Receptus Is Not Wang Bi's Text

The received "Wang Bi edition" of the Laozi (the standard transmitted text) is **not identical to
the Laozi text Wang Bi himself used**. In 79 passages, Wang Bi's own commentary quotes the Laozi
differently from the text printed above it. The "Wang Bi Laozi Receptus" is a later Heshang Gong
text superimposed over Wang Bi's commentary. Wagner reconstructs Wang Bi's original Laozi from:
- Wang Bi's own quotations within the commentary
- The Guodian bamboo slips (4th c. BCE) and Mawangdui manuscripts (2nd c. BCE)
- Fu Yi's and Fan Yingyuan's "Old Manuscripts"

## Wang Bi's *Laozi weizhi lüeli* (LZWZLL) — "The Structure of the Laozi's Subtle Pointers"

This is Wang Bi's own programmatic essay on how to read the Daodejing. Wagner considers it "the
most important surviving Chinese philosophic treatise of the third century." Key arguments:

### 1. The Dao as Featureless/Nameless Root
> "It is generally true with regard to that by which things are created — that [things] are
> necessarily created out of the 'featureless'; and that by which achievements are brought about —
> that [achievements] are based on the 'nameless.' The featureless and nameless is [what the Laozi
> calls] the 'ancestor of the ten thousand kinds of entities.'" (LZWZLL 1.1–6)

The Dao's **negative ontology** — being without features (*wu xing*) and without name (*wu ming*)
— is not a mere limitation but the precondition of its universality. If the Dao warmed, it could not
cool. If it sounded the note *gong*, it could not sound *shang*. Only by being featureless can it be
the root and master of ALL features.

### 2. Why the Laozi Uses "Pointers" Rather Than Definitions
The Laozi's writing strategy — formulae like "I call this X" (*wei zhi X*), sudden reversals,
paradoxes — is not accidental but philosophically necessary. The Dao **cannot be defined** (because
all definition carves out a determinate form from the undifferentiated). It can only be **pointed
at**. The 81 chapters are all "subtle pointers" toward a single unnameable center.

Wang Bi's anti-reading rule: **"He who imposes discursive analysis upon the textual patterns of
the Laozi will miss what he points at; and he who wishes to put the weight on the particular term
will deviate from the overall meaning."** The reader must perform an "implosive" reading — each
passage read in light of all others, all converging on the same center.

### 3. Polemic Against Other Schools
Wang Bi explicitly attacks readings that reduce the Laozi to:
- A Yin/Yang binary (Zhuang Zun's approach)
- School-specific doctrines (Daoist, Confucian, etc.)
- Surface verbiage (*zhangju* commentary style — paraphrasing word-by-word)

None of these "stare at the text properly"; they cling to the surface rather than following
where it points.

### 4. Political Application
Embedded in the LZWZLL is an essay on how the featureless/nameless Dao translates into
political practice. The sage-ruler embodies *wu* (non-being/featurelessness): he does not impose
a single standard on the myriad entities, and so all entities can flourish in their own nature.
Imposing one's particular will or value creates dissonance; ruling by not-imposing creates harmony.

## Wang Bi's Hermeneutical Method (per Wagner)

Wang Bi writes in **Interlocking Parallel Style (IPS)** — a rigorous binary framework where claims
are made in paired chains (a/b, a/b...) that mirror the ontological binary of being/*you* and
non-being/*wu*. Understanding Wang Bi's commentary requires recognizing this formal structure.

He uses two technical terms:
- ***ming*** (名) — definition: what something *is* (its fixed essence)
- ***cheng*** (稱) — inferred designation: an approximative name for what resists definition

The Dao can only be *cheng* (approximatively designated), never *ming* (defined).
