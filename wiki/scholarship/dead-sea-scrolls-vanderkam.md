# The Dead Sea Scrolls Today (rev. ed.) — James C. VanderKam

**Full title**: *The Dead Sea Scrolls Today*, revised edition  
**Author**: James C. VanderKam (University of Notre Dame; former member of the international Cave 4 editorial team)  
**Publisher**: Eerdmans, 2010  
**Coverage ledger**: Read in full — lines 1–2968 of the source file

## Overview

A mid-level scholarly introduction designed for educated non-specialists but grounded in VanderKam's own firsthand involvement in Scrolls editing. Seven chapters covering: discovery and archaeology, the texts themselves (chapter-by-chapter survey), the Essene identification debate, OT text and canon, NT parallels, and the publication controversy. The 2010 revised edition adds a substantial postscript to Chapter 7 updating the access controversy through c. 2009.

VanderKam's overall stance: the **Essene hypothesis** remains the most persuasive identification of the Qumran community, resting on two independent pillars — Pliny's geographic testimony and text-comparison with Josephus/Philo. Competing theories (Golb's Jerusalem library thesis, Schiffman's Sadducee theory) are examined and rejected on specific evidential grounds.

## Chapter 1 — Discovery and Archaeology

### Discovery sequence
- 1947: Muhammad edh-Dhib and associates in Cave 1; seven complete scrolls eventually sold to Sukenik and St. Mark's Monastery
- 1949–56: Caves 2–10 found; most by controlled excavation; Cave 4 the mother lode (c. 15,000 fragments, 550+ texts)
- 1951–58: Khirbet Qumran site excavated by de Vaux; 11 seasons total

### Magness revision of de Vaux
De Vaux's phasing: Phase Ia (pre-communal) → Phase I (sectarian, c. 100 BCE) → earthquake gap → Phase II → Roman destruction 68 CE.

**Jodi Magness's revision** (published 1992; not yet in 1994 first edition but incorporated by VanderKam): No Phase Ia is identifiable. The gap between Phase I and Phase II is likely **months, not 27 years** — de Vaux assumed a 31 BCE earthquake damaged the buildings so severely that they were abandoned until Herod's death in 4 BCE; Magness found no reason for such a long gap. The earthquake debris was cleared and rebuilt quickly. This eliminates a puzzling 27-year silence in community history.

Other Magness corrections: the cylindrical jars used to store scrolls are a uniquely Qumran type; the presence of a scriptorium (*locus 30*) remains probable; the inkwells and writing tables are consistent with scribal activity at the site.

### Site as library and settlement
VanderKam endorses reading the site as a **sectarian settlement** with a scriptorium, ritual baths, communal dining room, and cemetery. Against Golb's "library only" thesis: the consistent Essene theological profile of texts written at the site demonstrates in-house composition, not wholesale import from Jerusalem libraries.

## Chapter 2 — The Texts

### Major text groups
VanderKam provides a systematic survey across:
- **Biblical manuscripts** (all OT books except Esther; Isaiah most extensively attested: 2 complete scrolls + 20+ fragments)
- **Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha**: Tobit (both Hebrew and Aramaic), Jubilees (15+ copies), 1 Enoch (Aramaic fragments minus the Similitudes), Sirach
- **Sectarian texts**: Community Rule (1QS + Cave 4 copies), Damascus Document, War Scroll, Thanksgiving Hymns, Temple Scroll, MMT, Angelic Liturgy
- **Pesharim**: Habakkuk (most complete), Nahum, Isaiah pesher, Psalms commentary, Hosea commentary
- **Calendrical texts** (mishmarot): The festival calendar corpus — see Calendar section below
- **Other notable texts**: 4Q246 (Aramaic Apocalypse / "Son of God"), 4Q285 (Rule of War = "Pierced Messiah"), Copper Scroll, Genesis Apocryphon

### Cave 4 variants of the Community Rule
VanderKam specifically notes that **4Q259 (copy e)** of the Community Rule lacks:
- The **Two Spirits section** (1QS III–IV), suggesting it was an insertion into the parent text
- The **Two Messiahs formula** ("Messiahs of Aaron and Israel," 1QS 9.11), replaced by different content

This confirms the Community Rule underwent **textual development** and did not exist in a fixed, uniform form throughout the community's history. The theological weight placed on 1QS 9.11 for the Two Messiahs doctrine is thus qualified: that formula is absent from at least one copy.

### Paraphrases vs. pesharim
VanderKam refines the taxonomy of biblical commentary at Qumran, distinguishing "**paraphrases**" (4Q123, 4Q127, 4Q382, 4Q422) from the pesharim proper. The paraphrases rewrite biblical narratives without the *pesher* formula ("its interpretation concerns…"). See [[pesher]].

## Chapter 3 — The Community and Its Practices

### Essene identification: the two pillars
1. **Pliny the Elder** (*Natural History* V.73): places the Essenes on the western shore of the Dead Sea, north of En-Gedi, in a community of men who live without women. The geographic precision matches Khirbet Qumran almost exactly.
2. **Text-comparison with Josephus/Philo**: The Community Rule's organization (the Many, the Overseer, three-year probation, communal property, daily bathing, white garments, communal meals) matches Josephus's *War* II description point for point.

### Two types of Essene communities
VanderKam is more careful than Vermes on this: Josephus distinguishes **two orders of Essenes** — one celibate, one marrying. The Damascus Document's **camp communities** (with families, wives, children) and the Qumran settlement's apparently celibate male community map onto these two types. Both orders are properly "Essene"; the Qumran settlement represents the celibate subset. This is not a contradiction within the sources but a real institutional diversity.

### Calendar: solar and lunar together
VanderKam's important correction to simplified accounts:

The Qumran community used **both** a **364-day solar calendar** AND a **schematic (non-observational) lunar calendar** — coordinated via the **mishmarot** (priestly rotation) texts. Key facts:
- The **solar year** = 364 days (52 weeks exactly); every festival falls on the same weekday every year
- A **schematic lunar year** = 354 days; Qumran used a theoretical lunar calendar (not observation-based), running alongside the solar calendar
- The **mishmarot** texts show coordinated priestly rotations integrating solar years, lunar months, and sabbatical years into a grand 6-year cycle

**Implication**: Earlier descriptions (including in [[dead-sea-scrolls-vermes]]) that present only the solar calendar as the Qumran calendar are incomplete. The lunar calendar was real but was not the cultic lunisolar calendar of the Jerusalem Temple — it was a schematic system running in parallel. See [[two-spirits-doctrine|Qumran calendar debate]].

## Chapter 4 — Who Wrote the Scrolls? The Sectarian Theories

### The Essene hypothesis defended
VanderKam rehearses the Essene case (above) and examines alternatives:

**Golb's Jerusalem library thesis**: All the scrolls are from multiple Jerusalem libraries, moved to Qumran for safekeeping before the 70 CE revolt. Refuted by VanderKam:
- Consistent *Essene theological profile* throughout the corpus — particularly predestinarian dualism, solar calendar, anti-Jerusalem-Temple polemic — is impossible for a random cross-section of Jerusalem libraries
- Pliny explicitly locates a living Essene community at this geographic location
- The buildings show scribal and sectarian use, not just a storage depot

**Schiffman's Sadducee theory**: The *halakhic* positions of MMT align with some Sadducaean legal rulings; therefore the community was Sadducean. Refuted by VanderKam:
- The Scrolls are thoroughly **predestinarian** (Two Spirits doctrine) — Sadducees explicitly rejected divine determinism (Josephus)
- The Scrolls have extensive, positive **angelology** — Sadducees denied angels (Acts 23:8)
- The Scrolls expect a **resurrection of the dead** (4Q521) — Sadducees denied resurrection (Matt. 22:23)
- Legal overlap does not establish identity; both Essenes and Sadducees may have shared certain priestly-legal traditions without being the same group

### Groningen Hypothesis
**Florentino García Martínez** (Groningen) proposed that the "**Wicked Priest**" designation does not refer to a single individual but to **an office** — six successive Hasmonaean high priests, each of whom fell into the role of Wicked Priest at various moments in relation to the community. This explains why the Habakkuk Commentary's descriptions of the Wicked Priest seem to describe different individuals and situations.

VanderKam notes the hypothesis's attractions but also **implausible elements**: the pesher texts seem to use "the Wicked Priest" (*ha-Kohen ha-Rasha'*) in a relatively consistent narrative about one individual's career — his rise to power, corruption, persecution of the Teacher, defeat, and death. Multiple referents make this narrative incoherent. VanderKam does not adopt the Groningen Hypothesis but treats it as a live scholarly debate. See [[wicked-priest-identity]].

## Chapter 5 — The Scrolls and the Old Testament

### Three textual theories for OT text families
VanderKam surveys the scholarly debate on OT textual diversity:

1. **Cross (local texts/geographic families)**: Three text families corresponding to geographic centers — Babylonian (proto-MT), Palestinian (LXX Vorlage), Egyptian (Samaritan Pentateuch type). Cross finds evidence at Qumran for all three families.

2. **Tov (five text groups with percentages)**:
   - Proto-Masoretic texts (c. 60%): matching the later MT
   - Pre-Samaritan texts (c. 5%)
   - Septuagintal texts (c. 5%): closer to LXX Vorlage
   - Non-aligned texts (c. 20%): not consistently close to any known text tradition
   - Qumran practice texts (c. 10%): distinctive scribal features, freer treatment of text

3. **Ulrich (successive literary editions)**: Emphasizes that the textual diversity reflects different *editions* of biblical books, not mere copying variants — some books (Jeremiah, Joshua, Samuel) underwent deliberate literary reworking at different stages, and Qumran preserves both earlier and later editions. This is a more radical claim than Cross or Tov: the OT books were still being editorially revised during the Qumran period.

### Canon at Qumran
VanderKam argues the Qumranites had **no closed canon**. The evidence:
- 15+ copies of Jubilees, explicitly cited as Scripture in the Damascus Document as "the Book of the Divisions of the Times into their Jubilees and Weeks" — making Jubilees quasi-canonical alongside Torah and Prophets
- Aramaic Enoch fragments from multiple copies — the Enochic literature had authoritative status
- The absence of the Similitudes from Qumran (present only in the Ethiopic tradition) raises the question whether the **Book of Giants** occupied the slot of the missing Similitudes in the Qumran "Enoch collection"
- Canon formation (by the evidence of Luke 24:44, 4 Ezra 14, Josephus *Against Apion* 1.37-43) was not complete even in broader Judaism until after the 1st century CE

## Chapter 6 — The Scrolls and the New Testament

### Methodology and scholarly history
Dupont-Sommer (early 1950s) and Edmund Wilson overclaimed: the Teacher of Righteousness "foreshadows" Jesus; the scrolls are damaging to Christianity's uniqueness. VanderKam insists on moderation: parallels show the Jewish matrix of Christian origins, not direct dependence or identity.

### Linguistic parallels
- ***ha-Rabbim*** ("the Many") → NT "the congregation" / "the assembly"
- ***mevaqqer*** ("Overseer") → *episkopos* ("bishop/overseer" in 1 Tim 3; Acts 20:28)
- **Beliar/Belial** → 2 Cor 6:15 "what accord has Christ with Belial?"
- **"Sons of Light"** → Luke 16:8; John 12:36; 1 Thess 5:5; Eph 5:8

### 7Q5 and O'Callaghan
José O'Callaghan (1972) claimed a Greek fragment from Cave 7 (7Q5) was from Mark 6:52-53. VanderKam dismisses the identification as extremely improbable: the text is too small and fragmentary to support a specific NT identification; the identification has not been accepted by the scholarly community.

### 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1
This passage — "Do not be yoked with unbelievers… come out and be separate" — contains a striking cluster of Qumran vocabulary (Belial, sons of light/darkness, separation language). VanderKam suggests it may be an Essene fragment that Paul adapted or incorporated. Source-critical questions remain open.

### John the Baptist
Isaiah 40:3 in 1QS 8.12-15 uses the passage to justify Qumran's wilderness retreat: "Prepare in the wilderness the way of the Lord." All four Gospels apply the same verse to John the Baptist. VanderKam notes the parallel without claiming John was an Essene — the exegesis is shared; the institutional connection is not established.

### 4Q521 (Messianic Apocalypse) and NT miracle lists
**Critical VanderKam correction**: In 4Q521, the sequence "heal the wounded, revive the dead, bring good news to the poor" is performed by **God (the Lord)**, not by the messiah. Luke 7:22 (// Matt 11:4-5) parallels this list of miraculous deeds as a response to John's question about Jesus — but in 4Q521 the agent is God, not an anointed figure. Both texts draw on Isa 35:5-6 and Isa 61:1. The unique addition in both (raising the dead before bringing good news to poor) suggests a shared interpretive tradition. See [[dead-sea-scrolls-vermes]] for Vermes's account, which does not clearly distinguish the divine vs. messianic agent. See also [[two-messiahs]].

### Messianic banquet (1QSa 2.11-22)
VanderKam quotes the messianic banquet passage at length. Key Schiffman interpretation: the Qumran regular meals (with at least 10 men, priest leading, blessing of bread and wine) were **noncultic pre-enactments** of the eschatological banquet. The messianic banquet statute explicitly governs *every* meal with 10 or more men (1QSa 2.21-22). Parallels to NT eucharistic practice: messianic character, bread and wine, repeated regularly, explicitly eschatological.

### Annie Jaubert's calendar hypothesis
Jaubert (1953ff.) proposed that the synoptic/Johannine discrepancy on the Last Supper date (synoptics: Passover; John: eve of Passover) is explained by the Qumran solar calendar: the Synoptics used the solar calendar, John used the official lunisolar calendar. VanderKam finds the hypothesis **attractive but flawed**: the two calendars rarely aligned in the required way; there is no evidence Jesus or early Christians used the Qumran solar calendar.

### 4Q246 (Aramaic Apocalypse) — "Son of God"
The text contains "Son of God" and "Son of the Most High" titles for an unnamed figure alongside a prediction of an eternal kingdom — strikingly parallel to Luke 1:32-35 (the Annunciation). VanderKam notes scholarly disagreement: does the figure represent a messianic savior, or a wicked ruler who falsely claims divine titles? **VanderKam's own view**: the latter seems more likely, which limits the Christological parallel.

### 4Q285 (Rule of War) — the "Pierced Messiah" controversy
Robert Eisenman (1991) read 4Q285 as saying "they killed the Prince of the Congregation, the Branch of David" — a text supposedly suppressed because it paralleled the crucifixion. VanderKam's analysis:
- The text echoes Isaiah 11:1-5 throughout; Isa 11:4 says the Davidic figure "shall kill the wicked"
- Line 4 should be translated: "the prince of the congregation, the branch of David **will kill** him" — the messiah is the one doing the killing, not being killed
- Syntactic and exegetical reasons make Eisenman's reading "quite unlikely"
- No Vatican conspiracy; the text is a straightforward eschatological war text

### Shared eschatological exegesis
Both Qumran pesharim and early Christian texts (Acts 2:15-17, quoting Joel) apply biblical prophecy to their own present as the eschatological fulfillment. Paul's use of Hab 2:4 (Gal 3:11; Rom 1:17) vs. 1QpHab VIII:1-3: same proof-text, radically different interpretations — Paul's "faith" as trust in God's promises vs. 1QpHab's "faithfulness to the Teacher of Righteousness." Both, however, identify the verse's *faith/faithfulness* as the mechanism of deliverance.

### VanderKam's conclusion on NT parallels
Christianity's uniqueness "lies less in its communal practices and eschatological expectations than in its central confession that the son of a humble woman and a carpenter from Nazareth in Galilee was indeed the messiah and son of God." The Scrolls show Christianity's Jewish matrix; they do not undermine its distinctive claims.

## Chapter 7 — The Publication Controversy

### The problem
Cave 4 yielded c. 15,000 fragments; original 7-person team (Cross, Milik, Allegro, Starcky, Skehan, Strugnell, Hunzinger) appointed 1953-54, with no new scholars added for decades. The original arrangement gave each editor exclusive publication rights to their assigned texts — a monopoly structure with no enforced timeline.

### Key milestones and episodes
- **1956**: All Cave 1 texts published; Caves 2-3, 5-10 published promptly
- **1960s-1980s**: Cave 4 publication stalls; only 7 DJD volumes published 1955-82
- **1987**: Strugnell becomes chief editor; begins enlarging team (VanderKam himself added 1989, receiving texts from Milik)
- **Nov 1990**: Strugnell interview in Ha'aretz; calls Judaism "a horrible religion"; removed from editorship December 1990
- **1990**: Emanuel Tov appointed editor-in-chief; team eventually expanded to c. 50 members
- **Sept 4, 1991**: Wacholder and Abegg (Hebrew Union College) publish *A Preliminary Edition of the Unpublished Dead Sea Scrolls* — reconstructed from the pre-1960 concordance using reverse-engineering
- **Sept 22, 1991**: Huntington Library (San Marino, CA) announces it will open its complete photographic archive of the Scrolls to all researchers; IAA protests but eventually acquiesces
- **Nov 1, 1991**: Eisenman announces discovery of the "Pierced Messiah Text" (4Q285) via a Huntington Library photograph; VanderKam's detailed refutation above
- **Nov 19, 1991**: Eisenman and Robinson publish *A Facsimile Edition* with 1,785 photographs (including a Hebrew transcription of 4QMMT without crediting Qimron)
- **1991**: Baigent and Leigh, *The Dead Sea Scroll Deception* — Vatican conspiracy theory, Catholic team suppressing anti-Christian scrolls; VanderKam calls this "bizarre" and notes that full publication refuted every conspiratorial claim
- **1993**: IAA grants all four photographic centers the right to make photographs available to researchers; Brill publishes *Dead Sea Scrolls on Microfiche* (~6,000 photographs of all Qumran texts)
- **1994**: DJD 10 (official 4QMMT edition) published
- **4QMMT copyright case**: Israeli courts ruled Qimron's transcription was copyrightable; Shanks/Eisenman/Robinson ordered to pay damages (NIS 100,000+ plus fees); upheld on appeal in 2000; García Martínez later showed Strugnell had substantially completed the text by 1961, 24 years before Qimron began

### Postscript (2010 update)
By 2010 (December 2009 writing): **all Dead Sea Scrolls have been published** — DJD volumes 8-31 and 33-40 published 1990-2010 (39 volumes total plus indexes); the final DJD volume (32, the two Isaiah scrolls from Cave 1) was at press. The SBL (1991) and ASOR (1992) issued policy statements on scholarly access to discovered texts, prompted by the Qumran experience. Multi-spectral imaging (MSI) has made previously illegible fragments readable.

## Key Corrections and Nuances vs. Vermes

| Topic | Vermes position | VanderKam correction/nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Calendar | 364-day solar calendar | Both solar and schematic lunar calendars used; mishmarot texts show integrated coordination |
| 4Q521 agent | Implies messianic figure | **God** (not the messiah) heals the wounded and revives the dead |
| Wicked Priest | Jonathan Maccabaeus (one person) | Groningen Hypothesis: possibly 6 successive Hasmonean high priests; VanderKam skeptical but acknowledges debate |
| Magness archaeology | Follows de Vaux phasing | No Phase Ia; gap between I and II is months not 27 years |
| 1QS textual tradition | Single stable text implied | 4Q259 (copy e) lacks both Two Spirits section and 9.11 Two Messiahs formula |
| Book of Giants | Separate text | May have occupied the slot of the missing Similitudes in Qumran's Enoch collection |
| Jubilees citation | Authoritative text (from DSS fragments) | Damascus Document explicitly calls it "the Book of the Divisions of the Times" — closest to Scripture citation |
