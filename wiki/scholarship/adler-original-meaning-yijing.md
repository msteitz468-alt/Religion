# Adler (trans./ed.) — *The Original Meaning of the Yijing* (Columbia, 2020)

This is the first complete Western-language translation of [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] (1130–1200)
*Zhouyi benyi* (周易本義, "The Original Meaning of the Zhouyi"), completed in 1188. The translator
is Joseph A. Adler, Professor Emeritus of Religious Studies at Kenyon College and the leading
English-language authority on Zhu Xi and the Neo-Confucian Yijing tradition. It is published in
Columbia University Press's "Translations from the Asian Classics" series (2020, ISBN
9780231191241).

The *Zhouyi benyi* contains (a) Zhu Xi's nine preparatory diagrams with their explanations;
(b) his preface from the lost earlier commentary (1177); (c) full commentary on all 64 hexagrams
(including hexagram statements, line statements, Tuanzhuan, Xiangzhuan, and Wenyan); (d) commentary
on the Xici zhuan (Treatise on the Appended Remarks, Parts A and B); (e) commentary on the Shuogua
zhuan (Treatise Discussing the Trigrams); (f) commentary on the Zagua zhuan (Commentary on Assorted
Hexagrams); and (g) the Shiyi (Divination Ritual). The Xugua (Sequence of Hexagrams) is the only
appendix omitted — the only one for which Zhu Xi himself provides no commentary.

## Adler's Introduction: Key Claims

### Historical Background of the Yijing

- The **Zhouyi** (Zhou Changes) = the Bronze Age core, probably 11th–9th c. BCE: 64 hexagrams +
  hexagram statements (guaci, attributed to King Wen) + line statements (yaoci, attributed to the
  Duke of Zhou).
- The **Ten Wings** (Shiyí) = seven philosophical appendixes added c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE, traditionally
  attributed to Confucius; modern scholarship rejects this attribution.
- The **Yijing** = the inclusive title for Zhouyi + all appendixes.
- From Han Wudi (r. 140–87 BCE) the Yijing was the first of the Five Classics in the official
  curriculum.

### Two Interpretive Schools

- **Xiangshu** (Image and Number): focuses on the graphic elements — yin/yang line positions, the
  Eight Trigrams' symbolic correlates, numerological diagrams (Hetu / Luoshu, Fuxi sequences). Han
  masters: Jiao Yanshou, Meng Xi, Jing Fang, Yu Fan. Song representative: **Shao Yong** (1012–1077).
- **Yili** (Meaning and Principle): focuses on the written text for moral guidance; the image is
  "the snare" — once you've caught the rabbit (the idea), forget the snare. **Wang Bi** (226–249)
  introduced this approach; Wang's commentary was enshrined in the Tang official canon. **Cheng Yi**
  (1033–1107) followed Wang Bi and made it central to Song Neo-Confucian practice.

### Zhu Xi's Hermeneutical Innovation

Zhu Xi's fundamental hermeneutic principle: **"The Yi was originally created for divination"**
(*Yi ben wei bushi er zuo*). Announced 1175 in a letter to Zhang Shi; maintained throughout the
*Yulei* (Classified Conversations, ~11% on the Yi).

This claim has three implications:

1. **The "original meaning"** (*benyi*) is Fuxi's intention in creating the hexagram system — as a
   method by which ordinary people (not only literate *junzi*) can access the cosmic-moral order
   through divination.
2. **Against Wang Bi and Cheng Yi**: since the hexagram statements are "based simply on the images,"
   the yin-yang structural analysis of lines must precede the textual moral reading — not be replaced
   by it.
3. **Universal scope**: Fuxi's Yi was created for "scholars, farmers, artisans, and merchants" —
   everyone can use it in divination; each gets the meaning appropriate to their station and
   circumstance. "If this sort of person divines, he will make this sort of use of it."

Zhu Xi accordingly synthesizes xiangshu (from Shao Yong) and yili (from Cheng Yi): his commentary
routinely begins with the yin-yang structural analysis of a hexagram (which trigrams, which line
occupies which position, which lines are "central and correct") and only then derives the moral
implication.

His hermeneutic is what Adler calls "editorial conservatism + hermeneutic activism": by keeping
the commentary minimal and brief, Zhu Xi forces the diviner into an active encounter with the text
— the "original meaning" can only emerge in the reader's personal engagement with the text through
divination, not in a comprehensive exegesis.

### Fuxi and the "Succession of the Way" (Daotong)

Zhu Xi regarded **Fuxi** as the first Confucian Sage — the person who first intuited the linkage
between the natural order (*tianli*) and the moral order (*daoli*) and embodied it in the hexagram
system. The Yijing is therefore not only a text about moral cultivation but the *first event* in the
[[daotong|daotong]] (Succession of the Way) — the tradition running from Fuxi through Yao, Shun,
King Wen, the Duke of Zhou, and Confucius (the Yi's Ten Wings), and then through Mencius, Zhou Dunyi,
the Cheng brothers, and finally Zhu Xi himself.

### The Zhouyi benyi: Textual History

- Completed 1188; Zhu Xi's second Yi commentary (the first, *Yizhuan* [1177], is now lost except its
  preface, which Adler translates).
- Zhu Xi restored the **original arrangement** of the text (appendixes separate from hexagrams),
  deliberately reversing Wang Bi's collated arrangement. Most editions — including the two in the
  *Siku quanshu* — followed Wang Bi's collated format. Adler follows the collated arrangement
  as more readable.
- The **Nine Diagrams** prefaced to the text (including the Hetu/Luoshu, Fuxi's Sequence, King Wen's
  Sequence, and the Hexagram Fluctuation diagram) were probably added by Zhu Xi himself.
- Between 1265 and 1918, at least 30 editions of the *Zhouyi benyi* were published in China alone.

## Zhu Xi's Commentary: Method in Practice

### Structure of Each Hexagram Entry

Under each hexagram Zhu Xi provides (in the collated arrangement Adler uses):

1. **Commentary on the hexagram statement** (*guaci*): identifies which Sage composed this level;
   analyzes the yin-yang structure; identifies the "ruling line" (*zhugua zhi zhu*); states the
   prognostication for the diviner.
2. **Commentary on each line statement** (*yaoci*): identifies the line's position (correct/incorrect,
   central/peripheral), its correspondence with other lines, and derives the prognostication.
3. **Commentary on the Tuanzhuan** (Commentary on the Judgments).
4. **Commentary on the Xiangzhuan** — Larger Image (the combined trigrams) and Smaller Image (each
   individual line).
5. **Commentary on the Wenyan** (for hexagrams 1–2 only).

### Key Technical Terms Used

- **Central and Correct** (*zhong zheng*): a line is "central" if it occupies position 2 or 5 (the
  midpoints of the component trigrams); "correct" if a yang line is in an odd-numbered position or
  a yin in even-numbered. A line that is both central and correct is most auspicious.
- **Prognostication** (*zhan*): when Zhu Xi says "the prognostication is X," he means: when the
  diviner receives this hexagram and this line is changing, the oracle says X.
- **Hexagram fluctuation** (*guabian*): the derivation of one hexagram from another through changing
  lines; Zhu Xi uses this for 19 specific hexagrams.
- **Ruling line** (*zhugua zhi zhu*): the one line that most clearly embodies the hexagram's meaning.

### Sample: Hexagram 1 (Qian, Creating)

The hexagram statement (*yuan heng li zhen*, "Supreme and penetrating, appropriate and correct"):
Zhu Xi: *yuan* = "great and beginning"; *heng* = "penetrating"; *li* = "appropriate"; *zhen* =
"correct." These are the Four Virtues of Heaven's Way. The prognostication: a diviner who gets this
hexagram finds that the course of action is greatly penetrating — but only if solidly correct.

Line 2 (dragon appearing in the field): "firm and creative, central and correct, emerging from hiding
... therefore the image is a dragon appearing in the field, and the prognostication is appropriate
to see the great person." The structural fact (firm yang in the second, correct-but-not-central) and
the image (the dragon's emergence) together ground the oracular pronouncement.

## The Xici zhuan: Key Philosophical Passages

The *Xici* (Treatise on the Appended Remarks) is the most philosophically rich of the Ten Wings and
the primary source for Song Neo-Confucian metaphysical vocabulary:

- **A.1.1**: "Heaven is honorable, Earth is lowly; thus are Qian and Kun determined." — The Yi's
  structure reflects and embodies the cosmic structure of yin-yang differentiation.
- **A.5.1**: "The alternation of yin and yang is called the Way." Zhu Xi: "Yin and yang constantly
  revolving is *qi*; its principle (*li*) is what is called the Way."
- **A.5.2**: "Carrying it out is good. Completing it is the nature." — *Sheng* (nature) = what
  things receive from Heaven; the Way is completed in the natures of individual things.
- **A.5.6**: "Generation and regeneration [*sheng sheng*] are the meaning of change." — The cosmos's
  incessant creativity is both its fundamental character and the ground of human moral creativity.
- **A.11.5**: "In Change there is the Supreme Polarity, which generates the Two Modes. The Two Modes
  generate the Four Images, and the Four Images generate the Eight Trigrams." — The foundation of
  the [[taiji|taiji]] doctrine.
- **A.12.4**: "What is above form is called the Way; what is within form is called implements." — The
  *xing er shang* / *xing er xia* distinction; the metaphysical / physical distinction in Chinese
  thought.
- **B.5**: "The Yi is without thought and without action; silent and unmoving, when stimulated it
  penetrates all circumstances under Heaven." — The Oracle as the intersection of the formless
  (*li*) and the formed (*qi*).

Zhu Xi's comments on *sheng sheng* (A.5.6) and on Fu hexagram (24) — "In Fu is seen the mind of
Heaven and Earth" — link the cosmic creativity to moral creativity: the moment when yang is
reborn at the base of pure yin is the moment of moral incipience in the human mind.

## Hexagram Fu (Return, #24) as the Theological Heart

The Fu hexagram (one yang line at the bottom, five yin above) held special significance for Zhu Xi
and the whole Cheng-Zhu school. The Tuanzhuan says: "In Fu is seen the mind of Heaven and Earth."
Zhu Xi:

> "Below the accumulated yin a single yang is reborn. 'The mind of Heaven and Earth to give birth
> to things' is incipient in extinction. Reaching this point, its return can be seen. In human
> beings it is activity at the maximum of stillness; goodness at the maximum of the bad; the original
> mind beginning to reappear just at the point of vanishing."

This connects to the [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucian]] doctrine of interpenetration of stillness
and activity, the *ji* (incipience) at which self-cultivation must operate, and the "mind of Heaven
and Earth" as the ground of moral creativity.

## The Shuogua: Trigram Symbolism

The Shuogua ("Discussion of the Trigrams") contains the canonical catalogue of the Eight Trigrams'
symbolic correlates used throughout Yijing commentary:

| Trigram | Image | Other correlates |
|---------|-------|-----------------|
| Qian ≡ | Heaven | Father, jade, round, ruler, horse |
| Kun ≡ | Earth | Mother, cloth, yellow, ox |
| Zhen ≡ | Thunder | Eldest son, dragon, black, yellow |
| Sun ≡ | Wind | Eldest daughter, wood |
| Kan ≡ | Water/Abyss | Middle son, ditches, drains |
| Li ≡ | Fire/Sun | Middle daughter, lightning, brightness |
| Gen ≡ | Mountain | Youngest son, back roads |
| Dui ≡ | Lake/Marsh | Youngest daughter |

The first three sections of the Shuogua are cosmological (similar in style to the Xici); sections
4–11 are the systematic symbolic catalogue. The cosmological sections were found combined with Xici
material in the Mawangdui *Yizhi yi* (1973).

## Historical Significance

The *Zhouyi benyi* dominated Yijing scholarship from the 13th century onward:
- Immediately overshadowed Cheng Yi's commentary (even while both were paired in the Qing
  *Zhouyi zhezhong*).
- Became the basis for Yijing literacy in China, Korea (Joseon dynasty), Japan (Tokugawa period),
  and Vietnam.
- At least 30 editions published in China between 1265 and 1918.

This 2020 Columbia translation is the first complete English rendering and makes the text fully
accessible to Western scholarship for the first time.
