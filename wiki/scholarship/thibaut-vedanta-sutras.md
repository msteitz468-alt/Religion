# The Vedānta-Sūtras with Śaṅkara's and Rāmānuja's Commentaries (trans. Thibaut)

[[george-thibaut|George Thibaut's]] *Sacred Books of the East* translations of the
**[[brahma-sutras|Brahma Sutras]]** with the two commentaries that founded the rival Vedanta schools.
The wiki's grounding for **how the same ~555 aphorisms generate opposite metaphysics**.

## The Text and Its Structure

The Brahma Sutras are organized into **four *adhyāyas*** (chapters), each the engine of a major Vedanta
theme:

1. **Samanvaya** ("harmony") — that all the [[upanishads|Upanishadic]] passages converge on
   [[brahman|Brahman]] as the one cause. The school-defining battles are fought here over individual
   sutras.
2. **Avirodha** ("non-conflict") — refuting rival systems (Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Buddhism, Jainism) and
   removing apparent contradictions.
3. **Sādhana** ("means") — the path: meditation, the role of works, the journey of the soul.
4. **Phala** ("fruit") — [[moksha|liberation]] and the state of the freed soul.

## Śaṅkara's Reading (SBE 34/38) — [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]

*(SBE 34 = Adhyāya I + Adhyāya II Pādas I–II; SBE 38 = Adhyāyas III–IV. Only SBE 34 is in `raw/`;
SBE 38 has no corresponding file.)*

### The Adhyāsa-Bhāṣya — Shankara's Preamble

Śaṅkara does not begin with the first sūtra. He prefixes the entire *bhāṣya* with an independent
philosophical essay on **[[adhyasa|superimposition]]** (*adhyāsa*) — making the diagnosis before
the remedy.

**The definition** (Thibaut's translation, verbatim):

> "The apparent presentation, in the form of remembrance, to consciousness of something previously
> observed, in some other thing."

Three competing definitions are surveyed and harmonized: (1) the *anyathākhyātivādin* view that
superimposition is attributing the qualities of one *existing* thing to another existing thing;
(2) the *akhyātivādin* view that it is error due to non-apprehension of difference; (3) the view that
it is fictitious assumption of attributes contrary to the thing's own nature. Śaṅkara's definition
encompasses all three, with the qualification that **remembrance** (not renewed perception) is the
mechanism — illustrated by the *classical examples*:

- "Mother-of-pearl appears like silver" — the absent silver is remembered and superimposed on the
  present nacreous surface
- "The moon appears as if she were double" — one moon, doubled by optical error
- Rope/snake — not a parallel Śaṅkara uses in the *bhāṣya* itself (that is the traditional example
  later associated with the school)

The **specific application**: Subject (the Self, sphere of the "I") and Object (the not-Self, sphere
of the "Thou") are as opposed as light and darkness. Yet human beings naturally fail to distinguish
them, superimposing the Self's characters on the not-Self and the not-Self's characters on the Self.
Concrete examples:

- Extra-personal attributes → Self: "I am well/ill because my wife and children are well/ill"
- Bodily attributes → Self: "I am stout, lean, fair, standing, walking"
- Sense-organ attributes → Self: "I am deaf, one-eyed, blind"
- Internal-organ attributes → Self: "I am desirous, doubting, determined"
- Self → internal organ: the producer of the "I"-notion (the *ahaṃkāra*, internal organ) is treated as
  the real Self; but the real Self is the pure witness of all modifications

**The objection and reply**: Can the Self — which is not properly an object — be the locus of
superimposition? Śaṅkara replies: the Self is not an absolute non-object; it *is* the object of the
"I"-notion (*asmat-pratyaya*), known through immediate, intuitive self-presentation (*aparokṣatva*).
Nor is the rule absolute that superimposition requires sensory contact: "non-discerning men superimpose
dark-blue colour on the ether, which is not the object of sensuous perception."

**The structural consequence**: This mutual superimposition = ***avidyā*** (Nescience). Its removal by
discriminative ***vidyā*** = liberation. Every act of knowing, every Vedic injunction, every use of
perception, inference, or scripture **presupposes** this superimposition — which is why scripture itself
can only operate in the sphere of nescience, and why the study of the Vedānta-texts is undertaken *with
a view to freeing oneself from this wrong notion*. Men and animals act in the same way under
superimposition; the texts merely divert the stream of activity inward. On this diagnosis the entire
*Śārīraka-mīmāṃsā* ("enquiry about the embodied Self") is grounded.

### Commentary on the Key Opening Sūtras

**I.1.1 — "Then therefore the enquiry into Brahman"**

"Then" (Sanskrit *atha*) denotes **immediate consecution**, not a new independent topic or an
auspicious formula. The enquiry into Brahman differs from the Pūrva Mīmāṃsā enquiry into religious
duty in three ways: (1) its fruit is eternal bliss, not transitory happiness dependent on action;
(2) Brahman is an already-accomplished *existing substance*, not something to be done; (3) the means of
knowledge are Vedānta-texts *plus* intuition (*sākṣātkāra*), not scripture-plus-inference alone.

The special prerequisite for the Brahma-*mīmāṃsā* is not prior completion of the Pūrva Mīmāṃsā but the
**four spiritual qualifications** (*sādhana-catuṣṭaya*):
1. Discrimination between the eternal and the non-eternal (*viveka*)
2. Renunciation of desire to enjoy the fruit of one's actions, both here and hereafter (*vairāgya*)
3. Acquisition of tranquility, self-restraint, and the other aids (*ṣaṭ-sampat*)
4. Desire for final release (*mumukṣutva*)

**I.1.2 — "From which the origin, subsistence, and dissolution of this world"**

The formal definition of Brahman: the omniscient, omnipotent cause of the "origin, subsistence, and
dissolution" (*utpatti, sthiti, pralaya*) of "this world — differentiated by names and forms, containing
many agents and enjoyers, the abode of the fruits of actions, whose arrangement cannot even be conceived
by the mind." Neither the non-intelligent *pradhāna*, nor atoms, nor non-being, nor a transmigrating
being can account for this arrangement; only an omniscient Lord. Importantly, this is not a mere
inference — the sūtras "string together the flowers of the Vedānta-passages" and point to Taittirīya
Up. III,1: *"From whence these beings are born..."*

**I.1.3 — "From its being the source of Scripture"**

Brahman's omniscience is confirmed from its being the source (*yoni*) of the entire Vedic corpus. Just
as Pāṇini the grammarian had more extensive knowledge than even his comprehensive grammar, so the Lord
who "in sport as it were, easily as a man sends forth his breath, has produced the vast mass of holy
texts known as the Ṛg-veda, &c." must be of surpassing omniscience.

**I.1.4 — "That (Brahman is known from Scripture) because it is connected (with the Vedānta-texts)
as their purport"**

The most extended argument in the opening pāda: a systematic rebuttal of the Mīmāṃsā position that
Vedic texts have authority only as injunctions or as supplements to injunctions.

Śaṅkara's core arguments:
- The Vedānta-passages (*sat eva... tat tvam asi...* etc.) have Brahman as their direct purport, not as
  a supplement to actions
- **Mokṣa is eternal**: "eternal without undergoing any changes" (*kūṭastha-nitya*), omnipresent as
  ether, not composed of parts, self-luminous — "the same as Brahman in the enquiry into which we are
  at present engaged." It cannot be an effected result; if it were, it would be non-eternal like jars
  or sour milk (mere modifications)
- *"He who knows Brahman becomes Brahman"* (Muṇḍaka III,2,9): liberation follows *immediately* on
  knowledge; no intervening action. The snake/rope analogy applies: "as soon as we comprehend Brahman,
  all our duties come to an end and all our work is over"
- The *mahāvākyas* ("great sayings" — *tat tvam asi*, *aham brahmāsmi*, *ayam ātmā brahma*) are not
  (a) figurative combinations, not (b) mere ascriptions for meditation, not (c) figurative identities
  founded on sharing an activity — they express **real identity**. Violence would otherwise be done to
  the grammatical connection of the words
- **Knowledge ≠ action**: "An action is that which is enjoined as being independent of the nature of
  existing things and dependent on the energy of some person's mind." Knowledge, by contrast, "depends
  entirely on existing things, and not either on Vedic statements or on the mind of man. Although mental
  it thus widely differs from meditation (*upāsanā*) and the like."

### The Method of Adhyāya I

The body of Adhyāya I (SBE 34, pp. 10–233) follows a single pattern of *adhikaraṇa* argumentation:
a Vedic term or entity (*ākāśa*, *prāṇa*, *jyotiḥ*, "the golden person in the sun," *ānandamaya*,
the "small ether within the heart," the *antaryāmin*) is put in doubt — does it refer to the highest
Brahman or to the *pradhāna* (Sāṃkhya's non-intelligent primary matter) or to the individual soul?
The *pūrvapakṣa* (prima facie view) argues for one of the alternatives; Śaṅkara's *siddhānta*
(established conclusion) always returns: the term/entity refers to the **highest Brahman**.

The recurring argument-pattern:
1. The attribute of knowing/intelligence mentioned in the passage cannot belong to the non-intelligent
   *pradhāna*
2. The general topic (*prakaraṇa*) of the chapter under discussion is Brahman, so individual terms must
   be read in that light
3. Scripture says there is "no other seer but he" — the *antaryāmin* texts preclude any other ruling
   principle
4. The difference of individual soul and Lord is "*not real, but due to the false limiting adjuncts of
   the highest Self*" (= practical difference valid at the empirical level, dissolved at the ultimate
   level)

A philosophically key statement at I.2.20 (on the *antaryāmin*): citing Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV,5,

> "Where there is duality, as it were, there one sees another" — the practical world exists only in
> the sphere of Nescience.
> "But when the Self only is all this, how should he see another?" — the practical world vanishes
> in the sphere of true knowledge.

This is Śaṅkara's **two-truths in exegetical action**: difference-texts are honored as valid at the
empirical (*vyāvahārika*) level; identity-texts are honored as expressing ultimate (*pāramārthika*)
truth.

Thibaut notes in his Introduction that the first adhyāya is best understood as **anti-Sāṃkhya**
throughout: the Sāṃkhya school uniquely among non-Vedāntic systems claimed scriptural support, and
Adhyāya I systematically strips that claim from every Upanishadic passage the Sāṃkhyas might invoke.
The organization of Adhyāya I follows the order of passages in the **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** as its
spine, with passages from other Upaniṣads interleaved as opportunities offer. The fourth pāda is
explicitly anti-Sāṃkhya and the only pāda that does not simply follow the Chāndogya order.

## Rāmānuja's Reading (SBE 48) — [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Viśiṣṭādvaita]]

Rāmānuja's *Śrī-Bhāṣya* opens by **attacking Śaṅkara's preamble** — the *Mahāpūrvapakṣa* ("great prior
objection") against the very coherence of *avidyā* and *nirguṇa* Brahman. His own system: souls and
matter are the real **body (*śarīra*)** of [[brahman|Brahman]] = the personal God Viṣṇu; unity *and*
real distinction; [[maya|māyā]] is God's real creative power, not illusion; the path is **bhakti** and
*prapatti* (surrender).

### The Mahāpūrvapakṣa — Attack on Advaita's Foundations (Adhyāya I intro)

Before engaging the sutras, Rāmānuja levels seven interconnected objections against Advaita's core
assumptions. The target is the coherence of "non-differentiated Brahman plus *avidyā*":

1. **Avidyā cannot be defined.** It cannot be (a) non-existent (then why does it bind?), (b) existent
   (then it is real, and non-dualism collapses), or (c) both (self-contradiction). "Inexplicable" is
   not a definition.
2. **Avidyā cannot have Brahman as its locus.** Brahman is pure self-luminous consciousness — pure
   consciousness cannot be the substrate of *nescience*; they are definitionally incompatible.
3. **Avidyā cannot have Brahman as its object.** Any object must be other-than-Brahman; if Brahman
   has no other, *avidyā* has no object.
4. **The self cannot be unknowing to itself.** Consciousness is self-revealing by definition; an
   unknowing pure consciousness is a contradiction.
5. **The individual self cannot be identical with Brahman.** If the self is already Brahman, the
   instruction "know that thou art Brahman" has no agent and no object — a paradox, not a teaching.
6. **Liberation cannot be "knowledge."** If bondage is *avidyā* (knowledge of the wrong kind), its
   cure requires *other* knowledge — but how does one self-luminous consciousness know itself
   differently? The Advaita account is circular.
7. **The texts' own logic demands a real relation.** Texts like "Brahman is the cause of the world"
   presuppose a real cause-effect distinction; allegorizing this away via two-truths is arbitrary.

Rāmānuja's conclusion: any coherent Vedāntic theology requires *real* souls and *real* matter —
organized as the body of a personal God.

### The Formal Body Definition (Adhyāya II)

The structural cornerstone of the system is the **body-soul (*śarīra-śarīrī*) relation**. Rāmānuja
offers a formal definition:

> "A body is any substance which a conscious being is able to control and support for its own
> purposes, and which stands in an entirely subordinate relation to that being."
> (*śeṣatva* — having the character of a remainder/accessory)

This is not metaphor. Under this definition:
- Souls are the body of [[brahman|Brahman]], who is their inner controller (*antaryāmin*)
- Matter is the body of Brahman, who directs it from within
- "That thou art" (*tat tvam asi*) = the self, as Brahman's body/mode, is *identical with Brahman in the
  qualified sense* — not as an absolute equal, but as an inseparable attribute

This allows Rāmānuja to accept ALL Upanishadic texts at face value — both identity texts ("the Self is
Brahman") and difference texts ("the individual soul worships Brahman") — without the Advaita device of
two-truths. Identity-texts name the qualified unity; difference-texts name the real distinction between
controller and controlled. See [[sarira-sariri|the body-soul relation]].

### Souls (*cit*) — Nature and Properties (Adhyāya II, Pāda III)

The individual soul is:
- **Knower** (*jñātṛ*) — consciousness is its essential nature, not an accidental property; Advaita's
  claim that the soul is pure consciousness misidentifies the soul's mode as its essence
- **Agent** (*kartṛ*) — actually performs actions; not merely an apparent agent under *avidyā*
- **Atomic in size** (*aṇu*), residing in the heart; pervades the body through the spreading of
  consciousness (as a lamp illuminates a room while the flame is in one place)
- **Part of Brahman** (*aṃśa*) — not a fragment broken off, but a mode (*prakāra*) — an attribute that
  cannot be realized apart from the whole. The soul's relation to Brahman is analogous to a quality's
  relation to its substrate.
- **Many, not one** — the plurality of souls is real; liberation does not merge them into a single Self

The soul is NOT:
- Omniscient in itself (its intelligence is contracted by *karman*)
- Autonomous (its qualities depend on the Supreme Person for their very permanency)
- Identical to Brahman in the unqualified sense

### Matter (*acit*) and the Cosmological Sequence (Adhyāya II, Pāda III)

From Brahman as cause, the cosmological sequence is:
Brahman (*chit* + *achit* as its body) → *ākāśa* (ether) → *vāyu* (air) → fire → water → earth

The subtle body (*sūkṣma śarīra*), composed of the five elements in their subtle form, accompanies the
soul through all births. Matter (*achit*) = non-sentient, incapable of directing itself; entirely
governed by Brahman as its inner Self.

Key Sāṃkhya refutation: Sāṃkhya's *pradhāna* (undirected primordial matter as cosmological cause) is
impossible — matter can only be directed by intelligence; Scripture requires a personal directing cause.

### The *Arambhaṇa* Adhikaraṇa (II,1,15–20)

Rāmānuja's response to the Advaita claim that creation = illusion:

> "In the world, pots and other effects are produced only from clay by the clay's own modification
> (*pariṇāma*). However, clay does not become non-existent at any stage; the pot-state is equally real.
> Brahman's creation is genuine transformation (*pariṇāma*), not appearance (*vivarta*)."

The classic analogy: just as the clay is the "real cause" (*upādāna*) and the pot is a real effect that
does not falsify the clay, Brahman creates real souls and matter without becoming lesser. The world is
NOT Brahman's *appearance* under *māyā* (Advaita's position) but Brahman's real body in a new state.

This is the *pariṇāmavāda* ("transformation doctrine") vs. Advaita's *vivartavāda* ("appearance
doctrine") — a central fault-line in Vedānta philosophy. See [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the
controversy comparison]].

### Refutation of Rival Schools (Adhyāya II, Pāda II)

Rāmānuja refutes in sequence:
- **Advaita** (*māyāvāda*): shown incoherent on *avidyā*, *nirguṇa* Brahman, and the self's
  "apparent" agency (see above)
- **Sāṃkhya**: *pradhāna* cannot direct itself; the cosmological sequence requires a sentient cause
- **Vaiśeṣika**: the atomic theory cannot explain how atoms combine to form bodies without a directing
  intelligence; "adṛṣṭa" (the unseen force) cannot self-organize
- **Buddhism**: the consciousness-only school (*vijñānavāda*) refuted — external objects are real (we
  do not simply project them); the momentariness doctrine is incoherent (causation requires continuity)
- **Jainism**: the *syādvāda* ("perhaps-saying," non-absolutism) — the claim that all propositions are
  partially true and partially indeterminate — leads to epistemic collapse
- **Pāśupata** (Śaiva) and **Pāñcarātra** (Vaiṣṇava): Rāmānuja defends the *Pāñcarātra* texts as
  authentic Vedic revelation. The *vyūha* doctrine (the four forms of Viṣṇu — Vāsudeva, Saṃkarṣaṇa,
  Pradyumna, Aniruddha) is reconciled with Brahman as the cause: Saṃkarṣaṇa, etc. are real forms of
  Brahman, not separate gods. The Pāñcarātra defense is particularly careful because critics (including
  Śaṅkara) had used it to discredit Vaiṣṇava orthodoxy.

### The *Antaryāmin* (*Inner Ruler*) Doctrine

A doctrine central to the entire system. The *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* III,7 ("who, dwelling within the earth,
rules the earth from within — that is thy Self, the inner Ruler, the Immortal") is read by Rāmānuja
as a systematic proclamation:

- The inner Ruler dwells within *every* thing — earth, water, fire, atmosphere, wind, sky, sun, moon,
  the *ākāśa*, darkness, light, and *within individual souls themselves*
- Each clause ends: "that is thy Self, the inner Ruler, the Immortal"
- The crucial point: Brahman rules from within souls, yet those souls are real distinct modes of Brahman
- "Immortal" = the inner Ruler is untouched by the imperfections of the bodies it inhabits

This directly refutes the Advaita reading that the *antaryāmin* passages teach identity (Brahman is the
soul), and also refutes any reading that makes Brahman a passive witness. The inner Ruler is active,
personal, omniscient, and directing — while remaining distinct from what it rules. See [[antaryami]].

### Brahman's Twofold Character (Adhyāya III, Pāda II)

Against the objection that Brahman, as the inner Self of all bodies (including evil ones), is tainted by
those bodies' imperfections, Rāmānuja draws a fundamental distinction:

Brahman has **two essential sets of qualities**:
1. **Freedom from all imperfections** (no evil, no sin, no decay, no hunger, no thirst)
2. **Possession of all blessed qualities** (omniscience, omnipotence, bliss, creative power, perfect will)

The imperfections of the bodies Brahman inhabits do not touch Brahman because:
- Brahman's relation to its body is as the *controller and support*, not as the *experiencer*
- *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* IV,4,25 ("no aging, no death") explicitly confirms this non-contamination
- The connexion with bodies is **sport (*līlā*)** for Brahman — it does not bind Brahman as it binds souls

### The *"Neti Neti"* Interpretation (Adhyāya III, Pāda II,17)

Rāmānuja's reading of *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* II,3,6: "not so, not so" (*neti neti*) does NOT negate Brahman's
attributes. The denial is directed at the **restriction** of Brahman to a previously stated "so much" —
it denies the exhaustiveness of any finite predicate, not the predicate's truth. This is confirmed by
the positive qualities that follow immediately: "True of the True" (*satyasya satyam*). The *neti neti*
formula is a teaching device pointing beyond each finite characterization, not an apophatic denial of all
positive attributes — which would make Brahman and the concept of *moksha* (positive bliss) incoherent.

## Adhyāya III — The Path (*Sādhana*)

### Meditation (*Upāsanā*/*Dhyāna*) — Core Requirements

*Upāsanā* = not a one-time cognitive event but **repeated, continued representation** (*dhyāna*). The
Sanskrit roots *vid*, *upas*, *dhyai* are used interchangeably in the same texts — all mean the same
meditative practice. Rāmānuja insists this must **continue until death**: "acting thus as long as life
lasts he reaches the world of Brahman."

Key rules governing meditation:
- **The object** of meditation is the **highest Brahman** (not the individual soul, not an effected
  Brahman like Hiraṇyagarbha). Several vidyās (named Upanishadic meditations) converge on the same
  highest Brahman.
- **Essential attributes** (bliss, knowledge, freedom from evil, infinity) must be included in ALL
  Brahman-meditations, regardless of the vidyā's particular focus.
- **Figurative representations** ("bliss is its head," etc.) are aids to visualization, not real
  attributes — they would imply increase/decrease, contradicting Brahman's infinity.
- **The meditating subject** must conceive of itself as having the nature of the *released* Self (freed
  from sin, freed from the body's limitations) — the soul is itself a mode of the object meditated
  upon, so must be apprehended in its pure form.
- **Mental fire-altars** (*mānasa agnihotra*) are meditations only, not alternatives to action — proved
  by direct textual statement, inferential mark, and syntactical connexion.

Symbol meditations (meditating on *prāṇa*, the sun's disc, etc., *as* Brahman) have a different
structure: Brahman's aspect is superimposed *on* the symbol (the superior on the inferior, not vice
versa). Symbol-meditators are NOT led to the highest Brahman on the Path of the Gods.

### The Three Auxiliaries of Knowledge (Adhyāya III, Pāda IV,39)

Rāmānuja follows *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* III,5 in naming three auxiliaries:
1. **Paṇḍitya** (*paṇḍitya* = scholarship): full mastery through hearing and pondering; not booklearning alone but the complete *śravaṇa-manana-nididhyāsana* sequence
2. **Bālya** (child-like quality): NOT naïveté — specifically **not manifesting pride or arrogance**. The "childlike" quality is the absence of the sage's tendency toward self-display.
3. **Mauna** (silence): NOT outer silence — specifically **reiterated vivid representation** (*upāsana*). The "silence" *is* the meditation practice; the root *man* in *mauna* connects it directly to *manana* (pondering).

All three auxiliaries must be maintained **until death**. The śramaṇa wanderer who has achieved
knowledge is not exempt from them.

### Works and Knowledge (Adhyāya III, Pāda IV)

Rāmānuja's central disagreement with Jaimini on the relation of *karma* (Vedic works) and *jñāna*
(knowledge):
- **Jaimini's position**: knowledge is an *auxiliary* to works; the Vedic ritual system is the primary
  vehicle; knowledge assists it.
- **Rāmānuja's position** (following Bādarāyaṇa): knowledge has an **independent fruit** — attaining
  Brahman. It does not dissolve into the ritual system.

For **householders**: all prescribed works (Agnihotra etc.) remain obligatory and are prerequisites to
knowledge — they please the Lord who then grants the grace enabling *jñāna*. The works' only effect for
the knower is to give rise to knowledge; their karmic result is neutralized.

For **those outside the āśramas**: prayer, fasting, and penance suffice; figures like Raikva, Bhīṣma,
and Samvarta are cited as qualified for knowledge outside the āśrama system.

**Lapsed Naishṭhikas** (celibate students who have "fallen"): no expiation can restore their purity —
"I see no expiatory performance by which he, a slayer of the Self, could become pure." The āśrama
obligations are taken seriously.

## Adhyāya IV — Liberation (*Phala*)

### Destruction of Karman (Adhyāya IV, Pāda I)

Rāmānuja systematizes three categories of *karman* relative to the knower:

1. **Anārabdha-kārya** ("not-yet-commenced-works") — karma whose fruits have not yet begun: destroyed
   *immediately at the rise of knowledge*. The mechanism is theological: knowledge propitiates the Lord,
   whose displeasure (which held these works in reserve) is thereby dissolved.
2. **Ārabdha-kārya** ("commenced works") — karma whose results have already begun (the body one is
   currently experiencing): NOT destroyed by knowledge, but must be exhausted by retributive experience.
   These may require multiple additional bodies before being fully discharged.
3. **Post-knowledge karma** — works performed after the rise of knowledge: these "do not cling" to the
   knower; they have no binding force.

Good works at the moment of death are received by friends; evil works by enemies. The Agnihotra and
other prescribed works must still be performed by the knower — their function for him is now solely to
give rise to ongoing knowledge.

### Departure from the Body (Adhyāya IV, Pāda II)

The sequence of departure:
1. **Speech** (as sense organ itself) withdraws into *manas* (mind)
2. **All other organs** follow the same withdrawal sequence
3. **Mind** withdraws into *prāṇa* (the breath/vital principle)
4. **Prāṇa** joins the individual soul; soul + prāṇa → all five elements together (the tripartite
   composite described in *Chāndogya* VI)

The common departure sequence (up to the beginning of the journey path) is shared by the knower and
the non-knower.

**The knower's exit** is through the **100th+1 artery** (*suṣumnā*), "going up to the crown." The
Supreme Person dwelling in the heart illuminates this path; the knower is assisted by the Lord's grace.
The **subtle body persists** on the journey (required for the soul's colloquy with the moon at various
stages — this colloquy requires an instrument of experience).

**Death at any time** is permissible for the knower — night, dark fortnight, southern solstice; the
"two paths" passage in *Bhagavad Gītā* VIII,23–26 is a description for Yogins to **remember and
meditate on** daily, not a timing injunction for dying.

### The Path of the Gods (*Devayāna*) (Adhyāya IV, Pāda III)

Rāmānuja harmonizes the multiple accounts (Chāndogya, Kauṣītaki, Bṛhadāraṇyaka) into a single road
beginning with **light**. The **order of stages**:

light → (day) → (bright fortnight) → (six months of the northern solstice) → **Vāyu** (the world of
the gods) → **sun** → **lightning** → **Varuṇa** → **Indra** → **Prajāpati** → **Brahman**

**Conducting deities** lead the ascending soul; the "not-human person" at the lightning stage is the
first personal conductor. Varuṇa, Indra, Prajāpati are assistants along the way.

**Who is led by the conducting deities?**
- **Bādari**: those meditating on an *effected* Brahman (Hiraṇyagarbha); at the *pralaya* of
  Hiraṇyagarbha's world, those souls attain the highest Brahman together with their guru.
- **Jaimini**: those meditating on the *highest* Brahman directly; "Brahman" in the text = primary
  sense.
- **Bādarāyaṇa's resolution**: those NOT depending on symbols are led — both (a) those meditating on
  the highest Brahman, and (b) those meditating on the Self as free from Prakṛti with Brahman as Self
  (*antaryāmin*). Symbol-meditators (who meditate on fire, prāṇa, etc. as Brahman) are NOT led by the
  conducting deities; their meditation is contaminated by a non-intelligent element (*acit*) and they
  attain a corresponding non-final result. The principle *tatkratuḥ* applies: "according to what his
  thought is, so will he be" (*Chāndogya* III,14).

### The State of Release (Adhyāya IV, Pāda IV)

**Release is manifestation, not creation.** The "own form" the released soul assumes at Brahman
(Chāndogya VIII,12,3) is the manifestation of its *eternally existing* true nature — now freed from the
obscuration of *avidyā* and *karman*. The soul's intelligence and bliss are eternal; the state of
release reveals them as a gem's lustre is revealed by polishing (not created by it). This is **Saunaka's
analogy** (cited by Rāmānuja): "The lustre of the gem is not created by the act of polishing."

**The soul's identity with Brahman** at release: the released soul is conscious of itself as
**non-divided** from the highest Brahman — "I am Brahman, without any division." The *identity*-texts
and *difference*-texts are reconciled through the body-soul relation: the soul is a mode (*prakāra*) of
Brahman, identical as an inseparable attribute, distinct as a mode from its substrate. "I am Brahman"
= "I am non-divided from Brahman"; not "I am identical with Brahman in the sense of collapsing all
distinction."

**Three views on the soul's nature at release** (Adhyāya IV, Pāda IV, Sutras 5–7):

| Position | Teacher | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Brahman-character** | Jaimini | Soul manifests freedom from sin + laughing, playing, other positive divine qualities |
| **Pure intelligence** | Audulomi | Soul is "throughout a mass of knowledge" — like a lump of salt, which is taste through and through |
| **Both** | Bādarāyaṇa (final) | Soul is pure intelligence *throughout* AND possesses freedom from sin, bliss, etc.; the lump of salt has *one taste throughout* but also has color, hardness — multiple attributes do not contradict a unified nature |

**Powers of the released soul** (Sutras 8–14):
- **Moves and creates by mere will** — no effort required; "by mere will the Fathers rise" (Chāndogya VIII,2,1)
- **Self-ruler** — not subject to another's injunctions (Chāndogya VII,25)
- **With or without a body at will** (Bādarāyaṇa's synthesis): Bādari (no body) and Jaimini (with
  many bodies/forms) are both accommodated — as the twelve-day sacrifice can be either *sattra* or
  *ahīna* depending on the performer's purpose
- **Enters multiple bodies** like a lamp whose light fills the room while the flame is in one spot;
  the released soul (with non-contracted consciousness) can extend its consciousness into many bodies
- **Bodiless experience** is possible as in dream — the Lord creates the experiential context, as he
  creates chariots and horses in the dream state (Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV,3,10)
- **All-knowing** — "he who sees this sees everything" (Chāndogya VII,2,6); texts implying unconsciousness
  at release refer to deep sleep or *sampadyate* (death), not to the released state

**One crucial exception — world-ruling power** (Sutra 17, conclusive):
The released soul does NOT acquire Brahman's power of creating, sustaining, and dissolving the universe.
This "world-energy" (*jagat-vaiṣamya*) is the exclusive attribute of the Supreme Person, proved (a) from
the leading subject-matter of all texts on world-ruling power (they define Brahman specifically by it),
and (b) from the non-proximity of any such texts to the released soul's state. The texts saying the
released soul "moves in all worlds" mean participation in the enjoyment of Hiraṇyagarbha's and other
spheres within Brahman's *spheta* (manifestation) — not that the soul creates or rules them.

**Non-return from release** (Sutra 22 — repeated for emphasis):
The Supreme Lord will NOT turn the released soul back into *saṃsāra*. Grounds: (1) Chāndogya VIII,15:
"he does not return"; (2) *Bhagavad Gītā* VIII,15–16: "Having obtained me, great-souled men do not
come into rebirth"; (3) the released soul's karman is extinguished, its knowledge is complete, its
entire being is in the bliss of Brahman-intuition — it cannot *desire* anything else. (4) The Lord who
loves his Devotee will not eject him: "the wise man I regard as my very Self" (BG VII,17–19). The
repetition of "non-return" marks the conclusion of the entire work.

## Thibaut's Own Verdict

In his introductions Thibaut argues — as a critic, against both traditions — that **Rāmānuja's theistic
reading may better fit Bādarāyaṇa's original sutras** than Śaṅkara's non-dualism, making Advaita a later
(if greater) systematization. A clean example of the **historical-critical** voice that adjudicates
neither school's *theology* but questions both schools' *history*.

The third classical commentary — **[[madhva|Madhva's]]** Dvaita *bhāṣya* on the Brahma Sutras — is
