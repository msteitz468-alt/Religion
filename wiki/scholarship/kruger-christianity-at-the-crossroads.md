# Kruger, *Christianity at the Crossroads*

## Source Overview

**Author**: [[michael-kruger|Michael J. Kruger]] — President and Samuel C. Patterson Professor of NT and Early Christianity, Reformed Theological Seminary (Charlotte); PhD Edinburgh.

**Thesis**: The second century was the decisive transitional century in Christian history — the period when the Church separated from Judaism, learned to survive Roman persecution, developed its ecclesial structure, fought over doctrine, produced an enormous literary culture, and assembled its scriptural canon. Kruger calls it the "Cinderella Century" (following Hurtado). The book's implicit interlocutor throughout is Walter Bauer and his legacy; its argument is that Bauer was right about diversity but wrong about the dominance of heresy and wrong about the impossibility of distinguishing orthodoxy from heresy.

**Hermeneutical approach**: Social-historical; text-critical; canonical-critical. Evangelical-confessional perspective: Kruger defends the early emergence of both orthodoxy and canon formation, while engaging seriously with revisionist scholarship (Bauer, Ehrman, Koester).

**Explicit cross-reference with Hurtado**: Kruger acknowledges [[larry-hurtado|Hurtado's]] *Destroyer of the Gods* in his Introduction as "remarkably similar" in conclusions, noting both works were written independently but arrived at convergent findings about the distinctive nature of second-century Christianity.

---

## Five Transitions of the Second Century

Kruger's framework structures the book around five areas of transition:

1. **Sociological**: Parting from Judaism → Christianity becomes its own ethnically diverse movement (a "new race" per Epistle to Diognetus); new minority identity without the protection of *religio licita* status
2. **Cultural-political**: *Superstitio* label; Roman persecution; the "golden age" of apologetics
3. **Ecclesiological**: Plural elders → monepiscopate; house churches; Lord's Day worship practices
4. **Doctrinal-theological**: Doctrinal diversity (Ebionites, Marcionites, Gnostics, Montanists) vs. the unifying [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]]; the Bauer debate
5. **Textual-canonical**: Explosion of Christian literary production; apocryphal literature; emergence of a "core" NT canon by mid-second century

## Chapter Summaries

### Ch. 1 — "A Peculiar Identity" (Sociological)

The parting of the ways driven by: theological differences (worship of Jesus); Gentile influx into churches; political events (70 AD destruction of Jerusalem; Bar Kokhba revolt 132-135 AD); Roman perception of Christians as a distinct, non-Jewish movement (evident in Pliny, Tacitus, Suetonius). Socio-economic diversity: Pliny's "all ranks"; Clement of Alexandria's *Quis dives salvetur*. Literacy mostly low but textuality high. Women's role: Pliny's two female deaconesses; Blandina (Gaul); Acts of Paul and Thecla; Stark's thesis that Christianity was ~2/3 female; Celsus' mockery as inadvertent confirmation; positive sexual ethic for women.

### Ch. 2 — "A Strange Superstition" (Cultural-Political)

*Superstitio* as Roman category: Christianity was *superstitio* because it refused civic religious participation without ethnic exemption. Pliny's letter to Trajan (111–113 AD) as primary source for persecution practice. Martyrdom accounts: [[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius]] (martyrdom as imitatio Christi; anti-Docetic significance of his death); [[polycarp|Polycarp]] ("For eighty-six years I have served him"); Blandina; Scillitan Martyrs (Carthage, c.180). Intellectual critics: Lucian (*Peregrinus*), Galen (grudging respect as philosophy), Fronto (debauchery charges), [[celsus|Celsus]] (*True Doctrine*, c.177 — most sustained attack). Apologists: Quadratus and Aristides (to Hadrian, c.125); Epistle to Diognetus; [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr]]; Tatian (*Diatessaron*; *Oration to the Greeks*); Athenagoras; Theophilus of Antioch; [[tertullian|Tertullian]] ("blood of Christians is seed," c.197).

### Ch. 3 — "Worshipping Jesus" (Ecclesiological)

**Church governance**: At century's opening, most evidence shows plural elders/bishops (Didache; 1 Clement; Shepherd of Hermas; [[polycarp|Polycarp's Epistle to the Philippians]]). By mid-century, emergence of [[monepiscopate|monepiscopate]]: Ignatius gives clearest early evidence (bishop + presbyters + deacons as three distinct orders). [[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] later projects a continuous episcopal succession back to apostles — probably anachronistic but reflects concern to counter gnostic claims of secret apostolic tradition. Jerome explains transition: unity against heresy was the driving motive.

**House churches**: Christians met in private homes/tenements throughout the century; Celsus attests to "private houses"; Justin's church at bathhouse of Martinus; domus ecclesiae at Dura-Europos (early 3rd c.). Evidence from apocryphal acts (Acts of Paul, Acts of Peter, Acts of John) for household meeting venues. Some architectural modification for worship by late 2nd c.

**Worship elements**: Justin Martyr (*First Apology* 67) provides clearest single-source description of 2nd-c. worship: (1) **Gathering** — Sunday as "Lord's Day" (Didache, Epistle of Barnabas, Ignatius, Pliny); (2) **Teaching** — public Scripture reading (OT + "memoirs of the apostles") followed by sermon/exhortation; 2 Clement and Melito's *Peri Pascha* also illustrate; (3) **Eating** — Lord's Supper / Eucharist; connection to Christ's death; unity emphasized by Ignatius and Didache; (4) **Washing** — baptism in "living water" (Didache); Trinitarian formula; preceded by preparation period.

**Object of worship**: Christian worship of *Jesus specifically* — alongside Yahweh — was what scandalized both pagans (polytheists who saw no need for exclusivity) and Jews (monotheists who saw it as blasphemy). Pliny: Christians "sing hymns to Christ, as to a god." Kruger cites 1 Cor 8:5-6 and the Shema reformulation (cf. [[larry-hurtado|Hurtado]]).

### Ch. 4 — "Alternative Pathways" (Diversity)

**Bauer's thesis** (1934): heresy often preceded orthodoxy geographically; the distinction is anachronistic; "orthodoxy" simply = the party that won in the 4th century. Kruger's verdict: Bauer right on diversity, wrong on heretical dominance and on the impossibility of distinguishing heresy from orthodoxy.

**Diverse groups surveyed**:

- **Ebionites**: Jewish-Christians; law-observance required for salvation; reject Paul; adoptionistic Christology; Gospel of the Ebionites (modified Matthew). Opposed by Justin, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Origen, Epiphanius.
- **Nazarenes**: Also Jewish-Christian but orthodox Christology; accepted Paul; possibly the older tradition, with Ebionites as a second-century development.
- **Marcionites**: Marcion of Sinope (arrived Rome c.144); God of OT = inferior demiurge vs. God of NT = true gracious God; rejected OT; accepted only edited Luke + 10 Pauline letters; Docetic Christology; asceticism; refuted by Tertullian's *Against Marcion* (5 vols.).
- **Gnostics**: Nag Hammadi discovery (1945); spirit-matter dualism; demiurge as creator; salvation through *gnosis*; varied Christology (Docetic or separationist). Sub-figures: Cerinthus; Basilides (Alexandria, Hadrian era; 365 creator-angels; Simon of Cyrene substitution story); Valentinus (Rome 130s; most influential 2nd-c. heretic; school of Valentinus); Ptolemy (*Letter to Flora*); Heracleon (earliest commentary on John); Theodotus (*Excerpts* in Clement of Alexandria).
- **Montanists**: c.165-170, Phrygia; Montanus + prophetesses Priscilla and Maximilla; "New Prophecy"; ecstatic utterances; apocalyptic fervour (New Jerusalem at Pepuza); ascetic lifestyle; Tertullian eventually adopted some Montanist beliefs; generally orthodox Christology; condemned in Muratorian Fragment; excommunicated by Asian synods.

Four doctrinal flash points: (1) doctrine of creation; (2) status of the OT; (3) nature of salvation; (4) Christology.

### Ch. 5 — "The Great Church" (Unity)

**Rule of faith** (*regula fidei*): Consistent, widespread summary of apostolic teaching found in Tertullian, Irenaeus, Aristides, Epistula Apostolorum, Justin Martyr, Epistle to Diognetus, Ignatius, Clement of Rome. Geographical range: North Africa, Gaul, Rome, Syria, Greece, Asia Minor. Seven core affirmations: (1) one God, creator; (2) OT prophets spoke of Christ; (3) Jesus = Son of God, born of virgin; (4) Jesus = God in flesh; (5) Jesus saves; (6) crucifixion under Pilate, bodily resurrection, exaltation; (7) future return and judgment. The rule is a narrative (creation–fall–redemption–consummation) not merely doctrinal axioms.

**Refuting Bauer's dominance claim** — four lines of evidence: (a) **Leadership**: mainstream orthodox bishops dominated every major city; no heretical bishop named in 2nd c. sources; (b) **Geography**: Trebilco on Asia Minor, Hultgren, Bock all challenge Bauer's geographical claims; (c) **Literature**: NT papyri outnumber apocryphal 6:1 in 2nd c.; Clement of Alexandria cites Matthew 757×, Luke 402×, John 331× vs. apocryphal gospels ~16×; (d) **Rhetoric**: gnostics themselves (Theodotus, Heracleon) acknowledged they were "the few"; Celsus explicitly describes "the Great Church" as the mainstream group.

**Criterion for orthodoxy**: apostolic kerygma as preserved in Paul's seven undisputed letters — the earliest form of Christianity accessible by direct attestation. High Christology (1 Cor 8:5-6 Shema reformulation; Phil 2:6-11 citing Isa 45:23) pre-dates Paul and extends into the 30s-40s AD. Bauckham's "divine identity" Christology. Litfin: rule of faith coheres with earliest NT creedal material.

### Ch. 6 — "A Textual Culture" (Literary Production)

Christianity's uniqueness in the Greco-Roman world: its textual/bookish orientation. Gamble: "Christianity and Judaism stood apart from all other ancient religious movements" in their bookishness. Keith's definition of "textuality": knowledge, usage, and appreciation of texts regardless of literacy.

**Oral-written symbiosis**: Public reading was the primary mode of textual engagement for most Christians; but this was based on written texts. Papias (often misread) was not preferring oral tradition over writing — he was specifically seeking living eyewitnesses. Oral tradition and texts worked in tandem throughout the 2nd c.

**Types of Christian literature produced**: NT writings; Apostolic Fathers; theological treatises (Irenaeus *Against Heresies*, Clement *Miscellanies*, Tertullian *Prescription*); apologetic works; sermons (2 Clement; Melito *On the Passover*); commentaries (Heracleon on John = earliest extant); apocryphal literature (full taxonomy of gospels, acts, epistles, apocalypses).

**Book technology**: Codex adopted almost universally by Christians from the beginning of 2nd c. — distinctive break from both Jewish scrolls and Greco-Roman literary rolls. Codex ↔ canon consciousness (J.K. Elliott). Nomina sacra: first-century origin; organized, empire-wide scribal convention; marks a text as "Christian." Reader's aids (punctuation, sense-unit spacing) indicate manuscripts designed for public reading to diverse non-elite audiences.

**Textual stability**: 2nd-c. text is mixed (stable and fluid); evidence for copy centres ("master copies"); scribal corrections (P103 has second-hand corrections); Wisse on the "tenacity" of the text. No reason to posit greater fluidity in the earlier, unobservable stages.

### Ch. 7 — "A New Scripture" (NT Canon)

**Definition debate**: Sundberg's "exclusive definition" (fixed, final, closed list = 4th c.) vs. "functional definition" (books used as Scripture = much earlier). Kruger uses the functional definition.

**Core canon in place by mid-2nd c.**: Not just Irenaeus (~23/27 books). Confirmed by: Muratorian Fragment (~22/27; first canonical list; lists "in" and "out"; hard boundaries); Theophilus of Antioch (fourfold Gospel + Pauline letters as "divine word"); Clement of Alexandria (~23/27); Justin Martyr (reads "memoirs of apostles" = Gospels in worship; "as it is written" formula); Marcion (c.144) *cut down* a pre-existing larger corpus — orthodoxy didn't react to Marcion to *create* the canon; Aristides (c.125, invites Emperor Hadrian to read "the Gospel").

**Apostolic Fathers**: Epistle of Barnabas cites Matt 22:14 with "as it is written" (scriptural formula); Ignatius mentions a Pauline letter collection (Eph 12:2); Polycarp cites Ephesians as "Scripture" explicitly ("as it is written in these Scriptures"); 1 Clement knows Paul's letters as inspired ("in the Spirit").

**Papias** (c.125): Matthew and Mark known by name; apostolic connections (Mark from Peter; Matthew = apostle); evidence takes us back to the end of the 1st c.

**Key conclusion**: The canon was not the *result* of debates over heresy and orthodoxy; it was the *foundation* that enabled those debates. A "core" canon of four Gospels + Pauline letters + several others was effectively in place even before Marcion and well before Irenaeus.

## Key Contributions to the Wiki

1. **[[rule-of-faith|Rule of faith]]**: Full account of the *regula fidei* as the unifying theological core across diverse second-century Christianity — seven core affirmations, geographical spread, relationship to the apostolic kerygma.
2. **[[bauer-thesis|Bauer thesis]]**: Most sustained engagement with the Bauer debate in the wiki — Kruger provides the four-pronged refutation (leadership, geography, literature, rhetoric).
3. **[[monepiscopate|Monepiscopate]]**: Origin and development of the singular bishop office — from Didache/1 Clement (plural) through Ignatius (transitional) to Irenaeus (retrospective apostolic succession).
4. **[[nt-canon-formation|NT canon formation]]**: Functional canon in place by mid-2nd c.; Muratorian Fragment; Marcion as "cutter" not "creator"; Papias as witness from early 2nd c.
5. **Second-century Christian literary culture**: Codex, nomina sacra, copy centres, scribal practices, publication networks.
6. **Group profiles**: Detailed accounts of Ebionites, Marcionites, Gnostics (multiple sub-figures), Montanists.
7. **Figure pages**: Substantial data for Irenaeus, Ignatius, Polycarp, Tertullian, Marcion, Valentinus, Clement of Alexandria, Justin Martyr (expanded).

## Relationship to Other Sources in the Wiki

- **[[larry-hurtado|Hurtado, *Destroyer of the Gods*]]**: Directly complementary. Kruger explicitly acknowledges the parallel conclusions. Hurtado focuses on Christianity's *distinctiveness* (dyadic devotion, nomina sacra, codex); Kruger focuses on *transition* (five axes of change across the 2nd century). Where Hurtado argues from sociology and devotional practice, Kruger argues from doctrinal history and canon formation. Both converge on: (1) high Christology is original, not derivative; (2) Christianity's "bookish" character was distinctive; (3) the 2nd century is decisive.
- **[[kyrios-christology-origins|Kyrios-Christology Origins controversy]]**: Kruger confirms and expands the Hurtado/Bauckham position using Litfin's comparative analysis of the rule of faith and Pauline creedal traditions.
