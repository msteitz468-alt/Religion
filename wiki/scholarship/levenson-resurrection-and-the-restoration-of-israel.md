# Levenson, *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel: The Ultimate Victory of the God of Life*

A monograph by **[[jon-d-levenson|Jon D. Levenson]]** (Harvard) arguing that the
**[[resurrection-of-the-dead|resurrection of the dead]]** — the belief that at the end of
history God will bodily raise the dead — is neither a Christian innovation, nor a marginal
or dispensable item in Judaism, nor (in the main) a foreign Zoroastrian import, but a
**"weight-bearing beam in the edifice of rabbinic Judaism"** that developed *slowly and
organically* out of deep, native biblical roots. It is the wiki's primary source on the
biblical and Second Temple background of resurrection and a deliberate, scholarly
**qualification** of the [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|Zoroastrian-influence]]
thesis on the specific question of resurrection.

## The Consensus Levenson Rejects

Levenson opens by paraphrasing — in order to dismantle — the scholarly "consensus" he
regards as erroneous in every clause:

> The resurrection of the dead has no early roots in the Hebrew Bible; death there is an
> unproblematic part of God's plan; all the dead descend to the dreary, irreversible
> netherworld of [[sheol|Sheol]]; only very late in the Second Temple period did an
> alternative appear, prompted by the **scandal of the martyrs** (the undeserved death of
> the faithful under persecution); a major source of the new hope was **Zoroastrianism**;
> and the doctrine was thus a late, foreign importation, never deeply rooted in Judaism.

Against this he argues that the expectation "did not appear as a jarring innovation in
Second Temple Judaism but instead developed slowly and unevenly over the preceding
centuries," growing out of "the convergence of a number of biblical themes."

## The Argument, Chapter by Chapter

The book is *not* primarily about the doctrine's single point of origin (a quest Levenson
deems "fruitless and misguided") but about the **multiple linkages** between the new
teaching and the ancient tradition that it ultimately reshaped.

1. **The Modern Jewish Preference for Immortality** — Modern (especially Reform, and
   ambivalently Conservative) Judaism has quietly replaced bodily resurrection with the
   **immortality of the soul**, recasting the Amidah's second benediction (*Gevurot*,
   "who revives the dead") as "who has implanted within us immortal life." Levenson reads
   this as **assimilation** — to modern individualism, to scientific naturalism, and (in
   Jewish-Christian polemic, e.g. Abba Hillel Silver) to a wish to brand resurrection a
   foreign borrowing and so distance Judaism from Christianity. But the rabbis made
   resurrection a **defining, non-dispensable dogma** (Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1: one who says
   "the resurrection of the dead is not in the Torah" forfeits the world to come).
2. **Resurrection in the Torah?** — The rabbis found resurrection in the Torah by *derash*
   that augments but need not dislodge the *peshat*; their exegesis exposed a genuine and
   modern-overlooked biblical datum: **promises can be fulfilled after the death of their
   recipients**, because ancestors and descendants are inextricably linked.
3–4. **Up from Sheol / Are Abraham, Moses, and Job in Sheol?** — [[sheol|Sheol]] was *not*
   universally the destiny of all the dead, nor universally a Land of No Return. One must
   distinguish **fortunate deaths** (the patriarchs "gathered to their kin," old and
   contented) from **unfortunate deaths** (the God-forsaken "Pit"). A never-resolved
   tension between "all come to Sheol" and "God can deliver from Sheol" already *gestures
   toward* resurrection.
5. **Intimations of Immortality** — The "immortality" longed for in Israelite devotional
   literature is **not** a naturally indestructible soul (Greek/Platonic), but God's
   **gracious rescue from death** into renewed intimacy with him — already much closer to
   resurrection than to the immortality of the soul.
6. **Individual Mortality and Familial Resurrection** — The biblical idea of Israel as a
   natural family carries a "functional equivalence of **infertility with death**" and of
   **miraculous conception / the return of the lost child with resurrection** (the
   barren-matriarch and bound-Isaac narratives).
7–9. **The Man of God Performs a Resurrection / "Death—Be Broken!" / The Widow Re-Wed** —
   [[elijah|Elijah]] and [[elisha|Elisha]] raising dead sons show a firm, *early* faith in
   God's power over death; the **lost-and-restored child** becomes, in the prophets, a
   paradigm of **national restoration** (Israel as the bereaved widow whose children
   miraculously return).
10. **Israel's Exodus from the Grave** — [[ezekiel-prophet|Ezekiel's]] vision of the
   **valley of dry bones** (Ezek 37) makes national recovery itself a *resurrection from
   the graves*, back to the promised land.
11–12. **The Fact of Death / "He Keeps Faith with Those Who Sleep in the Dust"** — Death
   was always *theologically problematic* (a tension with God's preference for life); the
   first clear canonical affirmation, **Daniel 12:1–3** (cf. **Isaiah 26:19**, the Isaianic
   Apocalypse), has far deeper roots in prior tradition (the [[servant-songs|Suffering
   Servant]] of Isa 52–53 among its antecedents) than is usually noticed.
13. **God's Ultimate Victory** — The deepest root is the **Divine Warrior** pattern (an
   Israelite transformation of the Canaanite **Baal–Mot** myth): when God marches out in
   wrath, nature withers and dies; when he triumphs and is enthroned, nature revives and
   luxuriates. Within this "scenario of exceptions" — the blind see, the desert blooms,
   death itself is destroyed (Isa 25:8; 35) — resurrection "was hardly exceptional." See
   [[the-combat-myth|the Combat Myth]].
14. **Epilogue: The Two Horns of the Ram** — Resurrection is "both innovative and deeply
   conservative": innovative in envisioning a single end-time awakening of all the worthy;
   conservative in resting on ancient convictions about God's power over death, the eternal
   covenant, and the indefeasible promise to Israel.

## On Zoroastrian Influence (the qualification)

Levenson does **not** deny Persian influence outright — given two centuries of Achaemenid
rule, "it strains credulity to think they exerted no influence at all." But on
**resurrection specifically** he judges any influence "quite distant, a matter of milieu
rather than of direct borrowing." Citing **John Day**: the Zoroastrians neither **buried**
their dead nor spoke of resurrection in the language of **waking** — whereas burial and
awakening imagery are at home in **Canaanite** tradition. His verdict: if Jewish apocalyptic
is the *mother* of resurrection, Zoroastrian theology is "at best the **grandmother**"; and
if the Divine Warrior is the chief influence, Zoroastrianism is "at most a **collateral
relation whose influence is indirect**." This refines the wiki's
[[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|influence page]] on the resurrection line.

## Resurrection vs. Immortality of the Soul

A central, recurring distinction (developed at [[resurrection-of-the-dead|Resurrection of
the Dead]]):

| | **Resurrection of the dead** | **Immortality of the soul** |
|---|---|---|
| Locus | bodily, the whole person | a disembodied indestructible core (soul/spirit) |
| Agency | God's miraculous, gracious act | a natural property of the self |
| Takes death seriously? | Yes — death is real and must be *overcome* | No — death is not finally real for the soul |
| Frame | historical, communal, end-time; bound to **Israel's restoration** | individual, universal, often a-historical |
| Provenance | Israelite/biblical (Divine Warrior; covenant; family) | Greek/Platonic |

Levenson's polemical point: collapsing resurrection into immortality (as modern liberal
theology does) "miss[es] the promissory character of the expectation and its inextricable
connection to a natural family, the Jews." To frame the question as "*Will I* have life
after death?" is already "to get off on the wrong foot."

## Why It Matters for the Wiki

- It is the wiki's fullest treatment of **biblical death-and-afterlife** ([[sheol|Sheol]],
  fortunate/unfortunate death) and of **resurrection's pre-history**.
- It supplies the **Jewish counter-voice** to the [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|
  influence thesis]]: complementary to [[cohn-cosmos-chaos-and-the-world-to-come|Cohn]] (who
  credits Zoroaster with the linear-eschatological *frame*), Levenson roots *resurrection
  itself* in the Canaanite-Israelite Divine Warrior, demoting Persia to indirect influence.
- It corrects the popular notion that resurrection is **"Christian"** rather than centrally
  **Jewish**, and clarifies the resurrection/immortality distinction that the wiki's
  [[resurrection-body|1 Corinthians 15]] controversy also turns on.
