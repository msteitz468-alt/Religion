# Medieval Exegesis: The Four Senses of Scripture (Henri de Lubac)

Henri de Lubac's *Exégèse médiévale* is the definitive modern study of the Christian
**fourfold sense of Scripture** — the [[fourfold-sense|Quadriga]] of literal, allegorical,
tropological, and anagogical senses. Across four volumes (three in English translation) it
recovers the medieval Latin doctrine of the senses not as a "dryasdust collection" of
"artificial distinctions" (as the historiography had treated it) but as the carrier of "a
whole mental universe" — a unified, Christocentric theology of Scripture and history. It is
the sequel to de Lubac's earlier *Histoire et Esprit* (1950), which had studied the same
spiritual exegesis at its patristic fountainhead in [[origen|Origen]]; *Medieval Exegesis*
follows the tradition downstream into the Latin Middle Ages.

## The Central Thesis

De Lubac's controlling claim is that **the doctrine of the senses of Scripture and the
doctrine of the relationship between the two Testaments are, in essence, one and the same
thing**. The four senses are not four arbitrary "compartments" stacked on a text; they are
the articulation of how the one divine economy unfolds — from the *letter* of Old Testament
history (*littera/historia*), through its fulfillment in Christ and the Church
(*allegoria*), into the life of the believing soul (*tropologia*), and toward the
eschatological consummation (*anagogia*). Spiritual understanding is therefore not a
technique applied to the text but the **transformation of the reader** by the reality
(Christ) that the text contains — "an assimilating force that transforms the very
intelligence." This is why de Lubac insists the medieval material betrays its authors if
read only "superficially and in terms solely of their most immediate purport."

## Key Historical Findings

**The distich and its author.** The famous mnemonic — *Littera gesta docet, quid credas
allegoria, / moralis quid agas, quo tendas anagogia* — is usually attributed to Nicholas of
Lyra (who cites it c. 1330 in his *Postilla* on Galatians and his *Prologue* to the *Glossa
ordinaria*, "a passage on which all of the present study will merely be a commentary"). But
its real author is the Dominican **Augustine of Dacia** (a Scandinavian, d. 1282), who put
it at the end of the first chapter of his *Rotulus pugillaris* (c. 1260), a compendium
expounding the first Question of Aquinas's *Summa*. The verse condenses Thomas's teaching;
its variants ("anagogy is what you hope for") do not change the sense.

**Trichotomy vs. tetrachotomy.** De Lubac untangles a real **duality** in the tradition,
present *simultaneously* (not as successive stages): a **threefold** scheme (history /
tropology-morality / allegory-mysticism) descending from [[origen|Origen]] and Jerome, and a
**fourfold** scheme (history, allegory, tropology, anagogy) descending from
[[fourfold-sense|Cassian]], Augustine, Bede, and Rabanus Maurus. Bede in particular floats
between three and four terms depending on occasion; the word *tropologia* shifts meaning
according to the place it occupies in the order — the chief equivocation de Lubac sets out
to resolve.

**The four doctors mapped to the senses.** Medieval catalogers and preaching-manuals
distributed the senses among the great Latin doctors: **Jerome** in history, **Ambrose** in
allegory, **Gregory** in tropology, with **Augustine** presiding over the whole "in the
middle." Godfrey of Saint Victor pictures them as "bridge-builders" helping people cross
"from history to the spiritual sense, or from an ancient people to a new people" — de
Lubac's thesis in an image.

**Tropology's pastoral primacy in preaching.** Theoreticians of preaching (Guibert of
Nogent, Robert de Basevorn, the *Ars praedicandi* tradition) leaned on tropology as the
sense "best adapted to the needs of the interior man," since hearers lack morals more than
faith and an over-subtle allegory can confuse "unsubtle minds."

## The Polemical Frame

De Lubac writes against two adversaries. The first is the **post-Reformation and
Enlightenment dismissal** of allegory: Luther branded the distich's verses "impious" (on
Psalm 22), and most of Protestantism, then the Enlightenment historians (Mosheim,
Rosenmüller), condemned the fourfold sense as a Greek corruption from which "anything that a
person wants can easily be extracted." Catholic theologians (Cardinal Toletus went so far as
to call the doctrine "an article of faith") defended it as "something sprung from Tradition
that is divine itself." The second adversary is a **narrow modern historicism** that, even
when sympathetic, fails to perceive the doctrine's depth. De Lubac's *ressourcement* aim is
to show that spiritual exegesis and historical-critical reading need not be enemies: the
letter is the necessary foundation (the Thomistic *sensus literalis* priority), and the
spiritual senses grow from it.

## Why It Matters for the Wiki

- It is the primary scholarly grounding for [[fourfold-sense|Fourfold Sense (the Quadriga)]]
  and [[allegorical-reading|Allegorical Reading]] (both previously sourced thinly), supplying
  the Augustine-of-Dacia attribution, the trichotomy/tetrachotomy history, and the
  "senses = two Testaments" thesis.
- It connects the Christian Quadriga back to its patristic source in [[origen|Origen]] and
  forward to Thomas Aquinas's literal-sense priority.
- It sharpens the cross-tradition comparison with the Jewish [[pardes|PaRDeS]]: both are
  fourfold, but de Lubac shows the Christian senses are structured by the *Christ-event* and
  the two-Testament relation, not (as in Sod) by a sefirotic reality.
- It documents the **letter-vs-spirit controversy** at its sharpest (Luther's "impious
  verses"), feeding the controversy material on allegory.

## Volume 2: The Four Senses — Their Content and Inner Structure

*(Chapters 6–10 of the French original; ET Vol. 2. Read in full: approximately lines 140–1394.)*

### Chapter 6 — Names and Number of the Biblical Senses

De Lubac traces the vocabulary of the senses — *allegoria, mysterium, sacramentum* — back to Paul
(Gal 4:24; cf. Eph 1:9, 3:3–4, Col 1:27). The decisive move: **Christian allegory did not derive
from pagan/Stoic allegorism**, which *dissolved* the historical reality of what it allegorized (the
Stoics read the Olympian myths as cosmological metaphors that never literally happened — Sallust:
"it is not that these things have ever arrived!"). Christian allegory *presupposes and maintains*
the historical reality of the events it reads as figures. The Christian reads the crossing of the
Red Sea as a type of baptism precisely because the crossing *happened*.

The two formulas (threefold / fourfold) existed *simultaneously* throughout the tradition, not as
successive stages. The fourfold scheme's schematic form was fixed by **Aldhelm of Sherborne** and
**Bede** in the seventh century, with *tropologia* shifting meaning depending on whether it occupies
the third or fourth position.

### Chapter 7 — The Foundation of History

*Historia = littera* is the **permanent foundation** of spiritual understanding, not a scaffold to
be removed after the building is complete. De Lubac assembles a dossier from Origen through the
twelfth century: "first lay the foundation of history." The phrase "the letter that kills" (2 Cor
3:6) does not reject the historical sense; it means a *Judaic* reading that remains stuck at the
letter without perceiving Christ's fulfillment. The two readings are not two senses but two
orientations of the single reader.

Special case: the **Canticle of Canticles**. When the Fathers say there is "no *historia* in the
strict sense" in the Song of Songs, this is literary-genre recognition (the Song is not a narrative),
not a denial that the human love it depicts is real.

### Chapter 8 — Allegory, Sense of the Faith

**Allegoria facti et dicti**: medieval Christian allegory is "of deed AND of word" — the historical
*events* themselves (not merely the prophetic words about them) are figures. The burning bush,
the Passover lamb, the brazen serpent point to Christ not merely as objects described in texts but
as events that *occurred*.

The content of Christian allegory is always, without exception, **Christ and the Church**. De Lubac
specifies three dimensions of this content:

1. **Future** — OT figures point forward to the Christ-event (typology).
2. **Interior** — the mystery has infinite inexhaustible depth; each reading opens further.
3. **Celestial** — the allegorical sense opens onto eternity, not merely onto a historical event.

Christian allegory goes "from history to history" — from the literal history of the OT to the real,
publicly verified history of Christ's life, death, and resurrection. It does *not* move from history
to atemporal abstractions. This is its sharpest contrast with pagan allegorism.

### Chapter 9 — Mystical Tropology

Tropology admits of **two distinguishable types**: (a) simple moral tropology (the text as ethical
instruction for the soul); (b) **mystical tropology** — the *interiorization of the objective
mystery of Christ*, by which what the Spirit accomplished externally in Christ is appropriated
inwardly by the believer.

The key term is **quotidie** ("daily"): the mystery of Christ is not only past (allegory) but
is being fulfilled *every day* within the soul and the Church. Tropology is therefore not mere
moralizing; it is the spiritual participation of the believing subject in the objective drama.

**Church and soul** are in a relation of mutual symbolic identification: what is said of the Church
is said of the soul, and vice versa. A single tropology addresses both dimensions.

**Bernard of Clairvaux** is the culminating instance: "the last of the Fathers." His tropology is
rooted in allegory — the *objective* Mystery of Christ — rather than in subjective introspection.
He can speak of the believer's inner life only because the outer mystery (the Christ-event) is its
real ground.

### Chapter 10 — Anagogy and Eschatology

As with tropology, **anagogy has a twofold form**: (a) doctrinal/eschatological anagogy — the soul
directed toward the final things (resurrection, judgment, eternal life); (b)
contemplative/mystical anagogy — the soul's present ascent to God in contemplation, an anticipation
of eternity within time.

The **three-advent structure** provides the architectural key to the fourfold scheme:

- Christ's *first* (historical) coming = allegory
- Christ's *interior* coming (in the soul and Church) = tropology
- Christ's *eschatological* coming = anagogy

**Unity of the four senses**: Gregory the Great's image of "a wheel within the wheel" — Scripture
grows with the reader who grows with Scripture. The senses are not four independent compartments
but one progressive movement.

**Jerusalem** as the paradigmatic symbol: (1) the historical city = literal; (2) the Church Militant
= allegorical; (3) the faithful soul = tropological; (4) the heavenly city = anagogical.

## Volume 3: History of the Doctrine — Against the "Precursors of Literalism"

*(Chapters 1–6 of Vol. 3. Read in full: approximately lines 1–2191, through the notes.)*

The controlling argument of Vol. 3 is a point-by-point refutation of the thesis of Spicq, Mandonnet,
and especially **Beryl Smalley** (*The Study of the Bible in the Middle Ages*), who claimed that a
progressive "literalist" movement ran from Jerome through the twelfth-century schools to culminate
in Andrew of Saint Victor, preparing the way for modern historical-critical method. De Lubac shows
that every supposed "precursor" was either a thoroughgoing allegorist or was responding to concerns
that had nothing to do with privileging the literal sense over the spiritual.

### Chapter 1 — "The Indignations of Berno"

**Berno of Reichenau** (abbot 1008–1048) had been styled a "precursor of literalism" by Spicq,
based on Berno's *De varia modulatione* and on a formula attributed to him warning readers against
allegorizing everything. De Lubac's refutation: Berno's work was a liturgical tract on Gallican vs.
Roman Psalter usage, not an exegetical manifesto. The warning formula came from Jerome's Matthew
commentary via **Defensor of Liguge's** seventh-century *Scintillae* florilegia — it warned against
reading biblical passages *out of context*, not against allegory. Berno was a thoroughgoing allegorist
who admired Amalarius (the great liturgical allegorist) and used number symbolism freely.

**Gregory the Great's "I do not flee metacism or barbarism"** was similarly misread by later critics
(Barbeyrac etc.) as an anti-intellectual, anti-classical confession. De Lubac demonstrates Gregory
was a refined Latinist who defended classical education; the phrase was a deep theological affirmation
that the Incarnation of the Word "exceeds every rule" — human grammatical categories cannot contain
revealed truth. "We don't follow Donatus" is a theological statement, not obscurantism.

### Chapter 2 — "Subjectivism and Spiritual Understanding"

The patristic formula "Non tuo sensui attemperes Scripturas" ("do not adapt Scripture to your own
sense") was consistently an **anti-heretical** principle, not an anti-allegorical one. The "own
sense" (*proprius sensus*) targeted was the *literalist or subjective* reading of Gnostics,
Marcionites, Manichaeans, and Jews who rejected the spiritual/allegorical reading of the OT.

De Lubac assembles a massive dossier: Origen, Jerome, Augustine, Cassiodorus, Gregory, Bede,
Rabanus Maurus, Alcuin, Bruno of Segni, Rupert of Deutz, Gerhoh, Bernard, Hugh of Saint Victor,
Peter the Venerable, Alan of Lille. **Peter the Venerable** applies the formula directly to Jews in
the phrase *bovinus intellectus* ("cow-like understanding") — the Jew who clings to the literal flesh
of the Torah without perceiving its spiritual body. The point: far from restricting allegory, the
"own sense" formula *required* the allegorical reading as the objectively valid one.

The paradox: **Christian allegory is the most objective reading** because it reads Scripture in light
of the publicly verified Christ-event. Literalism — reading Scripture privately, without the
Christ-key — is the truly "subjective" reading. This is explicitly Augustine's argument against
Faustus the Manichaean: to allegorize the OT is not to "add to" it (Deut 4:2) but to receive it
in the Spirit in which it was given.

### Chapter 3 — "A Lineage Stemming from Jerome?"

Smalley's central claim was that a lineage runs from Jerome's literal-philological method through
specific twelfth-century figures to Hugh of Saint Victor, then to Andrew of Saint Victor and the
full "literalism" of the later medieval schools. De Lubac examines each alleged link:

- **Paschasius Radbertus** (†865): "deeply mystical," obsessively allegorical — his commentary on
  Lamentations applies the text to contemporary disasters through spiritual typology.
- **Christian of Stavelot** (†880): classical two-sense usage, no anti-allegorical tendency.
- **Bruno of Segni** (1045–1123): "best exegete of the Middle Ages" (de Ghellinck); his commentary
  on Job *outstrips* Gregory in allegorism.
- **Rupert of Deutz** (†1135): "whole work stuffed with allegorism" (de Ghellinck).
- **Alcuin**: classical both-senses usage; his *Interrogationes* on Genesis confine themselves to the
  historical sense because that is the genre of the work (a catechism), not because of any
  hermeneutical principle opposing allegory.
- **Stephen Harding** and **Nicholas Maniacoria**: practiced textual criticism (comparing Hebrew
  and Greek manuscripts to correct the Vulgate) — this is *philological* work, not a rejection of
  the spiritual senses.

Conclusion: **Jerome was never invoked against Gregory in the medieval sources**; the two were
always cited together as complementary authorities. The "lineage from Jerome toward literalism"
is a historiographical myth without textual support.

### Chapter 4 — "Hugh of Saint Victor"

Hugh of Saint Victor (c. 1096–1141), the teacher most consistently cited in support of the
"proto-literalist" thesis, is here shown to be a defender of the traditional synthesis.

**Section 2**: Hugh's warnings against *allegorical intemperance* (the image of "over-squeezed
udders" from Proverbs 30:33) are traditional — found in Gregory, Bede, Rabanus Maurus, Rupert of
Deutz, Paschasius, Hugh Metel, Peter Lombard, Peter the Eater. All go back ultimately to
Origen via Gregory. Hugh was criticizing *both* dialectical-rationalist excess (Abelardian
speculation) and allegorical excess with equal force; neither failure was his target exclusively.

**Section 3**: Hugh's elaborate *descriptions* of Noah's Ark were *not* historical-critical
literalism. His *De arca Noe mystica* announces itself as a "mystical description"; he states
explicitly that his descriptions are given "so that in them you may learn wisdom, discipline, and
virtue." Ford Lewis Battles calls Hugh "one of the two great allegorists of his age" alongside
Bernard. De Ghellinck diagnosed Hugh with "an exaggerated predilection for allegory."

**Section 4**: Hugh did *not* "exclude anagogy" from the fourfold scheme. He preferred a
tripartite scheme for symbolic reasons (the Victorine love of triads), but he consistently
distinguished anagogy either as a subdivision of allegory or as the ultimate contemplation beyond
both allegory and tropology. The *Speculum de mysteriis Ecclesiae* (dependent on Hugh) explicitly
counts four senses. Miss Smalley's assertion that "Hugh is criticizing the Gregorian tradition"
is flatly false; Hugh cites Gregory's *Moralia* as "sweeter than all others" (*mihi prae caeteris
dulcia*).

**Section 5**: Hugh's emphasis on "history" was **defensive**, not offensive. He was protecting
the historical foundation of revelation against: (a) Abelardian dialecticians who treated
theology as logical inference rather than reception of revealed facts; (b) cosmologists reading
natural philosophy into Scripture; (c) the nominalist logic that might "de-existentialize" the
facts of salvation. In his *De sacramentis*, a key passage on the progression of revelation is
taken "word for word" from a letter of **Bernard of Clairvaux**. Hugh "maintained" the historical
sense against new threats; he did not innovate a new hermeneutics.

### Chapter 5 — "The Victorine School"

**Andrew of Saint Victor** (†1175?): The genuine exception — Andrew did innovate, making exegesis
a "separate science" and drawing extensively on Jewish interpreters (he was accused of "Judaizing").
Hugh's conservative promise was *not* fulfilled in Andrew.

The other Victorines, however, demonstrate the opposite tendency:
- **Godfrey** (†1194): faithful, traditional, Gregory-admiring.
- **Gautier** (prior of Saint Victor): narrow integrist, cited Jerome >> Gregory, produced what de
  Lubac calls "cramped fundamentalism." "What a distance from Hugh to Gautier! What an abyss!"
- **Garnier**: compiled the *Gregorianum* — itself a demolition of the myth of Saint Victor as
  anti-Gregorian.
- **Adam of Paris** (†1192): traditional allegorical poet, "nourished on the same doctrinal,
  mystical, and symbolic sap."
- **Absalon** (†1203): authentic Victorine — balance, symbolism, Gregorian spirit.

**Parisian Masters** (not Victorines proper):
- **John of Salisbury** (†1180): humanist, four-senses adherent, admired Gregory.
- **Peter the Eater** (†1178): his *Historia scolastica* was *pedagogical specialization* only —
  not a literalist manifesto. His own sermons were "ultra-allegorical." He created a new
  category: the *litteratus/scholaris* as the spiritual elite who can allegorize precisely because
  he has mastered the literal foundation.
- **Peter the Cantor** (†1197): both ultra-allegorical Distinctiones AND moralistic sermons. His
  complaint that "the lifeblood of the Fathers is drying out" is a conservative lament, not a
  literalist program.
- **Stephen Langton** (†1228): faithful to four senses, but sometimes infected by what de Lubac
  calls "bad biblical scholasticism."

**Richard of Saint Victor** on Ezekiel's Temple: Richard defended the literal architectural
coherence of Ezekiel's visionary temple *against* Gregory's purely spiritual reading — but this
was "a very particular case" about one specific text, not a new hermeneutical program. Richard
was "a warm partisan of the fourfold exposition" and a great allegorist; he explicitly opposed
Andrew's "Judaizing."

**Explosion of the Three Disciplines**: Hugh's synthesis inadvertently split exegesis, theology,
and spirituality into autonomous disciplines. The high scholastic synthesis of Thomas and
Bonaventure temporarily re-achieved unity — but only temporarily. The split "would impede
[exegesis's] normal development for a long time."

### Chapter 6 — "Joachim of Flora"

The longest and most complex chapter, focused on Joachim of Flora (c. 1135–1202) and his
relationship to the medieval tradition of interpretation and eschatology.

**Joachim's "Typic Understanding"**: Joachim distinguished two kinds of understanding:
(a) *Spiritual understanding* (the traditional fourfold sense), and (b) **Typic understanding
(*concordia*)** — a *literal* parallelism between the sequence of OT history and the sequence
of Church history. "Concordia litterae" = concord of the letter — Joachim explicitly distinguished
it from spiritual/allegorical understanding: "some think that concord is the same thing as the
spiritual understanding, which properly is called allegory; it is first necessary for us to show
what difference there is between concord and allegory."

**The Three Ages**: Joachim's historical-theological framework:
- Age 1: Father / OT Israel / "science" (scientia)
- Age 2: Son / the Gospel letter / "knowledge of knowledge, letter from letter" (*scientia de
  scientia, littera de littera*)
- Age 3: Holy Spirit / spiritual understanding — "proceeding from both Testaments as the Spirit
  proceeds from Father and Son"

The formula: *duo significantia, unum significatum* (two signifying things, one signified thing).
The third age, initiated by Benedict of Nursia, was about to "fructify" around 1201–1260 CE;
the Cistercian order was its firstfruits; a new contemplative monastic religion would replace
the institutional Church.

**De Lubac's critique**: By making the Gospel letter the second age (= OT letter × Christ), Joachim
dissociates the Spirit from Christ. Gilson: "to preach a gospel of the Holy Spirit was completely to
reverse the economy of the ages of the world." Fournier: "the essential organs of Christian society
had been gravely threatened." Thomas Aquinas (*Summa*) directly opposed Joachim: "No status of the
present life can be more perfect than the status of the new law." Bonaventure: "After the New
Testament there will not be another one." Joachim was personally orthodox (he submitted all his
works to papal judgment, condemned the Cathars and the Greek schism), but his system was structurally
aberrant.

**Alleged "antecedents" refuted**: (a) Origen's "Eternal Gospel" ≠ Joachim's: for Origen it meant
either the spiritual comprehension of *one* Gospel by spiritual vs. carnal Christians, or the perfect
knowledge reserved for eternity — never a third earthly age. (b) Hugh of Saint Victor's "three
days" (fear / truth / charity) = interpenetrating spiritual states within the Christian life, not
successive historical ages. (c) Rupert of Deutz's trinitarian division = Creation (Father) /
Redemption (Son) / Sanctification-to-resurrection (Spirit) = the traditional Creed structure;
Rupert "positively excludes" Joachim's third age.

**Traditional Apocalypse commentary vs. Joachim**: The medieval tradition consistently read the
seven seals of the Apocalypse as *all opened at once* by Christ's first coming — the whole mystery
of salvation revealed in one stroke. "He broke the seven seals of that same book when he unlocked
the understanding of sacred eloquence for the minds of the faithful" (Bernard of Clairvaux). The
"time of the Church" is the single sixth age, unsubdivided in kind, homogeneous in quality
(all under the sign of the completed redemption), running from the Incarnation to the Last Judgment.
Progressive subdivision into seven "states" was an accepted literary genre of spiritual/moral
exhortation (Bede, Anselm of Havelberg, Richard of Saint Victor), but always remained *qualitative*
and *spiritual* — not Joachim's literal-chronological correspondence between OT history and Church
history feature for feature.

**The Antichrist tradition**: Medieval usage made "Antichrist" a common noun, applied to any
persecutor, heretic, or hypocritical prelate from Cain onward. The tradition consistently opposed
calculating the date of the end: "No one knows the day or hour" (Claudius of Turin, Paschasius
Radbertus, Hugh of Saint Victor, Martin of Leon). Gerhoh of Reichersberg wrote explicitly in his
*De investigatione Antichristi*: "I know by whom it was said that 'it is not our business to know
the times or moments.'"

Joachim, by contrast, drew up a specific historical list of the six precursors of the last Antichrist
(Herod, Nero, Constantius, Mohammed, Mesmot, Saladin = six heads of the dragon), with the seventh
(= first proper "Antichrist") still to come, and then the eighth = "the last and biggest." This
specificity and chronological exactitude is entirely his own.

**Sources of Joachim's system**: (a) progressive-revelation schemes (Anselm of Havelberg building on
Gregory Nazianzus): none went as far as a third earthly age. (b) Monastic circles exalting their
"order" as a new, superior level of the Christian life (Odo of Canterbury, William of Ramsey, Adam
Scotus): all stopped short of Joachim's system; William of Ramsey immediately qualified his
trinitarian schema. (c) Popular "parallelism" between OT and NT history (Honorius, Gerhoh): episodic
and not systematized into Joachimite concordia.

**Paul Alvarus of Cordoba** (854) is the critical contrast case: Paul applied OT prophecy about the
Antichrist to Mohammed, but stopped himself and returned to the Tyconian principle of the "transplanting"
of images — one prophecy holds good for multiple ages (*species* pointing to *genus*). Alvarus
invokes Jerome and Gregory together. Joachim reads the same material as specific chronological
prediction: Mohammed occupies a precise slot in the numbered sequence of the dragon's seven heads.

**De Lubac's closing assessment of Joachim**: "This man was great" — comparable in spiritual gifts
to Rupert of Deutz. He lacked the cultural formation that would have held his gifts in check. His
error was to "project in space and time the symbols that the meditation of Scripture caused to spring
up in his heart." His positive impulse: he "thirsted for the Spirit," struggled against the "deadly
desiccation" he saw threatening the Church in both its pastors and its scholars. The two equally
dangerous errors, de Lubac concludes, are: (1) dreaming of a Kingdom of the Spirit that outpaces
Christ, and (2) imagining a Christ who always tugs back *this side* of the Spirit. "Christ is capable
of embracing everything and his Spirit is capable of renewing everything."
