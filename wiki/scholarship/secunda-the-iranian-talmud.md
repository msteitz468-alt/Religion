# Secunda, *The Iranian Talmud: Reading the Bavli in Its Sasanian Context*

A field-defining monograph by **[[shai-secunda|Shai Secunda]]** — a Talmudist trained in Iranian
studies — arguing that the **[[talmud-bavli|Babylonian Talmud]]** (the Bavli) cannot be properly
understood apart from the **Sasanian Iranian** world in which it was produced, and in particular apart
from the **Middle Persian (Pahlavi) literature** of its **[[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]]** neighbors. It
is the wiki's foundational source for the comparative subfield **[[talmudo-iranica|Talmudo-Iranica]]**,
and the counterpart, on the Jewish-studies side, to the wiki's existing
[[skjaervo-spirit-of-zoroastrianism|Skjærvø]] (Zoroastrian-philology) source.

## The Problem the Book Addresses

Since the Middle Ages the Bavli has sat "at the nerve center of the Jewish canon" and was studied as a
**self-enclosed universe** — Secunda opens with H. N. Bialik's image of the yeshiva student "facing the
wall." While critical scholars have long read the Palestinian Talmud and Midrash against their
**Greco-Roman** context, study of the Bavli's **Iranian** context "petered out" after WWII. Secunda
takes his cue from **Yaakov Elman**, who at the turn of the 21st century revived the question of Persian
cultural impact on the Bavli. The book argues "the obvious": the Bavli is a **Sasanian** document and
must be read alongside its non-Jewish neighbors' texts.

Opening illustrations of what is lost when the context is ignored:

- **Rav Naḥman vs. Rav Yehuda** (b. Qiddushin 70a–b): Elman reads Rav Yehuda's mockery of Rav Naḥman not
  as mere pique but as a critique of an **acculturated, upper-class Sasanian** rabbi — Rav Naḥman serves
  citrons and unmixed wine, uses the Middle Iranian words *atrunga* and *anbaga*, lets his daughter
  **Dēnag** (an upper-class Zoroastrian theophoric name) serve guests. A *Kulturkampf* of accommodation
  (Maḥoza, near the capital Ctesiphon) vs. resistance (provincial Pumbedita).
- **Returnable gifts**: Rav Naḥman's innovation permitting a *temporary* gift of the four species
  (Tabernacles) parallels the developed system of **temporary ownership** in the Sasanian law book
  *Mādayān ī Hazār Dādestān* ("Book of a Thousand Judgments").
- **Ifra Hormiz** (b. Nidda 20b): the Sasanian queen-mother sends menstrual-blood samples to **Rava** to
  test rabbinic wisdom — unintelligible until read against the **competition between Jews and Zoroastrians
  over menstrual purity**.

## The Book's Program (chapter by chapter)

1. **The Sea of Talmud and Its Shore** — surveys the textual and material remains a "Talmudo-Iranicist"
   must learn: the Bavli, the Pahlavi (Middle Persian) corpus, Syriac Christian literature, Manichaean
   and Mandaean writings, and the **Aramaic incantation bowls**.
2. **In the Temple and Synagogue** — maps where Jews and Persians actually met in Sasanian Mesopotamia:
   everyday market encounters, a partly shared Aramaic linguistic world (with Iranian loanwords, calques,
   and legal/theological terms entering Jewish Babylonian Aramaic), **intermarriage and conversion**, and
   intellectual contact — including the warning that "he who learns something from a *magus* is worthy of
   death" (b. Shabbat 75a), read as backhanded evidence that Jews *were* studying Zoroastrian recitation
   (*amgushta*/"Magianism") with the magi.
3. **Constructing "Them"** — the mutual **"discourses of the Other."** Both communities shared a stark
   **social dualism** (Zoroastrian *ēr* / *an-ēr*; the rabbinic Jew/*goy* binary). The Bavli repeatedly
   **subverts older, pro-Persian Palestinian traditions** — Rav Yosef turns praise of Persian modesty
   and of Cyrus into derision ("Persians… like a bear"; Cyrus's "messiah" status downgraded).
4. **Closer Than They May Appear** — a *concurrent* set of texts that, paradoxically, draw the
   Zoroastrian "Other" **nearer**: magi and rabbis listed together as "those who hate each other"; rabbis
   as "ministering angels" *and* magi as "destroying angels" on a single comparative plane; the rabbinic
   reciter (*tanna*) likened to the mumbling magus (*ratin magosha*) — the two priestly/scholarly classes
   mirror one another.
5. **In Iran** — the methodological payoff (see [[talmudo-iranica|Talmudo-Iranica]]): a taxonomy of
   parallels and a move **"beyond influence"** toward reading the two corpora intertextually across a
   shared Sasanian **"text-scape."**

## Key Methodological Argument — "Beyond Influence"

Secunda's central contribution is to discipline the old hunt for one-directional **"influence."** Drawing
on **Jonathan Z. Smith** (on comparison) and **Michael Satlow** ("Beyond Influence"), he catalogs ways a
parallel can arise — "false friends" (Hebrew *manna* ≠ Oceanic *mana*), shared widespread practice
(menstrual taboo), shared circumstance, direct borrowing, **joint participation in a broader cultural
project**, and a "subtle interplay of reception, incorporation, rejection, and reaction"
(*ecotypification*). Because the Bavli and Pahlavi texts are each **insular** and rarely intersect
directly, he proposes reading them as producing meaning in the **same discursive space**, using theories
of **intertextuality** rather than chasing irretrievable channels of transmission.

The **visions of hell** case study exemplifies this (see
[[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|the influence page]]): the punishment of the spies at
b. Soṭa 35a (elongated tongues gnawed by worms) is paralleled in the Middle Persian *Ardā Wirāz nāmag* (a
"Zoroastrian *Divina Commedia*"), yet most of its elements are *already present* in the Palestinian
midrash *Ecclesiastes Rabba* — so the Bavli's imagery is neither pure borrowing nor purely inner-Jewish,
but a Palestinian inheritance **reshaped on a Zoroastrian template.**

## Hermeneutical Framework

**Historical-critical, source-critical, and philological** Talmud study (distinguishing tannaitic from
amoraic layers and the anonymous redactional *Stam*), wedded to **Iranian/Pahlavi philology**, and framed
by **comparative-religions theory** (J. Z. Smith; intertextuality). A standing caution runs throughout:
the **dating of Pahlavi literature** is treacherous (many texts written down only in the 9th c. CE), so
"swift and superficial contextualization" in an Iranian milieu is as dangerous as ignoring Iran entirely.

## Why It Matters for the Wiki

- Supplies the **Sasanian context** missing from the [[talmud-bavli|Bavli]] and
  [[rabbinic-judaism|Rabbinic Judaism]] pages: the Talmud as a product of a religiously plural Iranian
  world, not a hermetic legal universe.
- **Refines** the wiki's [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|Zoroastrian-influence]] page by
  replacing crude "influence" with a calibrated comparative method — and adds a concrete late-antique
  (not just Persian-period) case of rabbinic–Zoroastrian textual interaction.
- Grounds new pages on [[talmudo-iranica|Talmudo-Iranica]] and the [[magi|magi]] as a rabbinic foil.
