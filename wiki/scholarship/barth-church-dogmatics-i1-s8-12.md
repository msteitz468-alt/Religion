# Church Dogmatics I.1, §§8–12 (Karl Barth)

**Source**: `raw/scholarship/Church Dogmatics, Vol. 1.1, Sections 8-12 The Doctrine of the Word of God, Study Edition 2 (Karl Barth).txt`
**Original**: *Kirchliche Dogmatik* I.1 (Theologischer Verlag Zürich, 1932–1938); English Study Edition, T&T Clark, 2009

## Overview

§§8–12 constitute the second half of Vol. I.1 and the substantive doctrinal core of Barth's Prolegomena: after establishing what the Word of God is (§§3–7), Barth now asks who speaks it — and the answer is the Triune God. The doctrine of the Trinity, in Barth's handling, is not a speculative appendage but is derived directly from the structure of revelation itself: God is simultaneously Revealer, Revelation, and Revealedness (the "vestigium Trinitatis" of revelation). §§8–12 form a self-contained presentation of the doctrine of the Trinity that precedes and conditions all subsequent doctrinal work in the Church Dogmatics.

This is Barth's most sustained engagement with classical Trinitarian doctrine (Nicene, Cappadocian, Augustinian, Thomistic) as well as with modern critiques (Schleiermacher's subordination of Trinity to piety, Hegel's speculative Trinity).

### §8. God in His Revelation (lines 70–2532)

§8 opens with a thesis statement that functions as the architectural premise of everything that follows: "God's Word is God Himself in His revelation. For God reveals Himself as the Lord and according to Scripture this signifies for the concept of revelation that God Himself in unimpaired unity yet also in unimpaired distinction is Revealer, Revelation, and Revealedness" (lines 72–75). This threefold structure — Revealer / Revelation / Revealedness — is the conceptual pivot from which the entire doctrine of the Trinity is derived.

**§8.1: The Place of the Doctrine of the Trinity in Dogmatics (lines 76–476)**

Barth's first move is to justify placing the doctrine of the Trinity at the very head of dogmatics — a position he acknowledges is historically isolated (line 288), noting that only Peter Lombard's *Sentences* and Bonaventura's *Breviloquium* adopted a similar arrangement in the medieval period. The standard pattern, followed even by Melanchthon and Calvin (line 303), treats Scripture, then God's existence and attributes, and reaches the Trinity only later. Barth argues that this procedure is incoherent: "It is hard to see how what is distinctive for this God can be made clear if ... the question who God is, which it is the business of the doctrine of the Trinity to answer, is held in reserve, and the first question to be treated is that of the That and the What of God, as though these could be defined otherwise than on the presupposition of the Who" (lines 312–317). In support he quotes Calvin against Calvin: Calvin himself had written in the *Institutio* (I.13.21) that we must conceive of God as He has disclosed Himself to us and not inquire about Him from anywhere else than His Word (lines 319–339). 

The driving argument of §8.1 is that the biblical concept of revelation forces three questions simultaneously: Who is the self-revealing God? What does He do? What does He effect in those to whom He reveals Himself? Barth insists that in Scripture these three questions cannot be answered in isolation: "The answer to any one of the questions, for all the autonomy and distinctiveness it has ... is essentially identical with the answer to the other two" (lines 100–102). The formula "God reveals Himself. He reveals Himself through Himself. He reveals Himself" (line 103) encapsulates this. Barth defends this formula against critics who charged him with a "grammatical and rationalistic proof of the Trinity" (lines 114–116) derived from the subject-predicate-object structure of language; he replies that this formula was never intended as a proof but as "the subsequent formulation of something presented by the Bible" (lines 265–267). The doctrine of the Trinity is "what basically distinguishes the Christian doctrine of God as Christian" (lines 351–352).

**§8.2: The Root of the Doctrine of the Trinity (lines 477–1826)**

This is the longest and philosophically most complex subsection. Barth's central claim: revelation itself, as attested in Scripture, is the *root* (*Wurzel*) of the doctrine of the Trinity — meaning the doctrine grows from and is justified by the structure of revelation, not from Scripture proof-texts containing trinitarian language, and not from natural theology or philosophical inference.

Barth opens by defining revelation as "God's own direct speech which is not to be distinguished from the act of speaking and therefore is not to be distinguished from God Himself" — "revelation is *Dei loquentis persona*" (lines 491–494, Latin: "the person of God speaking"). He distinguishes the immediacy of revelation from the mediated character of Scripture and proclamation: "revelation does not have to become [God's Word]. The fulness of the original self-existent being of God's Word reposes and lives in it" (lines 545–547).

To call revelation the "root" of the Trinity means two things (lines 653–803). *Negatively*, the trinitarian dogma is not identical with Scripture; it is the Church's analysis of revelation, "Church exegesis" in different concepts than the biblical text. Barth notes that "already in the early Church the doctrine of the Trinity was attacked on the ground that it is not biblical" and affirms this (lines 677–680), while insisting — with Calvin and Thomas Aquinas — that faithful exposition of Scripture must use concepts alien to the original text in order to counter errors that arise in new historical situations (lines 684–727). *Positively*, to call revelation the root is to say the dogma is "indirectly, though not directly, identical" with the biblical witness — a necessary and well-grounded interpretation, not an arbitrary speculation (lines 728–803).

The substantive derivation of the Trinity from revelation occupies the central portion of §8.2. Barth traces three aspects of the biblical concept of revelation: (1) *Unveiling / Form* — God "takes form" (*nimmt Gestalt an*) in His self-disclosure; in the Old Testament this appears in the "hypostases" of Yahweh's name, Word, and Spirit, culminating in the "angel of Yahweh" who is both identical with and distinct from Yahweh (lines 1057–1131). In the New Testament, God's taking form reaches its irreversible consummation in the existence of Jesus of Nazareth: "the form here is that of the *humanitas Christi*" (line 1351). This moment of unveiling points to God the *Son*: "That He reveals Himself as the Son is what is primarily meant when we say that He reveals Himself as the Lord" (lines 1234–1236). (2) *Veiling / Freedom* — the God who reveals Himself is "the Deus revelatus who is the Deus absconditus" (line 1257), whose "presence is always God's decision to be present" (line 1283). Even in assuming form God remains sovereign over revelation: "The divine Word is the divine speaking. The divine gift is the divine giving. God's self-unveiling remains an act of sovereign divine freedom" (lines 1283–1285). This permanent freedom, this "second time in a different way" that "does not really prevent Him from remaining the same" (lines 1402–1404), points to God the *Father*: "God the Father is God who always, even in taking form in the Son, does not take form, God as the free ground and the free power of His being God in the Son" (lines 1419–1421). (3) *Impartation / Historicity* — revelation is always "imparted to men" in a specific historical event. The crucial term here is *pneuma* (line 1719): "The *pneuma* is the miracle of the presence of real men at God's revelation." Barth contrasts the *historical* (*geschichtlich*) character of revelation — definite event in definite circumstances — with both the *historicist* (*historisch*) "neutral observer" mode (lines 1440–1454) and the mythological mode (lines 1543–1623). Myth, unlike saga, "does not intend to be history but only pretends to be such" (line 1545); it expresses timeless anthropological truths. Biblical revelation is irreducibly particular — "Revelation comes vertically from heaven" (line 1653) — and this particularity points to the Spirit as God's third mode of being: "God reveals Himself as the Spirit, not as any spirit ... but as the Spirit of the Father and the Son" (lines 1760–1762). Barth summarizes: "if we have rightly emphasised in the biblical witness to revelation the three elements of unveiling, veiling and impartation, or form, freedom and historicity, or Easter, Good Friday and Pentecost, or Son, Father and Spirit ... then we may now conclude that revelation must indeed be understood as the root or ground of the doctrine of the Trinity" (lines 1780–1789).

A long historical excursus surveys the biblical passages that reflect trinitarian structure (lines 871–950) — Is. 61:1, Mt. 28:19, Rom. 1:1–4, Rom. 11:36, 2 Thess. 2:13, 1 Pet. 1:2, Rev. 1:4, 2 Cor. 13:13, Mk. 1:9–11 — noting that the classic trinitarian "order" (Father-Son-Spirit) is less historically fundamental than the order starting with the Son, since "the history of the doctrine of the Trinity out of the witness to revelation followed this route" (line 1003).

**§8.3: Vestigium Trinitatis (lines 1826–2494)**

The third subsection is a critical-polemical treatment of the tradition of finding "traces of the Trinity" (*vestigia Trinitatis*) in creaturely reality. Barth defines the concept: "an analogue of the Trinity, of the trinitarian God of Christian revelation, in some creaturely reality distinct from Him, a creaturely reality which is not a form assumed by God in His revelation, but which quite apart from God's revelation manifests in its own structure by creation a certain similarity to the structure of the trinitarian concept of God" (lines 1843–1848). He traces the concept to Augustine's *De Trinitate* IX–XI (lines 1849–1856) and surveys five domains in which vestigia have been identified:

(1) *Nature* — Anselm's spring/stream/lake analogy for the Nile as an image of the Trinity (lines 1940–1949); Luther's examples from sun, water, stars, plants (lines 1950–1959); three dimensions of solids, primary colours, musical harmony (lines 1957–1960). (2) *Culture* — teachers/soldiers/producers; epic/lyric/drama; Luther's Grammatica (Father), Dialectica (Son), Rhetorica (Holy Ghost) from his *Table Talk* (lines 1963–1968). (3) *History* — the three-kingdom schema of Joachim of Fiore (Petrine/age of fear, Pauline/age of truth, Johannine/age of love), which Barth notes was secularised by Moeller van den Bruck into the concept of "the Third Reich" (lines 1970–1994). (4) *Religion* — Wobbermin's three religious motifs (dependence, security, longing); non-Christian triads in Babylonian, Egyptian, Canaanite, Indian (Trimurti), and Buddhist religion (lines 1995–2019). (5) *The Human Soul* — Augustine's *mens/notitia/amor* and *memoria/intellectus/voluntas* (lines 2020–2068), which Barth identifies as the most influential and most dangerous form of the doctrine, directly linked to Schelling's subject/object/subject-object and Hegel's thesis/antithesis/synthesis (lines 2060–2064).

Barth's evaluation proceeds in three stages. First, he notes that the tradition itself was self-limiting: Irenaeus, Augustine, Peter Lombard, Thomas Aquinas, and Quenstedt all qualified the vestigia as supplementary illustrations, *not* proofs, operative only *trinitate posita* — "given the Trinity" (lines 2090–2101, 2149). Second, Barth argues that the fundamental problem is not the illustrations themselves but the collapse of the distinction between *interpretation* and *illustration* of revelation (lines 2384–2407). Illustration presupposes that revelation needs external confirmation — it already implies a loss of trust in revelation's self-evidential force and is tantamount to desertion of revelation. Third, Barth argues that when the vestigia tradition is pursued seriously it leads not to the Triune God of Scripture but to some other "god," variously the cosmos as divine law (Babylonian and Indian triads), the family relation (Egyptian and Etruscan triads), or the deified historical process (Buddhist three jewels) (lines 2250–2269). Above all the Augustinian argument from consciousness, taken seriously, produces not the Trinity but free self-determining humanity — Luther already saw this (lines 2280–2298), and "the development of anthropological speculation by way of Descartes and Kant to Schelling and Hegel and finally and logically Feuerbach has clearly justified him" (line 2289). Quenstedt is quoted approvingly: "*nulla vera et plena similitudo trinitatis in creaturis reperitur*" — "no true and complete likeness of the Trinity is to be found in creatures" (line 2332).

Barth's conclusion is not a simple prohibition but a qualified *No*: the finders of vestigia were not heretical in intention, and the vestigia tradition has a legitimate root in the problem of theological *language* — how to speak about the Trinity in human words at all. The error lies in reversing the direction: "not that they tried to explain the Trinity by the world but on the contrary that they tried to explain the world by the Trinity in order to be able to speak about the Trinity in this world" would have been legitimate; but what actually happened was the reverse — "polemics becoming apologetics" (line 2226), the world claiming revelation rather than revelation claiming the world (lines 2193–2213).

Barth closes §8 with a positive re-statement of the *one* true vestigium trinitatis: the form which God Himself has assumed in His revelation, present to us in the threefold form of the Word — revelation, Holy Scripture, and proclamation. "This is how God is present for us in His revelation. This is how He Himself obviously creates a vestigium of Himself and His triunity" (lines 2481–2482). This vestigium is not found by analysis of creaturely structure but is identical with the form God takes in His own self-disclosure. It is, Barth says, better described as *vestigium creaturae in trinitate* — trace of the creature in the Trinity — rather than the reverse (lines 2491–2493).

**Key interlocutors in §8**: Calvin (extensively on placing Trinity first and on the *who* question), Augustine (*De Trinitate* and *Confessions*), Anselm, Luther (Table Talk on vestigia), Bonaventura, Peter Lombard, Thomas Aquinas, Melanchthon, Schleiermacher (on the Trinity as inaccessible to religious self-consciousness), Harnack (history of dogma), Hegel and Feuerbach (as the logical outcome of the Augustinian consciousness argument), Joachim of Fiore, Wobbermin, Moeller van den Bruck. Orthodox Lutheran figures cited extensively in footnotes: Chemnitz, Gerhard, König, Keckermann, Calov, Turrettini, Quenstedt.

### §9. The Triunity of God (lines 2533–4264)

§9 carries the constructive argument that the Trinity is the necessary conceptual form of the revelation analyzed in §8. It has four sub-sections.

**1. Unity in Trinity (lines 2581–2782).** Barth opens by identifying God's *lordship* (established in §8 as the basic biblical concept) with what the patristic tradition calls the divine *essence* — *deitas, divinitas, ousia, essentia, natura, substantia* (ll. 2584–86). The unity of this essence is not merely compatible with the threeness of "persons" but is constituted by it: "its unity consists in the threeness of the 'persons'" (ll. 2592–93). The three are not three gods, nor three parts of one God, but "the one God in threefold repetition" (ll. 2604–08). Barth insists: we distinguish *alius-alius-alius* (one-another-another) but not *aliud-aliud-aliud* (one thing–another thing–another thing) (ll. 2610–11; citing Fulgentius). The trinitarian formulae (*Deus est Trinitas*) must always be glossed: *non triplex sed trina* — "not triple but threefold" (Council of Toledo IX, l. 2600).

A key polemical move: all antitrinitarianism — including Arianism, Pneumatomachian subordinationism, and Modalist Monarchianism — fails precisely *as* monotheism, not merely as a departure from orthodoxy. Arianism "does violence to the divine unity by the very adoration" it pays the creature (ll. 2731); Modalism distinguishes the *τρόπος ἀποκαλύψεως* (mode of revelation) from the *τρόπος ὑπάρξεως* (mode of existence), so that "God in His revelation is not really God" (ll. 2754–56). Conclusion: "Only the substantial equality of Christ and the Spirit with the Father is compatible with monotheism" (ll. 2777–78).

**2. Trinity in Unity (lines 2782–3481).** This section addresses what the "three" are — Augustine's question *quid tres?* Barth surveys the term *persona*/*hypostasis* and concludes it has never been satisfactorily clarified. Augustine spoke of using *tres personae* simply as a *necessitas loquendi* — "not in order to say that the three in God are precisely *personae* but in order to say with the help of the term *personae* that there are three in God" (citing *De Trinitate* V, VII; ll. 2916–18). Anselm called them *tres nescio quid* — "three I know not what" (ll. 2920–21). The concept of "personality" imported by the 19th century — self-conscious, individually willing subjects — leads straight to tritheism (ll. 3028–47); Neo-Protestant theology tried to escape by reducing the Trinity to an economic phenomenology of three persons as manifestations, but this risks Sabellianism or produces a hidden Fourth behind the three (ll. 3062–79).

Barth's constructive proposal: replace *person* with **"mode (or way) of being"** (*Seinsweise*, translating Greek *hypostasis* as *subsistentia* = mode of existence, not *substantia*): "the statement that God is One in three ways of being, Father, Son and Holy Ghost, means, therefore, that the one God, i.e. the one Lord, the one personal God, is what He is not just in one mode but … in the mode of the Father, in the mode of the Son, and in the mode of the Holy Ghost" (ll. 3125–30). *Seinsweise* is the literal translation of *τρόπος ὑπάρξεως* or the Latin *modus entitativus* (ll. 3131–33). The rationale: the *truly material determinations* of the threeness were always derived in the tradition not from the concept of person but from **relations** — and "modes of being" makes these relations central rather than obscuring them behind a person-concept with tritheistic overtones (ll. 3108–17).

Barth develops the doctrine of **intradivine relations**: from Tertullian through the Cappadocians, Augustine, Anselm, Thomas, and Calvin, the three modes of being are constituted by their distinctive *relations of origin* — fatherhood (*paternitas*), sonship (*filiatio*), and procession (*processio* / *spiratio passiva*). Luther's summary (Von den letzten Worten Davids, 1543) is quoted at length (ll. 3403–25). Thomas's formula: *paternitas est persona Patris, filiatio persona Filii, processio persona Spiritus sancti procedentis* (l. 3451). In Anselm's formula: *In divinis omnia sunt unum, ubi non obviat relationis oppositio* — "In the divine all are one, where the opposition of relationship does not stand in the way" (ll. 3394–95; confirmed at Florence 1441). God "possesses Himself as Father, i.e., pure Giver, as Son, i.e., Receiver and Giver, and as Spirit, i.e., pure Receiver" (ll. 3362–63). The section closes with characteristic humility: *tres nescio quid* remains the only final answer; "all rational wrestling with this mystery, the more serious it is, can lead only to its fresh and authentic interpretation and manifestation as a mystery" (ll. 3563–66).

**3. Triunity (lines 3570–3924).** Barth prefers *Dreieinigkeit* ("Triunity") to *Dreifaltigkeit* ("Trinity") because it holds both numerals together and stresses the *becoming* one as well as the *being* one (ll. 3584–91). Two corollaries:

- **Perichoresis** (*circumincessio*/*circuminsessio*): Following John of Damascus (*Ekdosis* I, 8 and 14), the divine modes of being "mutually condition and permeate one another so completely that one is always in the other two and the other two in the one" (ll. 3660–61). This "final sum" (quoting Pohle, l. 3698) of both unity-in-trinity and trinity-in-unity is developed through Fulgentius and Anselm: "the whole Father is in the Son and the Holy Spirit … No one of them is outside of any of the others" (ll. 3680–84).

- **Opera trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt** (The outward works of the Trinity are undivided): Materially from Augustine (*De Trinitate* I, 4), confirmed at Florence (ll. 3909–24). God's work as Creator, Reconciler, and Redeemer is one act, "simultaneously and in concert in all His three modes of being" (l. 3898). Distinctions between works are possible only per *appropriationem* — certain works have "affinity" to certain modes and so are appropriated to those persons in the Church's language (ll. 3846–73). Appropriations must be intelligible, non-exclusive, and derived from Scripture rather than invented.

**4. The Meaning of the Doctrine of the Trinity (lines 3925–4264).** Barth resists reducing the early Church's trinitarian work to Byzantine politics, Neo-Platonism, or ancient mystery piety (ll. 3963–4051). The doctrine answers one specific question about revelation: *who* is the One who reveals Himself? "One may sum up the meaning of the doctrine of the Trinity briefly and simply by saying that God is the One who reveals Himself" (ll. 4124–25). The doctrine has a double polemical front:

- Against **Subordinationism**: Father, Son, and Spirit are equally and fully God — "The subject of revelation attested in the Bible … is the one Lord, not a demi-god" (ll. 4173–74). Subordinationism ultimately "means the denial of revelation, the drawing of divine subjectivity into human subjectivity" (l. 4195).
- Against **Modalism**: Father, Son, and Spirit are genuinely ultimate in God, not phenomenal appearances behind a hidden Fourth. "When we ask about God, we can only ask about the One who reveals Himself" (l. 4208). Modalism "finally entails a denial of God" (l. 4221).

Positive statement: "The doctrine of the Trinity tells us … how far the One who reveals Himself according to the witness of Scripture can in fact be our God and how far He can in fact be our God. He can be our God because in all His modes of being He is equal to Himself, one and the same Lord" (ll. 4240–42). "As Father, Son and Spirit God is, so to speak, ours in advance" (ll. 4248–49).

### §10. God the Father (lines 4265–4929)

§10 applies the trinitarian framework to the first mode of being and has two sub-sections.

**1. God as Creator (lines 4270–4565).** Barth examines what "God the Father" means in the biblical witness before asking what the doctrine of the Trinity adds. The New Testament shows that the Father is primarily the God of Jesus — the one to whom Jesus prays, from whom he is sent, to whom he submits, and who raises him from the dead. The Synoptics especially, and Pauline texts (1 Cor. 8:6; 15:24–28; Eph. 1:17), consistently set the Father *above* and *beyond* Jesus (ll. 4337–44). Harnack is quoted approvingly: "Aim, power, insight, result, and stern compulsion — all come to Him from the Father" (l. 4333).

The Father's manward will is revealed not primarily as genial affirmation but in "a radical questioning and indeed abrogation of" human existence (ll. 4413–14): it is the God of the cross and resurrection, encountered at the frontier of death. "We sum all this up by saying that God the Father … is the Lord of our existence. He is this in the strict sense to the degree that He is the Lord over the life and death of man" (ll. 4504–06). And "the Lord of existence is the Creator" (l. 4518): our existence is "sustained by Him and by Him alone above the abyss of non-existence … It has an Author who calls into existence and sustains in existence out of free goodness … It has … a Creator" (ll. 4520–32). The name "father" from human life is only an analogy that must at once be transcended: "God our Father means God our Creator" (l. 4551). Crucially, God as Creator is not a natural truth but a truth of revelation — only through Christ the Crucified and Risen does the analogy acquire meaning that transcends its creaturely register (ll. 4553–64).

**2. The Eternal Father (lines 4566–4929).** The decisive dogmatic move: "God as the Father of Jesus Christ can be our Father because even apart from the fact that He reveals Himself as such He already is the One He reveals Himself to be, namely, the Father of Jesus Christ, His Son, who as such is Himself God" (ll. 4570–73). The New Testament's exclusivity — that the Father is known *only* through the Son (Mt. 11:27; Jn. 14:7–9; 1 Jn. 2:23) — forbids any abstraction of revelation's content from its form in Jesus. It follows that the fatherhood revealed in Jesus presupposes an *eternal* fatherhood in God: a fatherhood that "applies previously to Jesus, and therefore … corresponds to a sonship in Jesus, irrespective of what it means for us" (ll. 4651–53). God is "antecedently Father in Himself" (ll. 4659–60).

Barth draws on Cyril of Jerusalem: "Before every substance and before every perception, before time and before all ages, God possessed the quality of fatherhood" (ll. 4693–94). The eternal name "Father" is the *proper* use of the term; human fatherhood and even God's relation to creatures as Creator are improper but not untrue applications: "God alone as He who He is by Himself, and therefore as the eternal Father of His eternal Son, is properly and adequately to be called Father" (ll. 4704–06). "God's trinitarian name of Father … denotes the mode of being of God in which He is the Author of His other modes of being" (ll. 4717–19); He is *fons et origo totius divinitatis* — font and origin of the whole divinity (Council of Toledo XI, 675, Denz. 275; l. 4720) — though this origination does not imply superiority, since Son and Spirit are *homoousios* with the Father.

Two corollaries: (1) Because the eternal Trinity is undivided, creation is not the Father's work *alone* but the work of Father, Son, and Spirit together — *opera trinitatis ad extra sunt indivisa* (l. 4783). This forbids Enlightenment one-sided Deism, Pietistic Christocentrism, and isolated Spirit-veneration (ll. 4805–10). (2) The appropriation of creation especially to the Father is nonetheless commanded, not merely permitted — it expresses true though non-exhaustive knowledge, pointing to the "affinity between the relation of the Father to the Son … and the relation of the Creator to the creature" (ll. 4880–82). The eternal independence of the modes of being means that modalist conclusions (e.g., Patripassianism) are absolutely forbidden: "God the Father was conceived and born, … suffered, died and rose again" cannot be said (l. 4890). The section closes by affirming that the knowledge of the Father as Creator must remain *particular* knowledge even within its unity with the knowledge of Son and Spirit: "Only when it is taken seriously in its particularity does it lead to that awareness" (ll. 4927–28).

### §11. God the Son (lines 4930–7379)

§11 opens with the formal thesis: "The one God reveals Himself according to Scripture as the Reconciler, i.e., as the Lord in the midst of our enmity towards Him. As such He is the Son of God who has come to us or the Word of God that has been spoken to us, because He is so antecedently in Himself as the Son or Word of God the Father" (lines 4932–4936). The section has two sub-divisions: §11.1 "God as Reconciler" and §11.2 "The Eternal Son."

**§11.1 God as Reconciler (lines 4938–5692)**

Barth begins by identifying what distinguishes the Son's mode of being from the Father's in the economy of revelation: the Father is Revealer, Creator, Lord of existence — but the Son is Reconciler, dealing not merely with the distance between infinite Creator and finite creature but with the active enmity between God and sinner. The structural argument: the problem reconciliation addresses is categorically different from the problem creation addresses. Between Creator and creature there is infinite distance; between God and the sinner there is enmity — "sin and death lie between this world and our world" (line 5481). Reconciliation therefore requires a specifically different divine act and correspondingly a specifically different divine mode of being. To the extent that God in His revelation already treats His enemies as His friends, "revelation is itself reconciliation" (lines 5435–5436). This concurrence of revelation and reconciliation distinguishes §11 from §10: the Creator-creature distinction (infinite/finite) would not by itself require a Redeemer distinct from the Creator, but the God/enemy distinction (holiness/sin) does.

Barth surveys the high Christological titles of the New Testament — Kyrios, Son of God, Word, *monogenes*, the "second line" running from John 1:1 ("the Word was God") through 2 Cor. 5:19 ("God was in Christ reconciling") to the Christological hymns of Philippians, Colossians, and Hebrews — arguing that none of these texts is reducible to Ebionite or Docetic schemes. Against Dibelius (lines 5102–5108), who frames the NT problem as how "knowledge of the historical figure of Jesus was so quickly transformed into faith in a heavenly Son of God," Barth argues that the presupposition itself is wrong: the NT witnesses did not move from the historical to the heavenly; Christ's deity was not a *conclusion* but the *beginning* of their thinking. Barth then states positively what the deity of Christ requires: "who can reveal God except God Himself? ... If He reveals God, then irrespective of His creaturehood He Himself has to be God. And since this is a case of either/or, He has to be full and true God without reduction or limitation" (lines 5309–5317). A "superhuman or semi-divine event ... does not correspond to the seriousness of the problem it is supposed to solve" (lines 5522–5526).

**§11.2 The Eternal Son (lines 5692–7379)**

The decisive dogmatic move: "He does not first become God's Son or Word in the event of revelation. On the contrary, the event of revelation has divine truth and reality because that which is proper to God is revealed in it, because Jesus Christ reveals Himself as the One He already was before, apart from this event, in Himself too" (lines 5709–5716). A substantial digression (lines 5786–6013) examines the Reformation context: did Melanchthon (*Loci* 1521) and early Calvin attack the trinitarian dogma by their insistence on Christ's *beneficia* rather than his natures? Barth's answer is negative in both cases — these were tactical expressions of impatience with scholastic speculation, not principled rejections. Luther's theology of the cross remains the model: knowledge of God goes *through* Christ's humanity, but it genuinely ascends: "then one may ascend and see that He is God" (line 5969). Against Enlightenment critiques (Spalding, Ritschl, lines 6017–6101), Barth argues that the "antecedently in Himself" formula is not metaphysical speculation but the very safeguard against speculation: (1) denying it makes God's being-for-us a *necessary* attribute, destroying grace; (2) denying it collapses back into Ebionite or Docetic Christology; (3) without it there is no norm for knowing Christ except a humanly constructed one. "If we will not listen to the fact that Christ is antecedently God in Himself in order that in this way and on this basis He may be our God, then we turn the latter, His being God for us, into a necessary attribute of God. God's being is then essentially limited and conditioned as a being revealed, i.e., as a relation of God to man. Man is thus thought of as indispensable to God. But this destroys God's freedom in the act of revelation and reconciliation, i.e., it destroys the gracious character of this act" (lines 6069–6079).

The section closes with a commentary on six clauses of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (lines 6159–7375). On "one Lord Jesus Christ" (lines 6208–6243): Barth relates *Kyrios* to the Old Testament *YHWH* — the name above all names. On "only-begotten Son" (lines 6244–6306): *monogenes* means "of a unique kind," not merely "beloved" — uniqueness grounded in ontology, not affective preference. On "begotten before all time" (lines 6307–6359): the generation is eternal, not temporal; the creed directly counters the Arian "there was a time when He was not." On "light of light, true God of true God, begotten not made" (lines 6403–6696): three metaphors for the one mystery — light, God-from-God, begottenness. None is adequate, none is dispensable. The *how* of eternal generation "can only be indicated but never explained in theology" (lines 6569–6594); the generation is unlike all creaturely begetting, which posits a separate external reality, while the divine generation posits the Son as "God from God." On the *homoousios* (lines 6922–7115): Barth endorses the Athanasian-Augustinian interpretation as *tautotes* — identity of essence — over against the Neo-Nicene "equality of essence," since the latter's tendency toward tritheism requires the former as a safeguard. "In all its folly it is more true than all the wisdom which has voiced its opposition to it" (lines 7071–7072). On "through whom all things were made" (lines 7116–7379): the Son shares in creation not because creation is His work in distinction from the Father but because creation is the single undivided work of all three modes — yet the participation of the Son illumines *who* the Reconciler is: "the One in whom we are to believe is not a stranger in our world: He is the One who previously called us out of nothing into existence" (line 7239).

The Word metaphor alongside the Son metaphor (lines 6739–6876) receives careful treatment: both are complementary, neither subsumes the other. "Son" is more appropriate for understanding Christ's action materially as reconciliation; "Word" for understanding it formally as revelation. Both together prevent reduction — either to mere pedagogy (Word alone) or to mere kinship (Son alone).

**Key verbatim quotes — §11:**
- "revelation is itself reconciliation" (lines 5435–5436)
- "His deity creates revelation and reconciliation" [not the reverse] (line 5767)
- "We do not know what we are saying when we call God Father and Son" (lines 6663–6664)
- "The mystery of begetting is originally and properly a divine and not a creaturely mystery. Perhaps one ought even to say that it is the divine mystery" (lines 6655–6658)

### §12. God the Holy Spirit (lines 7379–9525)

§12 opens with the thesis: "The one God reveals Himself according to Scripture as the Redeemer, i.e., as the Lord who sets us free. As such He is the Holy Spirit, by receiving whom we become the children of God, because, as the Spirit of the love of God the Father and the Son, He is so antecedently in Himself" (lines 7381–7384). The section divides into §12.1 "God as Redeemer" and §12.2 "The Eternal Spirit."

**§12.1 God as Redeemer (lines 7385–8312)**

The governing question is how men come to say "Jesus is Lord" — the problem of *Revealedness*, the subjective side of revelation. If the Father is the Revealer's ground and the Son is the objective content of Revelation, the Holy Spirit is the event of that same revelation being received and made actual in the hearer. No amount of external proclamation is self-evidently its own completion: "man is always, and for the first time properly, the one he is" in relation to Christ (lines 7420–7421), yet a specific divine act is required by which man becomes a real recipient of revelation.

Barth surveys three functions of the Spirit in the New Testament (lines 7638–7799): (1) *Personal participation* — the Spirit guarantees "the Yes to God's Word which is spoken by God Himself for us, yet not just to us, but also in us" (lines 7640–7641). (2) *Instruction and guidance* — the Johannine Paraclete who teaches, reminds, and guides "into all truth" (Jn. 14:26; 16:13); the Spirit's guidance (*agein*) can take concrete shape in specific directions (Acts 8:29; 16:6). (3) *Authorization to speak* — the Spirit is "the great and only possibility in virtue of which men can speak of Christ in such a way that what they say is witness" (lines 7726–7728); Pentecost (Acts 2) is paradigmatic: the outpouring consists in the disciples beginning to speak. The Spirit's work is formally summarized as "freedom" — specifically, freedom *for* God, not merely freedom *from* constraint (lines 7818–7863) — and materially as "divine sonship": the cry "Abba, Father!" (Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6) is the distilled content of life in the Spirit. Barth cites Luther's exposition of Gal. 4:6 at length (lines 7934–7978): the Spirit's cry "pierces the clouds, fills heaven and earth" though the believer perceives it as "only a trifling sigh."

Crucial boundary: the Spirit does not have independent content beyond Christ. "He will not speak on his own authority ... He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you" (Jn. 16:13–14, cited at lines 7631–7633). 2 Cor. 3:17 ("the Lord is the Spirit") is *not* an identification of Christ and the Spirit but a statement about *lordship*: the Spirit shares the same mode of lordship as Christ (lines 7537–7545). Nor does man become the Spirit in receiving the Spirit; "even in receiving the Holy Ghost man remains man, the sinner sinner" (lines 8113–8115). The critique of Karl Holl (lines 8029–8077) illustrates the alternative: Holl substitutes for the Holy Spirit man's own faculty of conscience and judgment, producing faith as "a particularly profound and very conscientious conviction of truth" — a reduction that has no use for the category of the Spirit's deity.

An eschatological note closes §12.1 (lines 8203–8312): all statements about the man who receives the Spirit are eschatological — referring to the final divine fulfillment, not a present human possession. "The Spirit remains the Holy Spirit, wholly and utterly the Spirit of promise" (lines 8251–8252). What man experiences is "a human sign" of what God has given; the Spirit as God abides as the infinite sphere beyond the "tiny measure of faith." "To have the Holy Spirit is to let God rather than our having God be our confidence" (lines 8132–8134).

**§12.2 The Eternal Spirit (lines 8313–9525)**

As with the Son, Barth moves from the economic to the immanent: "The Holy Spirit does not first become the Holy Spirit, the Spirit of God, in the event of revelation. The event of revelation has clarity and reality on its subjective side because the Holy Spirit, the subjective element in this event, is of the essence of God Himself. What He is in revelation He is antecedently in Himself" (lines 8314–8318). Barth notes the slower historical development of pneumatology: even Nicaea (325) merely mentions the Spirit; Athanasius's letters to Serapion against the Macedonians and the Council of Constantinople (381) established the Spirit's full deity; the *Quicunque* gave it precise formulation; and the Western interpolation of the *Filioque* into the Creed (liturgically from the 6th century, Roman endorsement 1014) completed the development — at the cost of the 1054 schism (lines 8339–8358). The difficulty is "deeply rooted in the matter itself": to acknowledge the Spirit's full deity is to acknowledge that man's reception of revelation "is not his own work but is again in the full sense God's work" (lines 8371–8373).

The commentary on the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed's pneumatological article (lines 8413–9491) examines four clauses. On "the Lord": the Spirit is Lord in inseparable unity with Father and Son; but *pneuma* being grammatically neuter, the adjectival *kyrion* "indicates the special way in which the Holy Spirit is all this" (lines 8440–8453): the Spirit is "the common factor in the mode of being of God the Father and that of God the Son" — not a third "I" but the "act of communion" between them (lines 8477–8481). Augustine: "*Spiritus sanctus commune aliquid est Patris et Filii*" (*De Trinitate* VI.5.7, line 8454). The Spirit is the love in which God loves Himself as Father and as Son; "the 'result' of their common 'breathing,' *spiratio*" (line 8489). On "giver of life": the Spirit is not only Redeemer but also Creator Spirit (*spiritus vivificans*) — the Spirit hovering over the waters of Gen. 1:2 and the principle of life in all creatures (Ps. 33:6; Ps. 104:29–30); Barth cites Luther's distinction between *Spiritus animans* (animating spirit) and *Spiritus sanctus sanctificans* (lines 8658–8660).

On "who proceeds from the Father and the Son" — the *Filioque* — Barth provides the most extended treatment in the section (lines 8682–9355). His governing methodological rule: "statements about the divine modes of being antecedently in themselves cannot be different in content from those that are to be made about their reality in revelation" (lines 9011–9013). In revelation, the Spirit is the Spirit of both Father and Son; therefore He must be so antecedently. The Eastern position (*ek monou tou Patros*, "from the Father alone") isolates Jn. 15:26 from the many texts calling the Spirit the Spirit of the Son and elevates this isolation to a principle about God's eternal inner life — "a speculation which interprets individual verses of the Bible in isolation" (lines 9050–9053). The material argument: denying the *Filioque* means "the fellowship of the Spirit between God and man is without objective ground or content" (lines 9077–9078) — the God-man relation in the Spirit floats free of the Mediator. The positive formulation: "As God is in Himself Father from all eternity, He begets Himself as the Son from all eternity. As He is the Son from all eternity, He is begotten of Himself as the Father from all eternity. In this eternal begetting of Himself and being begotten of Himself, He posits Himself a third time as the Holy Spirit, i.e., as the love which unites Him in Himself" (lines 9191–9195). The Spirit proceeds "not from two sources but from one source" — the *unitas* of Father and Son as Father and Son (Council of Lyons II 1274; Florence 1439; cited at lines 9358–9368). Barth closes with Augustine's prayer from *De Trinitate* XV.28.51 acknowledging that all true speech is God's gift and human speech needs divine pardon (lines 9492–9524).

On "worshipped and glorified with Father and Son" (lines 9396–9491): the equality of the Spirit in worship repeats the material claim — to glorify the Spirit is to glorify God; any diminution of Spirit worship is diminution of divine worship.

**Key verbatim quotes — §12:**
- "The Holy Spirit does not first become the Holy Spirit, the Spirit of God, in the event of revelation" (lines 8314–8315)
- "God remains the Lord even and precisely when He Himself comes into our hearts as His own gift" (lines 8256–8258)
- "To have the Holy Spirit is to let God rather than our having God be our confidence" (lines 8132–8134)
- "The *Filioque* expresses recognition of the communion between the Father and the Son. The Holy Spirit is the love which is the essence of the relation between these two modes of being of God" (lines 9055–9057)
