# Rolt, *Dionysius the Areopagite: On the Divine Names and the Mystical Theology* (1920)

**Source:** `raw/scholarship/Dionysius the Areopagite: On the Divine Names and the Mystical Theology.md`
(CCEL/ThML transcription of the SPCK 1920 edition).

This volume is the standard early-twentieth-century English edition of the two
"speculative" treatises of [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]]. It contains
three distinct components, two of which are original scholarship rather than translation:

1. **C. E. Rolt's translation** of *On the Divine Names* (13 chapters) and *The Mystical
   Theology* (5 chapters) — a third complete English witness for this wiki alongside
   [[on-divine-names|Parker (1897)]] and [[sharpe-mysticism|Sharpe (1910)]].
2. **Rolt's Introduction** — a long, original philosophical reconstruction of the Dionysian
   system (its principal scholarly contribution; see below).
3. **W. J. Sparrow-Simpson's appended essay**, *The Influence of Dionysius in Religious
   History*, supplying the reception history Rolt's solitary working conditions had caused him
   to omit, plus the editor's bracketed critical **Notes** warning against Rolt's "Supra-Personal
   Deity" reading.

Rolt (a King's College School and Oxford scholar, Liddon Student) wrote the Introduction and
translation from the isolated village rectory of Watermillock in the Lakes, "shut off from
access to libraries," and died at 37. Sparrow-Simpson, his guardian and editor, notes that
Rolt's "fervid and enthusiastic disposition" led him to "unguarded language" and that the
bearing of the Supra-Personal Deity doctrine "on the Person of Christ and the Christian
doctrine of the Atonement requires to be more thoroughly defined than is done" — hence the
editorial notes (Preface).

## Rolt's Dating and Identification of Dionysius

Rolt rejects the apostolic claim flatly: it "collapses beneath a considerable weight of
anachronisms, by far the chief of which is the later neo-Platonism in almost every paragraph."
The writings "appear to reflect, and even to quote, the doctrines of the Pagan philosopher
Proclus, who began lecturing at Athens in A.D. 430," and Rolt identifies the **Hierotheus** of
the corpus with the **Syrian mystic Stephen bar Sudaili**, a contemporary of Proclus — placing
the corpus "near the very end of the fifth century." The author was "probably a monk, possibly
a bishop, certainly an ecclesiastic," likely Syrian. Rolt declines to call the pseudonym
"forgery" ("such a practice was in his day permitted and even considered laudable") and argues
the works keep their value because "they are not historical documents"; their "scope is with
the workings of man's mind and spirit in a region that does not change." See
[[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|the authorship controversy]].

## Rolt's Central Thesis: The Super-Essential Godhead = the Supra-Personal Absolute

The heart of Rolt's Introduction is a systematic reading of the **Super-Essential Godhead**
(*hyperousios thearchia*) as **Supra-Personality**, equated explicitly with the **Absolute** of
modern idealist philosophy:

- *Ousia* ("Essence"/"Being") in its highest sense denotes a **person**; therefore
  "Super-Essence" intends "**Supra-Personality**," and "the doctrine of the Super-Essential
  Godhead simply means that God is, in His ultimate Nature, Supra-Personal."
- Personality is intrinsically finite — "I am I and I am not you" — a faculty of distinguishing
  oneself from others. God, being infinite, "dwells on a plane where there is nothing whatever
  beside Himself"; His only consciousness is a "Universal Consciousness" that "does not
  distinguish Himself from us."
- "The Super-Essential Godhead is, in fact, precisely that which modern philosophy describes as
  the **Absolute**." It is neither One nor Many but a "**Super-Unity**"; not conscious, living,
  or existent "as we understand the term," because all those are finite states.
- Rolt embraces the deliberately provocative formula — which he attributes to **F. H. Bradley**
  ("regarded as a startling blasphemy") — that **"God is but an Appearance of the Absolute"**:
  i.e. the Scriptural Names, and the very name "God," apply to the Godhead *as manifested*, not
  to the ultimate Super-Essence. This he calls "merely a bold way of stating the orthodox
  truism that the Ultimate Godhead is incomprehensible."

The editor (Sparrow-Simpson) brackets several warning notes here; this is the reading he judges
insufficiently safeguarded against its Christological consequences.

## Rolt's Reconstruction of Creation: the Two Emanating Streams

Rolt's most original (and idiosyncratic) move is a systematic metaphysics of **two emanating
streams** that he reconstructs from Dionysius's scattered language:

- The Super-Essence's contained Plurality "compels It to an eternal act of Creation"; it "must
  overflow into multiplicity," passing "from Indifference into Differentiation." Creation and
  **Revelation (*ekphansis*)** alike issue from the Aspect of the Godhead "turned towards" the
  world.
- The **Trinity** is the *eternally differentiated* Manifestation: "the Absolute Godhead is the
  Super-Essence; the eternally Manifested Godhead is the Trinity." The Persons are
  **Super-Essential / Supra-Personal**, not personalities (Rolt cites the phrase
  "**Super-Essential Jesus**": Christ's *humanity* is personal, His *Godhead* supra-personal).
- The overflow runs in two streams: the **Universal Stream** (the *autoeinai, autozōē* — "Very
  Being," "Very Life," etc.; an abstract, "empty" existence, the formal law of the universe) and
  the **Particular Stream** (mere particularity, practically identified with **Non-entity**
  *to mē on*, the "rough material"). The streams "cross, but do not mingle" timelessly until,
  at the pre-ordained moment, they mingle and **Time and the temporal world begin** — the world
  arising first as "merely existent" dead matter (*ousiōdēs*), then ascending the scale
  Existence → Life → Sensation → Reason → Spirit.
- Every creature has **two sides to its existence**: one in the Super-Essence "wherein all
  things are One Thing" (the Neoplatonic radii-meeting-at-the-centre image), one in its own
  created being where all things are separate. "This paradox is of the very utmost importance."

Rolt deploys a battery of his own analogies (mountain of ore seen near/far, magnet with
positive/negative magnetism, light filling a void) to render the *proodos* by which "the
Super-Essence actually passes outside of Itself even while It remains all the time wholly within
itself" — which Dionysius "expresses in one place by saying that the act of Creation is an
ecstasy of Divine Love."

## Rolt on Evil

Rolt restates the Dionysian theory of [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation/non-being]] and
then *criticizes* it. Evil is "ultimately non-existent"; "a totally evil thing would be simply
non-existent." His memorable illustration: evil is **like zero in mathematics** — "non-entity
(since, added to numbers, it makes no difference) and yet has an annihilating force (since it
reduces to zero all numbers that are multiplied by it)." He links it to the **timeless self**
("even the lost souls in hell retain unaltered the ultimate nobility of their being" — Eckhart,
Tauler): "The soul is potentially divine, and therefore may be actually satanic."

But Rolt charges Dionysius with the "great weakness" of failing to "emphasize the ugliness of
evil" — of being "so dazzled with his vision of ultimate Reality" that he "does not feel with
any intensity the partial realities of this finite universe," gloze[ing] over real cruelties,
and giving too little prominence "to the Cross of Christ." He reads Dionysius's one-sidedness as
partly an anti-Manichaean polemic over-correcting against dualism. He distinguishes three
Dionysian senses of **Non-Existence** (formless "Matter"; evil as formless; the Super-Essence as
beyond Existence) and notes the "dangerous, yet true" corollary that "evil is a mistaken quest
for Good" (Augustine, Dante).

## Rolt on Contemplation, Unknowing, and Deification

Rolt frames the **Via Negativa** as the return-movement: man, floated into the world down the
Universal Stream, "plunges beneath the stream" into an undercurrent sweeping him back to the
Source by **spiritual concentration**, casting away the "husk" of each assimilated good. The
ascent passes the anthropomorphic stage, then the changeless-personal-God stage (built by the
**Discursive Reason**), until the **Intuitive Reason** seeks the Absolute Unity beyond; finally
the "Me"/"Thee" relation between contemplating subject and Uncreated Light is transcended in
**ecstasy** and **Unknowing** (*agnosia*).

Crucially, Rolt insists Unknowing is **not** Herbert Spencer's "Unknowable": Spencer means "a
state below the understanding," Dionysius (and Bergson) "a state above it" — "the Divine
Darkness is dark through excess of light." He marshals a chain of Western mystical witnesses to
the "merged yet distinct" self — **Ruysbroeck** ("they are That which they contemplate"),
**Eckhart**, **Tauler**, **St Bernard** (the drop of water in wine; iron in fire; air in
sunlight — "To experience this state is to be deified"), **Thomas à Kempis**, **Pascal** (*le
moi est haïssable*), and **Augustine** (the "could one silence…" passage from the *Confessions*).

On **deification** (*theosis* — see [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]]) Rolt is terminologically
precise about Dionysius's vocabulary: the Super-Essence is the *thearchia* / *ousiarchia*; from
it the Universal Stream emanates **Deity** (*theotēs / autotheotēs*); purified souls participate
in this effluence and so are "deified" (*theountai*), becoming derivatively divine (*theoi*).
Thus Dionysius "argues from the acquisition of actual divinity by certain men to God's
Supra-Divinity" — "only another way of saying that God is but the highest Appearance or
Manifestation of the Absolute."

## Dionysius and Modern Philosophy

A distinctive feature of Rolt's Introduction is its sustained comparison of Dionysius with
turn-of-the-century philosophy: **Bradley** (God as Appearance of the Absolute), **J. M. E.
McTaggart** (timeless selves as eternal differentiations of the Absolute — which Rolt critiques
for leaving "no room for worship"), **Bergson** (perception located outside us in the perceived
object; transcendence-of-knowledge), **Herbert Spencer** (contrasted), and a "Pragmatism /
Idealism" reconciliation via the two-sided self ("So build we up the being that we are" —
Wordsworth). Rolt defends Dionysius against the charge of **Pantheism** ("refuted by the very
extravagance of the terms in which he asserts the Transcendence of the Godhead") and against
hostility to the material world, while conceding "His doctrines are certainly dangerous… the
study of him is for the few."

## Sparrow-Simpson, *The Influence of Dionysius in Religious History*

The appended essay (by the editor) traces the corpus's reception and supplies the critical
history Rolt omitted. Key contents:

- **Maximus the Confessor** (d. 662): first great propagator; wrote notes ("scholia") on all
  four treatises, prized for the clarity Dionysius lacked. See [[maximus-the-confessor]].
- **Pope Martin I, Lateran Council 649**: already cites Dionysius in the West; complains the
  Areopagite was being *misrepresented* — credited with "one divino-human activity" (*una
  operatio deivirilis*) whereas he wrote "**a new divino-human activity**" (*kainē theandrikē
  energeia*). A datum in the Monothelite/[[chalcedonian-definition|Christological]] disputes.
- **John Scotus Eriugena** (9th c.): "in reality popularized Dionysius for Latin Christendom"
  via his Latin translation (commissioned by Charles the Bald) and built the Areopagite's
  principles into the *De Divisione Naturae*. The essay summarizes Eriugena's Master/Disciple
  dialogue on divine incomprehensibility, affirmative vs. negative theology (God as *plus quam
  bonitas*, *super bonus*), the denial that the Absolute can love, and the reduction of the
  Trinity to subjective "names." It reports **Theodor Christlieb's** verdict that Eriugena's
  reconciliation "ends in the supremacy of [philosophy]" and that "the teaching of Dionysius in
  the exposition of Erigena became scarcely distinguishable from **Pantheism**." See
  [[john-scotus-eriugena|Eriugena]].
- **John of Damascus** (Eastern reception): uses Dionysius "with discrimination" — "the
  Theologian in St. John Damascene controls the Philosopher," supplementing divine
  incomprehensibility with firm teaching on real hypostatic distinctions and the Incarnation.
  See [[john-of-damascus]].
- **Hugh of St. Victor** (10-book commentary on the *Celestial Hierarchy*; see
  [[hugh-of-saint-victor]]) and **Thomas Aquinas** (a "favourite authority," *Commentary on the
  Divine Names*, but used "critically," "profoundly averse from everything which resembles a
  Pantheistic tendency"; see [[thomas-aquinas]]).
- **The orthodoxy controversy**: once the apostolic identification was "completely dispelled" by
  17th-c. critics and the term **Pseudo-Dionysius** prevailed, suspicion arose. **Le Quien**
  (*Num Pseudo-Dionysius haereticus fuerit*) found confusion of divine/human in Christ and
  "monophysite tendencies"; **Bernard de Rubeis**, Paris, Louvain, and the Jesuits (notably
  **Lessius**) defended him. 19th-c. critics — **F. C. Baur** (the Trinity "reduced to little
  more than names"; no real Incarnation), **Vacherot** ("a neo-Platonist philosopher in
  disguise"), the Lutheran **Dorner** ("an important link of connection between Monophysitism
  and the doctrine of the Church"), the Roman Catholic **Bach** (Christ "driven into the
  shade"), and Bishop **Westcott** ("not wholly of a Christian type") — pressed the charge that
  "the philosophy prevails, not without serious results to the theology of the Church." See
  [[dionysius-christian-or-neoplatonist|the Christian-or-Neoplatonist controversy]].

## Distinctive Rolt Renderings (vs. Parker / Sharpe)

Rolt's English is markedly more fluent and literary than Parker's stilted version. Notable
terminological choices:

- *Mystical Theology* Ch. I titled **"What is the Divine Gloom"**; the Divine Darkness is "the
  **dazzling obscurity of the secret Silence**, outshining all brilliance with the intensity of
  their darkness."
- The DN Ch. II topic rendered **"the Undifferencing and the Differentiation in Divinity"**
  (Parker: "Unions and Distinctions"); the *autoeinai/autozōē* group rendered **"Very Being,"
  "Very Life," "Very Power."**
- The lost/other treatises named **"Outlines of Divinity"** (= *Theological Outlines /
  Representations*) and **"Symbolic Divinity"** (= *Symbolic Theology*).
- MT Ch. I cites "the blessed **Bartholomew**" (on the Gospel as "vast and yet minute").
- The final apophatic sentence: the Cause "transcends all affirmation by being the perfect and
  unique Cause of all things, and transcends all negation by the pre-eminence of Its simple and
  absolute nature."

## Significance for the Wiki

Rolt's edition adds three things beyond the Parker and Sharpe materials already ingested:
(1) a **third independent translation** of DN and MT; (2) a fully worked-out **philosophical
interpretation** that reads Dionysius through British Idealism (the Super-Essence as the
Absolute, the two-streams metaphysics) — a foil to [[perl-theophany|Perl's]] later, more
strictly Neoplatonic reading and to [[sharpe-mysticism|Sharpe's]] Thomistic one; and (3) a
compact **reception-and-criticism history** (Sparrow-Simpson) that grounds both the
[[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|authorship]] and the [[dionysius-christian-or-neoplatonist|orthodoxy]]
controversies.
