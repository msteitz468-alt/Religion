## Source Overview

A fresh scholarly translation by Brook Ziporyn (University of Chicago), translator of *Zhuangzi* and
*Tiantai Buddhism*. The 2022 Liveright edition comes with an extended Introduction (composite-text
thesis; manuscript history), a detailed Translator's Note (methodology; key-term analysis), and
chapter-specific notes explaining textual variants and interpretive choices.

This translation is now the wiki's primary working text for the Daodejing, superseding the Legge
(1891) translation for analytical purposes.

## Translator's Methodology

Ziporyn works from **all major ancient manuscript families simultaneously**: the received ("Wang Bi")
version, Guodian bamboo slips (4th c. BCE), Mawangdui A and B (2nd c. BCE), Beijing University
bamboo slips (2nd c. BCE), Yan Zun edition, Heshang Gong edition, Xiang'er fragment, and the Fu Yi
manuscript — using them all as a field for close cross-checking.

**Default text**: The received/Wang Bi version (also as the standard framework for the 81-chapter
arrangement), with explicit notes wherever a variant was adopted.

## Key Interpretive Claims

**1. Composite-text thesis**: The Daodejing is not a single-author work but an anthology — "a best-of
selection of exemplars of a defunct ancient genre." The Guodian materials (less than half the received
text, in different order) show the text was fluid and composite from early on. Ziporyn treats the
assembled text as yielding "a minimally discernible position" rather than a single author's voice.

**2. The A/B structure**: The Daodejing's consistent rhetorical move is **ironic inversion of value
polarity**. Valued items (A: being, strength, knowledge, action, fame, fullness, the high, the hard)
are consistently subverted by their disvalued opposites (B: nonbeing, weakness, ignorance, nondoing,
namelessness, emptiness, the low, the soft). This accounts for the text's pervasive paradoxes and
reversals.

**3. Key term translations**:
- *Dao* → **"the course"** (not "way"): covers both purposive and non-purposive senses; the text
  performs an ironic flip from the first to the second.
- *De* → **"virtuosity"** (not "virtue"): spontaneous efficacy, the power of being as one is.
- *Chang/heng* → **"constancy"**: the sustainable background that persists through value fluctuations.
- *Ziran* → **"of themselves, all on their own"** (translated adverbially as it usually functions).

## Manuscript History (per Ziporyn)

| Manuscript | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Guodian bamboo slips | c. 4th c. BCE | Less than half the received text; scrambled order |
| Mawangdui A & B | c. 2nd c. BCE | Full text; reversed order (chs. 38–81 first); *heng* for *chang* |
| Beijing Univ. bamboo slips | c. 2nd c. BCE | Donated 2009; another full-text version |
| Heshang Gong edition | c. 2nd c. CE | Associated with religious/longevity reading |
| Xiang'er fragment | c. 200 CE | From Dunhuang; 35 chapters only |
| Received / "Wang Bi" | 226–249 CE | The standard text; used as default here |
| Fu Yi manuscript | 2nd c. BCE (recovered 7th c. CE) | Tang dynasty commentator Fu Yi |

The historical Laozi — a 6th c. BCE archivist who composed the text under duress — is not supported
by early historical sources. The traditional biography dates from the *Shiji* (1st c. BCE) and later.
