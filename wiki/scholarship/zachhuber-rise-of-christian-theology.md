## What the Book Argues

Zachhuber offers the first full analysis of what he calls **Patristic philosophy** — a distinctive philosophical project (a "set of logical and ontological concepts underlying the articulation of doctrinal statements") that arose in the Eastern Church at the end of the fourth century and developed until John of Damascus. His central theses:

1. **Patristic thought is philosophy.** The medieval faculty-distinction between "theology" and "philosophy" is an ill fit for late antiquity, where pagans classed Christianity as a (inferior) philosophy and Christians called their activity "true philosophy." The conceptual apparatus underwriting doctrine (ousia, physis, hypostasis) can be appreciated or critiqued independently of the doctrines it supports, and so is properly philosophical.

2. **Doctrine was the incubator.** Because central doctrines were defined with non-biblical technical terms (*ousia*, *physis*, *hypostasis*) and were fiercely *contested*, their defense forced increasingly technical philosophical work. The **Trinitarian controversy** of the 4th century produced the first and most influential version ("the classical theory"); the **Christological controversy** of the 5th–7th centuries drove its repeated, increasingly radical modification.

3. **The Cappadocians were continuous with Hellenism; Christology was the rupture.** Against Lossky and Zizioulas (who locate an "ontological revolution" / "turn to the person" in the Cappadocian Trinity), Zachhuber argues the Cappadocian "classical theory" is an **ontology of being-as-one** in full continuity with Greek philosophy's emphasis on ontological unity. The genuine revolution — the "end of ancient metaphysics" — came later, when **Christology forced thinkers to conceptualize the individual *as individual***, producing anticipations of nominalism and of the essence/existence distinction that would later separate modernity from classical thought.

4. **Plurality, not unity.** Patristic philosophy did not converge on one system; the fragmentation of Eastern Christianity into rival (Chalcedonian, miaphysite, "Nestorian") communions produced several distinct, often brilliant philosophies. Completeness is not intended; authors are chosen for their philosophical quality and to represent the breadth of debate.

## Key Conceptual Vocabulary

- **The "classical theory"** — Zachhuber's term for [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|Cappadocian philosophy]] once it became, within a few decades, a shared intellectual *koine* (not a school) across the East. See the dedicated concept page.
- **Abstract vs. concrete account** — the two complementary versions of the Cappadocian theory (Basil's logical/grammatical account of properties; Gregory of Nyssa's physical/ontological account of universal nature realized in individuals). See [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|ousia/hypostasis]].
- **Miaphysite / Chalcedonian / "Nestorian"** — the rival Christological streams whose differing philosophies the book traces. Zachhuber adopts "miaphysite" (not the pejorative "monophysite") for the Cyrilline opponents of Chalcedon.

### Early-modern afterlife of the tritheism question (Ch. 10.3.4)
Recorded here rather than on a separate page: the Cappadocian-tritheism question resurfaced via Denis Pétau (Petavius), whose *Dogmata Theologica* (IV 9) surveyed the Greek Fathers; Joseph Bingham (Oxford sermon, 1695) used Petavius to argue for tritheism "reminiscent of John Philoponus"; Ralph Cudworth drew the same inference to commend his Platonist Christianity over the "tritheism" of Gregory of Nyssa and Cyril; Cudworth's work circulated in J. L. von Mosheim's Latin translation, and August Dorner's history of Christology drew on Petavius's account.
