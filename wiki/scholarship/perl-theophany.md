# *Theophany: The Neoplatonic Philosophy of Dionysius the Areopagite* — Eric D. Perl (2007)

**Eric D. Perl** (University of Dallas; Catholic University of America) presents [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]] as a coherent **Neoplatonic philosopher** in the tradition of Plotinus and Proclus — not merely a theologian who borrowed Neoplatonic vocabulary, but a thinker who takes up the deep philosophical insights of the Neoplatonic tradition into his own work. The book deliberately rejects the standard "Neoplatonism vs. Christianity" debate, which Perl regards as premised on a philosophically unsophisticated understanding of both traditions.

## Central Thesis and Method

Perl's central thesis: Dionysius's thought is best approached not as the theologian's question "does it conform to authentic Christianity?" but as the philosopher's question "what does it mean and how is it justified?" Characterizing the work as "Neoplatonic philosophy" is "deliberately and doubly provocative": it treats Neoplatonism not as a liability but as "a profound and well-argued understanding of reality and as the most promising resource to which we may turn in our present intellectual and cultural predicament" (Introduction, line 175).

The book is organized not as a sequential commentary on the Dionysian corpus but as seven interconnected philosophical essays following the structure of key chapters in [[on-divine-names|On the Divine Names]] (Chapters I, IV, V, VII) — each essay drawing on the whole corpus. Deliberately excluded: trinitarian doctrine, Christology, and liturgy — Perl focuses on Dionysius's **metaphysics and gnoseology**. The organizing concept is [[theophany|theophany]]: all being as God's self-manifestation.

## Introduction — The Neoplatonic Reading

*Source: endnotes to Introduction, lines 4678–4741. The range assigned to Agent A covers the endnote sections of the Introduction and Chapters 1–3; these notes are substantive philosophical discussions rather than bare citations. All content below is extracted faithfully from those notes.*

The methodological notes to the Introduction confirm and gloss the framing stated in the body. Perl deliberately omits the prefix "pseudo-" on account of its "verbal awkwardness and potentially pejorative connotations" [4679–4680]. Dionysius's background in earlier Christian thought — Golitzin's *Et Introibo ad Altare Dei* being the major recent study — is deliberately excluded "not because it is unreal or unimportant, but because it does not contribute to the specifically philosophical understanding of Dionysius" [4685–4686]. For Proclus, Perl relies chiefly on the *Elements of Theology* because it "most clearly and systematically presents the fundamental structural principles of his metaphysics," which "contribute most to a philosophical understanding of Dionysius" [4688–4691]. The historical identity of the author is bracketed: speculation about the writer's "real" identity is rejected as "philosophically irrelevant" and as presumptuously assuming "that the author must be someone who is independently known to history under another name" [4723–4726].

The notes map two dominant scholarly failure modes that Perl aims to escape. The first treats Dionysius as a Christian thinker who merely borrowed from Neoplatonism (Brons; Vanneste) [4699–4701]. The second defends Dionysius as genuinely Christian by arguing his thought differs significantly from Neoplatonism (von Ivanka; Golitzin) [4702–4704]. Golitzin himself recognizes the underlying problem: "the assumption that the Platonic tradition and Christianity are mutually exclusive" [4710–4712], yet still finds it necessary in reclaiming Dionysius as Christian "to argue that his thought is significantly different from Neoplatonism" [4714]. The rare exception is Werner Beierwaltes, who expressly concludes "Dionysius: Christianus simulque vere Platonicus," though even he maintains a significant difference between Dionysius and his Neoplatonic predecessors [4695–4698].

The notes underscore a pervasive hostility in scholarship: Balthasar observed the "bad humour and resentment with which the CD [Corpus Dionysiacum] is often approached" [4732–4733]; Golitzin identifies a "thinly concealed 'freight of venom'" in superficially neutral studies [4735–4736]; and Golitzin's general remark is that every treatment of the CD "has… engaged the particular scholar's sympathies and presuppositions—most often in a negative manner" [4738–4740]. Trinitarian theology, Christology, and liturgy are all excluded from scope — a note briefly acknowledges these elements ("See, however, 122 n. 24, on the Trinity, and 108–109, on the incarnation" [4718]) before setting them aside as outside the philosophical inquiry.

### Body Text (lines 145–247)

Perl opens by situating his project against a long-standing scholarly controversy. The study of Dionysius has been "obfuscated by a widespread bias against Neoplatonism among Christian theologians" (line 152), who have organized most of the scholarship around Luther's charge *plus platonizans quam christianizans* and its defenders' counter-claim *non platonizans sed christianizans*. Both sides, Perl argues, share "a somewhat simplistic and philosophically unsophisticated conception of Neoplatonism" (line 163) and "a somewhat narrow and monolithic view of what counts as authentic Christianity" (lines 164–165). The result is accusation and exculpation rather than philosophical understanding.

Perl's response is to sidestep the controversy. The subtitle "Neoplatonic Philosophy" is "deliberately and doubly provocative" (line 168): rather than "convicting or acquitting Dionysius of Neoplatonism, conceived as a capital offense for a Christian theologian" (lines 177–178), Perl proposes to "reclaim him as a Neoplatonic philosopher who not merely appropriates certain terminological or thematic elements from Plotinus and Proclus, but takes up their deep philosophical insights into his own thought" (lines 179–181). The guiding question is not the theologian's question ("does it conform to authentic Christianity?") but the philosopher's: "what it means and how it is justified" (lines 171–172). This requires extensive exposition of Plotinus and Proclus, since "to expound Dionysius in this way, it is necessary to give not merely references to his textual sources, but extensive explanations of the thought of earlier philosophers" (lines 146–148).

A crucial methodological consequence: Perl brackets Dionysius's discussions of "trinitarian doctrine, christology, and liturgy" (line 184). This is not a denial of Dionysius's Christianity — Perl sees "no fundamental opposition between Neoplatonism and Christianity" (lines 187–188) — but a delimitation of the inquiry to its philosophical dimension. Several features often cited as distinctively Christian innovations in Dionysius ("the immediate creation of all things by God, or God as ecstatic love") "can in fact already be found in non-Christian Neoplatonism" (lines 194–196), so the usual framing mislocates what requires explanation.

The book is structured not as a sequential commentary but as "a series of closely interconnected essays" (lines 199–200) following the philosophical structure of *On the Divine Names* I, IV, V, and VII. Chapters 1 and 2 correspond to *Divine Names* I, presenting first God's radical transcendence (Ch. 1) and then his immanence in all things (Ch. 2). The author himself "remains invisible" behind his works and "can be known only as he is manifest in them" (lines 237–238) — a circumstance Perl notes as encouraging a purely philosophical rather than biographical reading.

## Chapter 1 — Beyond Being and Intelligibility

*Source: endnotes to Chapter 1, lines 4742–4931.*

Chapter 1 argues that God's radical transcendence beyond being is not a rhetorical or devotional move but a strict philosophical necessity. The endnotes lay out the argument in full.

**Being as necessarily finite.** The philosophical foundation is the Platonic-Neoplatonic identification of being with intelligibility: to be is to be intelligible, and therefore to be finite and determinate. As Plotinus puts it, "Being… must be fixed by limit and stability; and stability in the intelligible world is limitation and shape, and it is by these that it receives existence" (V.1.7.24–27) [4939–4941]. If being is necessarily finite, then the infinite first principle cannot be a being. This is not a limitation on language but a "strict consequence of the principle that to be is to be intelligible and hence finite" [4895–4896]. The key move against O'Rourke's critique (that Dionysius inadequately treated being as necessarily creaturely) is: "This is precisely why Plotinus and Dionysius refuse to predicate 'reality' or 'being' of the One, or God" [4900–4901], and to predicate being of God would be to "treat God as one member of a larger totality and subordinate God to a term more universal than himself, viz. 'reality'" [4898–4900].

**Apophasis as philosophical necessity, not rhetorical device.** The notes explicitly correct two common misreadings. Rorem had written that "Negation is a human concept, and thus cannot capture an infinite and transcendent God… Negation is negated and the human mind, befuddled, falls silent" [4870–4873]. Perl corrects this: "human concepts and minds are unable to capture God, not because they are human, but because they are concepts and minds" [4873–4875]. The apophatic impossibility is not a feature of human limitation but of what it means to be a concept at all. Similarly, Derrida mischaracterized negative theology as always "eager… to remember that if the predicate is refused to God, this is to recognize for him a superior, inconceivable, ineffable mode of being" [4877–4879]; Perl's counter: "If to be is to be intelligible, then an 'inconceivable, ineffable mode of being' is a contradiction in terms" [4880–4882]. Negative theology in the Neoplatonic tradition does not reach a higher being; it leaves being altogether.

**Plotinus and Dionysius on the One's absolute transcendence.** Perl traces Plotinus's radical doctrine philosophically to the argument that "being qua intelligible is necessarily finite and hence derivative" [4816–4817], not merely to Plato's *Parmenides* textually. A significant point about the *extent* of negation: Plotinus writes that "There must therefore be a concentration into One, really outside all multiplicity and any simplicity whatsoever" (V.3.16.15–16) [4842–4844] — the One transcends even simplicity, not merely complexity. Gerson's reading that "'that the One is beyond essence does not mean that it is beyond existence or being altogether'" is rejected: "Plotinus' point is that existence or being necessarily implies both limitedness and complexity" [4824–4826]. A.H. Armstrong formulates the consequence: "beyond being" means God "has no intelligible content" — not merely that his intelligibility "transcends our limited and fallen human intelligences" [4831–4836].

**Dionysius following Plotinus on negating "One."** Lossky had tried to contrast Plotinus and Dionysius by noting that Dionysius negates even the name "One." Perl shows this fails: "in negating the name 'One' Dionysius is simply following the precepts of Plotinus" [4851–4852]. Dionysius's formulations are drawn more immediately from Proclus and Damascius than from Plotinus, but "the philosophical argumentation by which these doctrines are reached is already fully present in Plotinus" [4864–4866].

**The logic of silence.** Even the claim that nothing can be predicated of the One is not itself a predication in the ordinary sense. Perl engages Kordig's attempted *reductio*: if the One is such that no descriptive predicate applies, then "Proclusian" (= having no predicate apply) applies to it, but then so does its negation, yielding contradiction. Perl's reply: "This argument is flawed because the One is not 'any x'" [4914–4916] — the argument mistakes the One for an ordinary entity. Proclus's Parmenides Commentary ends: "by means of a negation Parmenides has removed all negations. With silence he concludes the contemplation of the One" [4918–4921]. Perl endorses this: "Precisely because nothing can truly be described as ineffable, the only recourse is silence" [4923–4925]. The mystical union with the One is not the rejection of intellection "but its proper culmination" [4856–4857].

### Body Text (lines 248–730)

Chapter 1 traces the philosophical argument behind Dionysian negative theology from its foundations in Parmenides through Plato and Plotinus to Dionysius. The driving principle is stated at the outset: "The foundational principle of Neoplatonic thought is the doctrine that to be is to be intelligible" (lines 264–265). Parmenides had made this explicit: "the same thing is for thinking and for being [*to gar auto noein estin te kai einai*]" (fragment cited at lines 281–283). Thought and being are coextensive — whatever is thought is necessarily thought as some being, and whatever is cannot extend beyond what can be thought.

Plato develops this by identifying being with form (*eidos*): "the forms, and only the forms, are 'really real,' precisely because they and only they are altogether intelligible" (lines 318–319). Sensibles are less than fully real because they are constituted as multiple appearances of unitary forms, apprehended not by intellection but by sensation and opinion. The Good is identified as the source of intelligibility and hence of being: "That which provides truth to the things known and gives power to the knower is the form of the Good" (*Republic* 508e1–3, lines 403–405). But because providing intelligibility constitutes beings as beings, the Good itself lies "beyond reality in seniority and power" (*Republic* 508e1–3, lines 420–421) — prior to the being it grounds.

Plotinus carries the argument to its conclusion by identifying being not only as the object but as the content of thought, making being and Intellect one: "'All together are one, Intellect, intellection, the intelligible [*nous, noesis, to noeton*]' (V.3.5.43–44)" (line 448). Being, as intelligible, is necessarily determinate and therefore multiple — "A substance [*ousian*] must be some one particular thing [*tode ti*], something, that is, defined and limited" (V.5.6.6–7, lines 460–461) — for a being is intelligible only by being distinct from other beings. This differentiation is what constitutes beings *as* beings. Consequently, being as a whole is dependent on the One as the source of unifying determination: "It is by the One that all beings are beings, both those which are primarily beings and those which are in any sense said to be among beings. For what could anything be if it was not one?" (VI.9.1.1–3, lines 491–493).

The One itself is therefore "beyond being" in the philosophically precise sense that it cannot be any being. Since being is necessarily finite and determinate, the infinite first principle cannot be a being: "Since the substance which is generated [from the One] is form… the One must be without form. But if it is without form it is not a substance… if all things are in that which is generated from the One, which of the things in it are you going to say that the One is? Since it is none of them, it can only be said to be beyond them. But these things are beings, and being: so it is 'beyond being'" (V.5.6.2–11, lines 533–542). Perl insists this cannot be softened into "infinite being": "Not because of an accidental restriction on the usage of the term 'being,' but because of the philosophically grounded principle that to be is to be intelligible, being necessarily entails finitude, so that 'finite being' is a redundancy and 'infinite being' a contradiction in terms" (lines 550–553).

Dionysius adopts this doctrine directly. He "expressly adopts the Parmenidean and Platonic account of being and thought as coterminous, and therefore locates God beyond both together: 'For if all knowledges are of beings and have their limit in beings, that which is beyond all being also transcends all knowledge' (DN I.4, 593A)" (lines 605–608). God's transcendence is not vague but metaphysically precise: "not in a vague, unspecified sense, but in the very precise metaphysical sense that he is not at all included within the whole of reality, of things that are, as any member of it" (lines 609–611). If our thought cannot attain God, "this is not because of our weakness but because there is no 'there' there, no being, no thing that is God" (lines 621–623).

Apophasis follows as a philosophical conclusion, not a rhetorical device. Even negations about God remain within the domain of conceptual thinking and thus still treat God as a determinate something. Dionysius therefore insists we "not think that the negations are opposed to the affirmations, but much rather that that which is beyond the privations is beyond every affirmation and negation" (*MT* I.2, 1000B, lines 641–644), and concludes: "the all-perfect and single cause of all things is above every affirmation, and the transcendence of that which is simply freed from all things and beyond the wholes is above every negation" (*MT* V, 1048B, lines 647–649). The conclusion of genuine apophasis is "the absolute silence of the mind" (lines 665–666), arising in "the cessation of every intellectual activity [*pases noeras energeias apopasin*]" (DN I.5, 593C, line 671) — which Perl describes as "the strictly philosophical consequence of the identification of being and intelligibility: as long as any speaking or thinking is taking place, we are necessarily in the realm of beings" (lines 683–685).

The chapter closes by placing this radical transcendence in relation to the choice between theism and atheism. Perl argues, following Marion, that both positions treat God as an entity — an idol within a conceptual system — and so equally misfire: "Theism or atheism bear equally on an idol. They remain enemies, but brother enemies in a common and insurpassable idolatry" (Marion, lines 720–722). For Dionysius, "it is as inadequate to deny that God is anything as to affirm that he is anything" (lines 726–727). The chapter then poses the question that Chapter 2 must answer: how God who is nothing can also be "all beings and none of beings" (DN I.6, 596C, line 728) and "all things in all things and nothing in any" (DN VII.3, 872A, line 729).

## Chapter 2 — Being as Theophany

*Source: endnotes to Chapter 2, lines 4932–5078.*

Chapter 2 turns from God's transcendence to his immanence: if God transcends being, how can all beings be his self-manifestation? The endnotes elaborate the resolution through a precise account of procession, participation, and the distinction between appearance and illusion.

**Procession as giving of determination.** The productive relationship of the One/God to beings is understood through the concept of giving intelligible determination. A Platonic term for world-making "connotes making by giving intelligible determination" [5004–5005]; God as "the measure of beings" (from Plato *Laws* 716c4, via Plotinus V.5.4.14; VI.8.18.3) implies "the idea of God as the determination of all things" [5006–5008]. Being is not an independent reality alongside God but the form or shape that God's self-expression takes in finite things. The Plotinian background: the One "provides 'shape' or determination" to being, and being is thus "under pressure" [4963–4964] from the One's productive power.

**Complicatio-explicatio: the One containing all things.** Perl uses the medieval Latin terms *complicatio-explicatio* (enfolding-unfolding) as "a succinct and accurate articulation of Plotinus' thought" even though "the concept of complicatio-explicatio is entirely Plotinian even if the words are not" [4977–4978]. The One "enfolds" all things by being the source of their determination; all things "unfold" the One by manifesting that determination in finite form. This is the structure of theophany: being is the unfolding (explicatio) of God, and God is the enfolding (complicatio) of all being.

**"All in all" — the metaphysical present.** Dionysius characteristically cites 1 Cor. 15:28 ("God all in all") as a metaphysical description of the present order rather than an eschatological promise: "Dionysius characteristically transposes the eschatological future into the metaphysical present" [4997–4999]. The terms Dionysius uses for God's processive presence — "Powers," "Participations," "Processions," "Providences," "Manifestations," "Distributions" [5000–5003] — all name aspects of a single act by which God is present in and as all things.

**Theophany and the Trinity.** A crucial note clarifies that "This 'divine difference' which is constitutive of all things has nothing to do with the trinitarian differentiation of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" [5017–5019]. The Godhead *simpliciter* — all three Persons indifferently, not the Father uniquely — corresponds to the Neoplatonic One: "Unlike Augustine and Eriugena, Dionysius expressly does not introduce the trinitarian differences into his account of being as theophany" [5024–5026]. Balthasar confirms: "Denys strictly denies any distinction between the functions and spheres of the three divine Persons in this world" [5026–5027]. The Godhead as the source of being is hymned as "neither monad nor triad known among us" (DN XIII.3, 980D) [5037–5039], and "although trinitarian doctrine is fully present in Dionysius… it does not enter into his philosophical understanding of being as theophany" [5040–5041]. The term *theophany* itself becomes technically central only in Eriugena, not in Dionysius — but as the note adds, "it quite neatly expresses what is meant here" [5043–5044].

**Appearance vs. illusion.** The most significant interpretive move of Ch. 2 is the distinction between appearance and illusion. Dodds had commented that for Proclus "the separateness of the lower is an illusion resulting from a partial point of view" [5059–5060], and that "the sensible and the intelligible cosmos are both of them appearance" — implying illusion. Perl corrects this: "It is true that for them 'the sensible and intelligible cosmos are both of them appearance,' but it does not follow that they are 'an illusion'" [5065–5067]. Appearance is real self-manifestation of the real; illusion is false seeming. Balthasar puts it well: "Manifestation conceived of in the Greek, not the Indian sense, as becoming visible in reality—not as maya, mere seeming illusion, but always as (real) manifestation of the unmanifest, of the ever greater God who can never be changed into simply comprehensible appearance" [5069–5072]. Theophany is manifestation in the Greek sense: God's genuine self-showing in real things, not a veil before him.

**True infinity preserves the finite.** Dupré's formulation captures the consequence: "If the infinite is opposed to the finite, it is limited and thereby ceases to be infinite. Instead we must show that the finite is in the infinite… True infinity, therefore, preserves the finite within itself" [5048–5053]. The transcendence of God does not negate or diminish finite beings but grounds and sustains them: God's "not-otherness" (Plotinus: "the One has no otherness" [5054–5055]; Nicholas of Cusa's *De non aliud*) is the condition of his being present in all things as their very being.

### Body Text (lines 731–1491)

Chapter 2 takes up the question that closes Chapter 1: if God is not any being, how can all beings be his self-manifestation? The answer turns on a distinctively Neoplatonic account of causation as participation — not the "horizontal" causation of one being by another within the same ontological level, but "vertical" causation: "as when we say that the intelligible form Fire (i.e. 'fieriness') is the cause of sensible fires in that it makes them to be what they are, to be fires, and so makes them to be" (lines 759–761). Formal determination is the cause of being to that which it determines.

The paradigm is Platonic participation. Plato's *Phaedo* provides the foundational formula: "if anything else is beautiful besides the Beautiful itself, it is beautiful through nothing else than that it participates in that Beautiful" (Phaedo 100d4–8, lines 836–840). As cause, the form is at once immanent (present as the nature by which instances are what they are) and transcendent (as one universal nature in many instances, other than and unconditioned by all of them): "Where, among the beautiful things in the world, is Beauty? Everywhere and nowhere: everywhere, in that wherever there is a beautiful thing, there is Beauty, as the nature which it has by which it is beautiful; nowhere, in that as one universal intelligible nature it is other than the many beautiful things and is not confined to any one of them" (lines 852–856). The form's "transcendence is thus a strict implication of its immanence" (line 857).

Plotinus develops this into an account of all being as the appearance of the One. The effects are not additional beings produced by the cause but the differentiated appearances of the cause itself, expressed through what Perl calls *complicatio-explicatio*: "The cause, therefore, is the enfolding or *complicatio* of the effects, and the effects are the unfolding or *explicatio* of the cause" (lines 1071–1072). The One "encompasses all the other things" (Plotinus V.5.9.6–10, lines 1063–1065) as their undifferentiated containment, while all things are nothing but the differentiated presentation of the One. "Being is a trace of the One" (V.5.5.14, line 1131), in that the multiplicity of intelligible forms is the One as it is given, as appearance, to Intellect.

Dionysius applies this structure to God's relation to all beings. The operative principle, which Dionysius cites explicitly, is that "the things that belong to the effects pre-exist in the causes [*proenesti ta ton aitiation tois aitiois*]" (DN II.8, 645D, lines 1234–1235). God is "the life of living things and being of beings [*ton zonton zoe kai ton onton ousia*]" (DN I.3, 589C, lines 1242–1243), present to all as the constitutive determination by which each is itself. The key formulation: God "is not. But he is being to beings [*autos esti to einai tois ousi*]" (DN V.4, 817D, lines 1246–1247). This is sharpened in the formula from the *Celestial Hierarchy*: "the being of all things is the divinity beyond being [*to gar einai panton estin he huper to einai theotes*]" (CH I.4, 177D, lines 1282–1283). God is the being that all beings have, and as such is beyond being in that he is not himself one of them.

The consequence is God's simultaneous total transcendence and total immanence. Dionysius expresses this using Procline vocabulary: "the divine processions are 'unparticipatedly participated [*amekhetos mekhomena*]'" (DN II.5, 644A, lines 1302–1303) — the same God is differently present in different things, unconstrained by any of them. God is "all things in all things and nothing in any [*en pasi panta esti kai en oudeni ouden*]" (DN VII.3, 872A, lines 1359–1360). God is even named "the Different" — the productive differentiation by which beings are distinct from one another: "his unitary multiplication [*ton heniaion autou polyplasasmon*] and the uniform processions of his multiple-generation [*polygonias*] to all things" (DN IX.5, 913B, lines 1379–1381).

Perl uses the concept of appearance to navigate between two errors. Against *monism*: "every being, precisely in that it is a being, i.e. something distinct, finite, and intelligible, *ipso facto* is not God" (lines 1439–1440). Against *dualism*: "All things are other than God, but God is not other than all things" (lines 1458–1459). Perl calls Dionysius's position, "for want of a better term, 'theophanism'" (line 1485): "The relation between appearance and that which appears is irreducible to either unity or duality and cannot be expressed in any terms other than those of appearance, manifestation, image, expression" (lines 1486–1488). In theophany, "wherever we look, we are not seeing God, in that every being, every object of thought, is not God; and wherever we look, we are seeing God, as he appears, for every being, every object of thought, is nothing but a presentation or appearance of God" (lines 1475–1478). This understanding is, Perl concludes, "a strict consequence, developed in the Neoplatonic tradition, of the original principle that to be is to be intelligible" (lines 1413–1414).

## Chapter 3 — Goodness, Beauty, and Love

*Source: endnotes to Chapter 3, lines 5079–5302.*

Chapter 3 treats DN IV.1–17, where Dionysius names God the Good, the Beautiful, and Love, and argues that these names describe not separate divine attributes but a single dynamic structure: procession from the Good and reversion to the Good, mediated by Beauty and Love.

**The Good as the transcendent source.** The chapter follows Plato and Aristotle in identifying the Good as the highest name for the divine. Aristotle's teleology converges with this: as Lear notes, "the desire which God inspires is none other than the desire of each organism to realize its form," and in trying to realize its form "the organism is doing all that it can do to imitate God's thought—and thus to imitate God himself" [5089–5092]. The realized form in any thing is thus the result of its desire for God. Armstrong's comment on Aristotle's unmoved mover applies equally to the Neoplatonic and Dionysian Good: "This is one of the fundamental principles of Greek philosophical thought" [5094–5095].

**Remaining, procession, reversion — ontologically simultaneous.** The triad of *monē* (remaining), *proodos* (procession), and *epistrophē* (reversion) is the fundamental structure of all reality. A critical point: "remaining, procession, and reversion are not sequential but ontologically simultaneous" [5127–5128]. Procession and reversion together constitute a single movement — "the diastole-systole which is the life of the universe" (Dodds) [5118–5119] — "another expression of the complicatio-explicatio structure of reality" [5121–5122]. The effect's existing consists in its reversion: "the effect's existing consists in its reversion" [5110–5111]. Proclus's *Elements of Theology* prop. 144 states the ontological stakes: "if anything fall away from the gods and become utterly isolated from them, it retreats into non-being and disappears, since it is wholly bereft of the principles which constitute it" [5105–5108].

**Beauty as the formal presence of the Good.** Beauty is the aspect of the Good that draws things back by appearing within them as their formal perfection. "The formal perfection of the effect is an image, in the sense of manifest presence, of the cause" [5161–5163]. The pun Dionysius derives from Proclus — *kállos*/kaléin (beauty/calling) — captures this: beauty "calls" all things toward it by being present in them as their own perfection (DN IV.7, 701C; Proclus, *Commentary on the First Alcibiades*, 328) [5158–5159]. The formal cause, final cause, and efficient cause are ultimately identical: Dodds notes this "identity of the efficient with the final cause… depends on the identity of both with the formal cause" [5100–5102].

**Love (*erōs*) as cosmic ecstasy.** God as Love is not merely the object toward which all things tend but the active ecstatic power by which he goes out of himself into all things. A decisive distinction: Dionysius says God goes out of himself *ἀνεκφοιτήτον ἑαυτοῦ* — "without going out from himself" (DN IV.13, 712B) [5181–5182]. "The idea that the One of Plotinus is 'self-contained' is expressly contradicted by Plotinus himself, and depends on the misconception of the One as something, as a being, having some definite identity of its own and therefore limited by that identity" [5183–5186]. God as ecstatic Love is thus not a specifically Christian transformation of Neoplatonism; it is the Neoplatonic structure properly understood.

**Challenging the "Christian transformation" narrative on *erōs*.** A recurring scholarly claim holds that Dionysius's use of *erōs* for God going out toward creatures is a distinctively Christian innovation over Neoplatonism. Perl systematically dismantles this. De Vogel argued that the term *erōs* is largely absent from Proclus's *Elementa Theologiae*; Perl replies: "it admits that everything Dionysius means by ἔρως is centrally present in Proclus" [5202–5204] even under different terminology (*pronoētikos*, *agathoeides*, *sōstikos*, *teleiotikos*, *sunektikos* [5197–5201]). Proclus's *Commentary on the First Alcibiades* 55–56 uses the concept of divine *ἔρως προνοητικός* — providential love — showing that for Proclus "ἔρως no longer necessarily connotes a lack or need, such that it could not be ascribed to God" [5191–5192]. On the eros/agape distinction more broadly, the notes state Dionysius makes no distinction in meaning between the two [5132–5133], and his use of both terms is interchangeable. Trouillard formulates the identity of descent and ascent: "Since the divine communication would lose its value if God did not remain the Absolute, the descent of God toward man is nothing other than the ascent of man toward God" [5213–5216].

**The shift from Aristotle to Neoplatonism.** The chapter's most pointed claim is that the difference between a God who is merely the object of love and a God who actively loves is "fundamentally a difference not between pagan and Christian but between Aristotelian and Neoplatonic conceptions of God" [5235–5237]. In saying that as Love God "is moved," Dionysius "directly targets the Aristotelian doctrine of God as the unmoved mover" [5221–5223]. Aristotle's God as "pure form, being (*ousia*), and intellect, keeps himself to himself. The God of Plotinus and Dionysius, as not any being but 'all beings and not even one,' 'all things in all things and nothing in any,' overflows, goes forth, or is differently present in all things" [5228–5232]. The ecstatic love is God's overflowing beyond-being, not a personal-relational category imported from Christianity.

**Creation and necessity.** The notes address the question of whether God creates by necessity or freedom. Trouillard's formulation in Perl's note 45: "The processive movement, strictly speaking, is neither necessary nor contingent. Neither necessary, since it creates, along the way, its own laws. Nor contingent, since it invents at once the possible and the real" [5250–5254]. The Neoplatonists acknowledge "the necessity of superabundance or of generosity" [5272–5274] — not logical or external necessity, but the necessity of the Good's own nature as gift. Trouillard puts it: "'God is love' signifies 'God gives himself to his creatures'" [5258–5259]. The irony Perl notes: "Plotinus has been condemned by Christian thinkers both for saying that God does not 'care for' his products and for saying that he is conditioned by a necessary relation to them, when in fact the sole meaning of the first claim is the denial of the second" [5260–5263]. Creation is neither necessary in the coercive sense nor arbitrary: it is the expression of what the Good essentially is.

### Chapter 3 Body Text Summary

*Source: Chapter 3 body text, range lines 1492–2257.*

**The Good as God's most proper name.** Perl opens by showing that the name "Good" best expresses God as both *archē* (source) and *telos* (end) of all things — a convergence already present in Plato's identification of goodness with intelligibility, in Aristotle's thesis that the formal cause is one with the final cause, and in Plotinus's principle that "everywhere what comes as a good is form" (VI.7.28.2) [line 1542]. Because goodness is the universal and constitutive character of all being — "to be intelligible, to be one, to be good, and hence to be, are all the same" [line 1586] — God as the Good is not a good thing among others but the universal formative principle by which all beings are beings. Perl concludes: "All the varied content of reality, therefore, consists of the many different modulations of goodness, of unity; all the different ways in which beings are, are so many different ways of being one and good" [lines 1587–1589]. Dionysius follows Proclus and Plotinus directly: "Every being is from the Beautiful and Good and in the Beautiful and Good and is reverted to the Beautiful and Good" (DN IV.10, 705D) [lines 1744–1745].

**The triad: remaining, procession, reversion.** Perl provides the most detailed treatment in the book of the Proclean cycle of *monē*, *proodos*, and *epistrophē*. Remaining designates the enfolding of effects in their cause; procession, the differentiation whereby effects are distinct beings; reversion, the relation of the effect to the cause as its end or good. The critical point is that these are not sequential stages but are "not temporally nor even ontologically subsequent" to one another [line 1646]: procession and reversion are "in reality the same relation of dependence" [lines 1725–1726] and both at once constitute the effect as a being. Proclus: "through that by which there is being to each thing, through this there is also being good" (El. Th., prop. 31) [lines 1653–1655]. Dionysius maps this directly onto the Good: "It is the Good...from which all things originate and are...and to which all things are reverted...and which all things desire" (DN IV.4, 700AB) [lines 1739–1743]. Because the effect's being consists in this active appropriation of its determination, "God cannot make beings without their active cooperation, for without that activity they would not be anything" [lines 1809–1812]. This introduces "something analogous to freedom and personhood at every level of reality, even in inanimate things" [lines 1815–1817].

**Beauty as the attractive aspect of the Good.** Perl follows Plotinus's identification of beauty with form: sensible things are beautiful insofar as they share in form, and since God is not form but "beautifulness" itself, "the beautiful things then must be measured and limited, but not the really beautiful...the primarily beautiful, then, and the first, is without form, and beauty is that, the nature of the Good" (VI.7.33.19–22) [lines 1843–1846]. For Dionysius: "the Beautiful beyond being is called Beauty on account of the beautifulness distributed from it to all beings in the manner proper to each" (DN IV.7, 701C) [lines 1857–1860]. Since to be is to be intelligible and to be intelligible is to be beautiful, "to be is to be beautiful. All being, therefore, qua intelligible or beautiful, is the unfolding or manifestation of Beauty itself" [lines 1875–1877]. Beauty is at once *archē*, *telos*, and paradigmatic cause: Dionysius states that "The Beautiful is the principle of all things, as making cause and moving and holding together the whole by the love of its proper beautifulness, and limit of all things...and paradigmatic, in that all things are determined according to it" (DN IV.7, 704AB) [lines 1886–1891].

**Love as cosmic ecstasy and the disclosure of God.** The culmination of Chapter 3 is Dionysius's identification of God with Love (*erōs*): "The cause of all things, through excess of goodness, loves all things, makes all things, perfects all things, sustains all things, reverts all things" (DN IV.10, 708AB) [lines 1897–1901]. Perl reads this as another presentation of the procession/reversion structure: God is not a self-contained self but "pure exteriority, having no inner core of 'selfhood'" [lines 1991–1992], present in all things as their constitutive differentiation. Dionysius states God "becomes out of himself in his providences toward all beings...without going out from himself" (DN IV.13, 712AB) [lines 1974–1981]. From this Perl draws a precise metaphysical consequence: "God is not a 'self' of his own, a being, but only the self, the determining identity, of others, of all things; and this is what Dionysius means by describing him as ecstatic love" [lines 1992–1995]. The erotic exchange is the procession/reversion cycle itself: "The divine love is ecstatic, not allowing lovers to belong to themselves, but to those beloved" (DN IV.13, 712A) [lines 2035–2037]. Every being must in effect say "I am, and yet not I, but God is in me — not as an obscure mystical paradox but as a precise metaphysical truth" [lines 2058–2061]. God as Love is expressed as "an eternal circle, whirling around through the Good, from the Good, and in the Good and to the Good" (DN IV.14, 712C–713A) [lines 2088–2091].

**On the question of necessity vs. freedom in creation.** Perl closes Chapter 3 by addressing whether God creates by necessity or freedom, arguing that the dichotomy is a misconception rooted in treating God as a being subject to logical conditions of possibility. Plotinus insists that the One produces by necessity but clarifies this means the One is not conditioned by any relation to its products: "[The One] is not held fast by necessity, but is itself the necessity and law of the others" (VI.8.10.34–35) [lines 2227–2228]. For Dionysius likewise: "Love...pre-existing in excess in the Good, did not permit it to remain unproductive in itself, but moved it to production, in the excess which is generative of all things" (DN IV.10, 708AB) [lines 2247–2250] — the "cannot not create" lies entirely in God himself as Love, not in any condition imposed on him.

## Chapter 4 — The Problem of Evil

*Note: Lines 5303–5371 in the assigned range file contain the endnotes to Chapter 4, not the chapter body text. The following is extracted faithfully from those endnotes, which contain substantial comparative and philosophical material.*

Chapter 4 addresses the problem of evil in [[on-divine-names|*On the Divine Names*]] IV.18–35. The endnotes reveal several key comparative moves Perl makes:

**Proclus vs. Dionysius on evil.** Perl engages Carlos Steel's comparative study "Proclus et Denys: De l'existence du mal," and explicitly disagrees with Steel's verdict. Steel "reads Dionysius' doctrine" in such a way as to judge it "greatly inferior to that of Proclus" [lines 5311–5312]. Perl's chapter is evidently a rebuttal of this judgment. A key difference: for Proclus, *daimones* "are wholly good spiritual beings" [line 5319] — so it "would be extremely misleading to translate this term as 'demons'" in the Proclean context [lines 5318–5320]. In Dionysius, by contrast, "they are not wholly good but are fallen angels" [lines 5323–5325] — a Christian transformation of the category.

**The Plotinian background.** Perl situates Dionysius in relation to Plotinus on evil, noting that in Plotinus (V.8.7.23–24), "this universe is all form" and even matter is "a sort of ultimate form, evidently meaning that matter is not a positive reality other than form and that it is a product of the Good" [lines 5314–5316]. The endnote to "they say this" in the chapter text specifies: "'they' must certainly include Plotinus" [lines 5326–5327]. A key difference is identified via Trouillard: "Plotinus judges that souls are ill insofar as they are not sufficiently minds. Dionysius treats minds as souls" [lines 5328–5330], reflecting the Christian priority of the personal over the purely intellectual.

**The demons and the fall.** Evil in souls is described (following Proclus, *De malorum subsistentia* 49.5–6, 12–13) as "weakness of activity" [lines 5331–5332]. The fall of the demons/angels is characterized in Dionysius's own term: not *anoetos* ("unintellectually") but *anoetos* in the sense of "unintelligibly" — "the fall itself cannot be understood" [lines 5348–5351]. Evil is thus not only parasitical on being but causally opaque: quoting John Jones, "The question 'Why is there evil at all?' is a mistaken question; for, it seeks an ultimate cause where there is none. However, in denying the legitimacy of this question we do not seek to explain evil away; rather, we indicate that evil is uncaused and unexplainable" [lines 5352–5357].

**Comparison with Augustine.** Perl draws an explicit parallel between Dionysius and Augustine: Dionysius's "view of evil as deficiency of being and hence as uncaused and unintelligible is thus closely comparable to Augustine's, although Augustine thinks more of efficient causality and Dionysius more of final causality" [lines 5358–5360]. Perl quotes Augustine (*City of God* XII.7–9): "Let no one, then, seek an efficient cause of an evil will. For its cause is not efficient, but deficient, because the evil will itself is not an effect of something, but a defect … the will is made evil by nothing else than the defection by which God is forsaken: a defection of which the cause, too, is certainly deficient" [lines 5361–5370].

**Connection to theophany.** The endnote on divine and creaturely causality (n. 18) quotes Trouillard on Aquinas/Neoplatonism in a way that illuminates how evil fits the theophany framework: "The more man acts, the more God acts. Grace does not limit freedom, it is freedom itself … One would be not able to say: If man creates himself, he has not use for God. Quite the contrary, he manifests in that itself the power of God, capable of producing genuine causes" [lines 5338–5344] — evil, as failure of activity, is the failure of this self-manifestation and thus the only category of thing that falls outside theophany rather than constituting it.

### Chapter 4 Body Text Summary

*Source: Chapter 4 body text, range lines 2258–2752.*

**The privation theory as philosophical doctrine.** Perl opens by framing the doctrine of evil as privation not as "cosmic optimism" or denial of evil's reality, but as "a profound and compelling theory which is more philosophically satisfying than many other accounts of evil" [lines 2272–2276]. The derivation follows necessarily from the principle that being is intelligibility: "since being is intelligible, therefore it has the Good beyond being as its first principle, and every being is a different manifestation of goodness" [lines 2283–2286]. A wholly evil being would be wholly unintelligible and therefore not a being at all — "Nothing can be and be evil, insofar as it is. A wholly evil being is a contradiction in terms" [lines 2292–2294]. Perl traces this from Plotinus — who identifies evil with matter as privation (I.8.5.8–13) [lines 2311–2316] and with the soul's descent from intellectual contemplation [lines 2344–2370] — through Proclus, who agrees that evil must be partial and parasitic but expressly rejects Plotinus's identification of evil with matter. Proclus: "the Evil altogether, is without a share in all goods...what is altogether evil is privation and lack of goods" (*De mal. sub.* 51.1–8) [lines 2383–2386]; and if matter is necessary for the cosmos it cannot be evil, for "evil is one thing, and the necessary another" (*De mal. sub.* 32.1–5) [lines 2397–2401]. Dionysius follows Proclus on this: "if they say that matter is necessary for the completion of all the cosmos, how is matter evil? For evil is one thing, and the necessary another" (DN IV.28, 729A) [lines 2484–2487].

**How evil can exist in a theophany system.** Perl's central philosophical move in the body text is to ground the privation theory in the active character of reversion: since beings must *actively* appropriate their constitutive determinations — "To be is the activity of a being" [line 2542] — there is a possibility of failure. "A being is evil, then, insofar as it does not perform the proper activities which are its mode of being, and to that extent it fails to be" [lines 2545–2547]. Evil is thus not a rival positive principle but the failure of theophany from within: "insofar as anything is evil, i.e. insofar as it is not, God is not productively present in it" [lines 2598–2600]. Evil as failure of reversion means it is a failure to love God: "since, as we have seen, to be is to love God, and anything can be only in and by desiring God, then insofar as anything does not desire God, it falls short of complete being" [lines 2550–2552]. And crucially, evil can occur at any level — including intellects — a significant extension beyond Proclus, who finds evil only at the level of human souls and bodies (Dionysius: "this is evil, in intellects and souls and bodies: the weakness and falling away from the condition of their proper goods," DN IV.27, 728D) [lines 2530–2531].

**The demon and fallen-angel problem.** Dionysius's most distinctive extension of the privation theory is to demons/fallen angels. Perl shows that Dionysius denies demons any positive evil nature: "the demons are not evil by nature" (DN IV.23, 724C) [line 2505]; they are evil "not insofar as they are, for they are from the Good and received a good reality, but insofar as they are not, by being weak in preserving their principle" (DN IV.23, 725A) [lines 2506–2508]. More precisely: "they are not evil by nature, but by the deficiency of angelic goods" (DN IV.23, 725B) [lines 2511–2512]. Demons "are not altogether without a share in the Good, insofar as they both are and live and think...and by privation and fleeing away and falling away from the goods that are appropriate to them they are called evil" (DN IV.23, 725C) [lines 2519–2522]. The soul's account is the same: "Neither, then, in demons nor in us is evil as an evil being, but as failure and absence of the perfection of our proper goods" (DN IV.24, 728A) [lines 2524–2526]. Since what demons lack is the proper perfection whereby they *are*, their evil is simultaneously a failure of being: demons exist but exist less than they should.

**Evil as causeless and unintelligible — the philosophical resolution.** Perl treats the causelessness of evil not as an evasion but as the doctrine's philosophical strength. Since causality requires goodness (every cause produces a good, and to explain anything is to show how it is good), an evil that had a cause would not be evil but good. "As non-being, as inactivity, evil is without cause (*anaition*)" (DN IV.30, 732A; IV.32, 732D) [lines 2663–2664]. Perl makes this the key argument: "if evil, the failure to desire the Good and so to be, took place for some reason, if it had a cause in terms of which it could be explained, that cause, that reason, would be a good. Evil itself, then, would not be evil but good" [lines 2700–2703]. Unintelligibility is the very meaning of evil: Perl quotes Dionysius's *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* that man "unintelligibly fell from the divine goods" [lines 2707–2709]. This is why theodicies fail when they succeed: "To the extent that they satisfactorily account for or make sense of evil, they tacitly or expressly deny that it is evil and show that it is in fact good" [lines 2730–2731]. Perl closes with Dionysius's catalogue: "Evil, then, is privation and lack and weakness and asymmetry and failure and aimless and beautyless and lifeless and mindless and irrational and purposeless and unstable and causeless and indeterminate and unproductive and inactive and ineffective and unordered and unlike and limitless and dark and insubstantial and itself no being whatever in any way whatsoever" (DN IV.32, 732D) [lines 2714–2721].

**Comparison with Plotinus and Proclus in the body text.** Perl's extended treatment in the body establishes that Dionysius makes the greatest change to both predecessors by (1) expressly rejecting Plotinus's identification of evil with matter as necessary (following Proclus) and (2) extending the privation theory to all levels of being, including intellects — whereas for Proclus "evil enters only when we come to the level of human souls" [lines 2406–2407]. On desire, Perl follows a common Greek principle through both Plotinus and Dionysius: "And beings desire the Beautiful and Good, and do all the things they do on account of what seems good...how will evil be among beings?" (DN IV.19, 716C) [lines 2563–2567]. And "desiring that which is not, [the demons] desire evil" (DN IV.23, 725C) — meaning that insofar as anything desires evil, it is "really just not desiring at all: a missing of genuine desire" (DN IV.34, 733D) [lines 2586–2590].

## Chapter 5 — The Hierarchy of Being

*Note: Lines 5372–5570 in the assigned range file contain the endnotes to Chapter 5, not the chapter body. The following is extracted faithfully from those endnotes.*

Chapter 5 corresponds to DN V.1–3 and elaborates hierarchy as the structure of theophany. The endnotes are especially rich in comparative material with Proclus and in theological applications.

**Wisdom as Intellect.** Dionysius "substitutes the biblical term Wisdom (*sophia*) for the philosophical term Intellect (*nous*), but the meaning remains the same" [lines 5380–5382]; Perl cites Plotinus (V.8.4.37; V.8.5.1–3, 15–20) to show this usage was not unprecedented in non-Christian Neoplatonism. The divine procession Wisdom "includes all modes of cognition, from angelic intellection down to the sensation of irrational animals" [lines 5377–5378].

**Proclus and the henads.** Perl engages Proclus's complicated theology carefully: "strictly speaking, for Proclus, only the henads are gods, while terms such as Being, Life, and Intellect are lesser causes which derive their being and causal power from their proper henads" [lines 5386–5389]. Each henad "symbolizes" the One on a different plane of reality, and by the principle of continuity this is true of all lower levels derived from them as well [lines 5395–5401]. The key interpretive move is to show that this is not polytheism in any vulgar sense: Perl quotes Plotinus's reply to the Gnostics — "It is not contracting the divine into one but showing it in that multiplicity in which God himself has shown it, which is proper to those who know the power of God" (II.9.9.36–42) [lines 5403–5408]. The endnote adds: "Just as the God of Dionysius and the Neoplatonists transcends the opposition between theism and atheism, so he transcends that between monotheism and polytheism, for he is strictly speaking neither one nor many in any positive sense" [lines 5412–5415].

**The structure of hierarchy: service, not domination.** Perl's chapter evidently argues that Dionysian hierarchy is not a chain of command but a structure of providential service. The endnote (n. 26) draws the explicit connection to Matthew 20:25–27 ("whoever would be great among you must be your servant") — "the higher any being is in the order of reality, the more it is in the service of — in Dionysius' terms, providentially proceeds to — all that is below it" [lines 5492–5496]. Rutledge is quoted to show how this overcomes the opposition of direct and mediated divine production: "If the whole being and activity of each member is received it seems to matter little whether we say he or God creates the one immediately below. The immanence and transcendence of God … must be stated with exactly the same emphasis" [lines 5499–5503].

**Sacramental extension of hierarchy.** The chapter apparently extends the sacramental principle to all reality: the footnote on hierarchy states that "the celebrant of any sacrament acts *in persona Christi*, and his sacramental activity is that of Christ. In Dionysius, this sacramental principle, in which the activity of the creature is the activity of God-in-it, is extended to all reality, which, as theophany, is sacramental in nature" [lines 5523–5527]. Being "by nature" vs. "by placement [*thesei*]" (i.e., by participation) is the technical distinction at stake [lines 5529–5530].

**Processive power and immanence.** Perl draws on Trouillard's summary of Proclus for the principle that every level of hierarchy participates in the processive power of the One: "every efficacy is a modalisation of unity … Every subordinate cause plays within the more comprehensive causality which upholds it … Nothing has power except by the omnipresent power of unifying unity. The number of mediations changes nothing in this" [lines 5483–5488].

**Hierarchy and sin.** The endnotes note a consequence Dionysius does not develop: "if any being fails to perform its proper function the order of the whole is impaired, just as, in a choral dance, if one dancer gets out of step the entire dance is disrupted" [lines 5552–5554]. Marion is cited: "a failure defeats not so much the one who commits it as the one near him, and so on … This is also called sin" [lines 5555–5558]. The communion of saints as Dantesque hierarchy is illustrated via *Inferno* II: Mary sends Lucy, Lucy sends Beatrice, Beatrice sends Virgil to rescue Dante — "Dante's salvation is thus effected by Love, or God, in Mary, Lucy, Beatrice, and Virgil" [lines 5567–5570].

### Chapter Five: The Hierarchy of Being — Body Text Summary

*Source: body text, lines 2753–3465.*

Perl opens Chapter 5 by establishing that Dionysius's concept of hierarchy spans the whole of reality, not only the angelic and ecclesiastical ranks. The divine names discussed in DN V — Good, Being, Life, Wisdom — are the modes of God's constitutive presence in beings ranked by the universality of their participation: the Good extends to all beings and non-beings; Being, to all beings; Life, to all living things; Wisdom, to all cognitive beings. Dionysius quotes DN V.1 (816B): "That of Wisdom is extended to all intellectual and rational and sensitive beings and is above all these things" [lines 2780–2781]. These "names" are thus "the modes in which God is constitutively present in the various ranks of beings" [line 2769], directly following Proclus's ordering of Being, Life, and Intellect by causal universality (El. Th., prop. 101). Crucially, Dionysius transforms the inherited scheme by refusing to "hypostasize" these perfections as separate productive divinities: "all the good processions and divine names hymned by us are of one God" (DN V.2, 816C–817A [lines 2832–2837]). Perl argues the difference is more religious than metaphysical: "Proclus hymns the many modes of unity as a multiplicity of gods; Dionysius hymns God as the many modes of unity" [lines 2876–2877], but for both, these are "the differentiated presentations of divinity" [line 2878].

A key philosophical moment is Perl's answer to Dionysius's hypothetical objection (DN V.3, 817A): if living things are above mere beings, should Life rank above Being? The reply turns on the internal structure of participation: Life is not superadded to Being but is the more specific and intense mode of Being proper to living things; Wisdom "nothing but a specification of Life" [lines 2972–2973]. Higher things are closer to God not because they stand between the lower and God, but because they "participate eminently" in all the universal perfections. God is analogously present to each: he "shines forth the ray beyond being... by illuminations analogous to each of beings" (DN I.2, 588CD [lines 2998–3001]), present in each thing "according to its rank (*kat' axian*)" so that "being is to a stone as life is to a plant, as sensation is to an animal, as reason is to a man, as intellection is to an angel: its proper mode of goodness and unity, of interiority and selfhood, its participation in God" [lines 3010–3012].

Perl's most pointed argument against conventional readings is that hierarchical order does not separate lower beings from God but is "itself the very ground of his immediate presence in all things" [lines 3085–3086]. The misconception arises from treating God as "the first and highest being" at the apex. But "since God is not any being but 'all things in all things and nothing in any' (DN VII.3, 872A)" [lines 3117–3118], he transcends and permeates the whole structure. "The goodness of the Godhead which is beyond all things extends from the highest and most venerable substances to the last, and is still above all" (DN IV.4, 697C [lines 3120–3122]): "The entire hierarchy of reality, therefore, from the highest seraph to the least speck of dust, is the immediate presence and manifestation of God" [lines 3122–3125].

For both Plotinus and Proclus (and Dionysius following them), the apparent opposition between God's direct productive presence and hierarchical mediation dissolves once procession is understood as manifestation rather than the manufacture of additional entities. Since the effect's whole content is nothing but the participated presence of its cause, the effect's own causal activity is that of its cause at work in it: "what is produced by a lower level is (not less but) more produced by the higher" [line 3227]. The chapter's central formula is what Perl calls "immediate mediation": "it is by the hierarchical mediation of beings that God is immediately constitutively present to all" [lines 3392–3393]. Each being's identity consists in its hierarchical relations to others; "there is no such thing as an individual, a being conceived as a closed, self-contained unit": "Each thing, indeed, is nothing but its relations to others, its place within the structure of immediate mediation. Hierarchy is thus, as its name signifies, the principle of sacredness, which is to say of being" [lines 3438–3441]. Perl closes by describing Dionysian hierarchy as a *thiasós*, a bacchic revel, and as "the communion of saints," whose "hierarchical structure of reality is the articulated manifestation of the divine love that constitutes all things" [lines 3455–3459].

## Chapter 6 — The Continuum of Cognition

*Note: Lines 5571–5691 in the assigned range file contain the endnotes to Chapter 6, not the chapter body. The following is extracted faithfully from those endnotes.*

Chapter 6 treats DN VII and the epistemological dimension of theophany. The endnotes confirm a reading of Dionysian cognition as a single continuum from sensation to mystical union — all modes of knowing are modes of knowing God.

**Consciousness as the key to Neoplatonic hypostases.** Perl draws on Hadot and Lloyd to frame the Neoplatonic background: "What makes a non-phenomenological description of any hypostasis inadequate is Neoplatonic idealism. The hypostases are experiences; they are types of consciousness; while, therefore, they have abstract and objective properties, they have also what we call phenomenological properties" (Lloyd, *Anatomy of Neoplatonism*, 126) [lines 5577–5581]. Gerson is cited for the principle that all forms of cognition are "images and inferior versions of the form of cognition that is found in the *archē*, Intellect … Plotinus holds that intellection … entails an identification of subject and object. Inferior forms of cognition … involve increasing qualifications of this identification in the direction of the externalization of the object in relation to the subject" [lines 5582–5589].

**One world, many modes of apprehension.** Perl rejects the "two worlds" reading of Neoplatonism, citing Deck: "Plotinus does not have two worlds, but only one. His world of true being is not, except metaphorically, a world above the everyday world. It is the everyday world, not as experienced by sense, by opinion, or by discursive reasoning, but as known by intellect, the Nous, the Knower" [lines 5598–5602]. The same point applies to Dionysius: "there is not a plurality of worlds but one world which can be seen and known in different ways. Thus we can know all that is in the most unified and complete way, through contemplation of intelligible beings, or we can know things in the least unified and complete way through the sensation of sensible beings" (Jones, *Divine Names*, 44) [lines 5619–5622]. Semmelroth is quoted: "In [Dionysius], as for Greek thought in general, a communion of knowledge is, at an entirely [different] level than in our thought, a kind of communion of being" [lines 5607–5609].

**All knowledge is knowledge of God.** The conclusion of the chapter's argument is stated by Jones: "For Pseudo-Dionysius, in all knowledge we always see and know the divinity, for the divinity is all in all" [lines 5629–5630]. Balthasar adds: "Denys contemplates the divine symbols in creation and the Church with an aesthetic delight. Things are not simply the occasion for his seeing God; rather, he sees God in things. Colours, shapes, essences and properties are for him immediate theophanies" [lines 5631–5634]. Perl explicitly notes this overturns the common view of Dionysius "as a denigrator of the senses and the sensible" [lines 5638–5639].

**The ecstatic summit: *agnosia*.** At the apex of cognition Dionysius places mystical unknowing. Perl situates this via an explicit allusion to Plotinus. Dionysius's term *epibolē* or *paradochē* ("direct awareness and reception") alludes to Plotinus VI.7.35.22–26: "Intellect also, then, has one power for thinking, by which it looks at the things in itself, and one by which it looks at what transcends it by a direct awareness and reception [*epibolē tini kai paradochē*] … And that first one is the contemplation of Intellect in its right mind, and the other is Intellect in love, when it goes out of its mind 'drunk with the nectar'" [lines 5670–5676]. The "divine darkness" beyond being is not in Plotinus by name, but "the same meaning is present in phrases such as 'You will not think' and 'Take away everything'" [lines 5683–5685]. Moses in mystical unknowing is described as "being all of him who is beyond all things, and of nothing, neither of himself nor of another" (MT I.3, 1001A) [lines 5686–5688].

**Mystical union not as "experience."** Perl follows Michael Sells in rejecting the framing of Dionysian mysticism in terms of (modern) "experience": "If the nonintentionality claims of apophatic mystics are taken seriously, and if experience is, by definition, intentional, it necessarily follows that mystical union is not an experience … The apophatic mystic speaks of the birth of the son in the soul, of annihilation, of an awakening without an awakener, but does not speak of 'the experience' of such birth, annihilation, or awakening" (Sells, *Mystical Languages of Unsaying*, 214) [lines 5648–5657].

### Chapter Six: The Continuum of Cognition — Body Text Summary

*Source: body text, lines 3467–4191.*

Perl opens Chapter 6 against what he characterizes as the "conventional" misreading: "A sharp dichotomy and dualism between sense and intellect, as two different cognitive faculties apprehending two different kinds of objects, is conventionally regarded as perhaps the most fundamental feature of Platonic thought" [lines 3467–3470]. His counter-thesis is that in the entire Platonic tradition "sense and intellect, with discursive reason as a mean between them, constitute a continuum of modes of cognition, articulated by the degrees of unity in which they apprehend reality" [lines 3472–3475]. Plotinus provides the tradition's sharpest formulation: "these sense-perceptions here are dim intellections, but the intellections there are clear sense-perceptions" (VI.7.7.29–31 [lines 3743–3744]). Intellection is not a different faculty aimed at different objects; it is the same cognitive activity at its highest degree of unity.

Dionysius inherits this structure directly. All cognition is "unitive of those who know and the things known" (DN VII.4, 872D [lines 3754–3756]). Angelic intellects grasp divine things "intellectually, immaterially, uniformly," with a "synoptic" power (DN VII.2, 868B [lines 3769–3772]); human souls proceed "discursively... by what is divided and manifold" (DN VII.2, 868BC [lines 3775–3777]); "one would not miss the mark in saying that even sense-perceptions themselves are an echo of Wisdom" (DN VII.2, 868C [lines 3780–3781]). Perl's conclusion: "For Dionysius as for Plotinus, then, sense, discursive reason, and intellection represent different points on a continuum of degrees of unity in the apprehension of reality" [lines 3819–3821].

The decisive philosophical move is the extension of this continuum simultaneously downward to sensation and upward beyond intellect. Since all cognition apprehends being, and being is theophany, God is the object of every cognitive mode — including sense. But since God is not any being, he escapes every cognitive grasp equally. Perl treats DN VII.3, 872A as the chapter's pivot: "God is known both through knowledge and through unknowing. And of him there is both intellection and reason and knowledge and touching and sense-perception and opinion and imagination and name and all other things; and he is neither thought nor spoken nor named... he is all things in all things and nothing in any, and he is known to all from all and to none from any" [lines 3882–3889]. Perl's emphasis: "'Of God there is sense-perception': this stunning but wholly consistent affirmation cannot be overemphasized. It overturns all conventional misrepresentations of Neoplatonism in general, and Dionysius' thought in particular, as a 'gnostic' repudiation of the senses and the sensible world" [lines 3896–3899]. Because God is "all things in all things," to perceive anything is to perceive God in that thing; "there is nothing to be known in anything but God-in-it" [lines 3893–3895].

At the upper end, the continuum culminates in the *agnosia*, the union above intellect — not a rupture but a continuation. "Souls, uniting and gathering their manifold reasonings into one intellectual purity, go forth... through immaterial and undivided intellection to the union above intellection" (DN XI.2, 949D [lines 4043–4045]): "the mystical unknowing of God as no thing is the inseparable completion of the knowledge of God, by every mode of cognition, as all things in all things" [lines 4048–4050]. The ecstasy — "purely, from yourself and all things, you will be led up to the ray beyond being of the divine darkness" (MT I.1, 997B–1000A [lines 4063–4066]) — is the complete unification of consciousness and so its proper consummation. For cognitive beings, the cognitive ascent and its culmination in agnosia *is* their mode of reversion to and participation in God: "for us as rational souls capable of ascending to intellect and beyond, the cognitive ascent is our reversion, and the culmination of the ascent in the ecstatic union above intellect is the fulfilment of our nature" [lines 4085–4089].

The chapter closes with the corollary on divine cognition. God does not know beings by receiving information from them; he "pre-contains and pre-gathers the vision and knowledge and being of all things from itself and in itself, as cause" (DN VII.2, 869AB [lines 4150–4153]), knowing and producing things simultaneously. "God 'knows things into being,' making all things in knowing them by knowing himself in them" [lines 4183–4184]; thus "all reality is the knowledge of God, in both the subjective and the objective sense of the genitive" [lines 4190–4191].

## Chapter 7 — Symbolism

*Note: Lines 5692–5768 in the assigned range file contain the endnotes to Chapter 7, not the chapter body. The following is extracted faithfully from those endnotes.*

Chapter 7 treats [[celestial-hierarchy|*Celestial Hierarchy*]] I–II.4 and the metaphysical grounding of Dionysian symbolism. The endnotes confirm that Perl reads symbolism as the *positive* face of theophany: if all being is God's self-manifestation, then material symbols are not inadequate stopgaps but the natural mode of divine appearance in the sensible order.

**Symbolism grounded in metaphysics, not arbitrary convention.** Perl follows Semmelroth: "As a Neoplatonist the Pseudo-Areopagite certainly represents a strong realism of symbols … Between sign and signified in the symbol obtains not only a connection of knowledge, but also a connection of presence … In the thought of the Pseudo-Areopagite the symbol is realistically bound with the symbolized" (*Θεολογία συμβολική*, 8–9) [lines 5701–5705]. Even "the conceptual expression still always itself remains a kind of symbol" [lines 5699–5700] — apophatic negations are not more "literal" than sensible symbols; all language, conceptual or imagistic, participates in the same symbolic character.

**Material symbols as appropriate medium.** The chapter addresses the use of apparently unworthy, even absurd, material images for the divine — Dionysius refers to God described as a "worm" (Psalm 22:6) [lines 5697–5698]. Perl's argument (in the chapter body, referenced by the note at line 5706) is that such images are not merely accommodations to limited minds but the appropriate vehicles: "in the absolute unification of cognition we do not merely see but become the darkness beyond being" [lines 5707–5709], suggesting that the symbol's function is to carry the reader beyond itself into union.

**Veiling and unveiling; *proballein*.** The key Dionysian verb *proballein* ("to project" or "to throw forward") is used, as O'Rourke observes, "in two closely related significations to convey the same fundamental reality from distinct viewpoints, namely both the veiling and the unveiling or unfolding of God through creation" [lines 5712–5714]. The creation of the visible universe is thus simultaneously a veil before the divine and a revelation of the divine — the symbol both hides and discloses.

**The angel as revealer who hides by revealing.** Perl draws out the precise connotation of Dionysius's term *exangellos* for the angel: not merely a "messenger" but "one who reveals something which remains hidden, and indeed hides it by revealing it, in that narration takes the place of direct portrayal" [lines 5736–5739]. The endnote adds that "the precise connotations of Dionysius' carefully chosen term are essential to his philosophical meaning" [lines 5738–5739].

**Connection to Proclean theurgy.** Perl notes that the Neoplatonic term *sinthema* (σύνθημα) "is used interchangeably with *symbolon* in late Neoplatonic theurgical texts" (van den Berg, *Proclus' Hymns*, 70, 79, 126) [lines 5718–5720] — connecting Dionysian symbolism to the theurgical tradition in which material tokens (*sinthemata*) participate ontologically in the divine realities they invoke.

**Sensible symbols vindicated.** Perl's Chapter 7 is explicitly described as overturning the reading of Dionysius as a "despiser of the sensible" [lines 5743–5744]. Balthasar is quoted: "The same knowledge of God demands both a deeper penetration into the image and also a more sublime transcendence beyond it, and the two are not separated one from another but are the more fully integrated, the more perfectly they are achieved" [lines 5745–5748]. The incarnation is invoked as the ultimate symbol: Jones asks whether "in its procession and reversion the divinity itself takes on being in its ecstasis? Are not beings an 'incarnation' of the divinity?" [lines 5749–5754].

### Chapter Seven: Symbolism — Body Text Summary

*Source: body text, lines 4193–4566.*

Perl opens the chapter with a philosophical program more ambitious than scriptural interpretation: "A philosophical examination of Dionysius' theory of symbols shows that being as such, not merely in its sensible aspect, is symbolic, and that there can be no non-symbolic knowledge of God" [lines 4204–4207]. Since being is theophany — God's self-manifestation in finite modes — every being is inherently symbolic: it makes God available to cognition while simultaneously withholding him, since the finite presentation is not the infinite presented.

The collapse of the distinction between "intelligible names" and "sensible symbols" is the chapter's first major argument. Dionysius himself places "Being, Life, and Good" in a continuous series with "sun, star, fire, water, wind, dew, cloud, rock itself and stone" (DN I.6, 596C [lines 4243–4245]). The theoretical ground: his treatment of "similar" (noble, exalted) and "dissimilar" (base, material) representations in the Celestial Hierarchy shows that even the "similar" intelligible names are equally deficient — "it is as false, and false in the same sense, to say that God is Word, Mind, or Being as to say that he is lion, stone, fire, or worm" [lines 4270–4272] (CH II.3, 140CD). Conversely, since "not even one of beings is altogether deprived of participation in the Beautiful" (CH II.3, 141C, citing Genesis 1:31 LXX [lines 4284–4287]), nothing is absolutely inappropriate as a symbol. All expressions are equally — infinitely — inadequate; all are equally genuine participations in God; therefore all have the same ontological status as symbols. "This is simply a restatement of the doctrine that being is theophany" [lines 4309–4310].

The dual nature of every symbol — simultaneously revealing and concealing — is captured in the verb *proballein*. "Etymologically, this means 'to throw forth,' and so comes to mean both 'present, put forward, expose' and also 'shield, screen, set up a defense'" [lines 4388–4390]. Dionysius uses this word to say at once that symbols are "manifest presentations of hidden things" and that they "screen the knowledge of the unspoken and invisible" (Ep. IX.1, 1105BC [lines 4404–4417]). In DN V.5–6 Perl shows the same double meaning applied to being itself: "being (*to einai*) is presented (*probeblētai*)" as the first of all God's gifts (DN V.5, 820A [lines 4441–4443]); and "the beyond-goodness-itself is hymned... as presenting (*proballomenē*) the first gift of being itself" (DN V.6, 820C [lines 4443–4446]). Thus "the very being of all things... is a presentation which screens and a screen which presents God" [lines 4446–4448]. From DN VII.3, 869C–872A: "from the order of all beings, as presented-as-a-screen (*probeblēmenēs*) from him... we go up... to the beyond all things" [lines 4449–4455].

Revealing and concealing are not two stages but one: "All symbols, in that they are both similar and dissimilar, at once reveal and conceal that which they symbolize, and this is the very nature of a symbol and hence of being as symbol... it conceals precisely in and as revealing, and reveals precisely in and as concealing" [lines 4312–4316]. Dionysius makes this paradox explicit in his description of angels: the Good gives them "to reveal in themselves the hidden Goodness, and to be angels as annunciative of the divine silence, and as presenting clear lights interpretive of that which is in the sanctuaries (*probeblēmenas*)" (DN IV.2, 696BC [lines 4466–4471]). Perl's comment: "The angels reveal what is hidden; they announce the divine silence; they present-as-screens lights which interpret what is inaccessible. These paradoxes capture the very essence of symbolism: to hide what it reveals by revealing it and to reveal what it hides by hiding it" [lines 4472–4476].

The chapter's practical consequence is the rejection of any "direct, non-symbolic knowledge of God unveiled": "Whatever is unveiled is not God" [lines 4370–4371]. To ascend from symbols to mystical union is not to discard them but to penetrate into them. Perl's analogy: a word is most perfectly known by the reader who "in the very act of perceiving the word, is oblivious to the word as such and attentive only to its meaning" [lines 4501–4503]; the reader "who in perceiving the word unknows it in itself... truly knows and appreciates the word as word" [lines 4510–4511]. So Dionysius: "It is necessary, then, for us... to cross over into (*eisō... diabainein*) the sacred symbols in a way befitting the sacred, and not despise them, because they are the offspring and impressions of the divine characters" (Ep. IX.2, 1108C [lines 4512–4516]). "The ascent from symbols is the penetration into them" [line 4517]. At the peak, all beings are "most perfectly known in being wholly unknown... because all beings are nothing but symbols of God" [lines 4519–4522].

Perl closes the chapter by reading the Incarnation as the fullest expression of being as theophanic symbol: "out of the hidden the beyond-being has come forth into manifestation according to us, becoming a being in a human way. But he is hidden even after the manifestation, or... even in the manifestation" (Ep. III, 1069B [lines 4534–4541]). "Being as symbol, as theophany, and hence as being, is perfectly realized in Christ, in God incarnate, the finite being which is God-made-manifest" [lines 4565–4567].

## Conclusion

*Note: Lines 5769–5824 in the assigned range file contain the endnotes to the Conclusion and the Bibliography, not the Conclusion body. The endnotes are brief but indicate the Conclusion's argumentative orientation.*

The Conclusion appears to situate Perl's reading of Dionysius against the broader intellectual context of Neoplatonism and its modern critics. The endnotes reference:

- **Dodds on Proclus** as "a philosophy which endeavours to explain all things by an analysis of consciousness, and builds up a world in the mind out of materials furnished by the mind itself" (Coleridge, quoted in Dodds, *Elements of Theology*, xxxiii) [lines 5770–5773] — Perl evidently positions Dionysius within this idealist tradition and defends it against the dismissal of it as "subjective."

- **Nietzsche** (*Twilight of the Idols*, 48) is cited [lines 5774–5775], likely as a representative of the modern critique that Neoplatonic idealism is world-denying.

- **Plotinus on the necessity of truth** in Intellect: "if there is not truth in Intellect, then an intellect of this sort will not be truth, or truly Intellect, or Intellect at all. But then truth will not be anywhere else either" (V.5.1.56–57, 66–69) [lines 5778–5781] — suggesting Perl's Conclusion defends the realist claim that the Neoplatonic-Dionysian system does not collapse into subjectivism.

- **Armstrong on the Neoplatonic unity of transcendence and immanence**: "That which is utterly beyond us and cannot be expressed or thought is by its very transcendence of distance and difference most intimately present. The Neoplatonists express this with particular force: it was from them that Christianity and Islam learnt their understanding of the unity of transcendence and immanence" [lines 5785–5789] — the closing note suggests Perl's Conclusion urges the continued relevance of this tradition.

The Conclusion thus appears to defend Dionysian Neoplatonism as a resource for contemporary thought against both the reductive (Nietzschean/materialist) dismissal and the purely confessional theological reception, returning to the methodological framing of the Introduction.

### Conclusion Body Text Summary

*Source: body text, lines 4568–4669.*

Perl's Conclusion returns to "the single fundamental principle that may be called Parmenides' Law: *to gar auto noein estin te kai einai*: to be is to be intelligible" [lines 4568–4570]. The entire philosophical vision of Dionysius, Plotinus, and Proclus — the One beyond being, procession, reversion, theophany, hierarchy, cognition, symbolism — flows from this premise. Perl turns directly to Dodds's critique of Proclus: that "its fundamental weakness seems to me to lie in the assumption that the structure of the cosmos exactly reproduces the structure of Greek logic" [lines 4575–4577] — that ontology becomes "the projected shadow of logic." Perl's response is that Neoplatonism matches not "Greek logic" as a culturally contingent artifact but "the universal nature of thought as such, which necessarily apprehends its objects as beings, characterized and constituted by certain determinations in virtue of which they are what they are and so are beings at all" [lines 4586–4589].

The alternative to the Neoplatonic premise — the denial that being is intelligible — is, Perl argues, nihilism: "If being is not what is apprehended by thought, then thought does not apprehend being. This in effect means that there is no being, since whatever we call 'being' is not being but a projection, interpretation, illusion — in short, nothing" [lines 4597–4600]. This is "the condition of much contemporary reflection, to the extent that it consists in the claim that we can never 'break out' of our own language and thought to get at reality" [lines 4604–4608]. Against this, "The Neoplatonic vision takes as its starting point the intelligibility, which is to say the reality, of being" [lines 4610–4611].

From the intelligibility of being follows its necessary determinateness and hence its derivative status as manifestation: "Hence Neoplatonism arrives at a God who is 'beyond being' in a stricter, stronger, more absolute sense than is often recognized. This philosophy thus surpasses any 'onto-theology'" [lines 4614–4616]. The culminating claim is the identity of transcendence and immanence: "In Neoplatonism, in Plotinus, Proclus, and Dionysius, divine transcendence is conceived so radically that it coincides with divine immanence. Transcendence and immanence are not opposed to each other, nor do they merely mitigate or even complement each other; they are, rather, strictly identical" [lines 4621–4624]. The consequence: "the absolutely transcendent God of Neoplatonism is therefore nothing but what is manifest in and as all things, 'all things in all things and nothing in any' (DN VII.3, 872A)" [lines 4629–4631].

Perl closes with two contemporary applications. First, dialogue with phenomenology and Heidegger: Heidegger's critiques of "onto-theology" and "metaphysics of presence," and his analysis of how the technological stance reduces being to *Bestand* (stockpile), find in Dionysius a contemplative-liturgical alternative: "being is not an object for mastery and exploitation but a gift which is received only in being given and a symbol which is known only in being unknown" [lines 4657–4660]. Second, environmental philosophy: "The Dionysian, Neoplatonic understanding of all things, including animals, plants, and minerals, as theophanies which analogously think, live, and love, may be set against the objectifying and reductionist view of nature which has led to its exploitation and destruction" [lines 4662–4665]. The Conclusion thus returns the entire argument — from Parmenides' Law through the analysis of theophany, hierarchy, cognition, and symbolism — to a defense of the vision itself as "a compelling and philosophically grounded vision of all reality as the presence and manifestation of transcendent divinity" [lines 4668–4669].

## Perl's Contribution to the Wiki

Perl's reading challenges or enriches several existing wiki pages:
- **[[apophatic-theology|Apophatic theology]]**: Perl reframes apophasis not as "what God is not" but as acknowledgment that God transcends the very distinction between affirmation and negation — a philosophical rather than a merely rhetorical move
- **[[evil-as-privation|Evil as privation]]**: Perl's exposition of DN IV.18–35 deepens the account of how evil can "exist" within a system where all being is divine self-manifestation
