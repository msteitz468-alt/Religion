# Church Dogmatics I.1, §§1–7 (Karl Barth)

**Source**: `raw/scholarship/Church Dogmatics, Vol 1.1, Sections 1-7 The Doctrine of the Word of God, Study Edition 1 (Karl Barth).txt`
**Original**: *Kirchliche Dogmatik* I.1 (Theologischer Verlag Zürich, 1932–1938); English Study Edition, T&T Clark, 2009

## Overview

The first volume of Barth's *Church Dogmatics* is a Prolegomena — an account of the foundations and method of dogmatics itself before any specific doctrinal loci are treated. §§1–7 constitute the first half of Vol. I.1 and establish: (1) what dogmatics is and why it needs prolegomena; (2) the nature of church proclamation as dogmatics' material; (3) the threefold form of the Word of God; (4) the nature and attributes of the Word; (5) the knowability of the Word; and (6) the relationship between the Word, dogma, and the discipline of dogmatics.

This work is systematic theology (not biblical commentary): Barth's theological method is exhibited *in* the performance of dogmatic construction, not expounded separately. The hermeneutical commitments visible throughout are: Christocentrism, rejection of natural theology, the threefold-Word schema as the structural backbone, and the insistence that dogmatics is a church act, not speculative philosophy.

### §1. The Task of Dogmatics (lines 132–1539)

Barth opens with the compact thesis (lines 133–134): "As a theological discipline dogmatics is the scientific self-examination of the Christian Church with respect to the content of its distinctive talk about God." §1 has three sub-sections.

**§1.1 The Church, Theology, Science (lines 135–534).** Barth distinguishes three senses of "theology": (1) the faith and life of every believer ("theology" in the broadest sense); (2) the service and ministry of the Church — preaching, sacraments, mission; (3) the strictest and proper sense, "the science in which the Church examines itself" with regard to talk about God (lines 157–158, drawing on J. Gerhard's three-fold schema). The Church confesses God both in the existence of individual believers and in communal actions (preaching, sacraments, worship, mission). From this third sense of theology Barth derives three overlapping disciplines: *biblical theology* (question of the basis of Christian utterance), *practical theology* (question of the goal), and *dogmatic theology* (question of the content) — lines 213–217.

Barth then addresses whether theology is, or needs to be, a "science" in the modern academic sense. He engages H. Scholz's six demands on any science (freedom from contradiction, unity of object, verifiability, physical congruity, independence, axiomatisability — lines 389–432) and rejects them all: "Not an iota can be yielded here without betraying theology, for any concession at this point involves surrendering the theme of theology" (lines 422–423). Nevertheless, theology should still call itself a science for three practical reasons (lines 501–530): (1) it recognizes solidarity with other human concerns for truth; (2) it protests against the unexamined pagan assumptions built into modern scientific canons; (3) it refuses to separate itself from the other sciences as though they were beyond redemption by grace. Crucially, Barth insists theology's separate existence as a discipline is only a *factual* and *practical* necessity, not a *principled* one — other sciences might in principle take up the theological task (lines 229–260).

**§1.2 Dogmatics as an Enquiry (lines 535–837).** Barth argues that dogmatics presupposes two things simultaneously: (1) that true knowledge of God *can* be had by man — on the basis of the *analogia fidei* (analogy of faith, Rom. 12:6, line 569); and (2) that this knowledge *must always remain* an enquiry, never a finished possession — because the creaturely form of knowledge is always partial ("we see in a mirror dimly," 1 Cor. 13:12, line 682). Dogmatics is therefore *theologia crucis* (line 700): knowledge "taken on the narrow way which leads from the enacted revelation to the promised revelation," always being thrown back to the beginning. Barth quotes Luther: "This is the reason why our Theology is certain: It snatches us away from ourselves and places us outside ourselves, so that we do not rely on our own powers ... but rely on that which is outside of us, that is, on God's promise and truth" (lines 602–604).

A sustained contrast with Roman Catholic dogmatics follows. Roman Catholicism equates divine revelation with a fixed deposit of propositions (Scripture + apostolic tradition) infallibly interpreted by the magisterium. Barth rejects this: "The concept of truths of revelation in the sense of Latin propositions given and sealed once for all with divine authority ... is theologically impossible" (lines 761–763), because "revelation is true in the free decision of God which was taken once for all in Jesus Christ, [and] it must always become true in the Church." Against the tendency in older Protestantism (Melanchthon, Heidanus) to reduce dogmatics to *assembling biblical teaching*, Barth insists the task is to ask "not what the apostles and prophets said but what we must say *on the basis of* the apostles and prophets" (lines 792–794). He invokes Anselm's distinction between *authoritative quotation* and genuine *intellectus fidei* (understanding of faith) as the model for dogmatic enquiry.

**§1.3 Dogmatics as an Act of Faith (lines 838–1237).** Over and above intellectual gifts, dogmatics demands Christian faith — "not simply [coming] of itself even with the deepest and purest surrender to this task" (lines 864–865). Barth traces a long tradition on the *theological habitus* — the condition of the theologian — through Anselm, Luther ("By living, no: by dying and being damned does one become a theologian, not by knowing, reading, or speculating," line 943), Melanchthon, and the Lutheran orthodox. He criticises two errors: (a) the later orthodox distinction between *habitus theologiae* and saving faith, which implicitly made faith a human experience and opened the door to Pietist anthropologising; (b) Schleiermacher's reduction of the theological requirement to a general "inner relation to religion" (lines 1015–1025) and, in the present generation, "existential theology" in the Kierkegaard/Pietist tradition which makes the theologian's biographical situation the criterion of theological speech.

The correct account: faith as the condition of dogmatics means "the grace of divine predestination, the free gift of the Word and Holy Spirit" (line 1078), not the theologian's experiential state. The section closes with prayer as the one human attitude corresponding to dogmatic work — "Prayer can be the recognition that we accomplish nothing by our intentions" (line 1214) — citing Augustine, Anselm's *Proslogion*, Thomas Aquinas's opening prayer to the *Summa*, and A. H. Francke.

### §2. The Task of Prolegomena to Dogmatics (lines 1540–2697)

Opening thesis (lines 1239–1240): "Prolegomena to dogmatics is our name for the introductory part of dogmatics in which our concern is to understand its particular way of knowledge." §2 has two sub-sections.

**§2.1 The Necessity of Dogmatic Prolegomena (lines 1241–1721).** Barth begins by noting that early and medieval dogmaticians gave minimal prolegomena (John Damascene, Peter Lombard, Thomas Aquinas, Zwingli, Schlatter) — not naively but perhaps out of a "profound realism" (lines 1278–1281). He then examines and rejects the standard modern argument that prolegomena are made necessary by the modern cultural crisis (Enlightenment, radical secularism, the "questioning of revelation as such" — citing Emil Brunner). Barth's three-part refusal:

(1) There is no theological basis for treating modern godlessness as uniquely acute — "Have not antiquity and the Middle Ages had their rationalists, atheists and secularists as forerunners?" (lines 1363–1365). (2) The apologetic/eristic project (Brunner's "eristics," Karl Heim's negative task for Christian philosophy) derails dogmatics entirely, substituting argument about revelation's possibility for actual engagement with revelation's content (lines 1392–1430). (3) Good apologetics cannot be planned or programmed — "Apologetics and polemics can only be an event and not a programme" (line 1492); the Gospel defends itself as God acknowledges the witness of faith.

The true and inner necessity of prolegomena comes from the problem of **heresy** — "faith in conflict with itself" (lines 1508–1614). Heresy, unlike pure unbelief, shares formal Christian presuppositions (Christ, Scripture, baptism, creeds) but diverges in content so radically that "we can understand its content ... only as a contradiction of faith" (lines 1543–1546). Barth's definition of heresy draws on Irenaeus (lines 1547–1552). Because heresy cannot simply be dismissed (it maintains common formal ground), the conflict with it is a conflict of faith with itself — urgent, not dismissible.

Concretely, the heresies confronting Evangelical dogmatics are (lines 1615–1721): (a) **Roman Catholicism** as constituted by the Council of Trent (the 16th-century post-Reformation form); (b) **Protestant Modernism** (rooted in medieval mysticism and the humanist Renaissance, arising within the Evangelical churches themselves). Modern secular unbelief is "quite irrelevant compared with this twofold fact" (lines 1620–1621). Schleiermacher is named "the great and mature classical exponent of Modernism" (lines 1719–1720) who gave Protestant liberalism its formal dogmatic foundation in the "doctrine of Christian piety as the being of the Church."

**§2.2 The Possibility of Dogmatic Prolegomena (lines 1722–2071).** Barth surveys three positions on where dogmatics locates the starting point for its prolegomena:

(1) **Modernist**: Human existence (anthropology, ontology) is the prior framework. Schleiermacher and De Wette grounded dogmatics in Idealist self-consciousness; Heidegger and Bultmann do so in existential-ontological analysis. Barth quotes his brother Heinrich Barth's critique: "That theology should begin with a definition of existence, or man ... is at root a piece of Liberalism" (lines 1759–1761). Even H. Barth's attempt to use existential philosophy "critically" and "retrospectively" (only as *analogy* to, not *prior condition* of, knowledge of God) is insufficiently guarded — the theological statement that human existence is an analogy of God has no theological foundation (lines 1888–1897).

(2) **Roman Catholic**: The starting point is the self-grounded reality of divine revelation, given in Scripture + tradition + the living magisterium, with the *fides catholica* as the corresponding subjective principle. Barth's critique: this absorbs the "free personal presence of Jesus Christ" into "a constantly available relationship" — *analogia entis* (line 1935), the claim that even in the fallen world there is a divine likeness in the creature on which natural and theological knowledge can be built. For Barth: "grace here becomes nature, the action of God immediately disappears and is taken up into the action of the recipient of grace" (lines 1928–1930).

(3) **Evangelical**: Prolegomena are possible only *as part of dogmatics itself* — "The prefix *pro* in prolegomena is to be understood loosely to signify the first part of dogmatics rather than that which is prior to it" (lines 1987–1989). The starting point is neither a prior anthropological possibility nor a subsequently available ecclesiastical reality, but "the present moment of the speaking and hearing of Jesus Christ Himself" (lines 1970–1972), which is always *actus purus* — pure act, divine event, not a standing possession.

The point of departure for such prolegomena is the bare fact that "the Christian Church ventures to talk of God" (line 2015) — itself a statement in need of critical examination. Asking what gives the Church the right and norm for this talk is *already* a dogmatic question, and its answer points to "the Word of God as the criterion of dogmatics" (lines 2038–2039). This is materially equivalent to the older Protestant topic *De scriptura sacra* ("On Holy Scripture") but must now be expressed as a full **doctrine of the Word of God** — including the Trinity and Christology as constituent parts — because in the modern three-way debate (Evangelical, Catholic, Modernist) what is at stake is not simply the authority of Scripture but the whole question of how God's address to man is structured (lines 2042–2070).

### §3. Church Proclamation as the Material of Dogmatics (lines 2698–4258)

§3 opens with a summary reprise: "As has been done, Evangelical dogmatics exegetes these concepts" (preaching, proclamation, sacrament) in specific antithesis to two rival positions — Modernist/liberal theology and Roman Catholic theology (lines 2704–2708). The section is organized around these two contrasts and then transitions to the internal question of dogmatics and proclamation.

**Contrast with Modernist/Liberal Theology.** Barth's main interlocutor here is Schleiermacher, whose concept of proclamation Barth summarizes from *The Christian Faith* §§133–135: the "divine Word" is simply "the spirit in all men," proclamation is "the act of the community as such," the "influence of the stronger on the weaker" through self-display and imitation (lines 2726–2736). Barth reads this as meaning that in proclamation "man is finally conceived of as conversing only with himself" (line 2747) — no genuine divine address reaches the human from without. He extends the critique to Paul Tillich (citing *Religiöse Verwirklichung*, 1930), who similarly reduces the Word to a symbol created by "the symbol-creating consciousness" (line 2755) and proposes a "new sacramental situation" to be "created" by reaching "the depths of our own undivided, pre-objective being" (lines 2737–2743). Against both, Barth insists that modernist proclamation "can end only with its dissolution" because it cannot maintain the essential distinction between human speech and a Word that genuinely addresses humanity from without (lines 2823–2835).

**Contrast with Roman Catholic Theology.** The Roman Catholic Church is "expressly and consciously the church of the sacrament" (lines 2852–2853); its dogmatics assigns to the sacraments the status of *canales gratiae*, the *fundamenta vitae christianae* (lines 2855–2866), and preaching receives "Silentium altissimum" — profound silence (line 2898). Barth traces this to a deeper theological disagreement: Roman Catholicism conceives grace not as the "connection between Word and faith" but as the "connection between a divine being as cause and a divine-creaturely being as effect" — not a historical personal encounter but "a physical, not a historical, event" (lines 3021–3032). Under this conception the sacrament's power is defined as *ex opere operato* — "independently of divine decision and human faith" (lines 3070–3074). Preaching can then only function as a preliminary and ancillary exposition. The Reformers' counter-thesis: grace must be understood "not as cause and effect, but as Word and faith," so that the sacrament exists "for the sake of preaching, not vice versa" (lines 3119–3133). Barth cites Luther extensively on the primacy of the preached Word (lines 3140–3185), and Calvin who called preaching (*doctrina*) the *anima ecclesiae* — "soul of the church" (lines 3184–3185).

**Dogmatics and Church Proclamation (§3.2, lines 3232–3947).** Proclamation is always also man's word, and "like all human action it is exposed to the question of its responsibility" (lines 3246–3248). The legitimate critical authority over proclamation is not the state, society, science, or aesthetic culture — proclamation that accepts such responsibilities "spells treachery to the Church and to Christ Himself" (lines 3255–3258). The real responsibility arises from proclamation's intention to be the Word of God, and it is God alone who presses this question. Dogmatics is the Church's organized response to this question: "Church proclamation has to be accompanied and confronted by Church theology, especially dogmatics" (lines 3431–3432). Barth rejects the Roman Catholic claim to an *infallibilis et divina regula* of Church teaching (citing Thomas Aquinas, lines 3411–3413), asking how seriously dogmatic work can be done if the Church equates its own *doctrina ecclesiae* with a *divina regula*. Three formal theses close this section: (1) dogmatics has a different necessity from proclamation — it is needed because proclamation is fallible, not because it is an independent access to God; (2) dogmatics serves proclamation, not vice versa; and (3) dogmatics cannot be an end in itself — "Church proclamation and not dogmatics is immediate to God in the Church. Proclamation is essential, dogmatics is needed only for the sake of it" (lines 3937–3939).

### §4. The Word of God in its Threefold Form (lines 4259–5762)

§4 opens with the structural thesis: "The presupposition which makes proclamation proclamation and therewith makes the Church the Church is the Word of God. This attests itself in Holy Scripture in the word of the prophets and apostles to whom it was originally and once and for all spoken by God's revelation" (lines 3949–3952). The section has four parts.

**§4.1 The Word of God Preached (lines 3953–4483).** Proclamation "must ever and again become proclamation" — from being an action that wants to be proclamation, it must become one; this event is identified with the Church itself continually becoming the Church (lines 3954–3961). Barth identifies four concentric circles in the relationship between the Word and proclamation: (1) The Word of God is the *commission* upon which proclamation must rest — not founded on human circumstances or convictions but on a command that "comes upon us ... absolutely from without" and "which we can only receive and have in the act of receiving" (lines 4007–4070). Real proclamation is "man's talk about God on the basis of God's own direction, which fundamentally transcends all human causation" (lines 4065–4070). (2) The Word of God is the *theme* — the object of proclamation "presents and places itself as an object over against us ... in the unavoidable medium of perceptual objectivity, but in this medium as the object which can never in any sense be our possession" (lines 4109–4119). (3) The Word of God is the *judgment* — the criterion that decides the truth of proclamation is one that "cannot be anticipated and never passes under our control" (lines 4141–4178). (4) Most fundamentally, the Word of God is the *event itself* in which proclamation becomes real: "The Word of God preached means ... man's talk about God in which and through which God speaks about Himself" (lines 4263–4264). In contrast to Roman Catholic teaching, Barth insists this does not dehumanize proclamation — "as Christ became true man and remains true man to all eternity, real proclamation becomes an event on the level of all other human events" (lines 4218–4226). Barth criticizes Roman Catholic doctrines of historical succession, *character indelebilis*, and infallible definitions as transforming vicariate so that Christ's lordship is "swallowed up in the decisions of these authorities" (lines 4434–4441).

**§4.2 The Word of God Written (lines 4484–5043).** Proclamation requires recollection of past revelation. One might imagine this recollection as an Augustinian/Neoplatonist *anamnesis* — the return to a timeless ground of being within the soul (*Sera te amavi, pulchritudo tam antiqua et tam nova!* — Confessions X, 27, 38; line 4530). Barth rejects this: Holy Scripture is "the bolt which in fact shuts out Platonic anamnesis" (lines 4594–4595). The Canon, Barth notes, originally meant *rod, ruler, standard*, and refers to what is normative and apostolic (lines 4598–4604). The Bible "constitutes itself the Canon. It is the Canon because it imposed itself upon the Church as such, and continually does so" (lines 4884–4885), because of its content: "The prophetic and apostolic word is the word, witness, proclamation and preaching of Jesus Christ" (line 4903). The Church's acknowledgment of Scripture means its proclamation "must all come from elsewhere, from without, and very concretely from without, in all the externality of the concrete Canon" (lines 4609–4614). Exegesis always combines "taking and giving, of reading out and reading in," creating the danger that the Bible will be "taken prisoner by the Church" (lines 4830–4836); the defence must be "left to the text itself" — "free exegesis for the sake of a free Bible" (lines 4851–4863). Holy Scripture is the Word of God in the same dynamic sense as proclamation: "The Bible is God's Word to the extent that God causes it to be His Word, to the extent that He speaks through it" (lines 4988–4989), and "the little word 'is' refers to its being in this becoming" (lines 5005–5007).

**§4.3 The Word of God Revealed (lines 5044–5508).** Revelation is the presupposition behind both the Bible's witness and proclamation's promise. "The Bible, speaking to us and heard by us as God's Word, bears witness to past revelation. Proclamation, speaking to us and heard by us as God's Word, promises future revelation" (lines 5050–5051). The Bible is not in itself and as such God's past revelation — it attests it. The prophets and apostles are witnesses who "point in a specific direction beyond the self and on to another" (line 5078). Barth cites Kierkegaard on "the difference between an apostle and a genius" (lines 5107–5108): what makes man a witness is "solely and exclusively that other, the thing attested, which constrains and limits the perfect or imperfect human organ from without" (lines 5104–5106). John the Baptist in Grünewald's Crucifixion — his "prodigious index finger" — is the visual model (lines 5113–5115). Revelation as such is not relative: "Revelation in fact does not differ from the person of Jesus Christ nor from the reconciliation accomplished in Him. To say revelation is to say 'The Word became flesh'" (lines 5455–5457), with an intertrinitarian basis "in the will of the Father and the sending of the Son and the Holy Spirit" (lines 5463–5465). The distinction between Scripture/proclamation and revelation maps onto *ubi et quando* (where and when it pleases God — for Scripture and proclamation) vs. *illic et tunc* (there and then — for revelation as accomplished fact, lines 5407–5412).

**§4.4 The Unity of the Word of God (lines 5509–5721).** "We have been speaking of three different forms of the Word of God and not of three different Words of God" (lines 5510–5511). There is "no distinction of degree or value between the three forms" (line 5515). The first form, revelation, "never meets us anywhere in abstract form. We know it only indirectly, from Scripture and proclamation" (lines 5524–5526). Barth draws an explicit analogy to the doctrine of the Trinity: "we can substitute for revelation, Scripture and proclamation the names of the divine persons Father, Son and Holy Spirit and vice versa ... one may see specific support for the inner necessity and correctness of our present exposition" (lines 5537–5548). The section closes with a critique of Protestant orthodoxy, which maintained unity but lost the dynamic of mutual relations, allowing proclamation to become merely a *medium salutis* (means of salvation) for private individuals — a key cause of orthodoxy's catastrophic collapse in the 18th century (lines 5671–5721).

### §5. The Nature of the Word of God (lines 5763–8564)

§5 has four sub-sections treating the question, speech, act, and mystery of the Word. Barth begins with an extended methodological self-correction before developing the positive doctrine.

**Sub-section 1: The Question of the Nature of the Word of God (lines ~5763–6055)**

Barth opens §5 with a sustained self-criticism of the first edition (*Christliche Dogmatik*, 1927). There he had attempted to develop the doctrine of the Word from an analysis of the human situation of the preacher and hearer — a "Church anthropology." He now repudiates this as a "false tendency." The methodological manifesto appears at lines 5815–5819: "If there is one thing the Word of God certainly is not, it is not a predicate of man, even of the man who receives it, and therefore not of the man who speaks, hears and knows it in the sphere of the Church."

Barth also engages at length with Friedrich Gogarten, who had criticized the first edition for lacking a "true anthropology." Barth reverses the charge: any anthropology serving as the ground of theology amounts to natural theology. His counter-argument culminates at lines 6037–6038: "There is a way from Christology to anthropology, but there is no way from anthropology to Christology." He criticises Gogarten's circle between the understanding of God and man as giving "already" priority to the understanding of man, implicitly presupposing a Bultmannian "pre-understanding." He also associates this approach with Schleiermacher and ultimately Feuerbach. The section closes by establishing that the doctrine of the nature of the Word will be developed before and independently of any anthropological framework. The epistemological constraint governing the entire section: we cannot say what God's Word *is* directly; we can only say *how* it is, by attending to how it is in its three forms. "This How is the attainable human reflection of the unattainable divine What" (lines 6085–6086).

**Sub-section 2: The Word of God as the Speech of God (lines 6087–6547)**

Barth insists on taking "the concept of God's Word primarily in its literal sense" (line 6093): God's Word means that God speaks. This is not a "symbol" as Tillich maintains (line 6094; against *Religiöse Verwirklichung*, 1930) but corresponds to what God has "chosen and actualised at all events in His Church." Act and mystery are exegeses of speech, not replacements for it: "Being explanations, they can only point us back repeatedly to this as the original text" (line 6117).

Three implications follow from the speech-character of the Word:

*Spiritual nature* (lines 6128–6261): The Word is primarily spiritual, not natural or physical, because "speech, including God's speech, is the form in which reason communicates with reason and person with person" (lines 6187–6189). There is "an upper and lower aspect, a first and second" between spirit and nature that must not be reversed. "The Word of God — and at this point we should not evade a term so much tabooed to-day — is a rational and not an irrational event" (lines 6193–6194). Against Rudolf Otto: the numinous as irrational cannot be the Word of God (lines 6224–6226). Luther is cited: "Christ's kingdom is a kingdom of hearing, not a kingdom of seeing" (line 6207).

*Personal quality* (lines 6262–6394): "God's Word is not a thing to be described nor a term to be defined. It is neither a matter nor an idea ... It is the truth as it is God's speaking person, *Dei loquentis persona*" (lines 6263–6265). This grounds in the identification of Word with Son: "God's revelation is Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (lines 6294–6295), appealing to John 1:1 and Rev. 19:12–13. The equation of Word and Son creates a barrier against reducing Scripture or proclamation to "a fixed sum of revealed propositions which can be systematised like the sections of a corpus of law" (lines 6315–6317). God is Lord of the wording of his Word; he "has free control over the wording of Holy Scripture" (line 6380) and reserves to himself the freedom to come to us in his Word "again and again" (lines 6392–6394).

*Purposive character / address* (lines 6395–6547): "We know [the Word] only as a Word that is directed to us and applies to us" (lines 6399–6400). But this purposiveness is free and unmerited — "God would be no less God if He had created no world and no man" (lines 6408–6409); this freedom makes the Word grace. Four dimensions of its purposiveness: (1) it is a Word we cannot say to ourselves — "encounter with the Word of God is genuine, irrevocable encounter ... that can never be dissolved in union" (lines 6479–6480); (2) it is the Word of the Creator that "smites us in our existence" (lines 6488–6505); (3) it is the Word of reconciliation — renewal of the disrupted Creator-creature relation (lines 6506–6521); (4) it is the eschatological Word of the Redeemer, promising God as "the hidden Lord of all times" (lines 6523–6547).

**Sub-section 3: The Speech of God as the Act of God (lines 6548–7426)**

The controlling thesis: "The Word of God is itself the act of God. It is act to a degree that everything else that we usually call act, event, practice, life, etc. ... can only seem to be very questionable as real act in comparison with it. The Word of God makes history in the supreme sense" (lines 6570–6576). The distinction between word and act that applies to human speech — where "mere word is the mere self-expression of a person, while act is the resultant relative alteration in the world around" (lines 6605–6607) — simply does not apply to God.

Three implications of the Word as act:

*Contingent contemporaneity* (lines 6621–6827): Barth distinguishes three times of the Word: the time of revelation (Christ), the time of the biblical witnesses, and the time of Church proclamation. These are differentiated not merely chronologically but by order — by "the different attitude of God to men" (line 6634). Modern theology, following Lessing's attempt to cross the "ugly, wide ditch" between Bible and us (line 6675), dissolved this differentiation by treating the times immanently. Barth's counter: when we eliminate our non-contemporaneity with Christ by putting ourselves on the same soil, "the concept of the Word of God is humanised" to the point of vacuity (lines 6720–6727). True contemporaneity across differentiated times is possible only as God's free act. The formula: *illic et tunc* (there and then) becoming *hic et nunc* (here and now) — always contingently, by God's act alone (lines 6811–6827). Underscored with Heb. 13:8: "Jesus Christ the same yesterday and today."

*Power to rule* (lines 6828–7165): When God speaks, "we come under a lordship" (line 6833). The Word transforms the situation of the hearer: "The promise of the Word of God is not as such an empty pledge ... It is the transposing of man into the wholly new state of one who has accepted and appropriated the promise" (lines 6995–6997). This power is absolute, unconditioned by the behaviour of its recipients. Ecclesiologically: the world already stands under Christ's lordship whether it acknowledges it or not (lines 7087–7165). The source in Luther's *regnum Christi* as *doctrinale regnum* / *ministerium Verbi* (lines 6909–6910) is cited at length alongside Calvin.

*Decision* (lines 7166–7426): The Word is not merely event but divine decision — freely and specifically addressed to this particular person. The Word "is not universally, i.e., always and everywhere present, and hence ... not universally ... ascertainable" (lines 7234–7235). It is always real "*suo modo, sua libertate, sua misericordia*" (in its own way, in its own freedom, in its own mercy; line 7245). As decision it implies choice: not every *klēsis* results in *eklogē*; calling can result in rejection (lines 7288–7358). The Word works on and in a human decision — creating the situation of faith or unbelief as distinct possibilities not derivable from general anthropology (lines 7360–7426).

**Sub-section 4: The Speech of God as the Mystery of God (lines 7427–8564)**

Even careful theological delimitation risks claiming mastery over God's Word. "Mystery" is added not as a fourth attribute alongside speech and act, but as a qualification reminding that "only the Logos of God Himself can provide the proof that we are really talking about Him when we are allegedly doing so" (lines 7494–7496). Mystery here draws on the NT sense: "the concealment of God in which He meets us precisely when He unveils Himself to us, because He will not and cannot unveil Himself except by veiling Himself" (lines 7555–7557).

Three forms of the Word's mystery:

*Secularity* (lines 7563–7999): "When God speaks to man, this event never demarcates itself from other events in such a way that it might not be interpreted at once as part of these other events" (lines 7564–7566). The Church is also a sociological entity; the Bible is also a Near Eastern historical record; "Jesus Christ is also in fact the Rabbi of Nazareth who is hard to know historically" (line 7570). This secularity is not a misfortune but essential — "Revelation means the incarnation of the Word of God. But incarnation means entry into this secularity" (lines 7732–7733). God veils himself in unveiling and unveils himself in veiling. The framework is Luther's *theologia crucis* against *theologia gloriae* (lines 7650–7671); *speculatio Maiestatis* is ruled out. This section also contains Barth's most extended response to both Gogarten (who wants to erase the distinction "God in Himself"/"God for us") and Przywara (who detects a reduction of all revelation to divine address). Against Gogarten: the distinction between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity must be maintained to protect the freedom and graciousness of revelation (lines 7838–7999). Against Przywara: the "God for us" is a free divine act, not a structural necessity in God's nature (lines 7901–7999).

*One-sidedness* (lines 8000–8351): The Word does not meet us "partly veiled and partly unveiled, but either veiled or unveiled, yet without being different in itself" (lines 8002–8005). We always receive only one side; the other is grasped only in faith. "Our thinking can be realistic or idealistic but it cannot be Christian" (lines 8063–8064). Faith commits the synthesis of form and content to God. Illustrated with Ex. 19–20 and Jer. 31 (Sinai and new covenants), the Synoptic/Johannine relation on Christ's humanity and deity, and Paul's cross/resurrection dialectic (lines 8253–8316). Classic case: Luther's sermon on the Syro-Phoenician woman (Mt. 15:21ff.) — holding onto the hidden "yes" beneath the apparent "no" (lines 8124–8176).

*Spirituality* (lines 8352–8564): "The Lord of speech is also the Lord of our hearing. The Lord who gives the Word is also the Lord who gives faith" (lines 8374–8376). There is no method — no technique of proclamation, hermeneutical skill, "borderline experience" (against Tillich, lines 8436–8465), or philosophical "inalienable verities" (against Spranger, lines 8529–8555) — that can guarantee the hearing of the Word. "Faith is the work of the Holy Spirit" (line 8516). The section closes: "the question 'What is the Word of God?' can be answered only by indicating the How of God's Word in an interpretation of its threefold form ... It is on our lips and in our hearts as the mystery of the Spirit who is the Lord" (lines 8560–8563).

**Key interlocutors in §5**: Friedrich Gogarten (true anthropology / "from man"); Paul Tillich (speech as symbol; borderline-situation method); Lessing (ugly wide ditch; historical immanence); Schleiermacher; Bultmann (pre-understanding); Erich Przywara SJ (analogy of being; correlation theology); Rudolf Otto (numinous as irrational); Eduard Spranger (inalienable verities). Luther and Calvin cited extensively and approvingly throughout.

### §6. The Knowability of the Word of God (lines 8565–11829)

**Thesis statement** (lines 8567–8570): "The reality of the Word of God in all its three forms is grounded only in itself. So, too, the knowledge of it by men can consist only in its acknowledgment, and this acknowledgment can become real only through itself and can become intelligible only in terms of itself."

**§6.1 — The Question of the Knowability of the Word of God** (lines 8571–9047)

Barth opens by noting that the concept of the Church already presupposes that certain men in certain situations can know the Word of God (lines 8583–8605). He defines knowledge: not mere cognizance but the event in which an object's truth becomes a "necessary and inward determination" of the knowing subject — "Cognizance becomes knowledge when man becomes a responsible witness to its content" (lines 8606–8623). He distinguishes: "How *do* men know?" (the reality of knowledge, answerable only by proclamation) vs. "How *can* men know?" (the possibility, which can be investigated; lines 8649–8661). The question is not about *man in general* but the specific man addressed by God within the Church (lines 8663–8679). He warns against importing a prior epistemological definition: "the concept of its knowledge cannot be definitively measured by the concept of the knowledge of other objects or by a general concept of knowledge but ... can be defined at all only in terms of its own object" (lines 8708–8711).

**§6.2 — The Word of God and Man** (lines 8712–9047)

Barth surveys the shift in Protestant orthodoxy from theology as a science of God to a science of man's relation to God (lines 8757–8802), climaxing in Schleiermacher who "for the first time fundamentally relates this newly discovered independent reality of religion to a corresponding possibility that can be generally demonstrated anthropologically" (lines 8810–8831). Two Schleiermacherian principles: (1) encounter with God is a human religious experience establishable historically and psychologically; (2) this experience is the actualization of a generally demonstrable religious *capacity* of man (lines 8814–8826).

Barth's objection: given what the Word addresses — the creature encountering its Creator, the sinner encountering grace, the one who waits encountering the coming Lord (lines 8879–8903) — the Word addresses a man who "cannot bring any capability of his own to this knowledge." The corresponding human possibility "represents an inconceivable *novum* compared to all his ability and capability, and ... is to be understood as a pure fact" (lines 8901–8903). Proof text: 1 Cor. 2:6–16, with Luther and Irenaeus (lines 8904–8931).

Cartesianism rejected (lines 8932–8979): "We do not find the Word of God in the reality present to us. Rather—and this is something quite different—the Word of God finds us in the reality present to us" (lines 8955–8959). Counter-thesis: "Men can know the Word of God because and in so far as God wills that they know it ... and because and in so far as there is a revelation of God's will in His Word in which the impotence of disobedience is set aside" (lines 8975–8979).

**§6.3 — The Word of God and Experience** (lines 9048–10337)

Barth affirms genuine human experience of the Word: "the determination of their existence as men by God's Word" (lines 9085–9087), elaborated through nine dimensions of *acknowledgment* (*Anerkennung*; lines 9340–9483): (1) knowledge — the Word is primarily rational speech; (2) personal relation, God as person; (3) submission to the Word as Lord; (4) acknowledgment of a contingent contemporary event (*hic et nunc*); (5) submission to the Word's power; (6) decision — disobedience as well as obedience "acknowledges" the Word; (7) halting before mystery and concealment; (8) continual movement between aspects of the Word, never resting in synthesis; (9) pure acknowledgment in which meaning and basis lie wholly outside the acknowledger. At this frontier "by taking place as an experience this experience ceases to be an experience" (lines 9484–9485).

**Against indirect Christian Cartesianism** (lines 9586–10337): Wobbermin and Schaeder rightly deny an *a priori* human capacity and affirm the order runs from God to man, but at a decisive point their theology reverses: the gift passes "out of God's hand into the hands of man" (lines 9637–9638), creating a "conjunction or synthesis" — the *homo capax verbi Dei* — in which the believer's "own personal experience of faith" becomes an "opposite pole" and "methodological aid" for knowledge of the Word (lines 9675–9680).

Barth's counter: no certainty can be attained by investigating human religious experience, because the act of acknowledgment is indistinguishable from other human acts, including those of "other religions" and of "culture generally" (lines 9862–9886). "The possibility of knowledge of God's Word lies in God's Word and nowhere else. In the absolute sense its reality can only take place, and it can do so only as a miracle before the eyes of every man, secular and religious, Greek and Jew" (lines 10131–10135).

Positive conclusion (lines 10174–10313): this possibility is affirmed with "unheard-of assurance" — but as the assurance of *faith*, the assurance of expectation, not possession. "It can only be the assurance of faith, the assurance of the man whom God in His free grace has not passed by but whom He has called to faith by His Word" (lines 10207–10209). "He is not sure of himself but of the Word of God, and he is not sure of the Word of God in and of himself but in and of the Word. His assurance is his own assurance, but it has its seat outside him in the Word of God" (lines 10215–10219).

**§6.4 — The Word of God and Faith** (lines 10338–11421)

Faith is "the making possible of knowledge of God's Word that takes place in actual knowledge of it" (lines 10360–10362). Drawing on the full range of NT *pistis* usage (lines 10372–10455), Barth shows *pistis* always denotes "the real event which rests on the will and Word of God and relates to the will and Word of God" in which "the knowledge of God becomes real" (lines 10413–10416). Faith necessarily involves *notitia*, *assensus*, and *fiducia*, but none of these as such constitutes faith — the object (Christ, the Word) makes faith faith (lines 10463–10485). Faith is defended as including knowledge against both anti-intellectualism and the reduction of *fiducia* to mere subjective trust: citations from Augustine, Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventura, Luther, Calvin, and the Protestant confessions (lines 10417–10451, 10725–10807).

Three definitions of the knowability of the Word in faith:

**(1) Primacy of the object** (lines 10463–10569): Faith is faith only because the Word presents itself as object. Anselm's *Proslogion* ch. 1 is read as illustrating that even within the Church one must still pray for God's presence — "I cannot seek You, unless you teach me how; nor find You, unless You show Yourself" (lines 10493–10498). "Faith and God belong together" (Luther, *Larger Catechism*, quoted lines 10621–10628) means not that faith constitutes God but that true God is accessible only in true faith, whose truth is not due to itself but to the Word.

**(2) Conformity to God / *analogia fidei*** (lines 10861–11271): In faith man has real experience of the Word; the *imago Dei* was "totally annihilated" by sin — not merely damaged (*contra* Brunner; lines 10952–10974). The "point of contact" for God's Word is the *rectitudo* restored in Christ, "not real outside faith; it is real only in faith" (lines 10967–10968). Man is in "conformity to God" in faith — but this is not the *analogia entis* of Roman Catholic theology (lines 10977–10991). The true analogy is the *analogia fidei* of Rom 12:6: "the likeness of the known in the knowing, of the object in thought, of the Word of God in the word that is thought and spoken by man" (lines 11231–11233). Man knows *as he is known by God* (lines 11244–11271).

**(3) Man grounded solely in the object** (lines 11272–11421): The believer exists "wholly and utterly by this object" (lines 11272–11280). Faith is not created by man but comes through the Word; Luther is preferred over Augustine here because Luther traces faith to "a purely divine work in us without any aid of ours" (lines 11380–11381).

Concluding statement (lines 11389–11421): "The Word of God becomes knowable by making itself known." The possibility of knowing is "God's miracle on us just as much as is the Word itself." Even the *sacrificium intellectus* is "only a last desperate attempt to make the knowledge of God a work of man" (lines 11407–11408). Final image: man must open the door (Rev. 3:20), but "the fact that this takes place is *quoad actum* and *quoad potentiam* the work of the Christ who stands outside" (lines 11415–11418).

### §7. The Word of God, Dogma and Dogmatics (lines 11830–13338)

#### §7.1 The Problem of Dogmatics (lines 11830–12605)

The opening sub-section concludes Barth's analysis of the two false resolutions of the relationship between Church proclamation and the Word of God, then states what dogmatics, rightly understood, must be.

**Roman Catholicism and Protestant Modernism as parallel errors.** The sub-section continues mid-argument (from §6) by contrasting two ways the Church can fail to allow the Bible to function as an independent court over its proclamation. In Roman Catholicism the teaching office (*magisterium*) constitutes the *regula proxima fidei* — not the Bible per se but "the verdict of the teaching office on the Bible" (line 11832). The Church, as both *norma normata* and *norma normans*, as both *ecclesia audiens* and *ecclesia docens*, has full authority to effect a synthesis between norm received and norm applied — making it ultimately "the judge in its own cause" (line 11862). Protestant Modernism reaches the same conclusion by a different route: Marheineke's Hegelian claim that the Christian community possesses "the holy spirit of Jesus Christ" who "is knowledge" (lines 11867–68) means the Bible becomes merely a stage in the self-movement of the Christian spirit that "has completely lost as such the character of a court that is independent of this self-movement" (lines 11883–84). Biedermann is cited as understanding dogmatics as the autonomous development of "experiential knowledge of the Christian act of faith" (line 11893), resulting in a "dialectical circle whose course is determined by the dogmatician himself" (lines 11902–03). The outwardly Protestant Wobbermin who insists Holy Scripture alone is the source of dogmatics still leaves the Church holding the Bible "over it only as it has it in it" (line 11855).

**The impossibility of formal proof and the sign of Scripture.** Barth refuses to offer a direct philosophical refutation of either position: "To prove that . . . the Bible as this supreme authority . . . encounters the Church . . . we should obviously have to put ourselves in a place above proclamation and the Bible" (lines 11929–30). Instead he points to a sign: the Bible has never wholly lost the significance of "a second voice" in the Church (line 11950). Even in Roman Catholicism and Protestant Modernism the Bible "found a voice and finds a voice in the Church" (lines 11964–65) — a sign that cannot be refuted but does not constitute proof. This sign points only to the *possibility* of faith, the event in which "man hears the Word of God" (lines 11982–83). The event of faith cannot be presupposed, argued from, or produced. Barth invokes *theologia gloriae* as the name for the error of treating faith as a present presupposition from which to argue: "The conclusion that because I believe . . . the Bible is the Word of God, destroys the divinity of the Word of God . . . This conclusion involves *theologia gloriae*" (lines 12022–25).

**Barth's Protestant opposition and the definition of dogmatics.** Barth describes his own stance as "an opposition . . . simply mounted factually and with no validating proof" (lines 12149–50). This opposition implies that "dogmatics as the question of the Word of God in Church proclamation must be the critical question as to the agreement of Church proclamation . . . with the revelation attested in Holy Scripture" (lines 12161–65). The task of dogmatics is "to deal with the problem of the equation of the Word of God and the word of man in its form as Church proclamation . . . by measuring Church proclamation as man's word by the second form of the Word of God, i.e., Holy Scripture, in so far as this itself is in turn witness to its third and original form, revelation" (lines 12167–72).

**Definition of dogma.** Dogmatics is *theologia dogmatica* — "the science of dogma," not "the science of dogmas" (lines 12174–75). Dogma is defined as "the agreement of Church proclamation with the revelation attested in Holy Scripture" (lines 12175–76). Barth explicitly rejects the Roman Catholic definition — *veritas a Deo formaliter revelata et ab Ecclesia definita* — on the grounds that "in dogmas it is the Church of the past that speaks" (line 12224); however venerable, "the Word of God is above dogma as the heavens are above the earth" (lines 12229–30).

Barth draws a three-level distinction: (a) **Dogma** — the agreement itself, never simply possessed by the Church; "not itself a proposition, not proclaimed by any church" (lines 12291–92); it is "the epitome of all possible propositions of this kind" (line 12289), a concept of relation; (b) **Dogmas** — the doctrinal propositions the Church confesses in its symbols, which *aim at* but do not simply *possess* the truth of revelation; (c) **Dogmatic propositions** — the products of dogmatic science. "Dogma is the essence of which dogmas and also dogmatic propositions . . . claim to be phenomena" (lines 12293–94). Because dogma cannot be demonstrated or presupposed, every result of dogmatics "can themselves only be new questions" (line 12329). Barth calls dogma an "eschatological concept" (line 12342): "If questioning ceased, if dogma itself came on the scene instead of dogmas and dogmatic propositions . . . then dogmatics would be at an end along with the *ecclesia militans*, and the kingdom of God would have dawned" (lines 12334–38).

**The word "dogma" and its false development.** Barth notes that "dogma" in biblical usage (Daniel 6, Luke 2, Ephesians 2, Colossians 2) meant primarily a *decree* or *command* (lines 12183–85); its secondary meaning — the philosophical or academic principle of a school — entered Christian usage in the 2nd century. Barth argues it was a "false development" (line 12367) to define dogma primarily as a doctrinal *proposition* rather than a *command*, because "veritas revelata is a command . . . one cannot give oneself a command in the way the Church gives itself Church dogmas" (lines 12497–98, 12501). The relationship between dogma and proclamation is to be understood as "the relation between command and obedience and only then . . . as also the relation between doctrine and instruction" (lines 12536–38), citing the Anselmian *credo ut intelligam* (line 12544). Dogmatics thus asks "the question of a servant who has to ask whether his actions agree with his master's intentions" (lines 12562–63).

#### §7.2 Dogmatics as a Science (lines 12606–13126)

Barth takes up the claim of dogmatics to be a *Wissenschaft* (science) without grounding it in any external general concept of science.

**The claim to scientific character.** Dogmatics calls itself a science "because it has no interest in de facto self-segregation from the other human efforts at knowledge that bear this name" (lines 12617–18) and "because it accepts a hidden but coming and therefore real unity of all such human efforts" (lines 12620–21). Its scientific character is not proved externally; it "consists in its orientation . . . to the question of dogma which is raised by the existence of the Church" (lines 12633–35).

**Regular and irregular dogmatics.** Barth distinguishes *regular dogmatics* (aimed at completeness, suited to the theological school) from *irregular dogmatics* (free discussion of specific problems, taking the form of sermons, treatises, theses, aphorisms, without primary concern for systematic completeness). Historical examples: Origen, John of Damascus, Peter Lombard, and the Scholastic Summas are regular; Athanasius, Luther, Hamann, Blumhardt, and Kutter are irregular (lines 12724–32). "On the whole it must be admitted that in spite of its name irregular dogmatics has been the rule, and regular dogmatics the exception, in every age of the Church" (lines 12736–38). What "is to be attempted here is regular dogmatics" (line 12767), with the note that "especially for Protestantism to-day an orderly school dogmatics might be healthier than a further plethora of irregularities" (lines 12770–72). Nevertheless, school dogmatics "should not try to regard itself as better dogmatics, only as a necessary second form" (lines 12806–07).

**Three marks of scientific dogmatics.** Barth enumerates three demands:

1. Scientific dogmatics must be oriented to *Church proclamation* and not to independent metaphysical problems. "Dogmatics as gnosis without regard for the task of Church proclamation, dogmatics as self-motivated and self-grounded metaphysics is unscientific as dogmatics no matter how profound, erudite and logical it may seem to be" (lines 12826–28). Dogmatic statements are "preparation for Church proclamation" (line 12846).

2. Scientific dogmatics must *criticise and correct* Church proclamation, not merely repeat it. It must be willing to "unsettle rather than confirm Church proclamation as it meets it in its previous concretions" (lines 12886–88). A dogmatics that merely certifies the Church's existing proclamation is unscientific: "The true Church is waiting for something far different from certification" (line 12904).

3. Most decisively, scientific dogmatics must measure Church proclamation by "the revelation attested in Holy Scripture and not a philosophical, ethical, psychological or political theory" (lines 12954–56). "Dogmatic work stands or falls by whether the standard by which Church proclamation is measured is the revelation attested in Holy Scripture" (lines 12954–55). Barth cites Luther on theology's own *phrasis Spiritus sancti*: *Non debet ars artem impedire, sed unaquaeque debet retinere suum quasi cursum et uti suis terminis* — "One art should not obstruct another, but each art ought to hold to its own course . . . and use its own terms" (lines 12990–91, citing Luther, *Comm. on Gen.* 1:14). "The only element in education that makes him a dogmatician is the one which . . . consists in indemonstrable and unassuming attention to the sign of Holy Scripture" (lines 12972–73).

Barth enters a "protest . . . against the corruption of theology which has now been in full swing so long and which has been brought about by trying to understand and treat it simply as a branch of the humanities in general" (lines 13027–30).

#### §7.3 The Problem of Dogmatic Prolegomena (lines 13127–13338)

This sub-section announces what the remainder of the Prolegomena must accomplish.

**What has been established.** The first chapter (§§1–7) defined the *criterion* of dogmatics: the Word of God in its threefold form, with Holy Scripture functioning as the concrete norm for Church proclamation. What has not yet been defined is the *path of knowledge* — how that criterion is to be applied.

**Three further chapters needed.** Barth identifies three topics for the remainder of the Prolegomena, which is to reverse the earlier order (proclamation → Scripture → revelation) and instead proceed revelation → Scripture → proclamation:

1. **The doctrine of Holy Scripture** — a full account of what it means to have the Bible as the sign of the promise, what it means to hear and accept it in the Church.

2. **The doctrine of Church proclamation** — the norm to which proclamation must be subject and the nature of thinking that submits to it, "in strict correspondence to the doctrine of Holy Scripture" (lines 13191–92).

3. **The doctrine of revelation** — most fundamental of all, because "the whole point of the correspondence between Holy Scripture and proclamation . . . consists in the fact that in both Holy Scripture and Church dogmatics we are dealing with the Word of God" (lines 13202–03). It is "the concept of revelation which must give us the key to an understanding of the relations between the two" (lines 13243–45). This must be the *concrete* concept of revelation — "the revelation of the God who according to Scripture and proclamation is the Father of Jesus Christ, is Jesus Christ Himself, and is the Spirit of this Father and this Son" (lines 13274–75), not a general concept of revelation in religion.

**The Trinitarian anchor.** "Analysis of this fact itself can only be a development of what has always played a distinctive role in dogmatics under the name of the doctrine of the Holy Trinity" (lines 13294–95), while "the answer to the question of the inner possibility of this fact will be provided on the one hand by the basic propositions of Christology and on the other hand by the basic propositions regarding the work of the Holy Spirit" (lines 13296–98). The section closes by announcing that the prolegomena will consist of three further chapters — on revelation, Holy Scripture, and Church proclamation — forming "an exposition of the doctrine of the three forms of the Word of God intimated in §4" (lines 13308–09), now treated in their inner structure and mutual relations rather than merely enumerated.
