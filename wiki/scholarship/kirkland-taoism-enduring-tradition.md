## Key Thesis

Twentieth-century scholarship fundamentally misrepresented Taoism along three axes:

1. **Text over people**: Scholars privileged texts — especially the *Lao-tzu* and *Chuang-tzu* —
   over the actual practices and communities of historical Taoists.
2. **Invented "schools of thought"**: The concept of *tao-chia* ("philosophical Taoism") versus
   *tao-chiao* ("religious Taoism") is a modern scholarly fabrication with no ancient basis.
   No premodern person ever identified as a practitioner of *tao-chia*. See [[taochia-taochiao-dichotomy]].
3. **Western projection**: Western interpreters projected Enlightenment values (naturalism,
   rationalism, anti-ritualism) onto selected texts and called the result "authentic Taoism,"
   dismissing the actual living religion as degraded superstition.

## Major Arguments by Chapter

### The Real Foundational Text: The Nei-yeh

The true foundational Taoist text is the **Nei-yeh** ("Inner Cultivation," *Kuan-tzu* ch. 49),
which Kirkland argues predates the *Tao te ching*. It is focused entirely on
**[[biospiritual-cultivation|biospiritual cultivation]]** — regulating *ch'i* (life-energy),
*ching* (vital essence), *shen* (spirit), and *hsin* (heart/mind). Unlike the *Tao te ching*:
- It never critiques Confucianism
- It does not discuss *wu-wei* as a political concept
- It has no gender imagery (no "feminine" idealization)
- It is not a political handbook

See [[nei-yeh]].

### The Tao te ching / Daodejing: Three Editions

Kirkland's three-editions analysis (updating what had been in the wiki from Ziporyn):
- **Guodian bamboo slips** (~300 BCE): the earliest known stratum; contains less than half the
  received text; focused on biospiritual cultivation with minimal political content; no "mother"/
  "feminine" passages
- **Ma-wang-tui silk texts** (~200 and 170 BCE): full text, in reversed chapter order
  (chs. 38–81 before chs. 1–37)
- **Received / Wang Pi text** (early CE): the standard version, politically redacted

**The Huan Yüan hypothesis**: Kirkland argues that the full-text version may have been
assembled at the Chi-hsia ("Jixia") academy in the state of Ch'i (~300–240 BCE), by
a scholar named Huan Yüan, who combined the older biospiritual cultivation texts of Ch'u
with new socio-political material reflecting the academy's political-philosophical concerns.
The "Lao-tzu" figure as historical author is a Han invention; there was no such person.

The feminine imagery in the received text (the "valley spirit," the "mother") was added or
emphasized in the full-text redaction for political-rhetorical purposes, not as statements
about gender.

### The Zhuangzi / Chuang-tzu: Kuo Hsiang's Creation

Our received 33-chapter *Chuang-tzu* was created by **Kuo Hsiang** (died 312 CE), who
selected from a 52-chapter original and wrote its preface — a preface lost until Japanese
scholars discovered it at a temple in Kyoto in the 20th c. In that preface, Kuo confesses
the original was so "daring" and "lofty" that much of it "made no sense to him," so he
edited toward "what is easy to understand." This means our *Chuang-tzu* is Kuo Hsiang's
bowdlerized vision of the text, not a clean original. See [[zhuangzi]].

### Taoism as a Social Entity: Only from the 5th Century CE

For Kirkland, *Tao-chiao* as a coherent identifiable community did not exist until the
**5th century CE**, when **[[lu-hsiu-ching|Lu Hsiu-ching]]** (406–477) organized the Ling-pao
texts into the first recognizable Taoist canon and constructed a unified Taoist identity.
Figures like the *Tao te ching* redactors, the Nei-yeh composers, and even early T'ien-shih
leaders did not think of themselves as members of a group called "Taoism."

### The Continuous Core: Biospiritual Cultivation

What actually persisted as the continuous thread of Taoism — not wu-wei, not "philosophical
mysticism" — was **biospiritual cultivation**: practical techniques for cultivating *ch'i*
(life-energy), *ching* (vital essence), and *shen* (spirit/consciousness). This thread runs:
Nei-yeh → T'ai-p'ing ching → Shang-ch'ing visualizational meditation → "Inner Alchemy"
(*nei-tan*) → Ch'üan-chen monastic practice → Lung-men today.

### The Major Taoist Communities (Historical)

**T'ien-shih (Heavenly Masters)**: Founded legendarily by Chang Tao-ling (covenant with
Lord Lao, 144 CE). The only truly populist Taoist movement. Women in sacerdotal roles
(*chi-chiu* libationers); parallel male/female titles (*tao-nan/tao-nü*; *tao-fu/tao-mu*).
The "Chang Tao-ling lineage" at Lung-hu shan claiming unbroken succession is a Sung-era
retroactive fabrication.

**Shang-ch'ing (Upper Clarity) revelations** (4th c. CE): Aristocrats in southern China;
revelations received by Yang Hsi and the Hsü family from "Perfected Ones" (*chen-jen*)
including the female transcendent Lady Wei Hua-ts'un. Visualizational meditation. Millenarian;
"seed people" (chung-min) to survive cosmic transformation. Marriage to female celestial
beings. Key text: the *Declarations of the Perfected* (*Chen-kao*). See [[shang-ching]].

**Ling-pao (Numinous Treasure) revelations** (late 4th c. CE): Ko Ch'ao-fu (reportedly
Ko Hung's grandnephew). Incorporated Mahayana Buddhist ideas (universalistic soteriology;
rebirth; the *tu-jen ching* [Scripture of Human Salvation]). Lu Hsiu-ching organized these
into the first Taoist canon (around 471 CE).

**T'ien-shih at Lung-hu shan / Cheng-i**: The "Heavenly Masters" of Dragon-Tiger Mountain
claimed descent from Chang Tao-ling. Recognized by Sung emperors, consolidated under Mongol
Qubilai Khan, and became the dominant southern tradition under the Ming (the Tao-tsang was
compiled under a Cheng-i leader, 1444/45). Declined under Manchus. Liturgical; not monastic.

**Ch'üan-chen (Quanzhen / "Integrating Perfection")**: Founded by **Wang Che** (Wang Ch'ung-yang,
1113–1170), a gentry scholar. His seven disciples included the woman **Sun Pu-erh** (1119–1182).
Focused on individual self-cultivation (hsing/ming integration); monastic setting; welcomed women
(20–40% of early clergy female). The only surviving new Taoist tradition from north China that
endured fully to the present. See [[quanzhen]].

**Lung-men (Dragon Gate)**: A Ch'üan-chen subtradition; in 1656 **Wang Ch'ang-yüeh** established
it at the **White Cloud Abbey** (Po-yün kuan) in Beijing, integrating "Inner Alchemy" with
Ch'üan-chen monasticism. Still the dominant Taoist tradition in China today.

### Taoism and the Dynasts (Ch. 4)

Contrary to the Confucian falsehood that Taoists were anti-political recluses, Taoism was
deeply embedded in Chinese imperial legitimation for centuries. Key examples:
- **Wang Yüan-chih** legitimized the founding of the T'ang dynasty
- **Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen** played "sagely advisor" to T'ang emperors, even redirecting imperial
  state cult ceremonies per Shang-ch'ing doctrine
- **Yeh Fa-shan**: T'ang wonder-worker; praised by emperors as realm-protector
- The T'ang emperor Hsüan-tsung sponsored the *chin-lu chai* (Golden Register ceremony) and
  incorporated the *Huang-t'ing ching* into civil service exams
- The Tao-tsang was an imperial compilation, not a sectarian project

The concept of **t'ai-p'ing** (Grand Tranquillity) — harmony of cosmic, social, and
individual orders — was a Taoist political theology, not escapism.

### Women in Taoism

Early T'ien-shih: formally equal roles (male/female libationers, parallel titles). Shang-ch'ing:
female Perfected Ones including Lady Wei. T'ang period: Huang Ling-wei; Pien Tung-hsüan; Tsu Shu
(founder of Ch'ing-wei); hundreds of Taoist abbeys for women. Ch'üan-chen: Sun Pu-erh as co-founder;
20–40% female clergy early on. Diminution of formal roles in late imperial era reflects broader
social changes (Confucian Neo-orthodoxy, foot-binding), NOT any change in Taoist ideology.

### The *Hsien* ("Transcendents")

The word *hsien* should not be translated as "immortal" in the sense of perpetuating the
physical body. For most Taoists, the goal was spiritual transcendence through biospiritual
cultivation: attaining an exalted state that will "not be extinguished even when the physical
body ceases." Diverse positions coexist in the tradition — acceptance of death (Chuang-tzu);
post-mortem transformation (Ling-pao); "mortuary liberation" (*shih-chieh*). There is no single
Taoist position on death; all positions are preserved.

### The Tao-tsang

The proper data set for understanding Taoism is the **Tao-tsang** (Taoist Canon) — a Ming
dynasty (1444/1445) compilation of all self-identified Taoist writings, assembled under Cheng-i
auspices. It includes texts as diverse as Mo-tzu and Han Fei-tzu. Most 20th-c. scholarship
ignored this corpus almost entirely in favor of the two "philosophical classics."

## Key Figures Introduced

- [[lu-hsiu-ching|Lu Hsiu-ching]] (406–477): practical founder of organized Taoism; compiler of
  first Taoist canon
- [[ko-hung|Ko Hung]] (c. 283–343): alchemist-scholar; *Pao-p'u-tzu*; NOT representative of Taoism
- [[ssu-ma-cheng-chen|Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen]] (646–735): T'ang literati Taoist; *Tso-wang lun*;
  sagely advisor to emperors
- [[tu-kuang-ting|Tu Kuang-t'ing]] (850–933): greatest Taoist historian; *Yung-ch'eng chi-hsien lu*
- Wang Che / Wang Ch'ung-yang (1113–1170): Ch'üan-chen founder
- Sun Pu-erh (1119–1182): Wang Che's female disciple; co-founder of Ch'üan-chen
