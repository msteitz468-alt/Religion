# Doniger, *The Hindus: An Alternative History*

[[wendy-doniger|Wendy Doniger's]] *The Hindus: An Alternative History* (2009) is a chronological,
deliberately **counter-establishment** history of Hinduism that foregrounds the people the mainstream
tradition marginalized — **women, the lower castes, and animals** — and argues that Hinduism has **no
single essence, founder, or canon.** It is a major modern-critical voice and (as much for its method as
its content) one of the most publicly **contested** books on Hinduism.

## What This Scope Establishes (Doniger's Method and Thesis)

From **Ch. 1**, the book's stated aims: (1) Hindus "have been enriched by the contributions of women, the
lower castes, and other religions"; (2) **no belief is true of *all* Hindus** — the shared factors are
"overwhelmingly outnumbered" by the idiosyncratic; (3) the greatness of Hinduism lies in the very
"earthy, vivid" qualities some modern Hindus would deny; (4) the tensions *between* Hinduisms "undergird
the violence of the contemporary Indian political and religious scene."

Method points the wiki should track:
- **"Available light."** A Sufi parable (the man searching under the streetlamp, not where he dropped his
  key) frames her caution: historians of Hinduism cross the centuries on an "archipelago of stepping
  stones" lit by good scholarship, and must beware looking only where the light is convenient.
- **Against Orientalist timelessness.** The first European scholars took from some Hindus the idea of a
  "timeless, eternal, unchanging" Hinduism; Doniger insists the "central ideas" — *karma, dharma,
  samsara* — **arise at particular moments and keep changing**. She dates Hinduism's textual history
  while denying it an unchanging core.
- **Myth vs. history.** A myth is "a story that a group of people believe for a long time despite massive
  evidence that it is not actually true." One may use history to explain *why* a myth was told, but
  **cannot use myth to reconstruct events** (the *Ramayana*'s ogres are not a record of conquered
  tribals). Yet "ideas are facts too": stories drive history forward — myth as "the smoke of history."
- **Texts, but read against the grain.** A self-described "**recovering Orientalist**" and Sanskritist,
  Doniger defends *texts* as a source for the marginalized — recovering women's and low-caste "**perfumes**"
  (*vāsanās*, tracks) left in Brahmin-authored texts, with a "hermeneutic of suspicion."
- **Animals as social cipher** — "Follow the monkey," or "the horse": **horse = power** (Kshatriya,
  *foreigner*, since horses must be imported), **cow = purity** (Brahmin, the vegetarianism debate),
  **dog = pollution** (the Pariah/Dalit). See [[wendy-doniger|the commentator page]].

From **Ch. 25** ("The Abuse of History"): the polemical payoff. "**You could easily use history to argue
for almost any position in contemporary India**" — that Hindus were vegetarian and were not, tolerant
and were not, oppressive of women and fought for them. The chapter opens by juxtaposing **Golwalkar**
(RSS, 1939: non-Hindus must live "wholly subordinated to the Hindu Nation… not even citizen's rights")
against **Gandhi** (Hinduism "essentially inclusive and ever-growing"), and warns against those who would
"set up a papacy in India, smuggling into Hinduism a Christian idea of orthodoxy." See
[[who-defines-hinduism|the controversy page]].

## Scope 2 — Prehistory: Indus Valley to the Aryan Question (Chs. 2–4)

What this scope established (filed to dedicated pages):
- **[[indus-valley-civilization|The Indus Valley Civilization]]** (Chs. 3–4) — the grid-planned, brick-and-
  drainage Harappan cities; the undeciphered seal script; Doniger's skepticism about reading later Hindu
  religion into the remains ("Is Indus religion a myth?"); and the **proto-Śiva "Pashupati" seal 420**
  debate (Marshall's identification + ten rivals, unadjudicated). *(Flagged gap: no Śiva page
  yet.)*
- **[[aryan-migration-question|Origins of the Vedic people]]** (Ch. 4) — the four "guesses" (invasion /
  migration / Out-of-India / IVC=Vedic), the racial history of the word "Aryan," and the **horse argument**
  ("no Indus horse whinnied in the night"); resolved by Doniger into **fusion/bricolage** (the "non-Veda"
  as a co-*fons et origo*).
- **[[yugas|The four Yugas]]** (Ch. 2) — the dice-named declining Ages; Indian time as **both linear and
  cyclical**; the *pralaya* dissolution and the **Manu-and-the-fish flood myth** (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa).

Method note: this scope is a sustained demonstration of Doniger's "**siren song of hindsight**" caution —
her refusal to read Vedic/Hindu meaning back into pre-textual remains — and of her **"follow the horse"**
use of animals as historical and social evidence.

## Scope 3 — Veda & Brāhmaṇas (Chs. 5–6)

**Ch. 5 ("Humans, Animals, and Gods in the Rig Veda, 1500–1000 BCE")** — Doniger's social-historical
reading of the Rig Veda. The **three alliances** structuring Vedic religion (gods+humans vs. Asuras;
ascetics vs. gods; bhakti). The world as "Indians as cowboys": **cattle raiding** (*gavisthi*) as the
primary social violence; **horse** = royal/Kshatriya power (imported); **cow** = Brahmin purity. The **oral
refusal to write** as religious act. The **Puruṣa-sūkta** (RV 10.90) as social rationalization of the four
varnas rather than pure cosmology (→ [[varnas|Varnas]] page). Women in the RV: Apala and Indra; rival-wife
spells; ambiguous widow status. **Henotheism/kathenotheism** confirmed per Müller — serial situational
monotheism. Filed to: [[rig-veda|rig-veda]] (extended); [[varnas|varnas]] (new).

**Ch. 6 ("Sacrifice in the Brāhmaṇas, 800–500 BCE")** — Shift from RV's questioning to Brāhmaṇic
confidence: "you can fix anything with the sacrifice." **Brahmin-Kshatriya conflict** ("inner conflict of
tradition"). The **horse sacrifice** (*aśvamedha*) as political annexation + fertility rite; the queen's
ritual role and the text that reads it as the king *raping the people*. The **recycling of sacrificial
quality**: human → horse → bull → ram → goat → grain. Early **ahimsa stirrings**: "be sure to eat animals
the right way," not yet vegetarianism. **Dogs'** declining status (scapegoat role). **Soma scarcity** driving
development of yoga/breath control. **Women and urbanization**: the Urvashi myth transformed — insatiable-
woman stereotype emerging; "gates of sexual freedom clang shut" in the Ganges valley cities.

**Death and re-death.** The Brāhmaṇas' great anxiety: **recurrent death** (*punarmrtyu*) — not one death but
perpetual dying. Transmigration mentioned. The Bhrigu-in-hell story (eating and being eaten) → Nachiketas
(precursor of Kaṭha Upaniṣad). Sacrifice as the (tentative, conditional) solution. Filed to: [[samsara]]
(extended); [[yajna|yajna]] (extended — horse sacrifice + brahmanic anxieties).

**Evil and the gods.** Indra's **Brahminicide** after slaying Vritra (now a Brahmin in the Brāhmaṇas);
distribution of sin to earth/trees/women (menstrual blood as Indra's transferred pollution); the gods
**intentionally placing evil** (dice, desire for women, anger) in humans to prevent them from reaching
heaven. Major shift: gods as jealous, vicious kings rather than morally neutral nature-forces. Filed to:
[[indra|indra]] (extended).

## Scope 4 — Upaniṣads & the Aims of Life (Chs. 7–8)

**Ch. 7 ("Renunciation in the Upanishads, 600–200 BCE")** — Doniger's distinctive contributions beyond
Dasgupta's philosophical account:

*Social context*: Ganges Valley second urbanization (Magadha, Koshala-Videha kingdoms; trade, moneyed
economy, social mobility); the claustrophobia of city life as one driver of renunciant movements.

*Karma's six meanings*: Doniger traces karma from (1) action, (2) ritual action, (3) morally charged
action, (4) retributive karma across rebirths, (5) fate/innate role (*sva-karma*), to (6) transferable
karma. Filed to [[karma|karma]] (extended, sources 2→3).

*Two paths and the third*: path of flame (brahman, no return) vs. path of smoke (samsara, rebirth), with
a third state (worms, No Exit). Doniger's "overcrowding" theory. The *positive* face of samsara: the erotic
Upaniṣads (BU 6.2.13 etc.). Filed to [[samsara|samsara]] (extended, sources 2→3).

*Moksha's genealogy*: freedom from death → freedom from samsara → renunciation → beyond good and evil.
*Neti neti* as the negative description. Five solutions to the householder/renunciant tension. Filed to
[[moksha|moksha]] (extended, sources 3→4).

*Non-Brahmin sources*: Kshatriyas as secret teachers (Jaivali, Ajatashatru, Janaka); Raikva the homeless
wanderer; Satyakama Jabala (son of a promiscuous woman accepted on the criterion of truth rather than birth).

*Shramanas vs. Brahmanas*: Ajivikas (no free will), Lokayatas/Charvakas (materialism, "the prattling of
knaves"), Nastikas — vigorous public intellectual combat in the 5th c. BCE.

*Women*: Maitreyī (theological wife vs. Katyāyanī the worldly wife); Gārgī (feisty public questioner);
Buddhism as offering women "double liberation — from rebirth and from the kitchen." Filed to
[[yajnavalkya|Yājñavalkya]] (extended, sources 1→2).

*Animals*: dogs as satirical Brahmins (CU 1.12-13 — "Song of the Dogs"); horse as chariot/senses metaphor
(KU 3.3-6); rebirth as animals not generating straightforward vegetarianism. Vegetarianism/ahimsa complex
relationship (distinction between killing, sacrificing, eating, and worshiping animals).

**Ch. 8 ("The Three (or Is It Four?) Aims of Life")** — Interlude on the puruṣārthas:

*Trivarga*: dharma/artha/kama as the three worldly aims; the three gunas and their social mapping
(sattva/Brahmins/cows; rajas/Kshatriyas/horses; tamas/lower classes/dogs). Filed to [[gunas|gunas]] (new).

*Dharma*: sanatana/sadharana dharma (universal) vs. sva-dharma (particular); the violence paradox (warrior
sva-dharma vs. universal ahimsa); the three shastra texts (Manu, Kautilya, Vatsyayana); the four ashramas;
five solutions to the householder/renunciant tension. Filed to [[dharma|dharma]] (new).

*Squaring the circle*: moksha as the fourth aim interloping on the Trio; the triad→quartet pattern throughout
Hindu thought (three gunas + purusa; three Vedas → four; three passions + moha; three priests + silent
Brahmin witness; the turiya "fourth" state). Filed to [[moksha|moksha]] (extended).

## Scope 5 — The Epics: Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata, Dharma (Chs. 9–11)

**Ch. 9 ("Women and Ogresses in the Ramayana, 400 BCE–200 CE")** — Doniger's feminist and mythological
reading of the Rāmāyaṇa:

*Sītā's four disappearances and two ordeals* — abduction by Rāvaṇa; fire ordeal (anti-suttee: enters
fire while husband lives, as "threat/ultimatum"); second exile (while pregnant); descent into earth
(abandonment of Rāma). Her ordeals are "supreme, defiant protest." Filed to [[sita|sita]] (new).

*Rāma's moral imperfections* — shooting Vālin in the back; doubting Sītā publicly; banishing her twice;
beheading the Śūdra ascetic Śambuka; taunting Śūrpaṇakhā. His relationship to his father (fear of
being like the lust-driven Daśaratha) structures his political decisions about Sītā. Filed to [[rama|rama]]
(new).

*The goddess dimension* — Sītā as "ex-goddess on the way down," Rāma as "god in the making on the way
up" — adjacent escalators. Equine-goddess pattern (Saraṇyū, Urvaśī): comes from another world, bears
twins, is mistreated, leaves forever. Sītā not present at Rāma's heavenly return.

*Ogresses as shadow-women* — Kaikeyī, Śūrpaṇakhā, Ṭāṭakā as the "shadow side of Sītā"; Brahmin
tradition suppresses this shadow; the ogress shackled to keep order. "The Valmiki Ramayana sowed the
seeds both for the oppression of women and for the resistance against it."

*Monkeys as dream-work* — Vālin/Sugrīva as displaced projection of Rāma's anger at half-brothers;
"in the magical world of the monkey forest, Rāma's unconscious mind is set free." Symbolic ogres: Rāvaṇa
= ego/rajas, Vibhīṣaṇa = superego/sattva, Kumbhakarṇa = id/tamas (three [[gunas|guṇas]] in narrative form).

*Language and compassion* — shloka meter born from Valmiki's grief at a dying crane; language = the
characteristic human act that separates us from animals. The Niṣāda who kills the crane is cursed to
"forever lack peace" — poetry's inverse origin. Filed to [[nishadas|nishadas]] (new).

*Challenges to caste* — Śambuka beheaded; Trishanku suspended halfway to heaven (Brahmin-Kṣatriya
power conflict; liminality as structural unresolved tension). Filed to [[ramayana|ramayana]] (new).

**Ch. 10 ("Violence in the Mahabharata, 300 BCE–300 CE")** — Doniger situates the Mahābhārata in
its historical moment:

*Aśoka (c. 265–232 BCE)* — Kalinga conquest; remorse; dhamma edicts on rocks and pillars; limited
vegetarianism (still killed 3 animals/day; still had capital punishment); Third Buddhist Council at
Pāṭaliputra; transformed Buddhism into a world religion. The pillars: Persian lion capitals + Indian
style + Indra's phallic pillar. Ashoka's "hedged" nonviolence: "locked the stable after the horse was
safely tethered." Filed to [[ashoka|ashoka]] (new).

*Rise of sectarian Hinduism* — Vaishnavas and Shaivas emerging c. 500 BCE; Patañjali mentions Śiva
worshiper; Gudimallam liṅga (first surviving Śiva liṅga, 1st c. BCE); Heliodorus pillar (115 BCE):
Greek becomes Bhāgavata. Pūjā begins (from Dravidian *pu* = "flower"). The eclipse of Vedic gods
(Indra, Soma, Agni) by Viṣṇu and Śiva.

*"Fish eat fish" (matsya-nyāya)* — big fish eat little fish: used both to justify coercive kingship
AND to argue violence is inevitable. Janamejaya's snake sacrifice (antisacrifice; never completed);
burning of Khandava forest. Filed to [[mahabharata|mahabharata]] (new).

*Yudhiṣṭhira's dog* — god Dharma incarnate; Yudhiṣṭhira refuses to abandon him; no dogs get into
heaven ("the story shows how rotten the caste system is but does not change it"). "Dog who would be
lion" — jāti = caste = cannot transcend your birth station.

*Ahimsa's complexity* — Aśokan dhamma as hedged nonviolence; Arjuna's argument that violence is
inevitable; King Śibi self-sacrifice as reductio; nonviolence can itself be violent (against the self).
Filed to [[ahimsa|ahimsa]] (new).

*Yudhiṣṭhira's horse sacrifice undermined* — the mongoose story: "not equal to one gleaner's grain of
barley"; Indra's animal sacrifice debate; "a snake behind every horse."

**Ch. 11 ("Dharma in the Mahabharata, 300 BCE–300 CE")** — Doniger's reading of the MB's moral
deconstruction:

*"Dharma is subtle"* — the MB constantly shows the impossibility of fulfilling dharma; every choice is
wrong; the text "deconstructs dharma." Mandavya impaled for childhood cruelty to butterflies; god
Dharma cursed to be born as Vidura (Śūdra-born). Filed to [[dharma|dharma]] (extended).

*Transfer of karma* — Yudhiṣṭhira in heaven and hell; his "cool sweet breeze" eases brothers' torments;
text "totters on the brink" of transferable merit. Karma's mobility vs. karma's isolation still unresolved.

*Bhagavad Gita synthesis* — three paths (karma/jñāna/bhakti); Krishna's cosmic theophany; "karma without
kāma"; ahimsa appears only in 4 lists, never discussed. Filed to [[bhagavad-gita|bhagavad-gita]] (extended).

*Origin of class colors* — varṇa from karmic fall, not divine creation; "it's their own damn fault" —
a major shift from the Puruṣa-sūkta's authoritative decree. Filed to [[dharma|dharma]] (extended).

*Nishadas* — house of lac (expendable Niṣāda substitutes); Ekalavya (thumb cut off by Droṇa to protect
Arjuna's status; Jaina retelling inverts the moral; modern Dalit hero). Filed to [[nishadas|nishadas]] (new).

*MB women* — polyandry rampant (Satyavatī, Kuntī, Draupadī); births of Vyāsa and Vyāsa's children
(blind Dhṛtarāṣṭra, pale Pāṇḍu, Śūdra-born Vidura); Kuntī's mantra; Draupadī born from fire altar
(dark-skinned/kṛṣṇā; worshipped by lower castes and Muslims in South India). "How different women's
lives would have been had Draupadī been the official role model." Filed to [[draupadi|draupadi]] (new).

*Rāmāyaṇa vs. Mahābhārata* — triumph vs. tragedy; "dharma can be mastered" vs. "dharma is too subtle";
the two texts in permanent conversation. Filed to [[mahabharata|mahabharata]] and [[ramayana|ramayana]] (new).

## Scope 6 — Shāstras, Bhakti, and Early Purāṇas (Chs. 12–14)

**Ch. 12 ("Escape Clauses in the Shastras, 100 BCE–400 CE")** — The "Age of Diversity" (not a "dark
age") between two empires (Mauryas and Guptas): Kushanas, Shatavahanas, Shakas. The three shastras as
competing social visions: Manu, Kautilya's Arthashastra, Vatsyayana's Kama-sutra. Filed new content to
[[dharma|dharma]]: āpad-dharma (the escape clause); Manu as meditation not law code; fantastical miscegenation
model of jati; the four addictions; tritiya prakriti (third nature; same-sex acts in the Kama-sutra vs.
Manu's penances); divergence of Manu/Kama-sutra on women.

**Ch. 13 ("Bhakti in South India, 100 BCE–900 CE")** — The Caṅkam poetry tradition and its landscape
metaphors absorbed into bhakti; Nayanmars (63 Shaiva saints) and Alvars (12 Vaishnava saints); female saints
(Antal/Andal, Karaikkal Ammaiyar); darshan and the temple as royal palace; bhakti and kingship; Kannappar the
Pariah hunter and the caste double-bind; bhakti violence (Campantar and the impaled Jainas); interreligious
exchange with Jainism, Thomas Christianity, and early Islam. Major expansion to [[bhakti|bhakti]]; new page
[[darshan|darshan]].

**Ch. 14 ("Goddesses and Gods in the Early Puranas, 300–600 CE")** — The Gupta "Golden Age" critically
examined; Kalidasa's Shakuntala vs. Mahabharata version (women stripped of agency); the Puranas as "Brahmin
filter" on folk material; Shiva linga origin myth; Chandika/Durga killing Mahisha (*Devīmāhātmya*); shakti
theology (tooth vs. breast goddess, Ramanujan/Doniger typology); Sati suicide (not suttee) at Daksha's
sacrifice; the Shakti Pithas (Vishnu dismembers Sati's corpse); Parvati winning Shiva; Kama burned; Ganesha
(parthenogenesis, elephant head, single-parent myth); Gauri/Kali split; Skanda; vahanas; proto-Tantric sects
(Pashupatas, Kapalikas). New pages: [[durga|durga]], [[parvati|parvati]], [[ganesha|ganesha]], [[shakti|shakti]];
extended [[sita|sita]], [[draupadi|draupadi]].

## Scope 7 — Tantra, the Sultanate, Later Purāṇas, Philosophy (Chs. 15–18)

**Ch. 15 ("Sects and Sex in the Tantric Puranas and the Tantras, 600–900 CE")** — The Tantric turn.
Harsha's eclectic kingdom (606–647); Bana's anti-suttee argument. Proto-Tantric sects: Pashupatas
(Lakulisha; stigmatizing behavior to transfer bad karma); Kapalikas (skull-bearers). Shiva's
Brahminicide (beheads Brahma → Bhairava → Release of the Skull shrine at Varanasi). Sati dismembered
extended: Vishnu's discus cuts her corpse → Shakti Pithas; Kamakhya (yoni fell there) = central Tantric
shrine. Durga's erotic dimension (Mahisha's infatuation; Durga's masculine self-identification; Kali on
Shiva's corpse; tongue as displaced vagina). Tantra defined as "Zen diagram." The Five Ms (*madya,
mamsa, matsya, mudra, maithuna*) inverts pañcagavya; three positions on literal vs. symbolic. Women in
Tantra: "gods, jewels" but "not much in it for them." Caste inversions (Kaulas; Pariahs and Yavanas
welcomed). Tantric temples: Elephanta (c. 550–575 CE), Ellora/Kailasanatha (8th c.), Khajuraho
(900–1150), Konarak (1238–58); 64 Yoginis. Filed to: extended [[parvati|parvati]] (Ch. 14/15 combined);
notes added to [[durga|durga]]; [[shakti|shakti]].

**Ch. 16 ("Fusion and Rivalry Under the Delhi Sultanate, 650–1500 CE")** — Islam's entry: Arab traders
(pre-650); Muhammad ibn Qasim (713; guaranteed Hindu/Buddhist establishments); the Delhi Sultanate
(1192–1526). Mahmud of Ghazni (raids 1001–1018; Somnath 1026). Doniger's nuanced treatment: temple
destruction is real but also exaggerated; motivated by political power and loot as much as iconoclasm.
Positive synthesis: Sufism (Chishti order; Khwaja Muin-ud-din at Ajmer); trade partnerships; Hindu
artisans building mosques. Kabir (c. 1398–1448): nirgun bhakti, vernacular, anti-caste, anti-sectarian.
Vijayanagar Empire (1336–1565): Rama cult; Hindu-Muslim synthesis in practice. Virashaivas (Basava,
1106–1167): body as temple, portable linga, anti-caste, "things standing shall fall"; Mahadevyyakka
(female saint; body-as-temple poetry). New pages: [[kabir|kabir]], [[virashaiva|virashaiva]]; extended
[[bhakti|bhakti]] (North India section).

**Ch. 17 ("Avatar and Accidental Grace in the Later Puranas, 800–1500 CE")** — Ten avatars of Vishnu.
Krishna's childhood from Harivamsha (family romance; doubly doubled god). Yashoda and Gopis as foster
mothers/lovers. Radha and the Gita Govinda (Jayadeva, c. 1179–1209). Shadow Sita reborn as Draupadi
(Brahmavaivarta Purana). Buddha avatar: three stages (assimilation → polemic → conciliation); Vishnu
as deluder of antigods. Kalki (messianic future avatar; white horse; barbarian destruction). Parashurama
(ax-wielding Brahmin who kills Kshatriyas). Vamana (dwarf); Narasimha (Man-Lion, Prahlada). Accidental
grace theology: bell-ringing thief; lamp-lighting adulterer; dog's trident-paw. Shri Vaishnava Cats
(passive grace) and Monkeys (active clinging). Zero-sum universe → infinitely expansible Shiva heaven.
Filed to: extended [[krishna|krishna]]; extended [[bhakti|bhakti]] (accidental grace, Cats/Monkeys).

**Ch. 18 ("Philosophical Feuds in South India and Kashmir, 800–1300 CE")** — Six Darshanas overview.
The three Vedanta schools: Shankara (Advaita, non-dualism); Ramanuja (Vishishta Advaita, qualified
non-dualism); Madhva (Dvaita, dualism). Their legendary conflicts: Shankara and the philosopher's
wife (learning eros by entering a king's body); the crocodile vow (deathbed renunciation); Madhva's
hagiography (miracles echoing Christ). Shaiva Siddhanta (pati/pashu/pasha — "Lord/beast/bond"). Shri
Vaishnava Cats and Monkeys (repeated here in philosophical context). Yoga-vasishtha: illusion of
gender (Chudala, king who is his own wife's student); illusion of caste (Lavana — king dreams he is
a Pariah; Gadhi — Brahmin dreams he is a Pariah who becomes a king). Kashmir Shaivism, Abhinavagupta.
Existing pages: [[advaita-vedanta|advaita-vedanta]], [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|vishishtadvaita]],
[[dvaita-vedanta|dvaita-vedanta]], [[kashmir-shaivism|kashmir-shaivism]] — no new pages created (already
covered); content confirms and extends what those pages contain.

## Scope 8 — Mughals & the British Raj (Chs. 19–22)

**Ch. 19 ("Hinduism Under the Mughals: Akbar, 1525–1600 CE")** — Mughal architecture as Hindu-Muslim
synthesis (Fatehpur Sikri; Akbar's Hindu temples at Vrindaban; Muslim artists on Hindu themes). The
horse culture: Mughal horse imports (150,000–200,000 cavalry under Akbar); equine myths (Dhyanu Bhagat
and the Goddess's coconut-for-head exchange); the mare vs. stallion shift under Muslim influence (Arab
horsemen rode mares; Hindu folk epics follow suit — Gugga's mare, Pabuji's Kesar Kalimi); Khandoba
(Shiva avatar) with Muslim horse-handlers at Jejuri; Muttal Ravuttan (Muslim horseman as Hindu folk
deity). Interreligious synthesis: Dara Shikoh's translation of the Upanishads into Persian; "the
essential nature of Hinduism and Islam are identical." Aurangzeb's reaction: reimposed jizya; destroyed
Vishvanatha temple; renamed Mathura "Islamabad." Akbar's pragmatism (boar avatar temple spared; Thursday
theological salons; Din-i-Ilahi). Shivaji founded Maharashtrian resistance at age 17 (1647).

**Ch. 20 ("Hinduism Under the Mughals: 1500–1700 CE")** — Tulsidas (1532–1623): *Ramcaritmanas*
(Hindi Rāmāyaṇa); shadow Sita in the fire ordeal (happy ending, Uttarakanda omitted); Rāma and the
Crow (Pariah's vision of god's universal form, through the masking device of an unclean scavenger).
Chandidas (14th c. Bengal): Radha already married when she goes with Krishna ("fatal flute"). Chaitanya
(1486–1533): dual avatar of Krishna and Radha; Sahajiyas (become the divine couple); Goswamins;
svakīyā/parakīyā debate settled in 1717 (adulterous love is orthodox); Radhavallabhas (Radha as Queen,
Krishna her "Secretary of State"). Ramprasad (1720–1781): Bengali Tantric poetry of poverty and Kali.
Tukārām (1608–1649, Shudra Maharashtrian): floating manuscripts story; "God's Dog" poem; Thug-as-
divine-love metaphor. Kshetrayya (c. mid-17th c., Andhra): Telugu courtesan poems (Muvva Gopala);
triple register (sex = religion = politics); "A Married Woman to Her Lover" (rape, abortion, divine
son). Mirabai (c. 1450–1525): forced marriage; "the Deluder" poem; Bhil woman poem; "anti-wife" type.
Suttee under Mughals: Akbar opposed but did not abolish; Jahangir personally dissuaded widows. Women
who ruled: Nur Jahan (de facto regent); Jahanara (Sufi author). New pages: [[mirabai|mirabai]],
[[tulsidas|tulsidas]], [[chaitanya|chaitanya]]; extended [[krishna|krishna]], [[bhakti|bhakti]].

**Ch. 21 ("Caste, Class, and Conversion Under the British Raj, 1600–1900 CE")** — Three waves of
the Raj: (1) Orientalists/conservatives (Warren Hastings; first curiosity about India; British
Kshatriyazation); (2) Evangelicals/missionaries (1813 charter; Wilberforce; Macaulay's "sea of
treacle"); (3) Utilitarians/Anglicists (post-1857; Victoria's proclamation). East India Company:
cotton, looting, "treaties." Black Hole of Calcutta (1756): 23 survivors; Robert Clive made £400,000
from Bengal's conquest. 1857 Rebellion: greased cartridges (pig fat + cow fat = dual pollution for
Muslims and Hindus); Mangal Pandey; Lakshmi Bai of Jhansi; Kanpur/Bibighar massacre. Translations:
Ezour Veda (fraudulent); Wilkins's 1785 Bhagavad Gita; Jones's Manu (basis of 80% of Indian colonial
law). "Colonial Stockholm syndrome": coconuts ("brown in colour but white in thought"). Deep Orientalism
and Edward Said. Transposed Heads myth in European reception: Goethe (1797), Yourcenar (1928), Thomas
Mann (1940), Glanville-Hicks opera (1954). Napier's "Peccavi" (Punch 1844) — apocryphal; Latin as
class code; "I have Sind/sinned." Kipling's *Kim*: horse as espionage code; "the Faiths are like
horses, each has merit in its own country"; Kim's vision of the universe echoing Yashoda's/Arjuna's.

**Ch. 22 ("Suttee and Reform in the Twilight of the Raj, 1800–1947 CE")** — Full analysis of suttee:
mythology misapplied (goddess Sati self-immolated; Sita's fire ordeal was an ordeal not a pyre); "did
she jump or was she pushed?" — eyewitness contradictions; gender logic (widow as "loose cannon"); the
1813 British circular that *increased* suttee from 378 to 839; Captain Robertson's slow-burning grass
experiment; Bentinck's 1829 ban ("the British law probably facilitated more women's deaths than it
saved"); Rup Kanwar 1987. European romanticization: Voltaire, Wagner (Brünnhilde), Goethe. Rammohun
Roy (1774–1833): founded Brahmo Samaj (1828); first use of "Hinduism" (1816?); Upanishad translations;
two anti-suttee tracts. Arya Samaj (Dayananda Sarasvati, 1824–1883; founded 1875): Vedas-only theology;
cow protection societies (1880s–1890s; communal violence against Muslims/Dalits); shuddhi reconversion
ceremony; Vegetarian/Flesh-eating split (1893). Amritsar Massacre (1919: Dyer; 500 killed). Gandhi
(1869–1948): satyagraha; fasting as weapon; Gita commentary (symbolic reading of the war); conflict
with Ambedkar; cow protection and Muslims; Partition failure; assassination by Nathuram Godse (RSS).
Adivasis and the Devi movement (Gujarat 1920s): toddy/daru; Parsi exploitation; goddess possessed
women; temperance as liberation. Chamars and Satnamis (Ghasidas; nirguna "True Name"). Ambedkar: Poona
Pact; conversion to Buddhism 1956 (5 million Dalits); "Being Scheduled Caste causes inferiority in our
minds. To be Buddhist, it makes me feel free." New pages: [[rammohun-roy|rammohun-roy]],
[[gandhi|gandhi]], [[ambedkar|ambedkar]], [[suttee|suttee]]; new sects: [[brahmo-samaj|brahmo-samaj]],
[[arya-samaj|arya-samaj]].

## Scope 9 — Modern Hinduism & Diaspora (Chs. 23–25)

**Ch. 23 ("Hindus in America, 1900–")** — "Reverse colonization": voluntary vs. coerced; Hindu words
in English (bungalow, karma, guru, yoga, avatar, loot). **First wave**: Vivekananda at the 1893 World's
Parliament of Religions; Ramakrishna Mission; anti-caste, pro-beef; Theosophical Society (Blavatsky
1875, Annie Besant). **Second wave**: ISKCON (Prabhupada, 1965, Los Angeles, traces to Chaitanya);
Siddha Yoga (Muktananda, 1974); Rajneesh/Osho (Oregon, 1981); Amritanandamayi Ma (Amma). **Virtual
Hinduism**: internet pujas; Ganges dipping-by-proxy (photo sent to real river); prasad by post; online
Brahmin proxies for Varanasi sacrifices. **American Kali**: Gunga Din (1939); Indiana Jones (1984);
Ginsberg ("Kali as Statue of Liberty"); porn stars as Kali. **American Gita**: Oppenheimer at Los Alamos
(Krishna revealing himself as "death, the destroyer of worlds"). **Kama Sutra commercialization**:
wristwatches, oils, Karamel Sutra, Pop-Up Kama Sutra. **American Tantra**: "Californication"; inverted
pizza effect; Abhinavagupta's Kashmiri Tantra as precursor of New Age seekers. **Three objections and
Doniger's responses**: (1) Americans got Kali wrong; (2) wrong sort of Hinduism; (3) only Hindus may
write about it — "stories, unlike horses, cannot be stolen." New pages: [[vivekananda|vivekananda]],
[[iskcon|iskcon]]; extended [[who-defines-hinduism|who-defines-hinduism]].

**Ch. 24 ("The Past in the Present, 1950–")** — Vedic sacrifice transformed: vegetable substitutes;
dough goats; Vedic ritual in London (1996) with "entirely imagined" animals. Sacred cows: not Vedic;
OED "sacred cow" as backhanded Hindu slur; Jaipur streets; Hindus secretly selling cows to slaughter.
Dog worship: Kal Bhairava at Varanasi; Khandoba devotees who bark; man marrying dog to atone (Tamil
Nadu 2007). Renouncing renunciation: Dalits and villagers indifferent to moksha ("Have you ever seen
moksha at any crossing place?"). **Ramayana in politics**: Babri Masjid (1992 destruction; JNU
historians' pamphlet "Political Abuse of History"; 1,000+ dead); Ram Setu / Sethusamudram canal
(2007; government withdrew its own scientific finding after BJP pressure); "300 Ramayanas" /
Ramanujan essay (removed from Delhi University curriculum 2008 after ABVP mob ransacked history
department). **Subversive Ramayanas**: M. M. Datta (Ravana as hero, Rama=British); Tamil counter-epic
(Ravana noble Tamil king, Rama evil Aryan); Dalit Sita rebukes Rama; Tribal version (Lakshman fire
ordeal). **Modern Sitas**: feminist retellings; Sharad Joshi (Maharashtra land reform via Sita's curse).
**Ekalavya** as Dalit symbol of revolt (poetry; education foundations). **Hijras** as Patna tax
collectors (2006; 4% commission). **New goddess Santoshi Mata** (emerged 1960s; film *Jai Santoshi Ma*
1975). **Mithila/Madhubani paintings**: women painters of Bihar (wall/floor ritual art; ephemerality
as value; Dalit women adding folk themes; colonial intervention 1966). **Jotiba Phule** (1885):
Vishnu's avatars = Aryan conquest; Bali Raja as original casteless king of Maharashtra. New pages:
[[babri-masjid-ayodhya|babri-masjid-ayodhya]]; extended [[ramayana|ramayana]].

**Ch. 25 ("Inconclusion, or The Abuse of History")** — Already covered in Scope 1; this reading
confirmed and deepened the framing. Golwalkar vs. Gandhi (covered in [[who-defines-hinduism|the
controversy page]]). Agni missile vs. Ghorid missile — reaching into Vedic/medieval history for
nuclear weapons names. "India is a country where not only the future but even the past is
unpredictable." The composite horse on the book's jacket: Persian origin → Mughal court → Orissa
village; Muslim image made Hindu; mind-controlling-senses metaphor; "like Hinduism, a collage made of
individual pieces that fit together to make something far more wonderful than any of them." New
concept: [[hindutva|hindutva]].
