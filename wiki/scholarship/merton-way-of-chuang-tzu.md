# The Way of Chuang Tzu
*Thomas Merton* — New Directions, 1965

## Coverage Ledger

**Read in full**: `raw/texts/daoism/The way of Chuang-Tzu - Zhuangzi.pdf` — Note to Reader, Part 1
(the full introductory essay "A Study of Chuang Tzu"), Part 2 (all ~60 "readings"), Glossary, Notes,
Bibliography. Full ingest complete.

## Source Overview

Merton's book is **not a translation**. He explicitly says he knows only a few Chinese characters.
It is a set of **"free interpretative readings"** — creative versifications of passages from the
Zhuangzi — grounded in a five-year engagement comparing four existing translations into Western
languages: Giles (English), Legge (English, section references used in the Notes), Wilhelm (German),
and Wieger (French). The work also includes a serious ~25-page introductory essay on Zhuangzi's
philosophy, the Confucian background, and the nature of wu wei. It was dedicated to John C. H. Wu,
who provided scholarly and interpretive guidance.

Merton writes from his identity as a Trappist monk at the Abbey of Gethsemani, Kentucky, and
explicitly makes a **limited and careful cross-traditional claim**: that a "monastic outlook...
common to all cultures" connects his Christian contemplation to Zhuangzi's solitude, but he
disclaims any attempt to "pull Christian rabbits out of a Taoist hat."

## Merton's Reading of the Zhuangzi

### Chuang Tzu in Context
- Chuang Tzu (c. 4th–3rd c. BCE) is the only classical Daoist whose historical existence can be
  reasonably verified (unlike Laozi). He flourished at the close of the classical period
  (550–250 BCE) alongside the Confucians, Mohists, and logicians (especially Hui Tzu, his
  constant interlocutor and "opponent").
- The Zhuangzi is not a single-author work: the Inner Chapters are probably by Chuang Tzu himself;
  much of the rest, especially later chapters, is by disciples. The whole book is "an anthology of
  the thought, humor, gossip, and irony that were current in Taoist circles in the best period."
  (An exact parallel to Ziporyn's composite-text thesis for the Daodejing.)
- The **true inheritors** of Chuang Tzu's spirit, per Merton: the Chinese Zen Buddhists of the
  Tang period (7th–10th centuries). Zen throws light on Chuang Tzu, and Chuang Tzu on Zen.

### Chuang Tzu's Critique of Confucianism
Merton carefully situates Chuang Tzu's critique of Confucian *li*, *jen*, *yi*, and *chih* as
**demanding MORE, not less**. Confucianism's problem is that its pursuit of virtue objectifies
virtue — treats the good as something to be *attained* — and this objectification leads to
alienation:

> "The more one seeks 'the good' outside oneself as something to be acquired, the more one is faced
> with the necessity of discussing, studying, understanding, analyzing the nature of the good. The
> more 'the good' is objectively analyzed, the more it is treated as something to be attained by
> special virtuous techniques, the less real it becomes."

The pursuit of ethical objectives ends in "organized despair." The Daodejing's paradox — "When all
the world recognizes good as good, it becomes evil" — is, for Chuang Tzu, literally true: moral
striving produces its opposite.

### Wu Wei: Not Passivity, but Perfect Action
Merton's most sustained philosophical contribution is his clarification of *wu wei*:

> "The true character of wu wei is not mere inactivity but **perfect action** — because it is act
> without activity. In other words, it is action not carried out independently of Heaven and earth
> and in conflict with the dynamism of the whole, but **in perfect harmony with the whole**. It is
> not mere passivity, but it is action that seems both effortless and spontaneous because performed
> 'rightly,' in perfect accordance with our nature and with our place in the scheme of things."

This is the reading illustrated by the Cook Ding parable (Cutting Up an Ox): the blade glides
through the natural openings of the ox because the craftsman's whole being apprehends (not merely
analyzes) the structure. "After three years I no longer saw this mass. I saw the distinctions. But
now I see nothing with the eye. My whole being apprehends."

### The Pivot of Tao: Complementarity of Opposites
Chuang Tzu's epistemology rests on what Merton calls "the pivot of Tao" — not a synthesis of
opposites but the **still point through which all opposites pass**. The wise person holds the pivot
and watches Yes and No revolve on the circumference; they "retain perspective and clarity of
judgment" without being "imprisoned by the limitations of the 'I'."

This is why Chuang Tzu can take different sides in different contexts: he is "not merely a partisan."
His criticism is never bitter because he knows the limits of all positions.

### The Fasting of the Heart
One of the key texts Merton renders is the *xinzhai* passage (the "fasting of the heart"):

> Confucius to Yen Hui: "The goal of fasting is inner unity. This means hearing, but not with the
> ear; hearing, but not with the understanding; hearing with the spirit, with your whole being...
> Fasting of the heart empties the faculties, frees you from limitation and from preoccupation.
> Fasting of the heart begets unity and freedom."

This passage Merton reads as the practical centerpiece of Zhuangzi's contemplative teaching —
equivalent in spirit to Christian *apophatic* practice.

### Chuang Tzu's Politics
Merton faithfully transmits Chuang Tzu's sharp anti-activist politics: benevolence and justice as
ideals create the instruments of their own betrayal. "Cracking the Safe" makes the point: all the
locks and bolts designed to protect property only make it a neater package for the bigger thief. The
more rulers systematize virtue, the more crooks shelter behind the system.

### Cross-Traditional Reading: Christian Analogies
Merton makes four explicit, limited analogies:
1. Wu wei ↔ Paul's teaching on faith vs. the "works of the Old Law" (Galatians/Romans)
2. The Zhuangzi ↔ Ecclesiastes (a "book of earth," awareness of life's absurdity)
3. Chuang Tzu's via negativa ↔ John of the Cross: "you enter upon this kind of way when you leave
   all ways and, in some sense, get lost"
4. The "man of Tao" losing himself ↔ "to lose one's life is to save it" (Gospel)

He explicitly guards against over-reading: Chuang Tzu lacks "the profoundly theological mysticism
of St. Paul," and he is not trying to identify them.

## Merton's Hermeneutic (per CLAUDE.md Framework)

Merton reads the Zhuangzi through **commentary-as-transformation** in the contemplative register:
the text is not data about philosophy but a practice of the mind that breaks habits of objectifying,
planning, and striving. His "readings" are themselves transformative in intent — not scholarship
but a version of *lectio divina* applied to a classical Daoist text. This distinguishes his work
from Legge (literal-historical) and from Wagner (philosophical-philological).

His framework is cross-traditional: monastic contemplation as a universal human orientation.
