# Alter, *The David Story* (1999)

Robert Alter's *The David Story: A Translation with Commentary of 1 and 2 Samuel*
(W. W. Norton, 1999) is a fresh English translation of the Books of Samuel accompanied by
a running literary commentary printed beneath the text. It is the application to the David
narrative of the program Alter set out in *The Art of Biblical Narrative* (1981): reading
the Hebrew Bible as **literature** — attending to its diction, syntax, repetition,
dialogue, word-motifs, type-scenes, and narrative gaps — rather than dissolving it into
hypothetical documentary sources. The volume covers [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]] and
[[2-samuel|2 Samuel]] in full, and Alter argues that the story's true conclusion is the
first two chapters of [[1-2-kings|1 Kings]], which later redactors detached to serve as a
preface to Solomon.

## Central Thesis

The David story is **"probably the greatest single narrative representation in antiquity
of a human life evolving by slow stages through time"** — David shaped and altered by the
pressures of political life, public institutions, family, body, spirit, and the decay of
the flesh. Alter reads it as the **first full-length portrait of a Machiavellian prince in
Western literature**, a work that, like Stendhal's *Charterhouse of Parma* or Faulkner's
*Absalom, Absalom!*, gives "unflinching insight into the cruel processes of history and
into human behavior warped by the pursuit of power." The Bible's "astringent narrative
economy" — its capacity to define character and crisis in a few strokes of dialogue — is
nowhere more brilliantly deployed.

## Method and Key Positions

1. **Literary unity over source atomization.** Alter concedes the precise literary history
   is "beyond recovery" but insists the architectonic cohesion from the birth of
   [[samuel|Samuel]] to the death of [[david|David]] is real, and that the older
   habit of imagining the book as a stringing-together of independent sources (a prophetic
   Samuel narrative, a Saul cycle, a History of the Rise of David, a
   [[succession-narrative|Succession Narrative]]) loses "much of the richness and
   complexity of the story." He follows literary readers — J. P. Fokkelman, Shimon
   Bar-Efrat, and (critically) Robert Polzin — over the form-critical tradition of
   Leonhard Rost (1926).

2. **The Deuteronomist edited with a light hand.** Against Robert Polzin, who
   sees the [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomist's]] perspective in the smallest
   details, Alter argues the bulk of Samuel shows none of the
   [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]] "nationalist pietism," and that the editors
   "did no more... than to provide some minimal editorial framing... and to interpolate a
   few brief passages." His showcase is **David's deathbed charge to Solomon (1 Kings 2)**:
   the pious, Torah-saturated opening verses are unmistakable Deuteronomistic phraseology
   bolted onto an otherwise cold, Mafia-style political testament — "nobody in the David
   story talks like this." The editor could not delete the ruthless material, so he
   supplied a high-minded counterweight.

3. **"Imagining of history," not invention.** Against the "minimalists" who treat David as
   a King-Arthur-like legend, Alter holds (with the "history-like" realism Hans Frei
   identified) that the narrative preserves the "known general contours" of real events
   while bringing "the resources of literary art" to imagine deeply the moral and
   emotional predicaments of political life — analogous to what Shakespeare did with
   historical figures in the history plays. The author "thought of himself as a historian"
   but with a notion of historical factuality "decidedly different from modern ones."

4. **Ideology held with "dialectic complication."** The writer does believe in the
   covenant, in prophecy, and in the divine election of the Davidic line — but holds these
   "only with enormous dialectic complication, an order of complication so probing that at
   times it borders on subversion." Alter rejects the source-critical reflex of sorting the
   book into "Davidide," "Saulide," and "prophetic" advocacy strands as "blind to the
   complexity of vision of this extraordinary writer."

5. **Translation philosophy.** Alter renders the Hebrew with deliberate literalism —
   preserving its parataxis ("and... and..."), its concrete idioms, its repetitions and
   key-words — against the modern tendency to smooth, explain, and elevate. The commentary
   continually shows how meaning rides on a repeated verb, a withheld epithet, or a
   syntactic pause.

## Readings of the Principal Figures

- **[[samuel|Samuel]]** — a "densely imagined" and "rather unattractive" character: a
  prophet genuinely confirmed by God, yet "implacable, irascible," proud, and
  self-interested; his antimonarchic stance mixes noble theology with resentment at losing
  authority. The narrative thereby "enormously complicates" prophetic ideology rather than
  promoting it. Alter cites Martin Buber's question of whether Samuel, in the
  [[amalekites|Amalek]] *ḥerem*, confused "his own human impulses with God's will."
- **[[saul|Saul]]** — "the closest approximation of a tragic hero in the Hebrew Bible," a
  man "deprived of the knowledge he desperately seeks," whose story is built on
  ambivalence and **paired/tripled episodes** (three coronation scenes; two "Saul among the
  prophets"; two spear-hurlings; two cave/camp sparings).
- **[[david|David]]** — "one of the most unfathomable figures of ancient literature." The
  narrative gives us Saul's churning inner life while **blocking access to David's inner
  world**; nearly every utterance before the story's midpoint is public and arguably
  contrived for political advantage. The **pivot** is David's response to the death of his
  infant son by [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] — "I am going to him. He will not come back to me"
  — the first words with "no conceivable political motive," a glimpse of his inwardness.
- The **Saul/David antithesis turns on the theme of knowledge**: Saul, denied oracles,
  ends resorting to the necromancy he had outlawed and learns only "the news of his own
  imminent doom"; David begins "peculiarly favored with knowledge" but declines into the
  isolated, "purblind" old king who must send intermediaries to identify Bathsheba and is
  oblivious to [[amnon|Amnon]]'s lust and [[absalom|Absalom]]'s plots.

## Why It Matters for the Wiki

Alter is the wiki's principal exponent of the **literary-critical** reading of biblical
narrative (the [[literary-reading-of-the-bible|literary approach]]), standing against both
confessional harmonization and the source-critical atomizing of the
[[documentary-hypothesis|documentary]] and form-critical schools. His treatment grounds the
**court-narrative figure cluster** — [[absalom|Absalom]], [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]],
[[uriah|Uriah]], [[joab|Joab]], [[nathan|Nathan]], [[abner|Abner]], [[amnon|Amnon]],
[[michal|Michal]], [[jonathan|Jonathan]] — and supplies a counterweight to
Robert Polzin's pan-[[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]] reading (recorded
alongside it per the Contradiction Protocol).
