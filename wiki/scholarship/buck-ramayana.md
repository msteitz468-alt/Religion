# Ramayana: King Rama's Way — William Buck's Prose Retelling

**Source:** `raw/commentaries/Ramayana King Ramas Way Valmikis Ramayana told in English Prose by William Buck (William Buck, Valmiki etc.).txt`

**Original publication:** 1976, University of California Press; Mentor/New American Library paperback 1978

**Nature of the work:** William Buck's *Ramayana: King Rama's Way* is a prose retelling and literary adaptation of Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana, based on English translations rather than direct Sanskrit work (Buck studied Sanskrit but the publisher's preface clarifies he worked from translations). Buck (1933–1970, died age 37) devoted his life to making Indian epics accessible to Western readers; he also produced a prose retelling of the Mahabharata and left an unfinished Harivamsa at his death. This is not a scholarly translation or a faithful rendering — it is a creative literary adaptation that preserves the narrative arc while streamlining repetition, theological digression, and interpolated material. B. A. van Nooten's introduction (University of California Sanskrit scholar) contextualizes Buck's work and notes its key divergences from the Sanskrit original.

**Key divergences from the Sanskrit Ramayana (per van Nooten's introduction):**
- Buck's narrative begins with Rama's children Kusa and Lava reciting the Ramayana in Naimisha Forest *after* the war — a frame narrative absent from Valmiki's opening; the original begins in Ayodhya with Dasaratha's preparations
- The fire ordeal episode at Sita's reunion with Rama is substantially changed: in Valmiki, Rama haughtily rebuffs Sita as unfit to be queen after her captivity; Sita requests immolation, the fire god Agni rises and refuses to burn her, chides Rama, and the couple reunite. Buck instead has Agni lead Sita to Rama — removing the rebuff
- Buck adds a posthumous stone letter from Ravana to Rama announcing reconciliation; no such incident exists in the Sanskrit

**Hermeneutical framework:** Buck reads the Ramayana as simultaneously: (1) a narrative of moral exemplars for imitation; (2) an avatar theology — Rama as Vishnu incarnate; (3) a text of dharmic instruction; (4) a romantic adventure. Ram Dass's foreword adds a bhakti/devotional reading frame, presenting Hanuman as the perfect model of selfless service (*seva*).

**Key theological concepts illustrated in Buck's retelling:**
- *Dharma* as personal duty, eternal law, and the standard of kingship
- *Karma* as retribution and reward governing the arc of lives
- The *avatar* concept (Vishnu incarnates specifically to kill Ravana, who obtained a boon excluding gods but forgot to exclude humans)
- The four *yugas* (world ages): the Ramayana takes place in the Treta-yuga, the second of four
- *Maya* (illusion/magical power) wielded by demons to change shape and deceive
- *Tapas* (ascetic heat) as a technology by which humans can compel even gods
- *Moksha* and liberation as the horizon of the narrative's devotional reading
