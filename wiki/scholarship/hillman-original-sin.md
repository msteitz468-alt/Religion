# *Original Sin: Ritual Child Rape & the Church* (Hillman, 2012)

**Author:** David C.A. Hillman, Ph.D. (Classics) and M.S. (Bacteriology), University of Wisconsin. Also authored *The Chemical Muse* (on drug use in antiquity). His dissertation committee refused to pass earlier work on psychedelic drug use in antiquity; he later published the material independently. The *London Times* described his research as "the last wild frontier of classical studies."

## Central Thesis

The book's argument is revisionist and polemical: that the **ritual sexual abuse of young boys** was not an occasional or accidental feature of early Christianity but was **institutionally designed** as a catechetical initiation rite — known as "the fires of temptation" — administered by specialist priests called "exorcists." Hillman's title repurposes the phrase "original sin" to mean not Augustine's doctrinal formulation but rather **the founding institutional sin of the Church**: child rape.

The core structure of his argument:

1. **The pagan-Christian cultural war** — Early Christianity arose in direct competition with Mediterranean mystery religions (Aphrodite, Dionysus/Bacchus, Isis, Cybele, Mithras) and their political institutions (oracular priestesses, the Medusae/Furies/Sphinxes as female judicial enforcers, democratic traditions). The Christians declared a "cultural war" with three planks: anti-drug legislation, anti-homosexuality (targeting Dionysus' cult), and suppression of the female political voice.

2. **The pagan oracular requirement of sexual purity** — Participants in pagan oracular rites were required to be sexually "pure" (unviolated). Therefore, sodomizing young boys was a means of ensuring they could *never* participate in pagan oracular ceremonies — permanently closing them off from paganism while psychologically bonding them to the Church.

3. **The catechetical sodomy ritual** — "Exorcist" priests administered a ceremony called "the fires of temptation" during catechesis (the period of preparation before baptism). This involved: stripping initiates naked, blindfolding them, anointing them with oil containing drugs, and then (per Hillman's reading of the sources) sodomizing them — all framed as summoning the devil, then having the boys "rebuke" him. Successfully rejecting the forced pleasure conferred salvation.

4. **The Holy Spirit as psychological cover** — The Holy Spirit replaced the pagan Muses as the theological lens through which victims were taught to interpret their abuse as purification.

5. **The invention of "pornography," "homosexuality," and Original Sin** — Hillman argues these are all Christian inventions designed to consolidate the cultural war against paganism and to protect the institution of ritual abuse from pagan scrutiny.

## Hillman's Interpretive Claims

### On Cyril of Jerusalem's Catechetical Texts

The most specific and consequential interpretive move: Hillman reads Cyril of Jerusalem's *Mystagogical Catechesis* — which does genuinely describe initiates being stripped naked, anointed with oil from head to toe, blindfolded, facing west to renounce Satan, then turning east to face Christ — as describing a ritual that culminated in anal intercourse administered by the "possessed exorcist." The specific phrase "blown upon and exorcised" (*emphysao*) he reads as sexually charged. The "fires of temptation" are what Hillman identifies as the act of sodomy.

**Mainstream patristics reading**: These are standard early Christian initiation rites. The stripping symbolizes putting off the old self; the anointing is prebaptismal chrism; the blindfolding prevents the initiates from seeing sacred mysteries prematurely; the renunciation of Satan involves facing west (traditionally associated with darkness). No mainstream patristics scholar reads these as describing sodomy. The "fires of temptation" are generally understood as the spiritual trials preceding baptism. See [[cyril-of-jerusalem|Cyril of Jerusalem (commentator)]] for the mainstream account.

### On Clement of Alexandria's *Exhortation to the Greeks*

Hillman reads Clement's sustained polemic against the cults of Aphrodite and Dionysus (including their use of drugs, their erotic iconography, their theatrical entertainments) as evidence of the political program to suppress oracular priestesses and female political authority. He reads the phrase "Sodomita Libido" (from Prudentius, not Clement) as revealing the preoccupation of the catechetical tradition with sodomy.

### On Origen's *Commentary on Song of Songs*

Hillman quotes Origen's transfer of erotic desire from women to Jesus as confirming that the catechetical schools redirected boys' sexuality from heterosexual desire to a "sexual" relationship with Christ — with the Holy Spirit as the vehicle. He treats Origen's castration as an extreme expression of the same anti-female sexuality program.

### On Ambrose's *On Virgins*

The passage describing Jesus with "milky breasts" and a "uterus" Hillman treats as Ambrose's deliberate adaptation of Cybele's hermaphroditic mystery cult theology — designed to absorb the followers of Cybele into Christianity. He sees this as evidence that early Christianity explicitly cultivated a "lady-boy" Christology to compete with hermaphroditic mystery cults.

### On Prudentius' *Psychomachia*

Hillman reads the poem's portrayal of "Sodomita Libido" as arch-enemy, and the speech of the virtue Hope praising the "flowering" of a pre-pubertal boy as the highest spiritual relationship with Christ, as evidence of the Church's systematic promotion of pederasty as the ideal sexual form for Christian men.

## Methodological Concerns (Contradiction Protocol)

Hillman's argument rests on several methodological choices that mainstream classicists and patristics scholars would contest:

1. **Causal leap from evidence to intent**: The existence of ritual nakedness, anointing, and breathing-upon in catechetical texts does not logically entail the occurrence of sodomy. Hillman makes this inferential leap without direct primary-source confirmation.

2. **Treating pagan accusations as factual reports**: Pagan polemicists (Minucius Felix reports pagans claiming Christians engaged in incestuous orgies and ate fetuses) routinely fabricated scandalous accusations against minority religions. Hillman treats these accusations as reliable historical evidence.

3. **Non-academic publication**: Published by Ronin (alternative press), without peer review. Hillman acknowledges his academic career was threatened by this work.

4. **Over-reading of grammatically ambiguous passages**: The term *emphysao* ("blown upon") in Cyril was not sexually charged in catechetical literature; it was a standard liturgical term for exorcistic breathing.

5. **Speculative pharmacological claims**: The claim that the Wolves used snake venom to lactate without pregnancy, and that infant exposure to such venoms produced hardened "snake-like" skin and poison glands, is not supported by toxicological evidence.

These concerns do not mean Hillman's **historical framing** — the cultural war between Christianity and pagan mystery religions, the suppression of female oracular authority, the anti-drug legislation, the anti-homosexual polemic — is wrong. Much of this framing is well-grounded in primary sources and echoed (less polemically) in mainstream scholarship. The specific catechetical-sodomy claim is where his interpretation departs most radically from the academic consensus.

## What Is Independently Verifiable

The following elements of Hillman's account are corroborated by standard scholarship:

- Early Christianity did engage in sustained polemical competition with pagan mystery religions (Bacchus, Isis, Cybele, Mithras) and explicitly targeted their drug use, theatrical entertainment, and oracular traditions.
- Early Church fathers (Tertullian, Jerome, Ambrose) did express strong misogyny, treating female sexuality as spiritually dangerous.
- Christian bishops did successfully advocate for Roman laws criminalizing pagan religious drug use in the 3rd–4th centuries.
- Cyril of Jerusalem's catechetical rites did involve stripping initiates, anointing with oil, blindfolding, and facing west to renounce Satan.
- Clement of Alexandria, Origen, and others did develop a theological framework redirecting erotic desire from women to Christ.
- Prudentius' *Psychomachia* does name "Sodomita Libido" as the arch-enemy and does praise the pre-pubertal boy David as the highest symbol of spiritual purity.
- Minucius Felix and others do report pagan accusations of sexual impropriety against early Christians (though the status of these accusations is disputed).
- Ambrose did describe Jesus in *On Virgins* in terms traditionally associated with Cybele's hermaphroditic mythology.
