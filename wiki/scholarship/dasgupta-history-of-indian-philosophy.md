# Dasgupta, *A History of Indian Philosophy* (Vols. I–V)

[[surendranath-dasgupta|Surendranath Dasgupta's]] five-volume *A History of Indian Philosophy*
(1922–1955) is the encyclopedic modern-critical survey of the Indian schools. This page records what the
ingest drew from it; the work is a **meta-source** touching nearly every Hindu page in the wiki, so only
its highest-yield contributions are summarized here.

## The Five Volumes

- **Vol. I (1922)** — the Vedas and Upaniṣads; the General Observations on the
  [[saddarshana|six systems]]; Buddhist and Jaina philosophy; Sāṃkhya–Yoga (Kapila/Patañjali);
  Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika; Mīmāṃsā; the [[shankara|Śaṅkara]] school of [[advaita-vedanta|Vedānta]].
- **Vol. II (1932)** — the Śaṅkara school in depth (Maṇḍana, Sureśvara, Vācaspati, Citsukha,
  Vidyāraṇya, Prakāśānanda); Yoga; medical/Sāṃkhya speculation; **the philosophy of the
  [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad-Gītā]]** (ch. XIV).
- **Vol. III (1952)** — the Bhāskara school; the Pāñcarātra; the Āḻvārs; the historical and philosophical
  survey of the **[[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Viśiṣṭādvaita]]** school ([[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]], Yāmuna,
  Veṅkaṭanātha); the Nimbārka school; Vijñāna Bhikṣu; the Purāṇas.
- **Vol. IV — *Indian Pluralism* (1961, posthumous)** — **[[madhva|Madhva]] and his school**; Madhva's
  interpretation of the [[brahma-sutras|Brahma-Sūtras]]; the general review of Madhva's philosophy and
  his [[panchabheda|defence of pluralism]]; Madhva logic; the **dualist–monist controversy**; Caitanya,
  Jīva Gosvāmī and the Bengal Vaiṣṇavas.
- **Vol. V — *Southern Schools of Śaivism* (1955, posthumous)** — Śaiva-siddhānta, Vīra-Śaivism, and the
  Southern Śaiva systems (the complement, on the realist/theistic Śaiva side, to
  [[kashmir-shaivism|Kashmir Śaivism]] in the North).

## Highest-Yield Contribution: Grounding the Dvaita Pole (Vol. IV)

The ingest's primary purpose. Until now the wiki's **[[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]]** coverage rested on
secondary knowledge, because [[madhva|Madhva's]] *Brahma-Sūtra-bhāṣya* has no complete free English
translation. Dasgupta's Vol. IV supplies a connected, source-based account:

- **Life and corpus** — Madhva (Pūrṇaprajña / Ānanda-tīrtha / Nanditīrtha), born near Uḍupi in the Tuḷuva
  country, disciple of Acyutaprekṣa, regarded by his tradition as an **incarnation of Vāyu** sent to
  destroy Śaṅkara's doctrine; he refuted twenty-one prior Brahma-Sūtra commentaries and wrote
  **thirty-seven works**, including the *Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya*, the *Anuvyākhyāna*, a *Gītā-bhāṣya*, the
  *Mahābhārata-tātparya-nirṇaya*, and an *Ṛg-bhāṣya*. **Dating is disputed**: Dasgupta (with Bhandarkar,
  Grierson) places him **1197–1276 CE** (lived 79 years), against the also-current traditional
  **1238–1317**.
- **Ontology** — reality is twofold: the **independent** (*svatantra*) = God = **Viṣṇu** (Nārāyaṇa /
  Vāsudeva), and the **dependent** (*asvatantra*) = everything else. Madhva counts twenty substances and,
  like the Vaiśeṣikas, a list of categories (*padārtha*).
- **The five differences** ([[panchabheda|pañca-bheda]]) — the eternal, real differences between (1) God
  and souls, (2) God and matter, (3) soul and soul, (4) soul and matter, (5) matter and matter (Vol. IV,
  "Defence of Pluralism (Bheda)").
- **Three classes of souls** — those **fit for emancipation** (*mukti-yogya*: gods, sages), those bound to
  the transmigratory cycle, and the demonic/lowest, **destined eternally for hell** (*tamo-yogya*) —
  Madhva's near-unique **doctrine of eternal damnation**, singular in Indian thought.
- **The dialectical school and the dualist–monist controversy** — Jaya-tīrtha (*Tattva-prakāśikā*,
  *Nyāya-sudhā*) and **Vyāsa-tīrtha** systematized Dvaita logic; Vyāsa-tīrtha's attack on Advaita
  (*Nyāyāmṛta*) drew the great Advaitin reply, **Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's *Advaita-siddhi*** — recorded
  here without adjudication (Contradiction Protocol).

## Inside Chapter XXVI: Madhva's Brahma-Sūtra Interpretation, Adhikaraṇa by Adhikaraṇa

Dasgupta's Ch. XXVI does not merely *name* Madhva's [[brahma-sutras|*Brahma-Sūtra-bhāṣya*]]; it traces
his reading **sūtra by sūtra against Śaṅkara's**, working through the Dvaita commentarial chain —
Madhva's own *Bhāṣya* and *Anuvyākhyāna*, Jaya-tīrtha's *Tattva-prakāśikā* and *Nyāya-sudhā*, and
Vyāsa-tīrtha's *Tātparya-candrikā*. The texture of the contest, on genuinely-read material:

**BS I.1.1 — *athāto brahma-jijñāsā* ("now therefore the inquiry into Brahman").** Where Śaṅkara reads
*atha* ("now") as *after* acquiring the sixfold moral discipline, and treats Brahman as the ever-self-
revealed Self that needs inquiry only because views of the Self diverge, **Madhva re-keys every word
theistically**: *atha* is an auspicious particle and a *name of Nārāyaṇa*; *atas* ("therefore") means
"through the grace of Viṣṇu" (the letter *a* signifying Viṣṇu); *Brahman* means "the great Lord Viṣṇu,"
the one of **fullness of qualities** (*guṇa-pūrti*), which by its very root sense cannot denote the
*imperfect* (*apūrṇa*) soul; and *jijñāsā* means **reasoned argumentation** (*manana*), not Śaṅkara's
"desire to know." Liberation comes only by the Lord's highest grace — knowledge *pleases* God, who then
removes the (real) bondage — so the order is the inverse of Śaṅkara's "knowledge directly dispels
ignorance." Madhva also drops Śaṅkara's prerequisite of *nityānitya-vastu-viveka*, while still honoring
the sūtras' bar on Brahma-inquiry for Śūdras.

**The reality of bondage (the anti-*ajñāna* polemic).** The chapter's longest stretch is Madhva's case,
via the *Anuvyākhyāna* and *Nyāya-sudhā*, that **bondage is real** because it is directly testified by the
witness-self (*sākṣin*): if sorrow were false there would have to be a *pramāṇa* establishing its
falsity, and any such proof would itself install duality. Śaṅkara's *ajñāna* (nescience) is attacked as
incoherent — a self-revealed Brahman has nothing for *ajñāna* to hide, and the machinery collapses into
either a vicious circle (*anyonyāśraya*) or a vicious infinite (*anavasthā*). This is the Dvaita
counter to [[adhyasa|adhyāsa]]/superimposition: the not-Self is *really existent* and merely
*misattributed* to the self, not illusory.

**Against the "indefinable" (*anirvacanīya*).** Through Jaya-tīrtha (drawing on Śrīharṣa's
*Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍa-khādya*), Madhva refuses the Advaita status of the world as "different-from-both-existent-
and-non-existent" (*sad-asad-vilakṣaṇa*): since the *non-existent can be known* (the conch-shell-silver is
simply non-existent — a case of *anyathā-khyāti*, not of an indefinable third category), the world need
not be called indefinable. Existence and non-existence exhaust the options; there is **no middle term**.

**BS I.1.2 — *janmādy asya yataḥ* ("that from which the origin, etc., of this world").** Śaṅkara reads a
threefold "origin, maintenance, dissolution"; **Madhva expands it to *eight*** divine functions —
creation, maintenance, destruction, *control* (*niyama*), *knowledge*, *ignorance*, *bondage*, and
*release* — all dependent on Viṣṇu (quoting the *Skanda-purāṇa*). This drives the chapter's sharpest
*metaphysical* dispute, over the kind of definition (*lakṣaṇa*) at work: the Śaṅkarites (Prakāśātman)
call causality only an **accidental** mark (*taṭastha-lakṣaṇa*) of a Brahman whose *essence* is contentless
bliss-intelligence; Madhva and Vyāsa-tīrtha answer that **Brahman's qualities are essential and infinite**
(*ananta-guṇa*), so causality is no less essential than *ānanda* — and that a truly attribute-less
(*nirguṇa*) Brahman could not be *defined at all*, since every definition proceeds by distinctive
qualities. To explain how one consciousness can bear many attributes (truth, knowledge, infinitude)
without plurality fracturing its unity, Madhva invokes **_viśeṣa_** — a "particularizing" principle
internal to each thing (his replacement for the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *samavāya*, which the Brahma-Sūtras
reject), of which a thing has as many as it has qualities, without vicious regress. On the great
identity-texts *tat tvam asi* and *satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma*, Madhva's lever is semantic: since they
**admit a direct (*mukhyārtha*) reading on the basis of difference**, it is illegitimate to force the
indirect, figurative (*lakṣaṇā*) non-difference meaning the Advaitin needs.

**BS I.1.4 — *tat tu samanvayāt* (the *samanvaya* / "harmonious purport").** This is Madhva's master key:
he reads it to mean that **all scripture agrees in pointing to Viṣṇu**, and indeed that *every word* of
the Upaniṣads ultimately denotes Viṣṇu. "Scripture" (*śāstra*) is restricted to the four Vedas (with the
Pañcarātra, Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa) and **pointedly excludes the Śaiva Āgamas, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, and
Pāśupata** texts — so the cause of the world is Viṣṇu, never Śiva.

**The general pattern (I.1.5–I.4).** Across the remaining adhikaraṇas the engine is monotonous and
revealing: for almost every contested term — *ākāśa*, *prāṇa*, *jyotiḥ*, *sat*, *ānandamaya*,
*vaiśvānara*, *akṣara*, *avyakta* — Śaṅkara concludes "this means Brahman/Īśvara (not *prakṛti* or
*jīva*)," and **Madhva concludes "this means *Viṣṇu* specifically,"** frequently anchoring the same point
in *entirely different* Upaniṣadic passages. This is Madhva's *vidvad-rūḍhi*: among the learned, "Brahman"
*always* means Viṣṇu, overriding the ordinary convention by which it might mean *jīva*. Recurrent
throughout is the polemic that the ***māyāvādins* are more dangerous than the Buddhists** — the Buddhist
attacks the Vedas from outside, but the Advaitin guts them from within (Śaṅkara, Madhva charges, lacked a
*guru-sampradāya* of true Veda-knowers) — and a striking **philological** observation by Dasgupta: Madhva
repeatedly *re-divides* the sūtras, draws different adhikaraṇa boundaries, and in places even *adds* a
sūtra absent from Śaṅkara's recension (e.g. splitting I.4.5; an extra sūtra in II.1).
