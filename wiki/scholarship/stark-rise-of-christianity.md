# Stark, *The Rise of Christianity* (1996)

## Bibliographic Note

Rodney Stark, *The Rise of Christianity: A Sociologist Reconsiders History* (Princeton University Press, 1996). Stark is Professor of Sociology and Comparative Religion at the University of Washington. The book originated from journal articles submitted from 1985 onward; each chapter was substantially a previously published essay revised for the book.

## Source Type and Method

Scholarship — sociology of religion applied to early Christian history. Stark describes himself as "a sociologist who sometimes works with historical materials." He self-consciously imports formal social scientific tools — rational choice theory (Iannaccone), social network theory (Lofland-Stark), dynamic population models, religious economies framework, social epidemiology, quantitative urban analysis — into the study of early Christianity. The book is explicitly non-theological; Stark neither affirms nor denies Christian faith claims, but insists that social explanations of Christian growth do not undermine the legitimacy of religious experience.

**Methodological signature**: "The arithmetic of the possible and the plausible." Stark uses known endpoints (estimated 1,000 Christians c.40 CE; 5–7.5 million c.300 CE) to derive the minimum growth rate needed — 40% per decade / 3.42% per year — and then tests that rate against independent historical and archaeological data, finding remarkable convergence. This discipline is used throughout to constrain historical speculation.

## Central Thesis

Christianity grew not through miraculous mass conversions or the political favor of Constantine, but through normal social processes operating over three centuries: interpersonal network conversion, superior nursing care during epidemics, favorable sex ratios from prohibiting infanticide, high organizational commitment generated by sacrifice and stigma, and an exclusive organizational model that outcompeted the non-exclusive client cults of paganism. Behind these social mechanisms stood theological distinctives — especially the command to love strangers and enemies — that generated behaviors paganism could not match.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Conversion and Christian Growth

At 40% per decade, Christianity reaches 6–7 million by 300 CE — exactly what historians estimate. No miraculous conversion rates required. The Mormon Church has maintained a nearly identical growth rate for 150 years. Key argument: conversion is driven by **social network attachments**, not ideological persuasion. The **Lofland-Stark model** (from 1965 field observation of the first American Moonies) shows that people convert when interpersonal ties to existing members outbalance ties to outsiders. Doctrinal appeal is secondary; people adopt theology *after* they join, not before. This means Christianity must have grown by maintaining open social networks — staying connected to the unchurched.

### Chapter 2: The Class Basis of Early Christianity

Refutes the **proletarian thesis** (Marx, Engels, Troeltsch, Kautsky, Deissmann) that Christianity was a movement of the poor and dispossessed. Christianity was a **cult movement**, not a sect. Cult movements — new religions rather than schisms from existing ones — consistently overrecruit from the more privileged because: (a) they require mastering new ideas (education helps), (b) they appeal to those whose conventional religion has lost credibility (the educated notice erosion first), (c) they are too "deviant" for the poor, who cannot afford the social cost. Data on modern cult movements (Mormons, Christian Scientists, Moonies, Hare Krishna) confirm this. Historical evidence (Judge, Meeks, Theissen) converges: early Christianity was middle- and upper-middle-class, not proletarian.

### Chapter 3: The Mission to the Jews

Contra the received wisdom, the Jewish mission was **not a failure**. Stark makes three moves:

1. **The 19th-century parallel**: Emancipated European Jews in the 1800s faced the same social marginality as Hellenized diaspora Jews in the 1st-3rd centuries CE. Reform Judaism was their solution — stripping ethnicity from theology. Christianity offered Hellenized Jews the same resolution: Jewish theological content without the ethnic burden of the Law.
2. **Cultural continuity**: Christianity offered twice as much cultural continuity to Hellenized Jews as to Gentiles (both Jewish and Hellenic dimensions). Nock's principle: people adopt religions that maximize retained heritage.
3. **Network argument**: The earliest missionaries were themselves Hellenized Jews going to communities they had prior connections with. Paul is paradigmatic.

Stark argues Jewish conversion continued in substantial numbers into the 4th and possibly 5th century. The Marcion affair confirms this: Marcion's anti-Jewish solution was *rejected*, which implies the mid-2nd-century church was still heavily Jewish in membership and sympathy. John Chrysostom's anti-Jewish polemics in the late 4th century reflect not "renewed conversion to Judaism" but ongoing Jewish Christianity being consolidated into catholic Christianity.

### Chapter 4: Epidemics, Networks, and Conversion

Two catastrophic epidemics struck the empire: the **Antonine/Galenic Plague** (165–180 CE, 25–33% mortality) and the **Plague of Cyprian** (251–260 CE, comparable or worse). Three theses:

1. **Theological credibility**: Pagan religion and Stoic philosophy had no coherent theodicy for mass death. Christianity offered explanation (divine pedagogy, martyr-glorification, eschatological hope) and specific behavioral prescriptions.
2. **Differential mortality from nursing**: Christian communities nursed the sick; pagans fled. Using McNeill's estimate that basic nursing cuts mortality by two-thirds, Stark calculates that Christian survival rates would have been substantially higher, producing a visible "miracle" and increasing Christian proportions even without conversion.
3. **Network restructuring**: Differential mortality and flight by pagans destroyed pagan social networks while preserving Christian ones. Pagans who survived now had proportionally more Christian than pagan contacts — structural pressure toward conversion. Julian's 4th-century complaint that paganism needed to match Christian charity confirms that pagan nursing efforts were systematically inferior.

### Chapter 5: Role of Women in Christian Growth

**Sex ratio argument**: Greco-Roman world had 131+ males per 100 females due to female infanticide and abortion mortality. Christian communities had the reverse: ~60–64% female within 50 years, due to (a) prohibitions on infanticide/abortion and (b) women converting at ~2:1 over men (consistent with modern data on new religious movements).

Guttentag and Secord (1983): women's status tracks sex ratios. Where women are scarce, they are enclosed and subordinated. Where they are numerous, they gain power and options. Prediction confirmed: Christian women enjoyed older marriage age, freedom from remarriage pressure after widowhood, deaconess roles, and protection from the double standard.

**Exogamous marriage** — Christian women marrying pagan men — created a steady stream of secondary converts. Christianity's high commitment meant Christian wives rarely became pagan; pagan husbands often became Christian.

**Superior Christian fertility**: no infanticide, no abortion, rejection of sexual practices that wasted seed (withdrawal, anal sex). The Christian population grew not only by conversion but by a higher birthrate.

### Chapter 6: Christianizing the Urban Empire

Quantitative analysis of the 22 largest Greco-Roman cities (population ~100 CE). Dependent variable: Christianization score (church before 100 CE = 2; before 200 CE = 1; none = 0). Key correlates:

| Variable | Correlation with Christianization |
|---|---|
| Distance from Jerusalem | −0.74** |
| Synagogue presence | +0.69** |
| Romanization (Distance ratio) | −0.71** |
| City size | +0.32 |

Synagogues and low Romanization together explain **67% of the variance** in when a city had a church. Christianity followed Jewish communities. More Roman/Western cities were last to be evangelized.

**Gnosticism**: A separate regression analysis shows that when Christianization is held constant, synagogue presence has no independent effect on Gnostic presence. This supports Harnack's original view: Gnosticism was a Christian heresy, not a parallel Jewish offspring (contra Pearson's "Jewish heresy" thesis).

### Chapter 7: Urban Chaos and Crisis: The Case of Antioch

A densely descriptive chapter: what was daily life in a Greco-Roman city actually like? Antioch (pop. ~150,000) had density of 195 people per acre (comparable to modern Bombay). No running water in most homes, no sewers, no soap, open ditches, chamber pots emptied from windows, smoke-filled tenements, disease, chronic filth, crime among rootless newcomers, 18 ethnic quarters in constant tension. Over 600 years, 41 major catastrophes (8 devastating earthquakes, 4 fires, 3 major epidemics, 5 famines, 11 military seizures).

Christianity was a **revitalization movement** that specifically solved urban disorder problems:
- Charity and community for the homeless and impoverished
- Immediate belonging for newcomers/strangers
- Extended family for orphans and widows
- Cross-ethnic solidarity in a fractured city
- Nursing infrastructure during epidemics and crises

### Chapter 8: Martyrs as Rational Choice

Total Christian martyrs: "hundreds, not thousands" (W.H.C. Frend). Roman persecution was haphazard and targeted leaders, not rank-and-file. The social drama of martyrdom:

Using Iannaccone's rational choice / sacrifice-and-stigma model:
- **Free-rider problem**: collective goods are exploited by those who benefit without contributing. Strict sacrifice requirements screen out free riders and raise average commitment.
- **Sacrifice and stigma**: high costs → higher average commitment → more collective goods → better "bargain" for committed members.
- **Martyrs as credibility proof**: those who die for their faith set the maximum possible value on the compensators their religion offers. No more credible witness is possible.

The case of **Ignatius**: month-long triumphal procession from Antioch to Rome; churches came out to meet him; he wrote seven letters from the road; he begged the Roman church not to save him. He was not a masochist but a man reaching for permanent glory, assured of sainthood, with immense social capital accruing in real time.

The martyrs of the 60s (James, Paul, Peter) solved the crisis of confidence from the delayed Parousia and the tiny size of the movement. Their deaths demonstrated the movement's worth to its members precisely when doubt was most acute.

### Chapter 9: Opportunity and Organization

**Religious economy framework**: The Greco-Roman religious market was relatively unregulated → extensive pluralism. But "excessive pluralism" by the 1st century had created a glutted market of nonexclusive, "client cult" firms with low commitment and no community.

**Exclusive vs. nonexclusive firms**:
- Paganism: nonexclusive; members construct religious portfolios (multiple cults simultaneously); no collective production; no community; low commitment; no charity infrastructure
- Christianity/Judaism: exclusive; demands total commitment; collective production of religious goods; genuine community and belonging; charity; higher credibility compensators

Paganism was already in decline before Christianity: Egyptian temple construction declining from the 1st century CE onward (Bagnall); Amesysia (Isis festival) last held 257 CE; widespread irreverence (blasphemous graffiti at Pompeii). The spread of the Isis cult correlated with Christian expansion (r = +0.67) — both tracked market openings left by declining traditional paganism.

**Organizational superiority**: Christianity was a mass movement funded by small voluntary donations — not dependent on elite patronage like paganism. The Romans persecuted only leaders, misunderstanding Christianity as a top-down organization (like paganism). It was not: "even a shattered remnant of survivors" could reconstitute the movement (Dodds). Person-to-person evangelism was its primary growth mechanism.

### Chapter 10: A Brief Reflection on Virtue

Doctrine mattered. The phrase "For God so loved the world" was genuinely novel. Classical philosophers (Aristotle, Plato) regarded mercy and pity as defects of character. Christianity made mercy the primary virtue. Demanding love for strangers — "all those who in every place call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" — was revolutionary.

Christianity's "revitalization" of the Roman world:
- Stripped religion of ethnicity → cross-ethnic solidarity
- Elevated the status of women
- Mitigated class differences
- Condemned gladiatorial spectacle and casual cruelty
- Gave converts, in Stark's phrase, "their humanity"
