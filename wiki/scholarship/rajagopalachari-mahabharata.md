# Mahabharata — C. Rajagopalachari

C. Rajagopalachari's prose retelling of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], first written in Tamil for the magazine *Kalki* (c. 1943) and translated into English by 1950. Published as the inaugural volume of Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's "Book University" series. The work covers all 106 episodes of the epic narrative from Bhishma's ancestry through Yudhishthira's ascent to heaven.

**Source type**: Popular adaptation/retelling — not a translation from the Sanskrit text; Rajagopalachari condenses, paraphrases, and selects from the full Mahābhārata (approximately 75,000 verses) into a prose narrative of ~107 episodes, stripping what he calls "accretions" to reveal the "noble epic" underneath.

## Rajagopalachari's Approach

Rajagopalachari states explicitly (Preface to 2nd edition) that he regards the Mahābhārata as layered: a noble epic core surrounded by centuries of interpolated material — "historical, geographical, legendary, political, theological and philosophical, of nearly thirty centuries" — added when interpolation into a recognized classic "corresponded to inclusion in the national library." His retelling strips these accretions to foreground the dharmic narrative and the characters' moral struggles.

His stated hermeneutical emphasis:
- **Dharma as the central thread**: the Mahābhārata teaches "that hatred breeds hatred, that covetousness and violence lead inevitably to ruin, that the only real conquest is in the battle against one's lower nature"
- **The Bhagavad Gita as the epic's climax** ("its core... the noblest of scriptures and the grandest of sagas")
- **Character study**: Bhishma ("the perfect knight"), Karna ("vain but chivalrous"), Duryodhana ("perverse pride redeemed by great courage in adversity"), Draupadi ("most unfortunate of queens"), Krishna ("most energetic of men, whose divinity scintillates through a cloud of very human characteristics")
- **Cultural continuity**: treats the Mahābhārata as continuous with modern Indian civilization and national character — not merely ancient mythology but "the stuff of the young philosophy of life" formed at the mother's knee

**Not an academic work**: Rajagopalachari makes no claim to philological or critical scholarship. The retelling is avowedly devotional and pedagogical in intent. Attribution to the Mahābhārata's actual content must be checked against the Sanskrit text; Rajagopalachari sometimes condenses, emphasizes, or frames episodes in ways that reflect his own moral and political outlook (Gandhi's ally; Indian nationalist; advocate of dharmic ethics).
