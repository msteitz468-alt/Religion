# Jude's Use of 1 Enoch and the Canon Question

The [[jude|Letter of Jude]] does something no other New Testament book does so overtly: it **quotes a
non-canonical Jewish work as prophecy**. Jude 14–15 introduces 1 Enoch 1.9 with "Enoch, the seventh
from Adam, **prophesied**…"; Jude 9 alludes to the **Assumption (Testament) of Moses** (the archangel
Michael disputing with the devil over Moses' body); and Jude 6 draws on the Enochic angelic-fall
tradition (1 Enoch 6–12). This was the *sole* basis of ancient doubts about Jude's canonicity, and it
raises a sharp question for any doctrine of inspiration: what does it mean that an inspired,
canonical book treats an *un*-canonical book as authoritative revelation? [[gene-green|Gene L.
Green]] (BECNT, 2008) surveys the options and stakes out a position.

## The Data

- **Jude 14–15** quotes **1 Enoch 1.9** verbatim-enough to be a citation, prefaced like an OT
  prophecy and crediting it to "Enoch, the seventh from Adam" — a phrase that itself underscores the
  figure's authority.
- **Jude 9** retells, as known fact, the dispute of **Michael and the devil over Moses' body**, drawn
  from the **Assumption/Testament of Moses** (a work known well enough that Jude need only allude to
  it).
- **Jude 6** presupposes the Enochic interpretation of Genesis 6:1–4 (the fall of the Watchers).

## The Positions

**1. Therefore 1 Enoch is canonical (Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria).** Some early writers
reasoned that if Jude quotes Enoch as Scripture, Enoch *is* Scripture. Tertullian defended 1 Enoch's
survival (preserved by Noah, or renewed by the Spirit) and concluded, "Enoch possesses a testimony in
the apostle Jude." This view survives today only in the **Ethiopian Orthodox** canon (which includes
1 Enoch) and among the Latter-day Saints.

**2. Therefore Jude is suspect (some ancients).** The mirror-image reaction: if Jude leans on
apocryphal books, perhaps Jude does not belong in the canon. Jerome reports that "because it quotes
from the apocryphal book of Enoch it is rejected by many"; the Syrian church was slow to receive it.
Jerome and Didymus nonetheless defended Jude (by its antiquity and use).

**3. Accept Jude but not its sources — the dominant solution (Augustine).** Augustine held that there
is "some truth" in the apocrypha but that the books "have no canonical authority because of the many
untruths they contain"; he accepted that "Enoch… wrote a number of things by divine inspiration,
since the apostle Jude says so," while denying canonicity to the book of Enoch. This both/and —
affirming the truth of what Jude quotes without endorsing the whole book — has held the field. Green
notes 2 Peter may be the **earliest witness** to this strategy: where Peter reuses Jude, he
**drops** Jude's explicit apocryphal citations (no Enoch quotation; the Michael–Moses dispute
softened, 2 Pet. 2:10–11).

**4. "All truth is God's truth" — Jude quotes incidental truth (modern apologetic).** As Paul quotes
pagan poets (Aratus, Epimenides, Menander) without endorsing them, Jude may quote a true line from
Enoch without investing the book with authority. *Green's objection*: Jude's quotation formula is
unlike Paul's. Jude introduces Enoch exactly as he introduces OT prophecy, calls him "the seventh
from Adam," and lines Enoch up *alongside Torah (vv. 5–7, 11) and apostolic prophecy (vv. 17–18)* as
authoritative testimony to the heretics' sin and doom. Enoch and the apostles are the only sources
Jude *quotes* (the rest he summarizes). So this is stronger than a Pauline incidental citation.

**5. Jude quotes Enoch because his *opponents* esteemed it (J. Daryl Charles).** On this reading,
"even *your* Enoch" condemns the false teachers on their own authority. *Green's objection*: Jude
writes to *the church*, not to the opponents, and gives no hint the authority is merely *ad hominem*;
Charles himself concedes the reading is conjectural and is driven by the theological worry of
"compromising Jude's view of an authoritative OT canon."

**6. 1 Enoch preserves an authentic pre-flood tradition.** A popular version of Augustine: the
specific prophecy Jude quotes really goes back to the historical Enoch, even if the book as a whole
does not. *Green's objection*: the survival of a genuine antediluvian oracle to the Hellenistic-era
compilation is historically remote, it does not account for the Assumption of Moses (v. 9), and
defending it by appeal to Jude is circular.

## Green's Position

Green argues, against the apologetic moves (4–6), that **Jude genuinely treats 1 Enoch (and the
Assumption of Moses) as authoritative, predictive revelation** — the most natural reading of his
quotation formula. But he stresses two mitigating points:
1. Jude's use is **judicious and limited**: he eschews the fanciful material and draws only what is
   tied to **canonical bedrock** — the angelic fall to Gen. 6:1–4; the Enoch oracle to Deut. 33:2
   (the Lord coming with myriads of holy ones); the Moses-body dispute to the obscure burial notice
   of Deut. 34:5–6 and the rebuke-formula of Zech. 3:1–2. "Jude's use of apocryphal texts is closer
   to canonical bedrock than is sometimes acknowledged."
2. The deepest tension — between a theology of inspiration and the historical facts of Jude's sources
   — is not fully soluble. Green commends **Augustine's humility**, holding both Jude's inspiration
   and the reality of its apocryphal sources, and confessing (with Augustine) that something here
   remains "hidden." One may side with historical investigation ("Jude was wrong about his source")
   or with Jude's authority and "work the historical details through as best one can."

## Why It Matters

The question sits exactly where a high doctrine of Scripture meets historical-critical data, and it
is the reason Jude has been marginalized ("the most neglected NT book"). It also illuminates the
**fluid state of "Scripture"** in first-century Judaism (1 Enoch was esteemed at Qumran, in Jubilees,
and by some church fathers) and the early church's process of canonical discernment — accepting Jude
while ultimately excluding its sources.
