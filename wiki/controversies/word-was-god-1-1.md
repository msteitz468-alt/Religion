# John 1:1c — "The Word Was God"

The third clause of John 1:1 — *kai theos ēn ho logos* ("and God was the Word," or "and the Word was God") — is the most-discussed single verse in Johannine scholarship and one of the most contested in the history of Christian theology. It bears on the divinity of Christ, the doctrine of the Trinity, and the question of whether the Gospel's Christology is "high" from the outset.

## The Greek

The clause reads: *kai theos ēn ho logos*

Word-for-word: "and God was the Word"

But Greek word order carries different emphasis from English. *Ho logos* ("the Word") has the definite article and is the *subject*; *theos* ("God") is the *predicate noun* and stands **first** in the clause (before the verb), without the definite article. The standard English rendering follows grammatical analysis, not word order: "the Word was God."

## The Problem: The Article

The Greek distinguishes between *ho theos* (the God, with definite article) and *theos* (God/god, without article — anarthrous). In the same verse:
- v. 1b: *pros ton theon* — "with God (*ho theos*)" — definite article present
- v. 1c: *theos ēn ho logos* — "the Word was God (*theos*)" — no article

This grammatical difference has generated three main interpretations.

## The Positions

### 1. "The Word Was God" — Trinitarian Orthodoxy

The majority reading in Christian tradition: the anarthrous *theos* does not weaken the claim but is simply a feature of Greek grammar when a predicate noun precedes the verb — it does not become indefinite ("a god") merely by lacking the article. The Word shares in the full divine nature. "The Word was God" in the same sense that "God is love" (1 John 4:8) — "love" lacks the article but no one thinks it means "a kind of love."

Supported by: the narrative logic of the Gospel (8:58 "Before Abraham was, I am"; 20:28 "My Lord and my God!"); the patristic tradition (Irenaeus, Athanasius, Nicaea); Colwell's Rule (E. C. Colwell, 1933: a definite predicate noun before the verb regularly lacks the article).

### 2. "The Word Was a God" — Indefinite (Jehovah's Witnesses)

The Jehovah's Witnesses' *New World Translation* renders: "the Word was a god." Argument: the absence of the article makes *theos* indefinite, meaning a subordinate or lesser divine being. This supports their position that Jesus is a created being, the "firstborn of all creation" (using Col 1:15), but not co-equal with Almighty God.

**Widely rejected** by NT scholars: Greek grammar does not mechanically equate anarthrous nouns with indefinite nouns. Philip Harner (1973) demonstrated from a study of predicate nouns in John's Gospel that anarthrous predicate nouns before the verb are typically *qualitative*, not indefinite — they describe the nature or character of the subject, not whether it is one of a class. Moreover, "a god" would be polytheism, incompatible with the monotheism the Gospel presupposes.

### 3. "The Word Was Divine" — Adjectival (Moffatt)

James Moffatt (1922 translation) rendered: "the Logos was divine." The argument is that without the article, *theos* functions as an attributive adjective rather than a noun. This softens the identification: not "God" fully, but "of divine nature" or "divine in character."

**[[j-ramsey-michaels|Michaels]]'s critique**: *Theos* is not an adjective; "divine" is. To say "the Word was divine" is not the same as "the Word was God," just as "God is light" says more than "God is luminous." The move to adjectival rendering loses the force without gaining precision.

### 4. "The Word Was Deity" — Abstract Noun

Some translators use "deity" to capture the abstract quality of the anarthrous *theos*: the Word participated in divine being or deity. This avoids both the full equation with the personal God and the indefinite article problem.

**Michaels's critique**: "the Word was deity" sounds too abstract and loses the simplicity of "the Word was God" without corresponding gain in accuracy.

### 5. Qualitative Predicate — Harner and Michaels

Philip Harner's 1973 JBL article ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns") is the most technically influential treatment. Harner argued that an anarthrous predicate noun before the verb in a clause where the subject has the article is typically **qualitative**: it describes the nature or attributes of the subject, not its identity with a class. Applied to 1:1c: the Word shares in the *nature* of God — "what God is, the Word also is." The Word has all the attributes of divinity, but the clause does not equate the two persons ("God is the Word" would be modalism).

**Michaels's reading** follows this line: while the Word is God, God is more than just the Word. The two are not interchangeable. "The word was God" (not "God was the Word"). This avoids both the modalist reading (collapsing Father and Son) and the Jehovah's Witness reading (the Word as a lesser divine being). It is consistent with v. 1b's distinction: the Word was "with" God (distinct), yet also "was" God (sharing divine nature).

## The Narrative Context

The controversy about 1:1c does not occur in isolation. The Gospel's conclusion provides a close parallel: Thomas's confession "My Lord and my God!" (*ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou*, 20:28) — where *theos* has the article — is Jesus' highest honorific in the narrative. The Gospel is framed between the theological affirmation of 1:1c and the narrative confession of 20:28.

## Resolution Status

**Tradition-specific.** NT scholarship is near-unanimous in rejecting the Jehovah's Witnesses' "a god" reading on grammatical grounds. Among the remaining options, the qualitative/orthodox reading commands the widest scholarly support. The theological question of how to relate 1:1c to trinitarian doctrine (distinguishing the Father and Son while affirming the Son's divinity) has occupied Christian theology since the second century and remains definitionally church-defining.
