# Coercion, Toleration, and Punishment of Unbelievers (Aquinas, ST II-II)

In the [[summa-theologica|*Summa*]] II-II Q10–12, [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] sets out a graded
position on religious coercion that turns on a single distinction: **whether a person has ever
received the Christian faith.** The conclusions are historically charged and are recorded here
without adjudication, with the tradition context of each.

## The governing distinction

The Church's coercive jurisdiction extends only to those **subject to her** — the baptized.
Citing 1 Cor. 5:12 ("What have I to do to judge them that are without?"), Aquinas holds that
those who have **never received** the faith — **Jews, pagans, and other unbelievers** — are
"without" and may not be punished *for their unbelief* by the Church (Q10, A8; Q12, A2). Those
who have **received and then abandoned or corrupted** the faith — **apostates and heretics** —
are subject to her and may be coerced.

## The positions

1. **No compulsion to believe (Q10, A8).** "Unbelievers… are by no means to be compelled to the
   faith, in order that they may believe, because to believe depends on the will." Faith requires
   free assent. Unbelievers may, however, be **restrained** from hindering the faith (blasphemy,
   evil persuasion, persecution).

2. **Toleration of unbelievers' rites (Q10, A11).** The rites of unbelievers may be **tolerated**
   for prudential reasons — to avoid a greater evil or to preserve a good. **Jewish rites** are
   specially tolerable because, foreshadowing the faith, they bear unwilling witness to it ("the
   enemies bear testimony"). Idolatrous rites, profiting nothing, are tolerated only to avoid
   scandal or discord.

3. **Children of Jews not to be baptized against parental will (Q10, A12).** Twofold reason: the
   **danger to faith** (a child might later be turned away) and **natural justice** — a child
   before the use of reason is under the parents' care "as a kind of spiritual womb." This became a
   standing Catholic principle.

4. **Heretics to be put to death (Q11, A3).** A heretic deserves "not only to be separated from the
   Church by excommunication, but also to be severed from the world by death," for corrupting the
   faith (which quickens the soul) is graver than forging money (which the secular power punishes
   with death). On the Church's side, **mercy admonishes twice** before she "delivers him to the
   secular tribunal to be exterminated thereby from the world by death."

5. **Relapsed heretics (Q11, A4).** Returning heretics are always admitted to **Penance** (the way
   of salvation is never closed); but on a **second relapse** they are received to penance yet "not
   delivered from the pain of death," because relapse proves inconstancy and the good of the many
   outweighs the good of one.

6. **Apostasy dissolves political allegiance (Q12, A2).** Unbelief in itself does not void
   dominion; but once **excommunication** is passed on a ruler for apostasy, "his subjects are *ipso
   facto* absolved from his authority." Worship "at the tomb of Mahomet" and circumcision are named
   as external signs of apostasy from the faith (Q12, A1).

## Tradition context and reception

- For **[[catholicism|Catholicism]]**, these articles articulated the medieval theology behind
  inquisitorial procedure and the canon-law treatment of heresy; the no-forced-baptism principle
  (3) and the no-jurisdiction-over-Jews principle (1–2) cut the other way, against forced
  conversion, and were repeatedly invoked.
- For **[[judaism|Judaism]]** and **[[islam|Islam]]** these passages are part of the record of
  Christendom's legal posture toward religious minorities; (4) in particular bears on the later
  forced-baptism disputes.
- Modern Catholic teaching on **religious liberty** (Vatican II, *Dignitatis Humanae*) stands in
  evident tension with position (4) on heretics; the relation of the *Summa*'s coercion theology to
  later magisterial teaching is itself contested.
