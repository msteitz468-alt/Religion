# Scientology — Religion, Commercial Enterprise, or Cult?

The central controversy about [[scientology|Scientology]] is **what it is** — and the answer has differed by
country, court, decade, and observer. Hugh B. Urban (*The Church of Scientology*, Princeton 2011) argues
that the more profound question is not "Is it a religion?" but rather **"Who gets to decide, and what's at
stake in calling something religion?"** Scientology is his test case for the historically contingent,
power-laden definition of religion itself.

## The Historical Stakes

What the "religion" label confers or denies:
- **Tax exemption** (US IRS, granted 1993 after 26-year battle)
- Protection from **FDA scrutiny** of the E-meter and Dianetics as medical devices
- **Copyright/trade-secret protection** for the confidential OT materials
- **First Amendment free-exercise** protection against government investigation
- **International recognition** and US State Department advocacy abroad (Germany, France)

The same label also opens Scientology to scrutiny — critics can target the claim of religious status and undermine it. The more Scientology insists it is a religion, the more others contest that claim.

## Positions

### 1. Bona Fide Religion

Scientology presents itself as a religion with scripture ("Source"), clergy (the [[sea-org|Sea Org]]), ordained ministers, sacraments (auditing as confessional), and a soteriology (freeing the [[thetan|thetan]]). The Church's scholarly case:

- **Bryan S. Wilson** (University of Oxford sociologist of religion) applied a 20-point inventory of religious features and concluded: "Scientology is a bona fide religion and should be considered as such" (in *Scientology: Theology and Practice of a Contemporary Religion*, 1998, commissioned by the Church)
- **US IRS granted tax-exempt status** to all Scientology entities on October 1, 1993, after the landmark $12.5 million settlement and formal recognition as a church (IRS's 13-point criteria satisfied)
- **US State Department** cited Germany for religious discrimination against Scientologists (1993 onward)
- Scholars of new religions (J. Gordon Melton, James R. Lewis) treat it as a legitimate NRM

### 2. Commercial Enterprise / "Religion Angle" as Strategy

The documentary evidence is explicit. Hubbard's **April 10, 1953 letter to Helen O'Brien**: "Perhaps we could better our income by . . . the creation of a religious fellowship. . . . It is not necessary to prove any specific doctrine." A 1962 policy letter states: "Scientology 1970 is being planned on a religious organization basis throughout the world. It is entirely a matter for accountants and solicitors." March 1966: transferred HASI assets to CSC to avoid scrutiny; "Parliaments don't attack religions."

**France** convicted Scientology of organized fraud (October 2009). **Germany** regards it as antidemocratic/totalitarian and has repeatedly moved to ban it. **UK and Ireland** have refused religious recognition. A Victoria, Australia Board of Enquiry (1965) concluded "Scientology is evil."

**Gerry Armstrong** (Hubbard's former personal archivist, now critic): "Scientology calls itself a 'religion' to obtain the benefits, privileges, protections and the benevolent public image that are conferred on religions." An ex-Scientologist quoted an alleged Hubbard statement: "RELIGION IS THE BULLFIGHTER'S CAPE THAT AVOIDS THE GORE."

### 3. Harmful / Coercive Cult

Ex-members and journalists document:
- **Fair Game** policy (1965): enemies "may be deprived of property or injured by any means … tricked, sued, lied to, or destroyed"
- **Disconnection**: members required to sever contact with "suppressive persons" (critics, critical family)
- **Rehabilitation Project Force (RPF)**: punitive labor program
- **Operation Freakout**: framing journalist Paulette Cooper for terrorism
- **Operation Snow White**: systematic infiltration of US government agencies; Mary Sue Hubbard and Jane Kember convicted (1979)
- **Lisa McPherson** (died 1995): thromboembolism after 17-day "Introspection Rundown" at Flag Service Organization; charges dropped but remains a touchstone
- **Sea Org labor conditions**: billion-year contracts; poverty wages; children in the org

### 4. Multifaceted Transnational (Kent)

Canadian sociologist **Stephen Kent** argues that Scientology is not a religion but a **"multifaceted transnational"** of which religion is only one component: "Other components include political aspirations, business ventures, cultural productions, pseudo-medical practices, pseudo-psychiatric claims, and (among its most devoted members who have joined the Sea Organization), an alternative family structure." Kent also argues (against Wessinger/Dawson) that the RPF constitutes genuine brainwashing.

### 5. Self-Conscious Construction of Religion (Urban)

**Urban's own position** is distinctive: Scientology is "a self-conscious attempt to *make* a religion — a concerted effort to use explicitly religious sorts of discourse to describe, defend, define, and redefine itself." This may be only in keeping with Hubbard's broader "postmodern" view: if thetans can create their own universes, why not deliberately construct a new religion and argue it is more true than existing ones?

This position does *not* adjudicate whether the result is "really" a religion — it simply notes that Scientology is among the most self-aware and deliberate deployments of religious-identity construction in modern history.

## The Theoretical Frame (Urban)

Urban deploys three theoretical tools to reframe the question:

1. **Bruce Lincoln** (*Holy Terrors*): Religion is not a thing but a type of discourse — one that claims a special, **transcendent, suprahuman authority**. By this standard, Scientology clearly *claims* religious authority; whether the claim is legitimate is a further question.
2. **Talal Asad** (*Genealogies of Religion*): The category "religion" is historically produced, not neutral. Its current shape is the product of Protestant-Christian assumptions; the debate over Scientology is part of the ongoing renegotiation of that category.
3. **Jonathan Z. Smith**: Religion "can be defined, with greater or lesser success, more than fifty ways." There is no essence to be captured; there are only competing definitions in service of different interests.

Urban's conclusion: the "Is Scientology a religion?" debate is best understood as a **live contest over how religion will be defined and who will have the power to define it** — involving academics, courts, Congress, the IRS, foreign governments, journalists, anticult groups, and anonymous internet activists. The stakes are concrete: millions in tax revenues and constitutional protections. This contest will never be settled; it will only continue to be renegotiated.
