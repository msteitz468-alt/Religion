# The Ephesians Household Code (*Haustafel*, 5:21–6:9)

The passage 5:21–6:9 addresses three pairs within the Greco-Roman household: wives and husbands (5:22–33), children and parents (6:1–4), and slaves and masters (6:5–9). The pericope is one of the most contested in the Pauline corpus — generating debates about gender, slavery, the nature of Christian ethics, and the relationship between the church and its social environment. The term *Haustafel* ("household table") was introduced by Martin Luther; its form has Jewish parallels (Philo, *Hypothetica* 7.1–14; Josephus, *Apion* 2.190–219) and Stoic-Greco-Roman parallels (Aristotle, *Pol*. 1.2; Hierocles).

## The Form and Its Background

The *Haustafel* form addresses each party in a household pair in turn, giving instructions specific to each role. Aristotle describes the household as constituted by three relationships: husband/wife, father/children, master/slave (*Pol*. 1253b1–14). These same three pairs appear in Ephesians 5:22–6:9 and Colossians 3:18–4:1 — the closest parallels. The form was common in Hellenistic moral philosophy and Jewish apologetics, where writers (Josephus, Philo) used it to show that Jewish morality was continuous with Greco-Roman civic virtue.

Key structural observation: every party is addressed directly, including those with least social power (wives, children, slaves). In Stoic household codes, only the *paterfamilias* is addressed; inferiors are discussed third-personally. Ephesians' form, by addressing wives, children, and slaves directly as moral agents, is already a subtle departure from the ambient form.

## The Pivot Verse: Ephesians 5:21

The entire household code is governed by 5:21: "submitting to one another (*hypotassomenoi allēlois*) out of reverence for Christ." The participial phrase *hypotassomenoi allēlois* ("submitting to one another") concludes the Spirit-filled-life section (5:18–21) and introduces the household code. Whether 5:21 governs the entire *Haustafel* is the first major controversy:

**Position A (mutual submission governs all)**: 5:21 establishes mutual submission as the operating principle; the specific directives to wives (5:22), children (6:1), and slaves (6:5) are specific applications of this mutual principle within each pair. On this reading, even the husband's "headship" (5:23) is a form of service under the umbrella of mutual submission. This reading tends to flatten the asymmetry between the wife's submission (5:22) and the husband's love (5:25) — they become equivalents, two expressions of the same mutual orientation.

**Position B (5:21 introduces, not equalizes)**: 5:21 is a transitional verse that introduces the household code but does not remove the asymmetry within each pair. The verb *hypotassō* in 5:22 lacks its own verb — it carries over from 5:21 — but the *direction* of submission in each subsequent pair is one-directional: wives to husbands, children to parents, slaves to masters. The husbands are told to *love* (5:25), not to *submit*; the different verb signals a different relationship to the norm of 5:21. "Mutual submission" in 5:21 refers to the general posture of the Spirit-filled community, but the specific applications show the asymmetry that persists.

[[frank-thielman|Thielman]] takes a nuanced Position B: 5:21 applies to the community as a whole but 5:22–33 addresses a specific asymmetrical relationship within it. He emphasizes that the husband's role in 5:25–30 is so demanding (self-sacrificial love modeled on Christ's sacrifice) that it constitutes a radical reframing of what patriarchal headship looks like — but he does not collapse the asymmetry into pure mutuality.

## Wives and Husbands (5:22–33)

**5:22–24 (wives)**: "Wives, submit to your own husbands (*tois idiois andrasin*), as to the Lord. For the husband is the head (*kephalē*) of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior. Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands."

The scope word "in everything" (*en panti*) is maximally demanding. The *kephalē* (head) analogy is unambiguous: the husband's headship is compared to Christ's headship over the church, not to some limited functional role.

**The *kephalē* debate**: The Greek *kephalē* (head) has generated an extensive secondary literature:
- **Authority interpretation**: *Kephalē* = authority/leadership. The body-image in both Greek and Jewish usage implies that the head directs. This is the traditional reading and, [[frank-thielman|Thielman]] argues, the contextually demanded reading in 5:23 given the parallel to Christ as head of the church (1:22; 4:15) — where Christ's headship is clearly one of authority and direction.
- **Source interpretation**: Stephen Bedale (1954) proposed that *kephalē* in Greek literature often means "source" (as of a river) rather than "authority," and that Paul means "the husband is the source of the wife" (a reference to Gen 2:21–23, woman from man). This interpretation has been defended by Gordon Fee, Alan Johnson, and others; challenged comprehensively by Wayne Grudem (*Trinity Journal* 6:1 [1985]), who found no instances of *kephalē* meaning "source" in Greek literature outside of a few anatomical texts where the "head" of a river means its beginning.

The "source" reading is now widely regarded as lexically unsupported among scholars, though it retains advocates. For Thielman, the authority reading in 5:23 is the contextually and lexically required one.

**5:25–33 (husbands)**: The demand on husbands is three times longer than the instruction to wives. Husbands are to *love* (*agapaō*) their wives:
- As Christ loved the church and *gave himself up for her* (5:25) — self-sacrificial death as the model
- As they love their own bodies (5:28–29) — an argument from natural self-care applied to the wife
- As each one loves himself (5:33)

The comparison to Christ's self-giving is the ethical weight of the passage. Thielman reads this as a radical constraint on the husband's exercise of "headship": he may not use authority for self-benefit but only for the wife's sanctification (5:26–27) and nurture (5:29). The direction of power runs downward in service, not upward for self-promotion.

**The church-marriage typology (5:31–32)**: Citing Gen 2:24, Paul declares that the husband-wife relationship is a *mystērion* that "refers to Christ and the church" (5:32). This typological reading cuts both ways: (a) it elevates marriage as a sacramental-like mirror of the Christ-church relationship (significant for later Catholic theology), and (b) it provides the ground for the wife's submission (the church submits to Christ) and the husband's love (Christ loves and dies for the church). The typology generates the ethics.

## Slaves and Masters (6:5–9)

**6:5–8 (slaves)**: Slaves are to obey their masters "as you would Christ," with sincerity of heart, not eye-service. Obedience is rendered "as to the Lord, not to men" (6:7). The argument is christological: faithful work is done *coram Deo*, seen by God who will render just reward regardless of social status.

**6:9 (masters)**: Masters are to do the same (*ta auta*) — the same orientation of service to Christ — and to "give up threatening (*tēn apeilēn*), knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him." God shows no favoritism between slave and master.

**The slavery question**: Does 6:5–9 endorse slavery? This is the most historically consequential interpretive question of the passage, having been used in antebellum America to argue for the divine sanction of slavery. The modern consensus cuts several ways:
- The code does not endorse slavery as a divinely ordained institution; it gives practical instruction to those already embedded in the institution
- The code does not call for abolition; within the Greco-Roman social world, this would have been a social impossibility for small congregations
- The theological pressure applied to masters (6:9) — "no partiality with God" — subverts the master's claim to ultimate authority
- Galatians 3:28 ("neither slave nor free") and Philemon (Paul's appeal for Onesimus's freedom or full reception) indicate that Paul envisions the dissolution of the slave-free binary within the community of Christ
- The question of whether the Ephesian code is an accommodation that later Christianity should move beyond (trajectory hermeneutics; Webb, *Slaves, Women and Homosexuals*, 2001) or a principled norm for all times is the deepest interpretive divide

## The "Early Catholicism" Reading

Lincoln, Käsemann, and others read the household code as evidence of pseudonymity: it reflects the "settling down" of Christianity into the social structures of the Greco-Roman world, characteristic of second-generation Christianity. The absence of Paul's eschatological urgency (the time is short! — 1 Cor 7:29–31) and the embrace of conventional social hierarchy suggest a community that has made its peace with the empire. On this reading, the code is pastorally prudent but theologically regressive relative to Galatians 3:28 and 1 Corinthians 7.

[[frank-thielman|Thielman]] rejects this reading: (a) 1 Cor 7:21–24 also tells slaves to remain in their present state; social conservatism is not absent from the undisputed Paulines. (b) Col 3:18–4:1 has a virtually identical household code, and Colossians is either Pauline (on Thielman's view) or the prior pseudonymous letter; either way, the code is not an Ephesian innovation. (c) The Ephesian code's theological grounding (Christ-church typology; "no partiality with God") is more theologically sophisticated than a mere accommodation to convention. See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

## Evangelical Egalitarian Reading (McKnight / Colossians Perspective)

[[scot-mcknight|McKnight]] (NICNT Colossians, 2018) offers a significant evangelical-internal challenge to the complementarian reading — coming not from feminist hermeneutics but from within the conservative evangelical tradition while reaching egalitarian conclusions.

Key moves in McKnight's argument (argued from Col 3:18–4:1 but directly relevant to Eph 5:22–6:9):

1. **The husband is never told to lead.** In the Colossian code, the husband is instructed to *love* (*agapaō*, 3:19) and not to be harsh. McKnight: "My contention is that greater attention to 'love' will necessarily lead to both a more precise understanding of subordination and a greater focus on what is most needed." The husband's role is defined by love, not by leadership or governance.

2. **The wife's submission is grounded in Christoformity, not hierarchy.** "As is fitting in the Lord" (Col 3:18) — and analogously "as to the Lord" (Eph 5:22) — grounds the wife's conduct in the Christoform pattern of living, not in any ontological inferiority or creation-order authority claim. McKnight: "fitting in the Lord" describes Christoformity; the submission is an instance of cruciform/Christoform living, not of absorbing the Roman way of life.

3. **Complementarian readings are "more Aristotelian than Christian."** McKnight explicitly charges: "In much teaching around this theme today, one hears too much Aristotle and not enough gospel Christoformity." He quotes Aristotle's *Politics* ("the male is by nature superior and the female inferior; and the one rules, and the other is ruled") and argues that this — not the Pauline texts — is the actual operative logic in complementarian commentary.

4. **The husband's "love" should be defined through Song of Solomon, not management theory.** McKnight reads the husband's love through Song of Songs: covenantal presence, advocacy, and flourishing growth into Christlikeness — empowerment, not governance. "The husband who so loves his wife will not make demands, will not overpower, and will not violate the integrity of a wife."

5. **The code is subversive of the Roman honor system, not an endorsement of it.** Running through the code is a challenge to the Roman paterfamilias culture of honor, status, and power. What was "linear and nonreciprocal" (the Roman way) is being subverted. McKnight: "Superiority, power, and status have all been eradicated in Christoformity."

6. **Col 3:11's equality claim frames the household code.** "There is no Greek and Jew... slave, free; but Christ is all, and is in all" (3:11) is not undone by 3:18–4:1 but grounds it. The household regulations explore the meaning of Christoformity in a world where these distinctions still operationally exist, while the gospel has already declared them transcended.

This reading represents a significant evangelical-vs-evangelical disagreement. McKnight and Thielman are both within the NICNT/BECNT evangelical commentary tradition, and both defend Pauline authorship; their disagreement on the household code's hermeneutical import is not a difference between critical and conservative scholarship but a live intra-evangelical dispute. See [[scot-mcknight|Scot McKnight]] and [[frank-thielman|Frank Thielman]] for their full positions.

## The Feminist and Egalitarian Critique

Feminist and egalitarian readings (Fiorenza, *In Memory of Her*, 1983; Gundry-Volf; Keener, *Paul, Women and Wives*, 1992) emphasize:
- The code's hierarchical elements are a culturally conditioned accommodation, not a timeless principle
- Galatians 3:28 ("no male and female in Christ Jesus") is the normative Pauline statement; the household codes are contextual applications
- The *kephalē* language may denote "source" rather than "authority" (the Bedale-Fee argument)
- The mutual submission of 5:21 effectively normalizes asymmetry out of the relationship

Complementarian responses (Grudem, Piper, Schreiner; the *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* tradition):
- Galatians 3:28 concerns spiritual standing before God, not social role in the home
- The Genesis 2 grounding of headship (Eph 5:31; 1 Cor 11:8–9; 1 Tim 2:13) shows creation-order roots, not mere cultural contingency
- The *kephalē* = "source" reading fails lexically
- The asymmetry in 5:22–33 is explicit and cannot be dissolved by 5:21

## Resolution Status

**Open, and intensely contested**. The household code generates more sustained scholarly and ecclesiastical debate than any other passage in Ephesians. Three fault lines:
1. **The scope of 5:21**: Does mutual submission govern all, or merely introduce the code?
2. **The nature of *kephalē***: Authority or source?
3. **Cultural contingency vs. creation order**: Are the asymmetries timeless norms or historically conditioned applications that later hermeneutics may move beyond?

No consensus is in view. The debate cuts across denominational, academic, and confessional lines.
