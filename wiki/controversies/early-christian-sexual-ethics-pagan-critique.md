# Early Christian Sexual Ethics and the Pagan Critique

A cluster of interrelated disputes about the **nature and purpose of early Christian sexual ethics**, the **meaning of catechetical initiation rites**, and the **relationship between Christianity's anti-sexual theology and the suppression of female political authority in the Greco-Roman world**.

The controversy has two distinct tracks:

1. **The pagan polemicist tradition**: Ancient pagan critics (Celsus, Porphyry, Minucius Felix reporting pagan views) accused early Christians of sexual crimes — incestuous orgies, ritual abuse, veneration of their founder's genitalia.

2. **Hillman's revisionist modern reading** ([[hillman-original-sin|Hillman, *Original Sin*, 2012]]): that the catechetical initiation rite of "the fires of temptation" described (obliquely) by [[cyril-of-jerusalem|Cyril of Jerusalem]] and others was a ritual of anal sodomy performed by "exorcist" priests on boys being prepared for baptism.

---

## Background: The Catechetical Initiation Rites

The primary textual evidence is [[cyril-of-jerusalem|Cyril of Jerusalem]]'s *Mystagogical Catechesis* (c. 348 CE), which describes the rites immediately preceding baptism in explicit liturgical detail:

> "As soon as you entered, you took off your tunic...Once you had taken it off you were naked...What a wonderful thing! You were naked in the eyes of all and felt no shame...Then, once you had removed your clothes, you were anointed with exorcised oil from the topmost hairs of your head to the lowest parts of your body." (*Myst. Cat.* 2.2)

And in *Procatachesis*:

> "Be earnest in submitting to the exorcisms. If you are blown upon and exorcised, the process brings you to salvation... We are trying to get pure gold. Can the impurities be removed without fire?" (*Procat.* 9)

Cyril explicitly forbids initiates from discussing the details of the ceremony with outsiders, citing their unfitness to hear them.

The term "fires of temptation" (*pyrosin tou peirasmou*) refers to the spiritual trials the initiate undergoes in the days before baptism. The stripping, anointing, and "blowing upon" (*emphysao*) are standard features of pre-baptismal catechetical rites across the early Church.

---

## Position 1: Mainstream Patristics

The standard scholarly reading treats these rites as symbolic:

- **Nakedness**: stripping the old self, modeled on Adam and Eve's pre-fall innocence ("naked and unashamed")
- **Anointing**: prebaptismal chrism, applying the protection of the Spirit; the oil is "exorcised" in the sense of being blessed and set apart for sacred use
- **Blindfolding** (where mentioned): protection from premature exposure to sacred mysteries
- **"Blown upon"**: standard liturgical exorcistic gesture, not sexually charged in this context
- **Facing west to renounce Satan, then east toward Christ**: directional symbolism

The "fires of temptation" are interpreted as the spiritual ordeal of fasting, constant doctrinal instruction, and public scrutiny that characterized the *competentes* (those formally enrolled for baptism). No mainstream patristics scholar (Kelly, Quasten, Whitaker, Yarnold) reads these texts as evidence of sexual abuse.

Sources: [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|Kelly, *Early Christian Doctrines*]], ch. XVI; E.J. Yarnold, *The Awe-Inspiring Rites of Initiation* (1972).

## Position 2: Hillman's Revisionist Reading

[[hillman-original-sin|Hillman (2012)]] reads the same texts as evidence of institutionalized ritual sodomy. His argument:

- The term *emphysao* ("blown upon") carried sexual overtones in the context of catechetical rites that borrowed from pagan mysteries
- The "fires of temptation" were literal sexual temptation administered by the priest's body — summoning the devil through the act of sodomy, then having the blindfolded boy "rebuke" the pleasure
- The secrecy demanded by Cyril ("tell no one the details") was not theological discretion but institutional suppression of evidence of abuse
- Prudentius' *Psychomachia* — naming "Sodomita Libido" as arch-enemy, then asserting boys' bodies were preserved for "the use of their own king" (Christ) — reveals that the catechetical program's logic involved the transference of sodomitic desire from the devil to Christ
- Origen's explicit redirection of erotic desire from women to Jesus in the *Commentary on Song of Songs* confirms the catechetical schools were sexualizing the Christ-initiate relationship

Hillman situates this within a broader political thesis: that pagan mystery cults required oracular participants to be sexually unviolated, and that Christian priests sodomized initiates *specifically* to disqualify them from ever serving as pagan oracles.

**Methodological challenges to Hillman's reading**:
- No primary text directly states that priests sodomized initiates; the identification of "fires of temptation" as sodomy is an inference
- Ancient pagan accusations of Christian sexual impropriety (incestuous orgies, etc.) were standard polemical topoi applied to many minority religious groups and were typically fabricated
- The term *emphysao* in catechetical contexts is consistently used for exorcistic breath in liturgical literature, without sexual connotation
- Hillman's publication is non-peer-reviewed and explicitly positioned as heterodox

## Position 3: Ancient Pagan Polemicists

Ancient critics of Christianity, writing in the 2nd–3rd centuries CE, leveled accusations of sexual impropriety that form the background to Hillman's argument:

**Celsus** (via Origen, *Contra Celsum*, c. 178 CE): Priests deliberately target uneducated youth: "On the other hand, wherever one finds a crowd of adolescent boys... there will the Christian teachers be also." He argues Christian teachers discourage education to maintain doctrinal control.

**Porphyry** (*Against the Christians*, c. 270 CE): Condemns St. Peter as a murderer (Ananias and Sapphira, Acts 5); accuses Christians of craving riches and power; describes their theology as suited for "the uneducated masses."

**Minucius Felix** (*Octavius*, c. 200–240 CE): Writing in the voice of a pagan critic, reports that pagans believe Christians:
- Venerate the genitalia of their founder ("Others say that they actually reverence the private parts of their director and high-priest")
- Gather at nocturnal banquets where, after feasting, "lustful embraces are indiscriminately exchanged" in darkness
- Initiate neophytes with rites "as revolting as they are notorious"

**Scholarly status of these accusations**: These topoi — nocturnal orgies, incest, sexual initiation rites — were standard accusations made against any secretive minority religious group in antiquity, applied also to Jews, Pythagoreans, and Gnostics. Most historians treat them as polemical invention rather than historical report. Hillman, however, treats them as corroborating evidence.

## Position 4: Feminist and Critical Historical Reading

Scholars such as Elaine Pagels (*Adam, Eve, and the Serpent*, 1988) and Peter Brown (*The Body and Society*, 1988) — mainstream but critically engaged — have documented:

- The early Church's pronounced **sexual renunciation ideology** and its consequences for women's roles
- The *suppression* of women from leadership positions that had existed in earlier Christian communities
- The **polemical use of sexual purity** as a boundary-marker distinguishing Christians from pagans
- The systematic disempowerment of female prophets, teachers, and community leaders by the male episcopal hierarchy

This mainstream feminist-critical reading shares with Hillman the historical claim that early Christian sexual ethics served political purposes — specifically the consolidation of male clerical authority. It differs from Hillman in not claiming that this political program extended to ritual sodomy of initiates.

## Related Theological Issues

**The Christian theology of sex**: The early Church's treatment of sexual desire as spiritually dangerous is well documented. Key formulations:
- Tertullian: "The Christian man, once saved, no longer sees women: he is blind to feminine allure"
- Jerome: young women are "death-dealing serpents" (Letters 107, 128)
- Ambrose: women are "lower than snakes" (*On Virgins*)
- Augustine: concupiscence (disordered sexual desire) is the *mark* of the fallen condition and the mechanism of original sin's transmission — see [[original-sin|Original Sin and the Fall]]

**The concept of "homosexuality" as a Christian invention**: Hillman argues that the modern category of "homosexual" as a distinct identity — rather than simply describing acts — was created by the Christian polemic against Dionysus' cult. This claim has independent support from social historians (Foucault, *History of Sexuality*, vol. 2; John Boswell, *Christianity, Social Tolerance, and Homosexuality*, 1980), though they locate the invention in a different theoretical context.

**The drug prohibition thesis**: Hillman's claim that Christians initiated the first western drug prohibition as a political weapon against pagan cults is heterodox but partially corroborated: the Theodosian Code does include laws from the 4th century prohibiting various pagan practices including certain ritual uses of substances, backed by Christian emperors.

---

## Resolution Status

**Open**. The mainstream patristics reading of Cyril's rites as symbolic is well-established and supported by the full body of catechetical literature. Hillman's specific claim — that these rites involved literal sodomy — rests on an inferential reading of oblique references and is not directly supported by any explicit primary-source statement. However:

- The pagan critique of early Christian sexual ethics as politically motivated and institutionally self-serving is not merely Hillman's invention; it reflects genuine ancient testimony
- The suppression of female religious authority and the sexualization of the Christ-relationship in Origen and the Alexandrian school are real textual phenomena warranting serious historical analysis
- The controversy remains open in the sense that Hillman's heterodox reading, while almost certainly not mainstream, has not been the subject of a dedicated mainstream scholarly rebuttal

See [[hillman-original-sin|Hillman, *Original Sin* (2012)]] for the full revisionist case.
