# Johannine Determinism — Faith and Rebirth

The Gospel of John presents a distinctive and challenging soteriology: it insists repeatedly both that people must "believe" in Jesus and that believing is possible only because of an antecedent divine initiative. The question of which precedes — and which causes — the other is one of the most significant theological disputes arising from the text, bearing directly on Calvinist/Arminian controversies about prevenient grace, election, and free will.

## The Key Texts

**Emphasizing divine priority/determination:**
- "All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never drive away" (6:37)
- "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draw him" (6:44)
- "This is why I said to you that no one can come to me unless it is given him from the Father" (6:65)
- "Whoever is from God hears the words of God. This is why you do not hear, because you are not from God" (8:47)
- "My sheep hear my voice… I give them eternal life" (10:27–28)
- "Those born not of blood lines, nor of fleshly desire, nor a husband's desire, but of God" (1:13) — Michaels reads this as affirming the divine origin of believers' status, not as the *result* of belief
- "You did not choose me, but I chose you" (15:16)
- Jesus can speak of "other sheep" which "I have" before they have believed (10:16); the scattered "children of God" who have yet to be "gathered" (11:52) — they are *already* children before being gathered

**Emphasizing human response and belief:**
- "To as many as did receive him he gave authority to become children of God, to those who believe in his name" (1:12) — seems to say faith precedes becoming God's children
- "Everyone who believes in him may have eternal life" (3:16)
- "Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life" (3:36)
- "This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent" (6:29)
- "If you believe in me, even if you die, you will live" (11:25–26)

## The Positions

### 1. Faith Precedes Rebirth (Arminian / Traditional Evangelical)

The standard evangelical reading takes 1:12–13 sequentially: those who *received* him and *believed* are the ones to whom he gave the right to become children of God, *born* of God. The causal direction runs: hearing → believing → rebirth. The divine sovereignty passages (6:44, 65) are read as statements about God's general provision (the Father "draws" everyone through Jesus' ministry) rather than individual election to faith.

### 2. Rebirth Precedes Faith — Father's Initiative Is Absolute (Calvinist; Michaels)

[[j-ramsey-michaels|Michaels]] argues this is the more careful reading of the Gospel as a whole. Key moves:

**The man born blind (ch. 9)** is the "classic case study." Jesus insists the man's blindness was not caused by sin — "neither this man nor his parents sinned, but [it was] so that the works of God might be revealed in him" (9:3). God was already at work in the man *before* he knew who Jesus was. He does not believe "in order to be born of God"; he confesses faith (9:38) *because* he was already the object of God's prior work. His belief *reveals* his status; it does not create it.

**John 6:37, 44, 65**: The language is absolute — "no one *can* come to me unless the Father draw him." This is not a description of insufficient effort but of ontological incapacity. Those who do not come to Jesus cannot, because they are not from God; those who do, do so because the Father has given them to the Son.

**1:12–13**: Michaels reads these as saying there is no explicit causal connection between "believing" and "being born of God" — the verse says both are true of the same people, but does not specify which produces which. By the time of 3:3–21, evidence for the opposite order accumulates.

**The accent on revelation, not conversion**: The words "repent" (*metanoeō*) and "repentance" (*metanoia*) never appear in the Gospel of John. The accent is not on "conversion" but on *revelation* — "the coming of Jesus into the world simply reveals who belongs — and who does not belong — to his Father, the God of Israel." Belief "reveals" prior election rather than constituting it.

**Summary**: "He did not believe in order to be born of God. He believed *because* he was born of God."

### 3. Bultmann's "Dualism of Decision"

Bultmann acknowledged the deterministic strain in John and named it "Johannine Determinism," but ultimately accented the *human* side of the dialectic. In his reading, the "Whence" (divine origin) of a person is not fixed in advance but is "constituted definitively" by the human decision for faith or unbelief. The Revealer's call opens the *possibility* of being otherwise — of choosing a new origin. Bultmann's existentialist framework preserves human freedom as constitutive of identity.

**Michaels's critique**: Bultmann ends up giving the human decision priority that the text does not clearly support. "The hearer's decision cannot change but only reveal what has gone on before."

### 4. The Paradox Maintained

Many interpreters (including some Calvinist and Arminian scholars) argue that John holds both divine sovereignty and human responsibility in genuine tension without resolving it, and that the attempt to assign causal priority to either is a category error the text resists.

## Theological Stakes

The controversy maps onto classic Calvinist/Arminian debates but has a specifically Johannine character:

- **Reformed/Calvinist**: John's "determinism" supports unconditional election; God's sovereign drawing of specific people to the Son underlies faith, not vice versa. John's language about the Father "giving" people to the Son (6:37, 39; 10:29; 17:2, 6, 9, 12, 24) resonates with Calvinist election theology.

- **Arminian/Wesleyan**: Prevenient grace (God's grace as antecedent to and enabling faith) is consistent with John's emphasis on divine initiative while preserving the human response of faith as genuinely free. The "drawing" of 6:44 is universal (as Jesus' death "draws all people," 12:32), not individual election.

- **Michaels on Reformation theology**: He explicitly notes that John "turns some versions of Reformation theology on its heads" — not by denying grace but by refusing to make justification by faith (faith as the cause of rebirth) the entry point. John's soteriology is revelation-centered, not juridically structured.

## Resolution Status

**Open / tradition-specific.** The text sustains both readings; which dominates depends on which passages carry more weight and how they relate. Calvinist and Arminian interpreters have read John for centuries; neither has succeeded in making the other's texts disappear. Michaels's reading is exegetically careful but represents a minority position in evangelical commentary.
