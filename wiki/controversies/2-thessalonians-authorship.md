# 2 Thessalonians — Pauline or Pseudonymous?

The authenticity of 2 Thessalonians is the most contested authorship question in the Pauline corpus after Ephesians. Until J. E. C. Schmidt in 1801, no one in the history of the church doubted its Pauline authorship. Today, a significant minority of scholars (perhaps 40% in recent surveys) conclude it is pseudonymous. No consensus has emerged.

## Historical Survey

- **Undisputed in antiquity**: External evidence for Pauline authorship is actually earlier and more extensive than for 1 Thessalonians. Polycarp (ca. 69–155 CE) cites 2 Thess 1:4 and 3:15; Justin (ca. 100–165) alludes to 2:3–4; Irenaeus explicitly cites 2:8 as Pauline. Included in Marcion's canon (ca. 140) and the Muratorian Canon (ca. 170).
- **Schmidt (1801)**: First major challenge; argued the eschatology of 2:1–12 contradicts 1 Thess 4:13–5:11.
- **Wrede (1903)**: Literary dependence argument; 2 Thessalonians mimics 1 Thessalonians, suggesting a pseudonymous author imitating Paul.
- **Trilling (1972)**: Watershed monograph that shifted the debate; marshaled four cumulative arguments; since then the pseudonymity position has commanded serious scholarly attention.

## Four Arguments for Pseudonymity (and Responses)

### 1. The Colder, More Authoritarian Tone

**For pseudonymity**: The "warm, personal" 1 Thessalonians contrasts sharply with the "detached, formal" 2 Thessalonians. The letter opens with "We ought to give thanks" (obligation) instead of "We give thanks"; the exhortations use "We command" (*parangellomen*) instead of "We appeal" (*parakaloumeN*); heavy appeal to tradition in 2:15 and 3:6.

**Pauline response** (Weima, Marshall, Malherbe): The difference in tone is exaggerated. 2 Thessalonians contains abundant warmth: the boasting in 1:4, "brothers loved by the Lord" in 2:13, the pastoral prayer of 2:16–17. Paul uses "brothers" at a rate per verse in 2 Thessalonians *equivalent* to 1 Thessalonians. More decisively: Paul adjusts his tone to fit his audience's situation (cf. Galatians vs. Philippians). Where a false prophecy has destabilized the community and a subgroup is in open defiance of apostolic instruction, a firmer tone is appropriate.

### 2. The Authenticating Comment of 3:17

**For pseudonymity**: "The greeting is in my own hand, that of Paul, which is a sign in every letter; this is the way I write" (3:17) is suspiciously self-conscious. No other Pauline letter contains this emphatic self-identification. Collins: "Hamlet's queen protests too much."

**Pauline response** (Weima): (1) Paul makes similarly emphatic autograph statements elsewhere (Gal 6:11). (2) If a forged Pauline letter ("as though from us," 2:2) was actually circulating, exactly this kind of explicit authentication would be expected. (3) 3:17 primarily stresses Paul's *authority* over recalcitrant idlers who may not obey (cf. 3:14's first-class conditional presupposing non-compliance), not the letter's authenticity. (4) The author's apparent acquaintance with no Pauline letter other than 1 Thessalonians actually *undermines* the forgery theory: a post-Pauline imitator with access to the full corpus would not have confined himself so narrowly.

### 3. Eschatological Contradiction with 1 Thessalonians

**For pseudonymity**: Bailey: "These two eschatologies are contradictory. Either the end will come suddenly and without warning like a thief in the night (1 Thessalonians) or it will be preceded by a series of apocalyptic events which warn of its coming (2 Thessalonians)."

**Pauline response** (Weima, Marshall, Wanamaker): This argument misreads 1 Thessalonians. The "thief in the night" passage (5:2–3) describes the day of the Lord's impact on *unbelievers*. Paul immediately distinguishes believers: "But you are not in darkness so that the day would surprise you like a thief, for you are all sons of light" (5:4–5). Believers *already know* the preconditions (5:1–2); Paul is not teaching the day's unexpectedness to them, but reassuring them that they will not be surprised. Both letters share the same basic eschatology: the day of the Lord involves prerequisites (2 Thess 2), and living in readiness is appropriate (1 Thess 5). Even Menken (who favors pseudonymity) concedes: "as far as eschatology is concerned, it is possible that Paul wrote 2 Thessalonians."

### 4. Literary Dependence on 1 Thessalonians

**For pseudonymity** (Wrede, Trilling, Furnish): 2 Thessalonians reproduces the structure, vocabulary, and phrases of 1 Thessalonians in ways more consistent with imitation than genuine authorship. The parallels are so close in some places that a later author must have used 1 Thessalonians as a model. Furthermore, the correspondence in wording is not always matched by correspondence in thought — which is what one would expect from a forger mining Paul's language without fully grasping his theology.

**Pauline response** (Weima, Fee, Marshall, Wanamaker): (1) Subjective: the same parallels appear to others as exactly what one would expect from the same author writing to the same church within weeks on overlapping topics. (2) Paradoxical: the author is accused simultaneously of being too similar (wording) and too different (theology) — undermining confidence that the parallels are actually measured correctly. (3) The close dependence on *only* 1 Thessalonians and on no other Pauline letter is actually a problem for the pseudepigraphy theory: a forger who had access to the whole Pauline corpus would be expected to draw on a wider range. Fee: "What is perhaps the most significant feature is that its author has a thoroughgoing acquaintance with 1 Thessalonians but knew next to nothing of the Paul of the later letters."

### The Temple Reference: An Additional Consideration

Weima adds a fifth argument not always given its due weight: 2 Thess 2:4 describes the man of lawlessness taking his seat "in the temple of God." If the letter were written after the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, a pseudonymous forger would almost certainly not have described a future desecration of a temple that had already been destroyed. The presupposition of a standing temple strongly suggests a pre-70 date of composition — i.e., within Paul's lifetime.

## The Cumulative Case Debate

Proponents of pseudonymity (Menken, Krodel, Furnish) acknowledge that *individually*, none of the four arguments is conclusive: "The difference of tone per se is not a sufficient reason to deny Pauline authorship" (Menken); "as far as eschatology is concerned, it is possible that Paul wrote 2 Thessalonians" (Menken). The pseudepigraphy case rests on cumulative force.

Defenders of Pauline authorship (Weima, Jewett, Marshall, Fee) respond that cumulative weakness does not produce cumulative strength. As Jewett: "The degree of plausibility with which the general conclusion can be advanced decreases with each new piece of marginal evidence." As Wanamaker: "A series of weak arguments based on marginal evidence does not add up to a strong case."

## Sitz im Leben Problem

An additional objection to pseudepigraphy: 2 Thessalonians addresses a very *specific* pastoral situation (false eschatological claim; rebellious idlers) in a named congregation. If written thirty or more years after Paul's death, who would have written a pseudonymous letter addressed to the Thessalonian congregation with such specificity? A general pseudonymous letter (like later examples in the Pauline tradition) would not need to address such localized concerns. As Donfried: "It is difficult to imagine a setting where a letter specifically addressed to the Thessalonians by Paul would be relevant and convincing to a non-Thessalonian church some thirty or more years after the Apostle's death."

## Resolution Status

**Open.** A majority of scholars probably still favor Pauline authorship (polls vary), but the pseudepigraphy position commands substantial scholarly support. Unlike 1 Thessalonians (unanimous Pauline) or letters like 1–2 Timothy and Titus (majority pseudonymous in critical scholarship), 2 Thessalonians occupies a genuinely contested middle ground.
