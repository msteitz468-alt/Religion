# 2 Thessalonians — Pauline or Pseudonymous?

The authenticity of 2 Thessalonians is the most contested authorship question in the Pauline corpus after Ephesians. Until J. E. C. Schmidt in 1801, no one in the history of the church doubted its Pauline authorship. No consensus has emerged.

[[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]] (*1–2 Thessalonians*, [[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|BECNT 2014]]) affirms Pauline authorship and treats the anti-authenticity case as subjective—“pseudonymity, like beauty, lies largely in the eye of the beholder”—built on exaggerated similarities/differences and forced readings. He notes that the four classic anti-authenticity arguments were already summarized by Hollmann (1904); Trilling’s 1972 monograph made them the watershed for a growing minority denial.

### Survey statistics (as Weima reports)

Weima (with Malherbe and Foster) holds that the **majority still affirm genuineness**. Foster’s *BNTC* 2011 survey of 109 respondents: **63 yes / 13 no / 35 uncertain** that Paul authored 2 Thessalonians. Jewett registers a visible post-Trilling (1972) shift against authenticity among those who do deny. By contrast, 1 Thessalonians has faced no serious authenticity challenge for over a century (apart from isolated exceptions such as Crüsemann 2010).

## Historical Survey

- **Undisputed in antiquity**: External evidence for Pauline authorship is, on Weima’s account, both earlier and more extensive than for 1 Thessalonians. Allusions/citations by Ignatius, Polycarp (e.g. 2 Thess 1:4; 3:15), Justin (allusion to 2:3–4), Irenaeus (explicitly cites 2:8 as Pauline), Clement, Tertullian; included in Marcion’s canon (ca. 140) and the Muratorian Canon (ca. 170). No early-church doubt.
- **Schmidt (1801)**: First major challenge; argued the eschatology of 2:1–12 contradicts 1 Thess 4:13–5:11.
- **Wrede (1903)**: Literary dependence argument; 2 Thessalonians mimics 1 Thessalonians, suggesting a pseudonymous author imitating Paul.
- **Trilling (1972)**: Watershed monograph that shifted the debate; marshaled four cumulative arguments (tone; 3:17; eschatology; literary dependence); since then the pseudonymity position has commanded serious scholarly attention.
- **Minority reverse order**: From Grotius (1640, in antichrist discussion) through Wanamaker, Trudinger, and others, some reverse 1/2 Thessalonians order. Weima: no a priori bar (sequence may reflect length), but the vast majority find traditional order best.

## Four Arguments for Pseudonymity (and Responses)

### 1. The Colder, More Authoritarian Tone

**For pseudonymity**: The "warm, personal" 1 Thessalonians contrasts sharply with the "detached, formal" 2 Thessalonians. The letter opens with "We ought to give thanks" (obligation) instead of "We give thanks"; the exhortations use "We command" (*parangellomen*) instead of "We appeal" (*parakaloumen*); heavy appeal to tradition in 2:15 and 3:6. Cosenders named only in the opening and never re-mentioned have also been treated (Trilling) as pseudepigraphic awkwardness.

**Pauline response** (Weima, Marshall, Malherbe): The difference in tone is exaggerated—what Marshall calls the “myth of the cold tone.” On Weima’s reading of 2 Thess 1:3–12 and related openings:

- “We ought to give thanks… as it is fitting” intensifies affection rather than cooling it; obligation-to-thank is a common Jewish/Christian theme (m. Pesaḥ. 10.5; 1 Clem. 38.4), and subsequent language is warm (“brothers”) and exuberant (faith “growing abundantly,” public boasting).
- The addition of “our” Father in the recipient formula (vs 1 Thess) creates solidarity and intimacy—overlooked by cooler-tone arguments.
- High “brothers” rate continues kinship language from 1 Thessalonians into a letter written shortly later (Malherbe).
- Malherbe further: readers of 2 Thessalonians had recently read 1 Thessalonians; Paul could assume the positive relationship already cultivated (1 Thess 3:6–9) and need not repeat the same relational work.
- Firmer “command” language fits crisis (false prophecy that the day of the Lord has come; idle rebellion)—cf. 1 Cor 4:21; Gal 4:19–20.
- Cosenders never re-mentioned is “imaginary” as a problem: same pattern for Silvanus (1 Thess 1:1a), Sosthenes (1 Cor 1:1), Timothy (Philem 1a) in undisputed Paulines.
- First-person singular shifts (2:5; especially autograph 3:17) strongly imply Paul is the real author; plurals are literary, not literal (parallel to 1 Thess 2:18; 3:5; 5:27).
- Preferring omission of possessive “our” in 1:2 (external + shorter reading) weakens pseudepigraphy: Fee asks whether a forger would “botch the borrowing” of this phrase from the rest of the corpus. Double God/Christ references (Richard: “anomalous, ponderous”) have undisputed parallels (e.g. 2 Cor 1:2–3; Gal 1:1, 3).

### 2. The Authenticating Comment of 3:17

**For pseudonymity**: "The greeting is in my own hand, that of Paul, which is a sign in every letter; this is the way I write" (3:17) is suspiciously self-conscious. No other Pauline letter contains this emphatic self-identification. Collins: "Hamlet's queen protests too much." Majority of commentators treat the “sign” as authentication against a forged letter (2:2); e.g. Danker/BDAG “mark of genuineness.”

**Pauline response** (Weima, with Marshall, Holmes, Wanamaker):

1. Paul makes similarly emphatic autograph statements elsewhere (Gal 6:11); “in my own hand” appears five times in Paul and implies an amanuensis wrote the body until the closing (cf. Rom 16:22 Tertius). Paul often names the shift because letters are read publicly, so handwriting change is not visible to all.
2. The neuter relative *ho* points to the **handwritten script**, not the greeting formula as content; greeting formulas are not in every letter—“this is the way I write” = manner of closing with autograph. Implies Paul always ends with an autograph even when unstated (e.g. likely 1 Thess 5:27 singular “I cause you to swear”).
3. **Authority / presence, not mere anti-forgery authenticity** (Weima’s distinctive emphasis): ancient autographs served multiple functions (legal binding, personal touch, confidentiality), not only authentication. Weima’s reading of 2:2/2:15 locates false day-of-the-Lord teaching more in a **prophecy claiming Paul’s authority** than in a forged letter (“supposedly through us” as instrumentality attributed to Paul across spirit/word/letter, not necessarily a counterfeit *from* him)—so primary concern at 3:17 is not proving this letter genuine against a circulating forgery. Prefer: the autograph intensifies personal, **authoritative presence** so words are harder to ignore—especially for rebellious idlers who may not obey (3:14 first-class condition presupposing non-compliance). Thus 3:17 “emphasize[s] the authority and authenticity of this letter rather than [merely] deny[ing] the authenticity of other alleged letters.”
4. The author’s apparent acquaintance with no Pauline letter other than 1 Thessalonians actually *undermines* the forgery theory: a post-Pauline imitator with access to the full corpus would not have confined himself so narrowly. Fee: thoroughgoing acquaintance with 1 Thessalonians but “knew next to nothing of the Paul of the later letters.”

### 3. Eschatological Contradiction with 1 Thessalonians

**For pseudonymity**: Bailey: "These two eschatologies are contradictory. Either the end will come suddenly and without warning like a thief in the night (1 Thessalonians) or it will be preceded by a series of apocalyptic events which warn of its coming (2 Thessalonians)."

**Pauline response** (Weima, Marshall, Wanamaker): This argument misreads 1 Thessalonians. The "thief in the night" passage (5:2–3) describes the day of the Lord's impact on *unbelievers*. Paul immediately distinguishes believers: "But you are not in darkness so that the day would surprise you like a thief, for you are all sons of light" (5:4–5). Believers *already know* the preconditions (5:1–2; “you yourselves accurately know”); Paul is not teaching the day's unexpectedness to them, but reassuring them that they will not be surprised. Both letters share the same basic eschatology: the day of the Lord involves prerequisites (2 Thess 2), and living ready/steady lives is appropriate (1 Thess 5). Differences are minor and situation-driven (false claim that the day “has come,” *enestēken* as presence not mere imminence). Even Menken (who favors pseudonymity) concedes: "as far as eschatology is concerned, it is possible that Paul wrote 2 Thessalonians." Related: English/Wuest/House readings of *apostasia* as “departure/rapture” are, for Weima, implausible—the noun means religious apostasy or political rebellion in LXX–NT, never positive rapture, and is paired with the man of lawlessness.

### 4. Literary Dependence on 1 Thessalonians

**For pseudonymity** (Wrede, Trilling, Furnish): 2 Thessalonians reproduces the structure, vocabulary, and phrases of 1 Thessalonians in ways more consistent with imitation than genuine authorship. The parallels are so close in some places that a later author must have used 1 Thessalonians as a model. Furthermore, the correspondence in wording is not always matched by correspondence in thought — which is what one would expect from a forger mining Paul's language without fully grasping his theology.

**Pauline response** (Weima, Fee, Marshall, Wanamaker): (1) Subjective: the same parallels appear to others as exactly what one would expect from the same author writing to the same church within weeks on overlapping topics (Weima: cosenders still present; short interval from Corinth, late AD 50 / early 51). (2) Paradoxical: the author is accused simultaneously of being too similar (wording) and too different (theology) — undermining confidence that the parallels are actually measured correctly. (3) The close dependence on *only* 1 Thessalonians and on no other Pauline letter is actually a problem for the pseudepigraphy theory: a forger who had access to the whole Pauline corpus would be expected to draw on a wider range. Fee: "What is perhaps the most significant feature is that its author has a thoroughgoing acquaintance with 1 Thessalonians but knew next to nothing of the Paul of the later letters." (4) Continuity of pastoral problems (idleness 1 Thess 4:11–12; 5:14 → command-escalation 2 Thess 3:6–15; Russell: “2 Thess 3.6–13 is a commentary on 1 Thess 4.11–12 and 5.14”) is, for Weima, what one expects under coordinated Pauline authorship, not merely mined imitation.

### 5. The Temple Reference: An Additional Consideration

Weima adds a **fifth** historical obstacle not always given due weight: 2 Thess 2:4 describes the man of lawlessness taking his seat “in the temple of God.” Weima (with Bruce, Marshall) takes *naos* as the historic Jerusalem sanctuary used **metaphorically/typologically** for usurpation of God’s authority—graphic desecration motif saying more about the lawless one’s character than about a required rebuilt future temple. Still, the presupposition of a standing-temple desecration motif is hard for a post-70 forger: Caligula’s AD 40 plan to set his statue in the Jerusalem temple was a “close call” ~11–12 years before 2 Thessalonians, keeping the motif alive while the temple still stood. Combined with specific localized crises (false day-of-the-Lord claim; rebellious idlers in a named congregation), rarity of pseudonymous *letters*, and early reception as Pauline (Polycarp already cites as Pauline), Weima judges the post-Pauline Sitz poorly explained. As Donfried: "It is difficult to imagine a setting where a letter specifically addressed to the Thessalonians by Paul would be relevant and convincing to a non-Thessalonian church some thirty or more years after the Apostle's death."

## The Cumulative Case Debate

Proponents of pseudonymity (Menken, Krodel, Furnish) acknowledge that *individually*, none of the four arguments is conclusive: "The difference of tone per se is not a sufficient reason to deny Pauline authorship" (Menken); "as far as eschatology is concerned, it is possible that Paul wrote 2 Thessalonians" (Menken). The pseudepigraphy case rests on cumulative force.

Defenders of Pauline authorship (Weima, Jewett, Marshall, Fee) respond that cumulative weakness does not produce cumulative strength. As Jewett: "The degree of plausibility with which the general conclusion can be advanced decreases with each new piece of marginal evidence." As Wanamaker: "A series of weak arguments based on marginal evidence does not add up to a strong case."

## Sitz im Leben Problem

An additional objection to pseudepigraphy (already folded into Weima’s fifth/historical considerations above): 2 Thessalonians addresses a very *specific* pastoral situation (false eschatological claim; rebellious idlers) in a named congregation. If written thirty or more years after Paul's death, who would have written a pseudonymous letter addressed to the Thessalonian congregation with such specificity? A general pseudonymous letter (like later examples in the Pauline tradition) would not need to address such localized concerns. Carson/Moo (as Weima reports): evidence for pseudonymous epistles is “meager at best”; early Christians guarded authenticity.

Related local cues Weima records against Trilling/Wanamaker reverse-order or pseudepigraphy inferences from 2:5: the imperfect *elegon* (“I was repeatedly saying”) and singular “I” mark repeated oral mission teaching on eschatology—different problem than 1 Thess 4:13–5:11—and the literary “we” with Silas/Timothy does not remove Paul as real author. Incomplete conditionals/ellipses at key points (Wanamaker) make a careful forger less, not more, likely.

## Weima’s Conclusion (Attributed)

Weima judges that the four Trilling-style arguments “fail to make a convincing case that Paul did not write this letter as the document itself claims (2 Thess. 1:1; 3:17).” He affirms authenticity, situates both letters from Corinth with Silvanus and Timothy still present (1 Thess late 50 / early 51; 2 Thess shortly after), and rejects stacking weak cumulative arguments as if they produced cumulative strength (Jewett, Wanamaker).

## Resolution Status

**Open.** On Weima’s reporting of Foster’s 2011 BNTC survey (109 respondents: 63 yes / 13 no / 35 uncertain), a majority still favor Pauline authorship, but the pseudepigraphy position commands substantial scholarly support and a visible post-Trilling shift. Unlike 1 Thessalonians (essentially undisputed Pauline) or letters like 1–2 Timothy and Titus (majority pseudonymous in critical scholarship), 2 Thessalonians occupies a genuinely contested middle ground. This page records positions; it does not adjudicate.
