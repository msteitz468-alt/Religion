# The Elihu Speeches — Original or Interpolated?

[[elihu|Elihu]] son of Barachel the Buzite appears from nowhere at Job 32, speaks four times without
interruption through ch. 37, is never mentioned in the prologue or the dialogue, is never answered by
Job, and is neither condemned nor commended in the epilogue. Whether this block belongs to the
original book is one of the standing disputes about [[book-of-job|Job]]'s composition.

## Position 1: A later insertion (the majority position, as Hartley reports it)

[[john-hartley|Hartley]] states plainly that "the majority consider them to come from a later hand,"
and reproduces the arguments:

- Elihu is unmentioned in the prologue and in the dialogue.
- The style differs markedly, and the speeches "lack the rich metaphors that permeate the speeches
  of the dialogue."
- Divine-name usage differs: *ʾēl* is preferred over *ʾĕlôah* and *šadday*.
- The shorter pronoun *ʾănî* is preferred over *ʾānōkî*.
- Archaic prepositions *bəmô, kəmô, minnî* appear in place of *bə, kə, min*.
- There are more Aramaisms.
- The speeches are structured differently — as a reasoned challenge to a position of Job quoted at
  the opening.

Proponents are not individually named in the commentary body.

## Position 2: Authentic to the author (Budde, Snaith, Gordis)

Hartley notes that "a few modern interpreters, e.g., Budde, Snaith, and Gordis, have championed
their authenticity."

- **Snaith** compared the alleged vocabulary differences between the Elihu speeches and the other
  major sections and "does not find the variations significant," concluding that positing another
  author is unnecessary.
- **Freedman** argues for sectional linkage: the first speech (32–33) answers chs. 12–14, esp. 13;
  the second (34) the third cycle; the third (35) the second and third cycles, esp. 21 and 22; the
  fourth (36–37) ch. 38.
- **Gordis** offers a motive: the author "chose Elihu to represent some insights he had gained about
  human suffering, insights that were too important to put in the mouth of the comforters and yet
  too limited to be assigned to Yahweh."

## Position 3: Integral to the final edition, with authorship left open (Hartley)

Hartley's conclusion separates two questions that are often run together. On **integration** he is
emphatic: "Clearly, then, the Elihu speeches are an integral part of the final edition of the work.
It is improper to judge them as a clumsy later addition or a sanctification of the heretical ideas
that Job has entertained." On **authorship** he declines to decide: "Whether they are the work of the
author of Job or of a final redactor is difficult to ascertain." He floats two speculative models —
the author working out the material in discussion with students, or a disciple composing material
that the master reworked into the final edition.

His arguments for integration:

- **The interpolation theory is self-defeating.** "Elihu's absence from the epilogue and the prologue
  seems inexplicable if one posits an interpolator… for he could have adjusted those places to
  accommodate his inclusion of Elihu."
- **Absence from the epilogue matches the author's practice.** "Neither the Satan nor Job's wife
  appears again, even though their absence from the epilogue leaves the modern reader with a sense
  of incompleteness."
- **Elihu is structurally necessary.** "If God had spoken immediately after Job's oath, it would
  appear that Job's oath had compelled him to answer. That God remains silent indicates that his
  coming appearance rests in his sovereign decision."
- **He is Yahweh's forerunner** both by position — between the avowal of innocence and the theophany
  — and by content. Hartley reads the name itself this way: Elihu means "he is my God," which
  "intimates that he functions as Yahweh's forerunner."
- **Speech-count architecture.** Elihu delivers four speeches, one more than each comforter, matching
  the four-part set formed by the two Yahweh speeches and Job's two answers.
- **The wordy prose introduction is characterization, not a seam.** "Compared to the brief, compact
  scenes of the prologue, this introduction is long and wordy, a foreshadowing of Elihu's style."
  Hartley likewise reads his verbosity as possibly "a sign of uncertainty" rather than pomposity.
- **Job's silence afterwards is plot.** "The silence of the loqacious Job may seem improbable, but it
  is part of the plot of the entire work… it must not then be thought that Elihu has reduced Job to
  silence."
- **He is tied into the other speeches** — e.g. "Elihu is also countering Eliphaz's position that
  there is no holy one to whom Job may turn for help," and, on the mediator of 33:23–24, "Since the
  speeches of Elihu are intricately tied to the entire book of Job, Elihu is no doubt showing Job who
  his witness (16:19) or his redeemer (19:25) really is."

## Difficulties Hartley concedes

- **The redundancy problem, in his own words:** "Elihu's optimism is supported by the fact that he
  makes a contribution found nowhere else. If, however, he should give the insight that he indicates
  he can give, there would be no need for the Yahweh speeches."
- **The epilogue's silence about Elihu** he calls "puzzling," offering two options and deciding
  between neither.
- **A tension in his own characterization.** Hartley's case rests on Elihu departing from the
  comforters by teaching disciplinary rather than punitive suffering. Yet on 34:12 he observes that
  Elihu is "reiterat[ing] Bildad's thesis," teaching that whatever misfortune a person experiences
  is justly deserved, with "no exceptions." Both claims stand in the same commentary; this wiki
  records the tension without resolving it.

## Reception note

Hartley reports, without endorsing, that Elihu's "stature has suffered greatly in biblical
interpretation. For example, some of the early church fathers consider him to be overconfident and
arrogant." The fathers are not named in the commentary body.
