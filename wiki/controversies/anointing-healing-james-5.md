# Anointing and Healing of the Sick (James 5:14–16)

James 5:14–16 is the NT's principal text on the church's ministry to the sick, and the proof-text
for the Roman Catholic sacrament now called the Anointing of the Sick (historically *extreme
unction*). Its details generate several interpretive disputes: (1) Is the "sickness" physical or
spiritual? (2) What does the anointing with oil *signify*? (3) Is the promise of healing
unconditional, and what is "the prayer offered in faith"? [[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC James, 2000; [[moo-james-pntc|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18,
dense claims on oil options, *astheneō*, prayer of faith, "enough faith" critique) takes positions
on each.

## Is the Sickness Physical or Spiritual?

A minority read *astheneō* ("be weak/sick") as **spiritual** weakness (linking "save" in v. 15 to
spiritual salvation). Moo judges the **physical-illness** reading "overwhelmingly likely": in the
Gospels (James's dominant influence) *astheneō* always denotes physical illness; the surrounding
vocabulary (anointing with oil, cf. Mark 6:13; the verbs "save"/"raise up," used of healing in the
Gospels) all point to bodily sickness. The sick believer is bedridden — the elders pray "over" him
and the Lord "raises him up" from the bed.

## What Does the Anointing Signify? (Four/Five Views)

1. **Medicinal.** Oil as an ancient remedy (cf. Luke 10:34). *Moo's objection*: no evidence oil
   was a remedy for *every* illness, and why summon the *elders* to apply a medical treatment others
   could give?
2. **Pastoral encouragement.** A physical sign to stimulate the sufferer's faith (as Jesus
   sometimes used physical "props"). *Moo*: has merit and can be folded into his view, but the value
   lies in symbolism, not in any physical connection to the malady.
3. **Sacramental.** The oil as a "vehicle of divine power" conveying healing grace, analogous to
   the Lord's Supper. This view arose early: the Greek church's *Euchelaion*; the Western church's
   later **sacrament of extreme unction** (developed AD 852; the priest given exclusive right to
   perform it). The Council of Trent found the sacrament "insinuated" in Mark 6:13 and "promulgated"
   in Jas. 5:14; Vatican II renamed it the "Anointing of the Sick." *Moo*: the *developed*
   sacrament — for the dying, for the remission of sin, performed only by a priest — "has little
   basis in James's text," which prescribes anointing for *any* illness, by the *elders*, aimed at
   *healing*. One's openness to a sacramental nuance depends on one's general view of sacraments;
   but v. 15 attributes the healing to "the prayer of faith," not the oil.
4. **Exorcistic.** The illness as demon-caused, the oil for exorcism (Dibelius). *Moo*: rejected —
   anointing is never linked to exorcism in the NT; Mark 6:13 distinguishes anointing the sick from
   casting out demons.
5. **Consecration symbol (Moo's view).** Anointing as a *physical action with symbolic
   significance*: setting the sick person apart for God's special attention and care, on the OT/NT
   pattern where anointing consecrates persons/things to God's service (Exod. 28:41; 40:15; Luke
   4:18). Moo notes James chose *aleiphō* (the ordinary verb for a physical rubbing) rather than the
   usual cultic *chriō* precisely because he refers to a concrete act the elders perform — but
   *aleiphō* can carry the consecratory sense (LXX Exod. 40:15; Num. 3:3). Moo also rejects (against
   Calvin and Luther) the claim that the practice was confined to the apostolic age: the assignment
   of the task to *regular church officers* (elders) implies its permanent validity — though, since
   anointing is mentioned only here in the Epistles and many NT healings lacked it, it is *not* a
   required accompaniment to healing prayer.

## "The Prayer Offered in Faith" and the Problem of Unanswered Healing

Verse 15 ("the prayer offered in faith will make the sick person well; the Lord will raise him up")
reads as an apparently *unconditional* promise of healing. Moo surveys the escape routes and the
dangers:

- **"Save"/"raise up" = spiritual or eschatological.** Some take "save" as spiritual salvation, or
  "raise up" as final resurrection (God will infallibly "heal" all believers in the age to come).
  *Moo*: this "robs the present text of its real point" — James envisions an immediate result of a
  specific prayer over a specific illness. *Sōzō* and *egeirō* are regular Gospel healing
  vocabulary; the context requires physical restoration ("never give a word more meaning than the
  context requires").
- **Confined to the apostolic age.** *Moo*: nothing in the text supports this restriction.
- **The "enough faith" error (Word-of-Faith / some charismatics).** The notion that the sufferer
  need only muster sufficient faith. Moo calls this "profoundly unbiblical" and pastorally
  "devastating," laying a double burden on the unhealed. Decisively, in James the prayer of faith is
  offered by the **elders**, not the sufferer (v. 14) — so the "enough faith" logic would have to
  blame the elders. The faith that heals is faith in God's power and will, not a quantity that
  guarantees the outcome.

Moo's net reading: a genuine, normative ministry — the sick believer summons the elders, who pray
in faith and anoint as a sign of consecration to God's care; God, who is sovereign over the
outcome, often heals — without the mechanical guarantee that some traditions and movements have read
into the text.

## Tradition Map

- **Roman Catholic**: a sacrament (Anointing of the Sick / extreme unction), grounded in this text
  and Mark 6:13; Trent.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: the *Euchelaion* / Holy Unction.
- **Mainstream Protestant**: a non-sacramental ministry of prayer and (often) symbolic anointing by
  elders/pastors — Moo's reading is representative of evangelical Protestantism.
- **Pentecostal/charismatic**: emphasis on healing and the "prayer of faith"; the more extreme
  "Word of Faith" strand is what Moo critiques.
- **Anabaptist**: see also [[james|James 5:12]] on oaths for the related Anabaptist literalism.
