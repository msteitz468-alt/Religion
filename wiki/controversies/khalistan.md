# Khalistan (the demand for a sovereign Sikh state)

**Khalistan** ("land of the Khalsa") is the demand for an independent sovereign [[sikhs|Sikh]] state. In
Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2 it recurs across the twentieth century — as a wartime
bargaining counter, then as the slogan of 1980s militancy and a [[jarnail-singh-bhindranwale|Bhindranwale]]-era
diaspora cause. Singh's persistent verdict is that it "failed to gain acceptance among Indian Sikhs."

## The Deep Background: "Raj Karega Khalsa"

Singh roots the idea in liturgy: "Ever since the days of Guru Gobind Singh, Sikh congregations have
chanted the litany *raj karey ga Khalsa* — the Khalsa shall rule." But the structural problem dogged
every concrete proposal: the Sikhs were never a majority anywhere. The pre-Partition variants — **Azad
Punjab**, **Sikhistan**, **Khalistan** — were, in his reading, voiced "not as something inherently
desirable, but simply as a point in an argument against Pakistan," and so "robbed… of any chance of
serious consideration." Cripps put it bluntly: the Sikhs were "5,500,000 against 90,000,000, and are not
geographically situated" to form a majority anywhere. See [[partition-of-punjab-1947|Partition of
Punjab]].

## The Modern Movement

A formal launch came in **September 1971**, when Dr Jagjit Singh Chauhan issued a Khalistan press
statement in London and advertised its "establishment" in the *New York Times*. In 1980 he announced a
National Council of Khalistan with a "cabinet" and "ambassadors"; "only Ecuador agreed to open a
Khalistan embassy." Supporters in Canada issued passports, currency and stamps. After
[[operation-blue-star|Operation Blue Star]] (1984) — which "gave the movement… its first martyr in
Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale" — extremists entrenched in the Golden Temple made a formal **Khalistan
proclamation on 29 April 1986**, believed instigated from a Washington-based Sikh organization.

## Singh's Assessment: a Diaspora Phenomenon

Singh is emphatic that Khalistan never took root among Indian Sikhs — only "raffish bands of the AISSF and
clerics of the Damdami Taksal" hoisted its flag; a 1983 survey concluded that diaspora Sikhs were "no
more than weekend Khalistanis." He documents the diaspora terrorist organizations (Babbar Khalsa,
International Sikh Youth Federation, Bhindranwale Tiger Force, and others) and the worst single atrocity,
the **Air India "Kanishka" bombing** (23 June 1985, all 329 aboard killed), "hatched in Amritsar by
members of the Babbar Khalsa to avenge Operation Blue Star." On foreign-state involvement he is careful:
"no hard evidence has so far been adduced to prove the involvement of any foreign government" (CIA or
Pakistan) — only the activity of individual Sikhs and organizations abroad.

## The End of the Insurgency

Singh's Epilogue records the defeat of Punjab terrorism by the early 1990s under police chief K. P. S.
Gill and Chief Minister Beant Singh (Operation Rakshak) — at the cost of accusations that the police
"murder[ed] in cold blood almost 2000 suspected terrorists." Over a decade, "over 25,000 innocent people
had lost their lives." By 2004 the demand for a separate state had "virtually disappear[ed]," and Singh
closes on the irony that two Sikhs (Manmohan Singh as PM, Montek Singh Ahluwalia) led India's government —
"a notional fulfilment of the prophecy *Raj Karega Khalsa* — The Khalsa Shall Rule."

## Positions Recorded

- **Separatist**: Khalistan as the destined sovereignty of the Khalsa, given martyrs by Blue Star and 1984.
- **Tactical-origins reading (Singh)**: the historic Sikh-state demand was a bargaining counter against
  Pakistan, never a viable majority project.
- **Diaspora-driven non-starter (Singh)**: real support lay abroad, not among Indian Sikhs, who returned
  to "amicable relations" with Hindus.
