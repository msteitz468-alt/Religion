The divinely commanded destruction (*ḥerem*) of Canaanite populations in the book of Joshua — and its extension in Saul's wars against Amalek (1 Sam 15) — constitutes the Hebrew Bible's sharpest ethical challenge for both Jewish and Christian readers. The controversy involves questions of historicity, hermeneutics, theodicy, and the nature of divine command. No tradition has fully resolved it.

## The Texts at Issue

The primary loci are:

- **Deuteronomy 7:1-5**: Command to destroy the seven Canaanite nations, make no treaties, show no mercy, intermarry with none
- **Deuteronomy 20:16-18**: Distinction between the rules for distant cities (terms of peace offered; survivors enslaved) and cities in Canaan (everything that breathes must die)
- **Joshua 6:17-21**: The *ḥerem* of Jericho — all inhabitants killed, except Rahab and her household; silver and gold devoted to YHWH's treasury
- **Joshua 7**: Achan's violation of the *ḥerem* and its communal consequences
- **Joshua 8:24-27**: Ai — all inhabitants killed; cattle and spoils taken (a partial *ḥerem*)
- **Joshua 10-11**: Summary of southern and northern campaigns; repeated formula "he devoted them to destruction as Moses had commanded"
- **1 Samuel 15**: Saul commanded to apply *ḥerem* to Amalek; his failure to destroy Agag and the best livestock is the occasion of his rejection as king

## Position 1: Divine Command Defense

**Representative**: [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] (and conservative evangelical tradition generally). Grounded below from [[woudstra-joshua-nicot]] (fidelity re-ingest digests A, C, D; 2026-07-18).

The commanded destruction is morally justified because:
1. **Accumulated guilt**: Genesis 15:16 — the Canaanites' "iniquity was not yet full" in Abraham's time; four centuries of continued wickedness reached the point of divine judgment. Lev 18:24-25 confirms that the land "vomited out" the Canaanites for their sexual and cultic practices. Woudstra reasserts this horizon on Josh 9:1–2 (kings’ resistance when the “iniquity of the Amorites” is full) and on 11:19–20 (hardening for destruction amid that full iniquity).
2. **Divine sovereignty over life and death**: YHWH is the giver of life and the judge of nations. The *ḥerem* is judicial execution, not murder — the same God who brought the flood, destroyed Sodom, and later sent Assyria against Israel. On Josh 6:17–21 Woudstra: the ban means both irrevocable consecration (Lev 27:28–29) and utter destruction (Deut 13:16); it teaches that “God the Creator may also destroy his creatures.”
3. **Historical particularity / degrees of application**: The *ḥerem* was a bounded divine command for a specific time and place — not a general mandate for warfare. Woudstra tracks **degrees** of the curse in Joshua: Jericho most rigorous (incl. cattle); Ai milder (spoil/cattle kept); Hazor made an example by burning among northern cities otherwise left standing; Gibeon an anomalous peace exception (Josh 9; 11:19).
4. **Revelation's normativity vs. the practice's normativity** (Introduction VI.1):

   > "While certain practices, such as the wholesale extermination of enemy populations by the ḥerem, have ceased to be normative, the revelation contained in the written record regarding such practices does not cease to be normative."

5. **Against “sub-Christian” labels** (Introduction VI vs Bright; Josh 6 n.): Because *ḥerem* “originates as a general rule from an order given by the Lord, one cannot correctly call it an expression of a ‘sub-Christian’ sentiment, lest he involve God in a gradual evolution from cruel to benign.” The conquest material is neither pre- nor sub-Christian but “God’s Word for the ages” within the whole canon’s interpretative context.
6. **Hardening without exoneration** (Josh 11:19–20): The Lord hardens Canaanite hearts so that they meet Israel in battle and fall under the ban without mercy, as Moses commanded; yet “this in no way exonerates the Canaanites. The other way was open, as is shown by what the Gibeonites did.” Human measurement of divine historical action is inadequate; the presentation must not be eliminated or modified.
7. **Eschatological prelude**: Temporal ban is “a prelude and a foreshadowing of a more final judgment… in the end of days” (on Josh 6); the kings-list (ch. 12) preludes a purified world given as inheritance (Ps 37:9; Matt 5:5).
8. **Historicity stance** (Introduction V): Woudstra believes “there was such a thing as a conquest of Palestine,” prefers text-centered caution over Alt/Noth gradualism, grants archaeological limits (Jericho, Ai “silent where eloquence wanted”), and treats wipe-out language alongside admitted survivors and long war (11:18; 10:20) as reliable theocratic historiography, not pure epic.

**Objections to this position**:
- It requires accepting that the wholesale slaughter of children, women, and non-combatants was divinely just — a claim that strains moral intuition even for many within the tradition
- The "accumulated guilt" defense does not easily account for infants and children
- It leaves the door open to later actors invoking "divine command" for conquest and genocide
- Hardening language (11:20) intensifies the theodicy problem for many modern readers even while Woudstra insists it must stand

## Position 2: Progressive/Developmental Revelation

**Representative**: Liberal Protestants; some Catholic theologians

The *ḥerem* reflects an early, primitive stage of Israel's theological development — a morally undeveloped stage that later OT revelation corrects. The trajectory runs from Joshua's ethnic holy war to Amos' universalism, Isaiah's servant who "will not break a bruised reed," and ultimately Jesus' "love your enemies."

Reading Joshua charitably on this view: the Israelites genuinely believed they were acting on divine command; the moral understanding of divine character was not yet refined enough to see the contradiction.

**Objections**:
- This implies a "canon within the canon" — ranking some OT texts as more authoritative than others based on modern moral criteria
- It creates a sliding-scale God whose commands evolve with human moral development
- Woudstra explicitly rejects the characterization of any OT sentiment as "pre-Christian" or "sub-Christian" on these grounds
- It does not explain why the prophets who critique Israel most sharply still affirm divine judgment on the nations (Amos 1-2; Nahum; Obadiah)

## Position 3: Historical-Critical Minimization

**Representative**: Critical scholars in the tradition of Noth, Alt, Finkelstein

The *ḥerem* texts are largely theological construction rather than historical reporting. Archaeological evidence suggests:
- Jericho (Tell eṣ-Ṣultan) shows no evidence of walls or destruction at the relevant period (Kenyon's excavations)
- Ai (et-Tell) was apparently unoccupied in the conquest period
- The actual Israelite settlement was likely a gradual process of infiltration, not a unified military campaign

On this view, the theological problem is reduced: if the events largely did not happen, the moral burden lightens. The *ḥerem* narratives express a theological ideology (YHWH's exclusive claim on Canaan) more than a historical memory.

**Objections**:
- Archaeological silence is not evidence of non-occurrence; the identification of sites remains disputed (Woudstra)
- This approach still must explain why the OT presents the *ḥerem* as divinely commanded, even if the events were not historical
- The theological ideology itself remains: even if the conquest was fictional, the text teaches that YHWH commanded genocide — the moral question is not dissolved by historicizing away the events

## Position 4: Moral Critique

**Representative**: Modern moral philosophers; some theologians (e.g., Walter Brueggemann in different register; outside the ingested sources)

The commanded *ḥerem* is morally indefensible regardless of the theological framework used to justify it. "Divine command" cannot convert genocide into righteousness — the very fact that it shocks moral intuition is evidence that the text reflects human tribal ideology projected onto God, not a genuine divine command.

This position often pairs with:
- **R. Warrior's indigenous critique**: The conquest paradigm has been deployed to justify the dispossession of indigenous peoples. The "promise" of a land already occupied by others has a bloody history of application.
- **Feminist critique**: The *ḥerem*'s inclusion of women and children in collective punishment

**Objections within the traditions**:
- This requires placing human moral intuition above Scripture as the criterion of divine revelation — a step most Jewish and Christian traditions resist
- It does not account for the OT's own theological logic (divine judgment, accumulated guilt, YHWH's ownership of the land)

## Position 5: Typological Reframing

**Representative**: Some patristic and evangelical typologists; Woudstra (partially)

The *ḥerem* is typologically transformed in the NT. The "holy war" of the OT becomes the spiritual warfare of Ephesians 6 (against principalities and powers, not flesh and blood). Joshua's conquest of Canaan is the type; Christ's conquest of sin and death is the antitype. The enemy is now spiritual, not ethnic.

This does not resolve the moral question about the original events but argues for a hermeneutical trajectory that renders literal application obsolete and points toward the type's completion.

Patristic extensions: Origen reads each Canaanite city as a vice to be conquered in the soul; Joshua leads the soul to "kill" lust, anger, pride. Woudstra rejects Origen's allegorization as excess but affirms the more restrained typology of Heb 4:8 (Joshua's "rest" → Christ's eschatological rest).

## The Achan-Ananias Parallel

Several commentators draw a parallel between Achan (Josh 7) and Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:1-11): both involve concealment of goods that belonged to God; both result in sudden death; both occur at the inauguration of a new community. Woudstra himself notes this parallel while cautioning against collapsing the distinction between old and new dispensation.

The parallel raises the question of whether divine judgment by death for covenant violation is a permanent feature of divine dealings with his people, or whether it is historically limited to these inaugural moments.

## Position 6: Canonical Literary Reading (Webb)

**Representative**: [[barry-webb|Webb]]

Webb's approach differs from both Woudstra's apologetic defense and the progressive-revelation dismissal. He begins from the premise that accepting Judges and Joshua as canonical *rules out* the standard strategies of avoidance:

- Cannot treat the conquest wars as a "Jewish affair" — the OT is the church's canon too
- Cannot apply the accommodation principle to make these commands merely reflective of ancient moral limitations
- Cannot dismiss the texts as historically legendary (the moral question remains even if the events are fictional)

His positive canonical case rests on three theological propositions embedded in the texts themselves (Judg 2:20–3:6):

1. **Culture is not morally neutral**: The Canaanites' way of life was not merely a "preferred lifestyle" — it embodied idolatry, injustice, and the oppression of the weak. YHWH's judgment on it belongs to the same category as the flood, Sodom, and the later exile of Israel itself.

2. **Evil is irremediable without radical judgment**: "Evil is so deep that nothing and no one is exempt from it, and only wholesale destruction can remove it." Webb quotes this as a genuine biblical teaching, not a rationalization.

3. **Not all religion is good**: Canaanite religion was not simply a different path to the same God. The biblical texts, OT and NT together, insist on this. The Judges material makes the point with particular force.

**The discontinuity Webb insists on**: In the NT, the mode of warfare changes completely. "God has taken the sword of his wrath from them and plunged it into his own heart." The church's warfare is now spiritual (Eph 6:10-18; 2 Cor 10:4-5). The state — not the church — wields the sword as agent of divine wrath (Rom 13). The conquest is not a model; it is a past episode in a trajectory that has been superseded by the cross.

**What remains**: The theology embedded in the conquest material — the seriousness of sin, the reality of divine judgment, YHWH's ownership of the land and the nations — is not abolished with the practice. A church that refuses to hear it will "have nothing to say to its culture, and in the end, no reason to exist."

**Comparison with Woudstra**: Both defend the canonical status of the conquest texts. Woudstra emphasizes the normativity of the record (judicial punishment of accumulated guilt); Webb emphasizes the canonical trajectory (the mode is superseded; the theology endures). Webb is less apologetic, more willing to acknowledge the difficulty, and more attentive to the NT's reframing.

## Position 7: Philological-Historical Reading (Tsumura)

**Representative**: [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]]

Tsumura does not develop a sustained theological defense of the *ḥerem* as a whole, but his treatment of 1 Sam 15 (Saul and Agag) makes several significant contributions to the controversy:

**Samuel's execution of Agag is the *completion* of the *ḥerem*, not a sacrifice**. McCarter suggests Samuel's action was "sacrificial butchering," pointing to the phrase "before the LORD" (15:33) and the context of a sacrificial site at Gilgal. Tsumura rejects this: Samuel's action is categorically the *ḥerem* (the "ban") — the same as Achan's judgment in Joshua 7. The phrase "before the LORD" marks a cultic setting but does not define the act as sacrifice. Saul failed to execute the divine command; Samuel completes it.

**Fidelity re-ingest loci (2026-07-18, [[tsumura-1-samuel-nicot]]):** On 15:9 Tsumura stresses that Saul *and the people* spare Agag and the best livestock — “partial fulfillment is not a possibility” in ban logic; purpose was God’s ancient command, not spoil; war “after fashion of other wars” profanes the holy (Hertzberg via Tsumura). Central verdict 15:22–23: “obedience is better than sacrifice”; rebellion = sin of divination; rejecting the word = rejection from kingship. Poetic *lex talionis* over Agag (15:33) framed as two-line parallelism.

**The theological climax of 1 Sam 15 is the equation of disobedience with idolatry** (15:23):

> "For rebellion is the sin of divination; presumption is wickedness and idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has rejected you from being king."

The *ḥerem* command is not one instruction among many — it is the word of the LORD whose violation is structurally equivalent to idolatry. In other words, Saul's failure is not merely tactical (sparing the best livestock "for sacrifice") but theological: he preferred the people's voice and his own judgment to YHWH's explicit command. This is the definitive instance of the vice-regent failing to remain under the prophetic word. See [[monarchy-request|The Monarchy Request]] and [[kingship-in-israel|Kingship in Israel]].

**The Agag death sentence as poetic justice**: Samuel's speech over Agag — "As your sword has made women childless, so your mother shall be childless among women" (15:33) — is poetic-prose that Tsumura analyzes as two-line parallelism. The execution is framed as *lex talionis* at the national level, not as an act of cruelty.

**Comparison with Woudstra and Webb**: Woudstra emphasizes the judicial character of the *ḥerem* as divine punishment for accumulated Canaanite/Amalekite guilt. Webb shifts attention to the canonical trajectory (cross redirects divine wrath). Tsumura focuses on the obedience-disobedience structure: the *ḥerem* command is the word of YHWH through the prophet; compliance or non-compliance is the measure of valid vice-regency. The moral burden falls on the obedience structure, not primarily on the ethical content of the act.

## The Historical-Critical / Archaeological Angle (Coogan)

A distinct line of response, registered from **[[coogan-oxford-history-biblical-world|Coogan,
ed., *The Oxford History of the Biblical World*]]** (2026-06-07 ingest), reframes the
problem by questioning whether the conquest happened **as described** at all:

- Of the ~31 cities Joshua claims to have taken, only **[[hazor]]** (and possibly Bethel)
  shows a destruction fitting the narrative; **[[jericho]] and Ai were essentially
  unoccupied** in the relevant period (Stager, Ch. 3). See [[jericho]], [[hazor]].
- Early Israel appears to have been a **rural subset of [[canaanites|Canaanite]] culture**
  — largely indistinguishable in language, dress, and material culture — emerging *within*
  the land (the "ruralization" model), not an external army exterminating its inhabitants.
  See [[israelites]].

This does **not** dissolve the moral problem (the *texts still command* ḥerem, and were
read as authoritative), but it relocates it: the conquest accounts function as later
**theological/ideological literature** (Deuteronomic History) rather than battle reports.
For confessional readers this can sharpen *or* soften the ethical sting, depending on one's
view of inspiration and historicity — hence it is recorded here, not adjudicated. It stands
in direct tension with the evangelical commentators (Woudstra, Webb, Tsumura) ingested
above, who assume the essential historicity of the narratives.

## Current Status

This controversy is **open** across all traditions. No consensus exists on how to read the *ḥerem* texts in a way that satisfies both the demands of Scripture's authority and the demands of moral reasoning. Ingested sources cover Position 1 (Woudstra: divine-command defense; grounded quotes from [[woudstra-joshua-nicot]] 2026-07-18 re-ingest), Position 6 (Webb: canonical literary reading), and Position 7 (Tsumura: philological-historical obedience framework). Positions 2-5 require additional source ingestion. The page does **not** adjudicate among them.
