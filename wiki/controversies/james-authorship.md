# James: Authorship and Date

Who wrote the [[james|Letter of James]], and when? For seventeen centuries the church almost
universally attributed it to [[james-the-just|James the Lord's brother]], leader of the Jerusalem
church (d. AD 62). Over the last two centuries the **pseudepigraphal hypothesis** — that an unknown
later Christian wrote in James's name — has become the dominant critical position.
[[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC, 2000; [[moo-james-pntc|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18)
defends the traditional view and argues for a strikingly **early date (the middle 40s)**, possibly
making James the earliest NT document. This page records the debate.

Moo notes the stakes are not merely antiquarian: 1:1 makes an authorship *claim*, so the
truthfulness of the letter and its place in the canon are implicated — *if* one concludes it is a
forgery passing for apostolic.

## The Range of Theories

1. **James of Jerusalem, the Lord's brother (traditional; Moo).** The only James prominent and
   long-lived enough to write so authoritatively under the bare name "James." Corroborated by
   verbal echoes between the letter and James's Acts 15 speech/decree (the greeting *chairein*,
   Jas. 1:1 / Acts 15:23; "listen, my brothers," 2:5 / 15:13; the "name... called over you," 2:7 /
   15:17).
2. **Pseudepigraphal (dominant critical view).** An unknown Christian, late 1st century, wrote in
   James's name (or the name was added later to an originally anonymous work).
3. **Another James** — the son of Alphaeus (Calvin) or an otherwise unknown James (Erasmus, Luther).
   Moo: this has "none of the strengths and all of the weaknesses" of the traditional view.
4. **A Christianized Jewish document** (a few older scholars) — refuted by how thoroughly the letter
   is permeated by the teaching of Jesus.
5. **Compromise: amanuensis** — James dictated; a secretary polished the Greek.
6. **Compromise: redacted homilies (Davids)** — Aramaic/Greek sermons of James, later edited and
   expanded by a redactor.

## The Four Critical Objections (and Moo's Replies)

1. **Silence about the kinship to Jesus.** *Objection*: a brother of the Lord would surely mention
   it. *Moo*: argument from silence; James's authority was spiritual, not familial (Acts never
   mentions the kinship either); the silence, if anything, tells *against* a forger, who would have
   exploited the connection.
2. **The quality of the Greek.** *Objection*: a Galilean carpenter's son could not write such
   idiomatic, semi-literary koine (with an incomplete hexameter in 1:17, philosophical-sounding
   phrases). *Moo*: the Greek is semi-literary, not literary (Ropes); Hengel showed Palestine was
   deeply Hellenized; many Galileans were bilingual; Sevenster's test case concluded James *could*
   have written it.
3. **The "liberal," ethics-only view of Torah** ("the law of liberty," 1:25; "the royal law," 2:8;
   silence on ritual law). *Objection*: incompatible with the Torah-zealous James of tradition (the
   "men from James," Gal. 2:12; the purification vow, Acts 21). *Moo*: the "torah-fanatic" James is
   tendentious legend (Hegesippus et al.); the real James was Torah-loyal but, like Jesus, could
   stress the ethical core; silence on ritual law reflects the letter's occasional purpose (it
   wasn't an issue for these readers, who had not yet mixed with Gentiles).
4. **The relationship to Paul on justification.** *Objection* (Kümmel): James 2:14ff. engages "a
   misunderstood secondary stage of Pauline theology," which requires chronological distance from
   Paul — impossible for a James who met Paul as late as AD 55/56. *Moo*: reverse the logic. The
   misunderstanding of Paul is most intelligible *before* the two met at the Jerusalem Council (c.
   AD 48). Paul preached in Tarsus (from c. 36) and Antioch (from c. 45); his "faith alone" slogan
   could have been heard and distorted in Syria well before AD 48. A pre-48 James would not yet have
   direct knowledge of Paul's actual teaching. See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on
   Justification]].

## Moo's Counter-Offensive Against Pseudepigraphy

Moo presses a **decisive objection** to the popular "transparent literary device" defense of
pseudepigraphy (the idea that ancient readers accepted such letters as a non-deceptive convention):
there is essentially *no evidence* that pseudepigraphal letters were accepted as religiously
authoritative when known to be forged. He quotes a researcher: "No one ever seems to have accepted a
document as religiously and philosophically prescriptive which was known to be forged. I do not know
of a single example." Therefore the canonical reception of James presumes the early church believed
James *wrote* it; those who doubted that excluded it. The "have-your-cake-and-eat-it-too" canonical
pseudepigraphon is not, Moo argues, a real option: one must choose between forgery (and exclusion)
or authenticity.

He also finds the **amanuensis** and **redacted-homilies** compromises unnecessary (he thinks James
could write the Greek) and problematic (the wording is too bound to the content to separate author
from composer; the unevenness Davids cites is normal for an Aramaic-native writing Greek, and
Dibelius judged the Greek "relatively homogeneous").

## The Case for an Early Date (Middle 40s)

Beyond the justification argument, Moo cites:
- **No awareness of the Gentile-Torah controversy** that erupted c. AD 47–48 (Acts 15:1). James's
  casual references to the law (1:24–25; 2:8–13) make better sense before that crisis.
- A historical fit: the Judean famine (c. AD 46, Acts 11:28) and rising social-economic upheaval
  match the letter's picture of oppressed, dispersed believers.
- Upper bound: James's martyrdom in AD 62 (Josephus) sets the latest possible date if he is the
  author.

Against the early date, other scholars favor a date near AD 62 (parallels with 1 Peter; a "settled"
church situation). Moo answers that the 1 Peter parallels are common traditional stock and the
problems addressed are typical of *young* converts, especially the displaced.

## Assessment

Moo's conclusion: "the simplest solution is to accept the verdict of early Christians" — the letter
was written by James of Jerusalem; "nothing in the letter is inconsistent with this conclusion, and
several, albeit minor and indecisive, points favor it." The critical majority remains unpersuaded,
holding that the developed engagement with Pauline justification language, the Greek, and the
catholic-epistle profile point to a later, pseudonymous origin. The dispute is **open** and tracks a
larger divide between confessional and historical-critical method.
