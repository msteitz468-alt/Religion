Whether the book of [[isaiah|Isaiah]] comes from one eighth-century author, or from two or
three (or many) hands spanning the exile and return, is — with the documentary hypothesis —
among the most contested questions in Hebrew Bible scholarship. Positions are recorded here
without adjudication; the fullest single-author case in the wiki is Oswalt's.

## Positions

**Multiple authorship (critical mainstream)** — traced by Oswalt to J. C. Döderlein (1775)
and J. G. Eichhorn (1780–83); Duhm (1892) added the Trito-Isaiah division of chs. 56–66.
Grounds: the dramatic stylistic change at ch. 40; Cyrus named ~150 years in advance
(44:28; 45:1); chs. 40–55 seem to address exilic conditions as present; and (usually
implicit) the premise that specific predictive prophecy is not possible. Later variants:
C. C. Torrey's rejection of Duhm's third author; Hanson's and Achtemeier's postexilic
composite for 56–66; Clements's and others' redactional continua; the "Isaiah school"
hypothesis sustaining a five-century tradition.

**Single essential authorship (Oswalt, NICOT)** — "the theological and ideological unity of
the book is a primary datum… the essential content of the book has come to us through one
human author, Isaiah the son of Amoz" (dated ~740–690 BCE), the book being "nearest to what
we call an anthology" arranged theologically, allowing editorial/transitional material by
Isaiah or those with him (he concedes in the endnotes he would accept amanuenses, but "cannot
conceive of the present unity being arrived at without the guiding hand of a single
master"). His main argumentative moves:
1. *No manuscript evidence of disunity* — "there is no concrete evidence that any part of
   the book ever existed without the other parts"; 1QIsaᵃ (~100 BCE) is continuous.
2. *The statistical-linguistics standoff* — Radday's stylometric case against unity cuts
   against the critical divisions too ("his findings do not fully satisfy anybody"), and
   conflicting computer studies (Adams/Rincher, Kasher) point different directions.
3. *The "school" is ad hoc* — the observed unity (e.g. the [[holy-one-of-israel|Holy One of
   Israel]] distribution: 13× in 1–39, 16× in 40–66, 7× elsewhere) forces critics to posit a
   school for which "there is no other evidence."
4. *Shrinking authentic corpus* — "the only genuine agreement is the negative one… the book
   of Isaiah is not a unity."
5. *Positive thought-structure case* — Margalioth's shared phrases; the servanthood/trust
   architecture "emerge[s] from an inductive study."
6. *Self-defeating theology* — chs. 40–48 stake YHWH's deity on predicting the exile and
   Cyrus; written post eventu, "the great exponents of ethical righteousness in human
   history falsified their evidence."

**Canonical final-form reading (Childs)** — the final form "alone bears witness to the full
history of revelation"; historical references in 40–66 were suppressed to strengthen
theological unity. Oswalt is sympathetic ("This approach has much to commend it") but poses
a dilemma: either the references were expunged or they were never there because the material
is predictive — "there appears to be no middle ground."

**Convergence datum acknowledged on all sides** — Oswalt quotes Brueggemann ("Second Isaiah
theologically is seen to be organically derived from First Isaiah") and Rendtorff (Third
Isaiah "depends so completely on I and II that an independent existence is hardly
conceivable"): the parts are organically related; the dispute is over what best explains
that.

## Tradition Contexts

Traditional Jewish and Christian reception took 1:1 as covering the whole book (Sirach
48:24 already echoes both halves); the multiple-authorship position arose within
Enlightenment-era criticism and is now the critical mainstream, with single authorship
defended chiefly (not exclusively) by confessional conservatives. The dispute is entangled
with the prior question of predictive prophecy — an empirical-theological premise, not a
purely literary one, as both sides note.
