# The Colossian Opponents ("The Colossian Philosophy")

## The Issue

Colossians addresses a threat to the Colossian church that Paul calls a "philosophy" (*philosophia*, 2:8) and "empty deceit." He identifies it through a series of practices and claims: dietary regulations (2:16), festival observance, new moons, and Sabbath (2:16); "worship of angels" (2:18); ecstatic visions (2:18); ascetic bodily practices — "do not handle, do not taste, do not touch" (2:21); and "harsh treatment of the body" (2:23). Paul's response is to assert the total sufficiency of Christ: all the fullness of deity dwells *bodily* in him (2:9); the powers were disarmed at the cross (2:15); in Christ the Colossians are already circumcised, buried, and raised (2:11–13).

The letter provides no direct quotation from the opponents and never names them. Scholarship has long debated their identity. The answer shapes how Paul's response is read.

## The Evidence in the Letter

The text provides the following identifiers:

- **Jewish elements**: Sabbath, new moons, annual festivals (2:16) — this triad is a standard biblical designation of the Jewish calendrical-sacrificial system (cf. 1 Chr 23:31; Neh 10:33; Hos 2:11). Dietary regulations (2:16; cf. 2:21) correspond to Jewish food laws.
- **Ascetic practices**: "Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch" (2:21); "harsh treatment of the body" (2:23); "false humility" (2:18, 23). These suggest bodily discipline aimed at a spiritual goal.
- **Visionary experience**: the opponents "go into great detail about what they have seen" (2:18); the term *embateuō* is associated with entering into mystical vision-experience or the climactic stages of initiation rites.
- **"Worship of angels"** (2:18): Greek genitive disputed — objective (worshiping angels), subjective (angels worshiping, i.e., angelic liturgy the opponents want to join), or descriptive (angel-like worship through asceticism).
- **Elemental spiritual forces** (*stoicheia tou kosmou*, 2:8, 20): the opponents' practices are connected to these "elemental forces." See [[stoicheia|Stoicheia (Elemental Forces)]].
- **The "fullness" (*plērōma*) claim** (2:9–10): Paul asserts the *plērōma* dwells in Christ — apparently counter to a claim that fullness is distributed across a hierarchy of spiritual beings or that additional practices are required to access the divine fullness.

Jerry Sumney's methodological framework (used by McKnight): distinguish *certain* evidence (explicit statements by Paul about opponents' views), *probable* evidence (reasonably inferred), and *possible* evidence (speculative). McKnight applies this stringently, ruling out much of the proto-Gnostic reading as possible-not-probable.

## Positions

### 1. Jewish-Christian Halakic Mysticism ("Halakic Mystics")

Associated with T. J. Sappington (*Revelation and Redemption at Colossae*, 1991), F. O. Francis (*Conflict at Colossae*, 1975), A. J. Bandstra, and especially [[scot-mcknight|McKnight]] (2018).

The opponents are Jewish Christians who combine Torah observance (food laws, Sabbath, calendar) with an asceticism-based program for *heavenly ascent* and participation in *angelic liturgy*. The "humility" (2:18) refers to ascetic fasting and self-denial designed to induce mystical visions; "worship of angels" = *angel-like worship* — joining the angels in their heavenly liturgy through ascetic transport. The evidence from Jewish mystical and apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch, Philo on Moses, 4Q Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice from Qumran, *Testament of Levi*) demonstrates that this combination of halakic observance and heavenly-ascent mysticism existed within Jewish and Jewish-Christian circles in the first century.

Key insight: this position makes the opponents *insiders* — Jewish Christians who accept Jesus but argue that Christ is *insufficient* without the full halakic-mystical program. They are not pagans threatening from outside but competitively spiritual Jewish believers from within.

McKnight's specific label: *halakic charismatics* or *halakic mystics* of Colossae. He argues there is no need to posit syncretism — Jewish mysticism alone explains all the evidence. Cf. Gupta's rhetorical reconstruction of an imaginary letter from the halakic mystics.

### 2. Phrygian Jewish Syncretism (Arnold; DeMaris)

Clinton Arnold (*The Colossian Syncretism*, 1995) argues for a distinctively *local* Phrygian syncretism combining Jewish elements (circumcision, food laws, calendar) with the Phrygian folk-religious practice of invoking angels for protection and healing. The Phrygian region of Asia Minor had a well-documented tradition of angel veneration (inscriptional evidence of local angel cults).

On this view, "worship of angels" means what it says: the opponents invoke/venerate angels for protection against the "elemental forces" (*stoicheia*) that they believe control fate and create vulnerability. Paul's response targets this double threat: both the Jewish-law component and the angel-veneration component are rendered unnecessary by Christ's supremacy (1:16: angels created *by and for* him) and the cross's defeat of the powers (2:15).

Difference from position 1: Arnold reads genuine *syncretism* (Jewish + Phrygian pagan elements), whereas McKnight insists the evidence is explainable within Jewish mystical tradition alone.

### 3. Proto-Gnostic Hellenistic Philosophy

Associated with earlier scholarship (Dibelius, Bornkamm, some readings of Lohse). The opponents held a proto-Gnostic cosmological dualism: matter is evil; spiritual liberation requires ascending through hierarchies of cosmic *aeons* or *archons*; the *plērōma* (fullness of divine being) is distributed across this hierarchy. The "worship of angels" is mediation through angelic intermediaries at each cosmic level; the asceticism is liberation from material bondage.

Problems: (1) no clear evidence of Gnostic systems this developed in the mid-first century; (2) the Jewish elements (Sabbath, food laws, calendar) are prominent and not explained by a purely Greek cosmological scheme; (3) Sumney's methodological filter eliminates most of the proto-Gnostic reading as speculative. This position has largely been abandoned in recent scholarship in favor of positions 1 or 2.

### 4. Jewish Apocalyptic-Ascetic Torah Observance (Without Mysticism)

James Dunn (*Colossians and Philemon*, NIGTC, 1996) and partly Jerry Sumney accept the Jewish character of the opponents but are more skeptical of the mystical-heavenly-ascent dimension. On this view, the opponents are essentially Torah-observant Jewish Christians (or sympathizers) whose requirements are halakic: food laws, Sabbath, circumcision. The "worship of angels" passage (2:18) is not evidence of mystical ascent vision but of the Torah's mediation through angels (Gal 3:19; Acts 7:53) — the opponents' conviction that the Torah's authority derives from its angelic delivery. The asceticism is practical-piety, not vision-inducing. Dunn: the opponents represent "the traditional Jewish way of life" applied to Gentile converts as a condition of full religious standing.

### 5. Platonic-Pythagorean Mysticism (Non-Jewish)

A minority view reading the opponents as essentially Greek rather than Jewish: Platonic soul-ascent mysticism or Pythagorean number-cosmology and dietary-purity practices. This view has few defenders in recent scholarship because it fails to explain the specifically Jewish markers (Sabbath, calendar, food laws).

## The Hermeneutical Stakes

The identification of the opponents determines how Paul's response is read:

- If the opponents are insiders (Jewish Christians), Paul is defending Gentile-inclusion soteriology against a competitor who denies it — continuous with Galatians.
- If the opponents are outsiders (pagan syncretists or mystery-religion devotees), Paul is primarily guarding against pagan cosmological speculation intruding on the church.
- McKnight's "halakic mystics" reading connects Colossians to Galatians: "We are thus drawn into the orbit of Gal 4:9–10 because (1) the same laws are mentioned and (2) the stoicheia are also mentioned there. The supposed distance of the opponents at Galatia and the opponents at Colossae has now dwindled to a common glance across the street."

## Resolution Status

**Open.** No consensus. The majority of contemporary scholarship accepts the Jewish character of the opponents (positions 1, 2, or 4 — Jewish mystical, Phrygian syncretistic-Jewish, or Jewish halakic respectively). Genuine proto-Gnostic and purely pagan readings have largely been abandoned. The key unresolved questions are: (a) whether "worship of angels" = mystical participation in angelic liturgy (Francis/McKnight) or veneration of angels as cosmic intermediaries (Arnold) or something else; and (b) whether the ascetic vision program is central to or peripheral to the opponents' claims. The letter itself provides insufficient explicit evidence for certainty.
