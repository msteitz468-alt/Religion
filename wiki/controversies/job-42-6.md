Job 42:6 is one of the most disputed verses in the Hebrew Bible. It is Job's final act before the epilogue and determines the entire theological conclusion of the book. The Hebrew reads:

> *ʿal-kēn ʾemʾas wəniḥamtî ʿal-ʿāpār wāʾēper*

Literally: "Therefore I reject/despise X and I am comforted/change my mind on dust and ashes"

The verse has two interpretive cruxes:

**1. What is the object of *em'as* (I reject / despise / retract)?**  
The verb has no explicit object in the Hebrew. Possible objects: himself, his words, his lawsuit, his previous position, nothing (intransitive).

**2. What does *niḥamtî* mean here?**  
The root *nḥm* means "to repent / be comforted / change one's mind." In contexts of suffering, it can mean "to be comforted." In moral contexts it means "to repent."

**3. What does "on dust and ashes" (*ʿal-ʿāpār wāʾēper*) mean?**  
Possibly: in a posture of mourning (sitting in dust and ashes, as Job began in 2:8); or as a contrast ("concerning dust and ashes" = Job's mortal lowliness).

## Positions

### Position 1: Recantation of Sin (Traditional Reading)

**Claim**: Job confesses genuine wrongdoing. He despises himself, repents in dust and ashes. The traditional evangelical and confessional reading: Job's complaints against God were sinful presumption; in the theophany he sees this and repents. The epilogue's restoration follows his repentance.

**Reading**: "Therefore I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes."

**In favor**:
- The natural English reading of most translations (RSV, NIV, ESV)
- Consistent with the Yahweh speeches' tone: "Who is this that darkens counsel?" (38:2) sounds like a divine rebuke
- The epilogue restoration fits the pattern: repentance → divine acceptance

**Problems**:
- God immediately says Job "has spoken rightly" (*dibartem ʾēlay neḵôn*) in 42:7 — a vindication. If Job had spoken sinfully, why would God vindicate him against the comforters who urged exactly this repentance?
- Job swears in 27:4-6 that he would *never* lie to God or falsely confess wrongdoing. For him to do so in 42:6 would make him a liar.
- The *em'as* verb has no explicit object; "myself" is an interpretive addition not present in the Hebrew.

### Position 2: Retraction of Claims about God (Moderate Evangelical)

**Claim**: Job retracts his accusations that God was governing the world unjustly — specifically the claims in chs. 9, 21, 24. He does not confess hidden sin, but acknowledges that his critique of God's governance was epistemically overreaching. He changes his mind about his ability to judge cosmic justice.

**Reading**: "Therefore I retract [my words about you] and repent of [speaking so presumptuously] in dust and ashes."

**In favor**:
- Consistent with the Yahweh speeches' focus on human cognitive limits, not Job's moral failings
- Compatible with God's vindication in 42:7 — Job's *moral* integrity is affirmed even though his *theological* claims about divine governance were overreaching
- Sometimes credited to Hartley, but see Position 3 — his own formulation is narrower: what Job
  withdraws is the avowal of innocence, not his claims about God's governance

**Problems**:
- Still requires *em'as* to have an implied object ("my words") not in the text
- Leaves some tension with the Yahweh speeches' opening rebuke ("darkening counsel")

### Position 3: Recantation of the Avowal of Innocence ([[john-hartley|Hartley]]'s Position)

> **Correction, 2026-07-18.** An earlier version of this page attributed to Hartley an intransitive
> reading of *ʾemʾas* ("I dissolve, melt, withdraw") and a surrender of the *lawsuit as such*. The
> fidelity re-ingest of [[hartley-job-nicot|his NICOT commentary]] does not support that. His actual
> position is recorded below; the superseded description is not retained, because it was never a
> position anyone held — it was a misreading of this source.

**Hartley's translation**: "Therefore I abase myself / and recant in dust and ashes."

**Claim**: The pivotal term is *niḥam*, and Hartley's central lexical claim is that "repent" is the
wrong English word for it: "The term recant (*niḥam*) means to turn from a planned course of action
and take up a new course. **It implies the strongest resolve to change direction, but not an attitude
of remorse.** It is affirmative action based on conviction."

What Job recants is neither a sin nor his claims about God's governance, but **his avowal of
innocence** — "In recanting Job surrenders to God the last vestige of his self-righteousness, i.e.,
he withdraws his avowal of innocence." Hartley is explicit that no sin is in view: "Job committed no
sin that led to his affliction and… he has not sinned in his lamenting. That is why Job does not
confess any sin here."

**"Dust and ashes"** carries three senses at once for Hartley: a self-assessment (Job "confess[es]
himself to be no better than the dust and ashes on which he has been sitting"); an allusion to
Abraham's intercession for Sodom ("I have been bold to speak, though I am but dust and ashes,"
Gen. 18:27); and the literal ash heap outside the city.

**In favor**:
- Requires no confession of sin, and so is compatible with God's vindication in 42:7 and with Job's
  oath in 27:4–6 never to speak falsely
- Gives *niḥam* a well-attested non-contrition sense
- Resolves the prologue's challenge on its own terms: by surrendering even his justification, "Job
  demonstrates that he serves God for himself alone and not for any personal gain or benefit, not
  even his own justification"
- Explains why the Yahweh speeches continue as long as they do — Yahweh "continues to speak until
  Job gives signs that his stubborn attitude has completely melted and he is willing to relinquish
  his avowal of innocence"

**Problems**:
- Still supplies an object for *ʾemʾas* ("myself") that the Hebrew does not state
- Requires that abandoning a *true* claim of innocence be the right outcome, which sits awkwardly
  with the book's insistence that the claim was true
- Hartley concedes the cost: "It has not been wrong for him to complain, even against God himself.
  Nor has it been wrong for him to swear an oath of innocence. But the zealous pursuit of a right
  eventually erects a barrier between God and the offended person."

**Scope note**: the fidelity ingest covered the commentary body, not the footnote apparatus, where
any survey of competing renderings of 42:6 would sit. Hartley names no alternative translation in
his running commentary; this should not be recorded as him reporting that none exist.

### Position 4: Transformation Without Repentance (Pastoral/Experiential Reading)

**Claim**: The theophany transforms Job's relationship to his suffering without resolving the intellectual problem. Job does not confess sin or retract claims but undergoes a *change of orientation* — from demanding an explanation to accepting the relationship with God itself as sufficient. "I see you now" (42:5) is the key: the personal encounter with God is the answer, not an argument.

**Reading**: "Therefore I melt/am moved, and I am comforted concerning my dust and ashes [= my mortal, suffering condition]."

**In favor**:
- Takes *niḥamtî* in its "be comforted" sense, consistent with Job's request for comfort throughout
- The theophany is the answer: Job needed *encounter*, not *explanation*
- Supported by the book's overall trajectory — Job wanted to *see* God, not receive an intellectual refutation

**Problems**:
- Does not fully account for the change in Job's posture from lawsuit to submission
- Leaves ambiguous whether Job's accusations against God stand or are abandoned

### Position 5: Protest Theodicy (Liberation / Post-Holocaust Reading)

**Claim**: Job does not repent or surrender but is *silenced* by divine power. The Yahweh speeches are a display of brute omnipotence, not an answer to theodicy. Job's "recantation" is coerced — God wins not by being right but by being overwhelming. The correct reading of the book's ending is subversive: God *should* have answered the lawsuit.

**Associated thinkers**: Gustavo Gutiérrez; Elie Wiesel; David Blumenthal (*Facing the Abusing God*)

**In favor**:
- Takes seriously the asymmetry of power between God and Job
- Resonates with post-Holocaust reflection on divine absence in extreme suffering
- Treats Job's complaints as permanently valid, not superseded by the theophany

**Problems**:
- Requires reading 42:7 (God vindicates Job) as rhetorical or ironic
- The epilogue's restoration is an embarrassment to this reading — usually dismissed as naive
- Requires an implicit God who is actually unjust, which most within the tradition resist

## The 42:7 Paradox

Hartley addresses this tension twice, at 38:2 and again at 42:7, and his resolution turns on a
difference of *kind* rather than degree. Against Job the charge concerns insight, not sin: "Although
Job has lacked insight, Yahweh does not say that Job has sinned. He never rebukes Job for swearing
his avowal of innocence." Against the friends it is "much stronger" — "Job has been genuinely groping
for the truth, but the friends have spoken falsely in their attempt to defend God," and their counsel,
had Job followed it, "would lead Job away from the true worship of Yahweh." He draws a structural
conclusion: "If the friends had not been reprimanded, they would have come out victorious and Job
alone would have been humbled." He also notes that [[elihu|Elihu]] is neither condemned nor commended,
which he calls "puzzling" and leaves undecided.


Whatever 42:6 means, the key interpretive challenge is that God *vindicates* Job in 42:7: "You have not spoken rightly about me, as my servant Job has." The paradox: Job spent the dialogue accusing God of injustice and demanding a lawsuit. The friends defended God's justice. God condemns the friends and vindicates Job.

Most resolutions require distinguishing *what* Job spoke rightly about from *what* he spoke wrongly about. Positions 2 and 3 make this distinction: Job was right about his innocence and his reliance on God as Redeemer; wrong about his claim to judge God's cosmic governance. The friends were wrong in their diagnosis (Job sinned), their application (mechanical retribution), and their theology (tempting Job to serve God for reward rather than for God himself).

### Position 6: Intellectual Transformation (Maimonides)

**Claim**: [[maimonides|Maimonides]] ([[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide*]] III:22–23) reads the
whole book as a parable and Job's resolution as an *epistemic advance*, not a confession of sin.
Job's original despair rested on knowing God "only by tradition"; once he "acquired a true
knowledge of God," he saw that true felicity lies in that knowledge and "no earthly trouble can
disturb it." The shift at 42:5 ("now mine eye seeth thee") is the move from hearsay to
demonstrative knowledge — which dissolves the theodicy problem because the real goods (intellectual
apprehension of God) cannot be taken by bodily affliction.

**Notable**: Maimonides **explicitly rejects** the Talmudic mitigation (B.T. Baba Batra 16a) that
God vindicated Job in 42:7 because "man is not punished for that which he utters in his pain."
That reading, he says, "does not agree with the object of the whole allegory"; Job is vindicated
rather because "he abandoned his first very erroneous opinion, and himself proved that it was an
error." This is a direct conflict between Maimonides and the rabbinic reading — recorded here
without adjudication. See [[divine-providence]], [[theodicy]].
