# Paul's Opponents in 2 Corinthians

The identity of the rival apostolic claimants who have arrived in Corinth by the time Paul writes 2 Corinthians (especially chs. 10–13) is one of the most persistently contested questions in Pauline scholarship. The difficulty is that Paul's explicit statements about the opponents are limited; his attention is focused on winning the Corinthians, not on refuting a systematic theological program. Any reconstruction must be cautious about over-reading the opponents' theology from Paul's implicit counterarguments — the methodological problem known as "mirror-reading."

## What Paul Explicitly Says about His Opponents

The opponents:
- Are itinerant preachers who have arrived in Corinth (not original members of the church)
- Are Jewish (11:22: "Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they offspring of Abraham? So am I")
- Claim to be "servants of Christ" (11:23) and "apostles of Christ" (11:13)
- Preach "another Jesus, another Spirit, another Gospel" (11:4)
- Display and commend themselves by outward marks: rhetorical skill and impressive *parousia* (physical presence; 10:10), claims to "apparitions and revelations of the Lord" (12:1), claims to apostolic "signs" (12:12)
- Bring letters of commendation (3:1) and boast of external credentials
- Accept financial support from the church — a practice that distinguishes them from Paul (11:7–11)
- Are not united with one another; are competing for Corinthian recognition (10:12–18)
- Are characterized by Paul as "false apostles, deceitful workmen, disguising themselves as apostles of Christ" and "servants of Satan" who "disguise themselves as servants of righteousness" (11:13–15)

What Paul does NOT say:
- That they demand circumcision or Mosaic Torah observance from Gentiles
- That they appeal to the authority of the Jerusalem apostles
- That they bring a developed alternative theology
- That they are unified in their message

## The Positions

### 1. Judaizers / Jerusalem Emissaries (Baur; Käsemann; Some Reconstruction)

The oldest major hypothesis, associated with F. C. Baur's Tübingen school (19th century): Paul's opponents are Jewish-Christian emissaries from the Jerusalem church (associated with James or Peter) who advocate circumcision and Torah observance for Gentiles. Paul's appeal to Moses and the old covenant / new covenant contrast in 2 Cor 3 is evidence of this — he must be responding to advocates of the Mosaic covenant.

**Seifrid's decisive rebuttal**: Paul never takes up "works of the law" or circumcision anywhere in the Corinthian correspondence, unlike Galatians where these are the explicit battleground. Paul's argument in 2 Cor 3 is addressed to the Corinthians as a whole, not to the specific theology of the opponents. Furthermore, any appeal to Jerusalem authority would have been complicated by the fact that a "Cephas faction" already existed in Corinth (1 Cor 1:12) — the opponents could not simply have overridden this. Paul refers to Jerusalem apostles positively (1 Cor 9:5; 15:5–7) without any qualification, as he would not have done if they had underwritten his opponents.

**Current status**: The Tübingen Judaizing hypothesis is widely rejected. Its assumption of a fundamental Jewish-Christian vs. Pauline conflict driving early Christianity has not survived the evidence.

### 2. Hellenistic Divine-Men / Jewish-Hellenistic Pneumatics (Georgi)

Dieter Georgi (*The Opponents of Paul in Second Corinthians*, 1964, ET 1986) proposed that the opponents are Hellenistic Jewish missionaries who present themselves as "divine men" (*theios anēr*) — a category from Greco-Roman religious culture in which certain figures displayed miraculous powers, wisdom, and divine authority as evidence of their connection to the divine world. On this reading, the opponents represent a Jewish-Hellenistic synthesis that Paul opposes with his theology of weakness.

**Arguments for**: The opponents' emphasis on rhetorical displays, ecstatic visions, and miraculous signs (12:12; 11:4–6) fits the *theios anēr* profile. They are compared to itinerant sophists and magicians. The contrast between their confident self-commendation and Paul's weakness could be read as precisely the contrast between the *theios anēr* type and Paul's cruciform apostleship.

**Arguments against**: The *theios anēr* category itself has been questioned as a coherent Greco-Roman type that can be imported into early Jewish-Christian contexts. Seifrid: the reconstruction tells us more about Georgi's scholarly framework than about the actual opponents. Paul's explicit statements give no indication of a developed theological program on their part.

### 3. Palestinian Jewish-Christian Pneumatics / "Super-Apostles" (Barrett; Thrall)

C. K. Barrett (*A Commentary on the Second Epistle to the Corinthians*, 1973) and Margaret Thrall (ICC, 2004) distinguish between "super-apostles" (11:5; 12:11 — possibly the Jerusalem "pillars") and the "false apostles" (11:13 — perhaps local Corinthian enthusiasts or Palestinian Jewish-Christian missionaries). On Barrett's reading, Paul must deal with a two-front war: the false apostles in Corinth and the implied authority of the Jerusalem apostles that the false apostles exploit.

**Current status**: The two-group hypothesis is acknowledged as possible but remains speculative. The "super-apostles" may be ironic references to the same group as the "false apostles."

### 4. Practical Theologians of Success — No Developed Program (Seifrid)

[[mark-seifrid|Seifrid]] (PNTC, 2014) argues for the most minimalist reconstruction. The opponents are Jewish (11:22), claim apostolic status, preach "another Jesus" (11:4), and present themselves by outward marks: rhetoric, charismatic presence, visions, and signs. But they have no unified theological program and are not in agreement with each other (10:12–18 — each seeks his own recognition in Corinth). What they share is a practical theology: they measure apostolic legitimacy (and therefore the presence of Christ and the validity of the Gospel) by the standards of visible success, power, and impressive appearance.

Seifrid's decisive methodological point: "It is questionable, in fact, whether they presented a united front." To attempt to read a coherent theology off of Paul's polemic in 2 Cor 3, 10, or 11 is to commit the fallacy of over-determining the opponents from limited and indirect evidence.

The real significance of the opponents, in Seifrid's reading, is not their theology but what they reveal about the Corinthians: the Corinthians have embraced a practical theology of glory that pre-dates the opponents' arrival and explains the entire Corinthian problem from 1 Corinthians onward. The opponents have merely radicalized a problem already present.

## Key Exegetical Cruxes

**"Super-apostles" (*hoi hyperlian apostoloi*, 11:5; 12:11)**: Are these the same group as the "false apostles" of 11:13, or are they the Jerusalem "pillars" (Peter, James, John)? The ironic context suggests the false apostles, not the Jerusalem leaders, though the ambiguity is genuine.

**"Another Jesus" (11:4)**: What "other Jesus" do the opponents preach? A Jesus without the cross? A Jesus who confers power and glory? A Jesus whose presence is demonstrated by ecstatic experience? Paul does not describe the content, only the difference. The opponents present a Jesus who looks like the Corinthians expect Christ to look — powerful, charismatic, visibly glorious.

**"Servants of Satan" (11:15)**: The severity of Paul's language is notable. These are not merely inadequate apostles but agents of the Adversary who "disguises himself as an angel of light." This anticipates the language of 2 Thess 2:9 (the lawless one with counterfeit signs). Seifrid: the opponents and the Corinthians themselves are caught in a *theologia gloriae* that is, at its root, idolatrous — substituting visible power for the hidden God of the cross.

**The collection (8–9) and the opponents**: The rivals accept financial support (11:7–11, 20). Paul's refusal to accept support from the Corinthians, which had already been a source of suspicion (1 Cor 9:1–18), becomes a further grounds for Corinthian doubt in the context of rivals who accept such support. Paul addresses this in 2 Cor 11:7–11 without retreating from his position.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** No consensus exists. The most widely held position among current commentators is something like Seifrid's minimalist reading (the opponents have no unified Judaizing or *theios anēr* program) combined with the acknowledgment that they are Jewish-Christian itinerants who leverage their Jewish credentials and claims to apostolic appearances. The Tübingen Judaizing hypothesis is almost universally rejected; the Georgi *theios anēr* hypothesis retains supporters but has lost ground.
