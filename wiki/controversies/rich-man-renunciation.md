# Must the Rich Man Sell All? — Literal vs. Spiritual Reading of Mark 10:21

When Jesus tells the rich man, "go thy way, sell whatsoever thou hast, and give to the poor... and
come, take up the cross, and follow me" (Mark 10:21), is this a literal demand for total dispossession
binding on every would-be disciple, or a summons to inner detachment whose literal force is particular
or symbolic? The question has divided Christian interpreters since antiquity.

## The Positions

### 1. Spiritual detachment — disposition, not dispossession
The classic statement is [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]]'s homily [[quis-dives-salvetur|
Quis Dives Salvetur]] (c. 200). Reading the text [[allegorical-reading|allegorically]], Clement argues
the command is "not... to fling away the substance that belongs to him... but to banish from the soul
its opinions about riches, its attachment to them, its excessive desire." [[use-of-wealth|Wealth]] is a
neutral instrument "to minister, not to rule"; the rich can be saved by right use and inner freedom. He
marshals counter-texts (the "mammon of unrighteousness"; Christ's wealthy hosts Zacchaeus and Levi) and
argues mere destitution is no virtue. This became the dominant patristic and later mainstream reading.

### 2. Literal renunciation
Others have read the command at face value as a real call to poverty. This stream runs through the
apostolic community of goods (Acts 2–4), the **monastic** movement (Antony of Egypt is famously
converted by hearing Mark 10:21 read aloud and literally selling his possessions), the **Franciscan**
embrace of *evangelical poverty*, and later radical and reforming movements that charged the wealthy
Church with evading the plain sense. On this reading, the spiritualizing interpretation is an
accommodation that blunts the Gospel's edge — exactly the worry the Delphi editor voices against
"the Alexandrine system of spiritual or allegorical interpretation" and its "avoidance of the plain
meaning."

### 3. Counsel, not precept
A mediating position (developed in medieval Catholic theology) distinguishes **precepts** binding on
all from **counsels of perfection** (poverty, chastity, obedience) freely undertaken by those called to
the religious life. The literal command then holds for the vowed religious as a counsel, while the laity
are bound only to right use — a synthesis that preserves both the monastic literal reading and Clement's
ethic of detachment for those in the world.

## Why It Matters

The dispute is a paradigm case of the [[allegorical-reading|literal-vs-spiritual]] hermeneutical divide
applied to ethics, and it set the terms for the entire Christian tradition of [[use-of-wealth|wealth
ethics]]. It recurs whenever the relation of the Church to wealth is contested. Per the wiki's
Contradiction Protocol, the positions are recorded without adjudication: each reflects a different
construal of how dominical commands bind, and a different tradition context (Alexandrian catechesis,
desert monasticism, scholastic distinction, Reformation and radical critique).
