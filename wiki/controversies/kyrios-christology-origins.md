# Origins of Kyrios-Christology

## The Question

How did earliest Christianity come to venerate Jesus as divine "Lord" (*kyrios*) alongside God the Father — and where and when did this begin? The answer has major theological stakes (was high Christology original or developed?) and historical-critical stakes (how do we explain the evidence?).

## Bousset's Position (*Kyrios Christos*, 1913)

Wilhelm Bousset, a leading figure of the German *religionsgeschichtliche Schule* (history-of-religion school), argued:

1. **The "Primitive Palestinian Community"** (earliest Aramaic-speaking Jewish Christians) venerated Jesus as "Son of Man" — a cosmic messianic figure who would execute God's eschatological judgment. This was a strictly Jewish category. Jewish monotheism precluded treating Jesus as a divine Lord receiving cultic worship.
2. **The "Gentile Christian Primitive Community"** (Greek-speaking Gentile converts in cities like Antioch, Damascus) came from a pagan background familiar with divine heroes, mystery cult deities, and the *kyrios* cult. Under this influence, they reformulated Jesus as the divine "Lord" (*Kyrios Christos*) — analogous to pagan deities. This was the decisive mutation.
3. **Paul** inherited this Gentile Lord-Christology from the Antioch tradition in which he was formed after his conversion. He neither invented nor directly received it from the Jerusalem church.
4. **Implication**: The devotion to Jesus as divine Lord is a secondary, historically conditioned development influenced by non-Jewish religious culture. For Liberal Protestant theology (Bousset's background), this meant it was potentially disposable.

Bousset's work was enormously influential for decades in German scholarship.

## Hurtado's Response (following Martin Hengel, et al.)

[[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]]'s position (developed in *One God, One Lord* [1988], *Lord Jesus Christ* [2003], and popularized in [[hurtado-destroyer-of-the-gods|*Destroyer of the Gods*]] [2016]):

1. **The evidence is too early for pagan influence**: Paul's conversion to the Jesus-movement occurred within at most a couple of years of the crucifixion (c. 32–34 AD). In Paul's letters — our *earliest* documents — the devotion to Jesus as Lord is already fully in place. 1 Corinthians 8:4-6, Philippians 2:9-11, and Romans 10:9-13 are not innovations but reflect established practice. There was no time for pagan mystery cult influence to have shaped a substantial Gentile community that could then have influenced Paul.

2. **The Aramaic evidence**: The Aramaic acclamation *Maranatha* ("Come, Lord" or "Our Lord, come") appears in 1 Corinthians 16:22 embedded in Greek text without translation. Paul assumes the Corinthians know this Aramaic formula. Since Aramaic was the vernacular of Judaea and Syria (not Greek-speaking Gentile cities), this formula must have originated in Aramaic-speaking Jewish-Christian circles — precisely where Bousset's theory cannot locate it.

3. **The "chief agent" framework inadequate but suggestive**: Hurtado grants that Jewish apocalyptic texts (Wisdom, Michael, the Chosen One in 1 Enoch) describe heavenly "chief agent" figures as exalted divine agents. These gave Jewish Christians conceptual resources to begin articulating Jesus' significance. But none of these figures received the systematic *cultic devotion* — corporate worship, hymns, invocations — that Jesus received from the very beginning of the movement. The *pattern* of devotion is new, not merely the specific figure.

4. **The mutation is early and Jewish**: The appropriate explanation is a *mutation within early Jewish Christianity* — driven by the overwhelming conviction of the resurrection and exaltation of Jesus, which led his followers to conclude that God had now given Jesus the divine name (Phil 2:9-11, citing Isa 45:23) and installed him at God's right hand (Ps 110:1, the most-cited OT text in the NT). This is Jewish exegesis applied to a Jewish Messiah, not pagan syncretism.

5. **Against Bousset's theological program**: Bousset's claim that Gentile pagan influence "corrupted" primitive Jewish Christianity served his Liberal Protestant agenda of recovering a purer, simpler Jesus. Hurtado argues this ideological motivation distorted Bousset's historical judgment.

## Current State of the Debate

Hurtado's position is broadly representative of the current scholarly mainstream. The German history-of-religion school's specific claims (substantial pagan mystery-cult influence on earliest Christology) have not held up under scrutiny:
- The proposed pagan parallels are much weaker than Bousset claimed.
- Bousset's use of late sources (e.g., Mandaean texts from centuries later) to illuminate first-century Christianity is methodologically indefensible.
- The Jewish matrix of early Christianity was more intellectually rich and capable of generating high Christology internally than Bousset recognized.

Active debates continue, however, on related questions:
- How precisely was the resurrection conviction translated into the devotional pattern? What was the mechanism?
- How quickly did the pattern spread to Gentile circles, and did Gentile participation modify it?
- Is the "Son of Man" category in Palestinian circles as distinct from "Lord" as Bousset claimed?
- Does the evidence in the Gospels (especially John) represent independent trajectories of Christological development?

## Significance for the Wiki

This controversy is the background of [[dyadic-devotion|Dyadic Devotion]] (Hurtado's analytical concept) and [[larry-hurtado|Hurtado]]'s overall argument in [[hurtado-destroyer-of-the-gods|*Destroyer of the Gods*]]. The Bousset position, if correct, would suggest that early Christianity's most distinctive claim (Jesus as Lord) was borrowed from the Roman religious environment rather than genuinely novel. Hurtado's position maintains the novelty: the dyadic devotional pattern was a mutation from within the Jewish tradition, unprecedented in the history of Jewish monotheism.

## Kruger's Contribution: The Pre-Pauline Creedal Layer

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] (*Christianity at the Crossroads*, ch. 5) reinforces and extends the Hurtado/Bauckham position by arguing that:

### 1 Corinthians 8:5-6 and the Shema

Paul's statement in 1 Cor 8:5-6 is a deliberate reformulation of the Jewish [[shema|Shema]] (Deut 6:4: "Hear O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one") to include Jesus:

> "For even if there are so-called gods, whether in heaven or on earth — as indeed there are many 'gods' and many 'lords' — yet for us there is **one God, the Father**, from whom all things came and for whom we live; and there is **one Lord, Jesus Christ**, through whom all things came and through whom we live."

The parallelism is structural: "one God / one Lord" maps onto the Shema's "one LORD." Paul distributes the Shema's affirmation across Father and Son. The implication, as Bauckham (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008) argues, is that Paul includes Jesus within the *divine identity* — not merely as a divine agent alongside God but as sharing the identity of the one God of Israel. This formula is not Pauline invention; its concise creedal form (*ex hou / di hou*; "from whom / through whom") suggests a pre-Pauline formula Paul is adapting.

### Philippians 2:6-11 as Pre-Pauline Hymn

Phil 2:6-11 is widely recognized by scholars of all perspectives as a pre-Pauline hymnic/creedal unit. Its structure (pre-existence → self-emptying → humiliation → exaltation → universal confession) and vocabulary (features not typical of Paul's prose style) indicate Paul is quoting something he received. The climax — "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow... and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is **Lord** (*kyrios*)" — is a direct appropriation of Isa 45:23, where *YHWH* says that every knee will bow to *him*. Paul applies YHWH's self-assertion to Jesus.

This is not a later development. If Phil 2:6-11 is pre-Pauline (probably from the earliest Jerusalem community in the 30s-40s), then the *divine identity* Christology is present at the very beginning of Christianity.

### 1 Corinthians 15:3-11 — The Received Tradition

Paul explicitly identifies the resurrection kerygma he received ("delivered to you as of first importance what I also received") in 1 Cor 15:3-5. The formula ("died for our sins / was buried / was raised on the third day / appeared to...") is the apostolic baseline — Paul says he received it, and that the other apostles preach it identically (v.11: "Whether then it was I or they, so we preach and so you believed"). This takes us back to the Jerusalem church in the 30s CE.

### The Rule of Faith as Confirmation

Kruger follows Bryce Litfin (*After Acts*, 2015) in demonstrating that the [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]] across all second-century orthodox witnesses coheres consistently with this Pauline creedal baseline — particularly on the points most contested by heterodox groups: one God as creator, real human birth, pre-existence and incarnation, physical death and resurrection. The persistence of this specific Christological profile across independent second-century witnesses (North Africa, Gaul, Rome, Asia Minor, Egypt) argues for a single, early common origin rather than multiple late developments.
