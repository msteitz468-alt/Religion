Genesis 6:1-4 narrates that "the sons of God" (*bĕnê-hāʾelōhîm*) saw the beautiful daughters of men (*bĕnôt hāʾādām*) and took them as wives, producing the Nephilim. This brief, enigmatic passage has generated interpretive controversy from the Second Temple period to the present.

## The Passage

> "When human beings began to multiply on the face of the ground, and daughters were born to them, the sons of God saw that they were fair; and they took wives for themselves of all that they chose... The Nephilim were on the earth in those days — and also afterward — when the sons of God went in to the daughters of humans, who bore children to them. These were the heroes that were of old, warriors of renown."

## The Three Major Positions

**Divine/angelic beings** (dominant in the Second Temple period; [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]]'s preference): *Bĕnê-hāʾelōhîm* in its other OT occurrences (Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7; Ps 29:1; 89:7) always refers to divine or supernatural beings in YHWH's court. The narrative describes illicit crossing of the divine-human boundary, motivated by lust. The Nephilim are the semi-divine offspring of this union. This reading is supported by Jude 1:6 and 2 Peter 2:4 (angels who "left their proper dwelling"), and by 1 Enoch, which expands this narrative extensively.

Hamilton notes this is the only place in Genesis where "sons of God" appears **negatively** — in contrast to the positive valence of God-relationship throughout the patriarchal narratives.

**The Sethite theory** (dominant in patristic and Protestant tradition; Augustine, Calvin, most modern evangelicals): "Sons of God" = the pious male descendants of Seth (Gen 4:26: "at that time people began to call upon the name of YHWH"); "daughters of men" = women from the line of Cain. Intermarriage between the two lines represents the corruption of the godly line by worldly influences, explaining the moral decline that leads to the Flood.

This reading avoids the theological problem of divine-human sexual relations but requires *bĕnê-hāʾelōhîm* to mean something it does not mean elsewhere in the OT.

**The dynastic/ruler theory**: "Sons of God" = kings or rulers (cf. Ps 82:6: "I said, you are gods... sons of the Most High"), who practice polygamy and take women by force. The Nephilim are simply powerful men, not supernatural beings. This avoids both the angelic-intercourse problem and the Sethite eisegesis but requires unusual interpretive moves.

## The Nephilim

The Nephilim appear both here and in Numbers 13:33 (the spies' report: "we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them" — Nephilim as the giant inhabitants of Canaan). The relationship between these two references is debated: either the Nephilim survived the Flood (the 6:4 note "and also afterward" suggests some continuity), or the Numbers reference draws on the Gen 6 tradition independently.

## Relationship to the Flood

The passage immediately precedes the Flood narrative, suggesting that the illicit unions are part of the moral corruption that provokes divine judgment. The **angelic reading** connects it to the *watcher* tradition of 1 Enoch, where the sin of the angels introduces violence and corrupted knowledge into the world, prompting the Flood.
