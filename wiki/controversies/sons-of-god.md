Genesis 6:1-4 narrates that "the sons of God" (*bĕnê-hāʾelōhîm*) saw the beautiful daughters of men (*bĕnôt hāʾādām*) and took them as wives, producing the Nephilim. This brief, enigmatic passage has generated interpretive controversy from the Second Temple period to the present.

## The Passage

> "When human beings began to multiply on the face of the ground, and daughters were born to them, the sons of God saw that they were fair; and they took wives for themselves of all that they chose... The Nephilim were on the earth in those days — and also afterward — when the sons of God went in to the daughters of humans, who bore children to them. These were the heroes that were of old, warriors of renown."

## The Three Major Positions

The three classical options remain standard across Jewish and Christian reception. **[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] does not adjudicate among them.** In [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot|Genesis 1–17]] he surveys all three and refuses dogmatic identification:

> “Suffice it to say, it is impossible to be dogmatic about the identification of ‘sons of God’ here. The best one can do is to consider the options… the evidence is ambiguous and therefore defies clear-cut identifications and solutions.” (Hamilton; on Gen 6:1–4)

### 1. Divine / angelic beings

Dominant in the Second Temple period (1 Enoch; Jubilees) and favored by the LXX’s trajectory. *Bĕnê-hāʾĕlōhîm* elsewhere in the OT (Job 1–2; 38:7; Pss) denotes the heavenly court; Ugaritic *bn il* is a formal parallel though OT angels are not a divine pantheon. Hamilton’s report of the view: illicit crossing of the divine–human boundary; reverse of Gen 1–11’s usual pattern (here the divine impinges on the human rather than humans grasping divinity). Objections he registers: “bizarre”; NT angels do not marry; mankind punished for angels’ sin — objections he does **not** treat as conclusive (Flood also destroys beasts for man’s sin; David’s census). Assumes corporeal appearance (angel of the Lord; incubus tradition).

### 2. Dynastic rulers / aristocracy

“Sons of God” = kings or early aristocracy practicing polygamous harems (// Lamech who “took wives”); Kline-type divine-kingship background (Keret *bn il*; judges as *ʾĕlōhîm*; Davidic son language). Pros Hamilton notes: non-mythological; serves as Flood introduction; fits Cain/Seth narrative context. Con: individual kings may be called God’s son, but groups of kings less clearly so. Cf. Ps 82:6: “I said, you are gods… sons of the Most High.”

### 3. Sethite / Cainite intermarriage

Dominant in much patristic and later Protestant reception (Augustine, Calvin, many modern evangelicals): “sons of God” = pious male descendants of Seth (Gen 4:26 Yahwism note); “daughters of men” = Cainite women. Forbidden intermarriage corrupts the godly line. Hamilton’s registered con: Sethites are never called “sons of God”; the reading forces dual sense of *ʾādām* in vv. 1–2 (though generic→specific shifts exist elsewhere). A reverse pairing (Cainite sons / Sethite daughters) has also been floated.

## Hamilton’s non-dogmatic cluster (related exegetical moves)

Even while leaving identity open, Hamilton stakes several **text-internal** claims that any view must engage:

1. **Offense is the union itself, not hybrid offspring.** Divine speech (6:3) sits *between* cohabitation and offspring: “this forbidden union itself is offensive to Yahweh, rather than the fact that such a union produced (hybrid) offspring.”
2. **120 years = grace period before the Flood** (// Jonah’s 40 days), not an absolute lifespan cap (patriarchs exceed 120; Spirit-withdrawal fits impending judgment better).
3. **Nephilim ≠ the *gibbōrîm* offspring.** Hamilton takes Nephilim as parenthetical contemporaries (“and also afterward”), not identified with the sons of God and not necessarily the hybrid children; *nĕpīlîm* = “fallen ones / those cast down” (qaṭīl), not active *nōpĕlîm*. **Only the mighty men (*haggibbōrîm*) are the offspring** — “of old,” “men of reputation”; fame-content untold.
4. **Group sin introduces the Flood.** Until 6:1–4 Scripture has discussed individual sins; now punishment is directed against mankind, not only a man.
5. **Marriage, not rape, is the surface grammar** of “took wives… of all that they chose.”

### NT appropriation (Hamilton)

Intertestamental literature heavily favored the angel reading (1 Enoch). 2 Pet 2:4 is mute on the *nature* of the angels’ sin (anarthrous *angelōn*). Jude 6: angels who left proper habitation; if *toútois* (v. 7) points to the angels, Jude reads Gen 6 as fornication/unnatural lust in the 1 Enoch/Jubilees line — Jude quotes and echoes 1 Enoch. Hamilton records this reception without making it the OT’s own settled identification.

## The Nephilim

The Nephilim appear both here and in Numbers 13:33 (the spies’ report: “we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers…” — Nephilim as giant inhabitants of Canaan). Continuity options: survivors of the Flood (the 6:4 “and also afterward” note), or independent Num 13 reuse of the Gen 6 tradition. Hamilton’s parenthetical reading loosens necessary identity between Nephilim and the *gibbōrîm* of the illicit unions.

## Relationship to the Flood

The passage immediately precedes the Flood narrative. On any of the three views it can function as the first **group** sin that escalates toward universal judgment (6:5ff.). The **angelic reading** specifically connects to the *watcher* tradition of 1 Enoch, where the sin of the angels introduces violence and corrupted knowledge into the world, prompting the Flood. Hamilton’s moral reading of Flood motivation (human wickedness, not ANE noise-irritation) stands independently of which identity of *bĕnê-hāʾĕlōhîm* is chosen — see [[flood-universality|Flood universality]] and [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot|Hamilton Genesis 1–17]].
