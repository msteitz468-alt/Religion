# Dionysius: Christian Theologian or Neoplatonist in Disguise?

Distinct from the [[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|authorship controversy]] (who wrote the corpus
and when), this is a dispute over the **theological character** of the
[[pseudo-dionysius|Dionysian]] writings: do they present an orthodox Christian theology dressed
in Neoplatonic vocabulary, or a Neoplatonic metaphysic in which the distinctively Christian
doctrines — the **Trinity**, the **Incarnation**, the **personal God** — are reduced to mere
names, abstractions, or moments of a cosmic process? The question is sharpened by the corpus's
central doctrine of the **Super-Essential Godhead** (*hyperousios thearchia*), which several
readers equate with the philosophical **Absolute**.

The dispute is documented in detail in W. J. Sparrow-Simpson's essay *The Influence of Dionysius
in Religious History*, appended to [[rolt-dionysius|Rolt's 1920 edition]], and is reframed by
[[perl-theophany|Eric Perl's *Theophany*]] (2007).

## The Core Tension

Two conceptions of Deity sit, uneasily, in the corpus (as Sparrow-Simpson frames it):

1. **The philosophic conception** — Deity identical with the Absolute: "beyond personality,
   beyond goodness, beyond consciousness, beyond existence itself," of which "nothing whatever
   can be predicated," where "Being is identical with nothingness."
2. **The religious conception** — Deity as "self-conscious personality," Creator and Father, an
   object of worship and prayer.

If the philosophic conception is the true one and the religious conception is, as in
[[john-scotus-eriugena|Eriugena's]] exposition, merely "the creation of the theologians for the
purpose of explanation and of faith," then (the critics urge) "that of which nothing can be
predicated cannot become the object of our worship," and "the proviso paralyses the wings of our
aspiration."

## Positions

### Defenders — the orthodox Christianiser
- **C. E. Rolt** ([[rolt-dionysius|1920]]) defends Dionysius against the charge of pantheism
  ("refuted by the very extravagance of the terms in which he asserts the Transcendence of the
  Godhead") and argues his Supra-Personal Absolute is reconcilable with Scripture and with
  Augustine and Aquinas — though he concedes the doctrine is "dangerous" and the Cross
  under-emphasized. His editor **W. J. Sparrow-Simpson** is more guarded, appending notes that
  the Supra-Personal Deity endangers the Person of Christ and the Atonement.
- **[[sharpe-mysticism|A. B. Sharpe]]** (1910) reads Dionysius as the orthodox Thomistic
  *Christianiser* of Neoplatonism who explicitly **rejects** his sources' pantheism.
- Historically: **Bernard de Rubeis**, the Universities of **Paris** and **Louvain**, and the
  **Jesuits** (notably **Lessius**, for whom Dionysius was a lifelong devotional patron) defended
  his orthodoxy; **Aquinas** used him heavily while reading him "critically" and rejecting any
  pantheist tendency. Defenders typically read him "in the light of strong Catholic
  presuppositions which… neutralized his over-emphasis, and supplemented his omissions."

### Critics — the Neoplatonist in disguise
- **[[john-scotus-eriugena|Eriugena's]]** 9th-c. exposition itself became, in Christlieb's
  judgment, "scarcely distinguishable from Pantheism."
- **Le Quien** (17th c.): Dionysius "confuses the Divine and the Human in our Lord," fails to
  distinguish person and nature, and betrays "monophysite tendencies."
- **F. C. Baur** (1842): in the Dionysian system distinctions exist only "in our subjective
  consciousness," not in God; the Trinity is "reduced to little more than names," and the
  Incarnation becomes "nothing more than the process from Unity to Multiplicity" — no
  "distinctive and peculiar Incarnation at all."
- **Vacherot** (1851): "a neo-Platonist philosopher in disguise, who while going over to
  Christianity retained his philosophic ideas"; the Trinity "reduced to a mere abstraction."
- **Dorner** (Lutheran): the Areopagite's Christology "forms an important link of connection
  between Monophysitism and the doctrine of the Church"; on his premises "all idea of distinct
  hypostasis in God ought consistently to be renounced."
- **Bach** (Roman Catholic): the corpus drives "the concrete personality of the God-man… into
  the shade"; its mysticism "is not founded on the historical person of Christ, nor on the work
  of Redemption."
- **Bishop B. F. Westcott** (Anglican): "Many, perhaps, will be surprised that such a scheme of
  Christianity as Dionysius has sketched should even be reckoned Christian at all… not wholly of
  a Christian type."

Sparrow-Simpson's summary of the critics: Dionysius "was far more strong as a philosophic
thinker than he was as a Christian theologian," and "in his efforts to reconcile Christianity
with neo-Platonism it is the philosophy which prevails, not without serious results to the
theology of the Church."

### Reframing the question — Perl (2007)
[[perl-theophany|Eric Perl]] rejects the "Christian vs. Neoplatonist" framing itself as
"premised on a philosophically unsophisticated understanding of both traditions." He reads
Dionysius as **"Christianus simulque vere Platonicus"** — a coherent Neoplatonic philosopher
whose apophaticism and theophanic ontology follow rigorously from the principle that to be is to
be intelligible. On Perl's account the trinitarian doctrine "is fully present in Dionysius… but
it does not enter into his philosophical understanding of being as theophany" — i.e. the
metaphysical argument is independent of, not reducible to *or* dependent on, the confessional
frame. This dissolves the older dichotomy rather than taking a side within it.

## Status

**Open.** Unlike the [[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|authorship question]] (effectively settled),
the theological character of the corpus remains contested, and the positions track confessional
and philosophical commitments (Catholic and Orthodox defenders; Protestant and critical-idealist
critics; a recent philosophical reframing). The contradiction is preserved here, not
adjudicated.
