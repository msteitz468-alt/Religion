# The Johannine Secessionist Crisis — Who Were They and What Did They Teach?

## The Issue in Dispute

1 John 2:19 records that "they went out from us, but they did not really belong to us." A group of people who had been part of the Johannine community seceded, organized itinerant preachers to propagate their views among remaining churches (4:1–3; 2 John 7–11), and caused deep confusion and loss of assurance among those who remained. The author of 1 John calls them "antichrists" (2:18, 22; 4:3; see [[antichrist]]) and "false prophets" (4:1).

The question: what exactly did these secessionists teach, and what is the identity of their movement in relation to known early Christian heresies?

## What the Author Says About Their Teaching

Reconstructed from a careful mirror reading of 1 John and 2 John:

**Christological denials**:
- They denied that Jesus is the Christ (2:22)
- They denied that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:2–3; 2 John 7)
- They denied that Jesus came "by blood" — i.e., that the incarnate Jesus's death was salvifically necessary (5:6)
- They did not confess the Son (2:23)

**Claims they made**:
- They claimed to have fellowship with God while walking in darkness (1:6)
- They claimed to know God while not keeping his commands (2:4)
- They claimed to be without sin (1:8, 10)
- They claimed to live in the light while hating their brothers (2:9)
- They claimed a Spirit-given anointing superior to the teaching received "from the beginning" (2:20, 27; 4:1)

**Ethical failures noted**:
- Lack of brotherly love toward those remaining in the author's community (2:9–11; 3:14–15; 4:20)
- Ongoing sinful conduct despite claims to sinlessness (3:6–10)
- Indifference to the need to "do right" (3:10)

## Positions on Their Identity

### 1. Cerinthian / Proto-Cerinthian (Traditional)

**Proposal**: The secessionists held views closely related to Cerinthus, a first-century heretic known from Irenaeus (Against Heresies 1.26.1). Cerinthus taught: (a) the world was not made by the supreme God but by an inferior power; (b) Jesus was the natural son of Joseph and Mary, righteous but merely human; (c) at Jesus's baptism, "the Christ" descended upon him in the form of a dove; (d) before the crucifixion, "the Christ" departed from Jesus, so Jesus alone suffered and rose while the divine Christ remained impassible.

**Fit with 1 John**: The "water and blood" formulation (5:6 — "not by water only, but by water and blood") maps precisely onto the Cerinthian scheme: the Cerinthians would say Christ came "by water" (his baptism) but not "by blood" (his death). The tradition that John confronted Cerinthus personally in an Ephesian bathhouse (Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3.4, citing Polycarp) strengthens the link.

**Objections** (Marshall, Schnackenburg, Kruse): The Cerinthian system involves elaborate cosmological claims (inferior creator-god, aeons) and a specific Christological distinction between Jesus as son of the inferior creator-god and the Christ from above — none of which appears in 1 John's rebuttal. The secessionists also made claims to sinlessness and Spirit-given revelation that are not attested in our accounts of Cerinthus. The identification is historically attractive but not exact.

### 2. Docetist (Ignatian Parallel)

**Proposal**: Schnackenburg's preferred identification: the secessionists are closer to the Docetists attacked by Ignatius of Antioch in his early-second-century letters (especially To the Smyrnaeans 1–7). Ignatius's opponents denied the real humanity of Jesus, his genuine suffering, and his bodily resurrection. Their ethical failure: "They have no care for love, none for the widow, none for the orphan, none for the afflicted" (Smyr. 6.2).

**Fit with 1 John**: The parallel ethical failure (lack of love; indifference to those in need; 1 John 3:17) is striking. The christological denial (Jesus did not truly come "in the flesh") is also parallel. The Ignatian letters were written from Antioch to Asia Minor c. AD 107 — shortly after the Johannine letters were likely written, from the same geographic area.

**Objections** (Schnackenburg himself concedes): Ignatius's docetists had "strong links with Jewish beliefs and practices," which are absent from 1 John. The secessionists in 1 John had previously been members of the author's own community — their trajectory is internal, not an external invasion of an alien Jewish-Christian movement.

### 3. Internally Generated from the Fourth Gospel

**Proposal**: Brown's influential theory. The secessionists were not influenced by external Gnosticism or Cerinthianism but were members of the Johannine community who read the Gospel of John's high Christology in a way that overemphasized the divine nature of Christ and deemphasized his human suffering and death. Ironically, both the author of 1 John and the secessionists appealed to the Fourth Gospel; the secessionists thought they were the true Johanninans and regarded the author's position as revising back to a more primitive, lower Christology. According to Brown, the secessionists did not deny the incarnation but denied that Christ's being in the flesh was *salvific*.

**Fit with 1 John**: Explains why the secessionists were once in the community; explains why the author appeals to "what was from the beginning" as the criterion; explains why 1 John can be read as a corrective to possible misreadings of the Gospel.

**Objections** (Marshall): The fourth Gospel in any plausible form does not provide a basis for denying the salvific significance of Jesus's death (John 1:29; 6:51; 19:31–37) or for dispensing with Jesus as the path to the Father (14:6). Even a hypothetical earlier form of the Gospel that lacked the Prologue and various later additions could not have generated the secessionists' specific Christology. Marshall: "It is impossible to explain the theology of John's opponents as arising from a misreading of the Gospel." Kruse notes the same difficulty.

### 4. Greek Philosophical / Pre-Gnostic Influence (Weiss)

**Proposal**: Klaus Weiss argued that the secessionists were not really Gnostics in any developed sense, but people influenced by popular Greek philosophy who had moved to a form of Christianity in which Jesus had no essential role. They claimed direct knowledge of God (as Father), regarded themselves as sinless on the basis of this knowledge, were indifferent to the historical Jesus, and regarded the remaining members of the community as "worldly." The parallels are closer to Paul's opponents at Corinth than to Cerinthus.

**Objections** (Marshall): This reconstruction makes it mysterious how the secessionists could ever have been regarded as Christians or been members of a Christian community. The specific emphasis on "water and blood" (5:6) requires an explanation in terms of a system that separates the divine from the human in Jesus — which Weiss's Greek philosophy does not provide.

### 5. Spirit-Experience-Centered / Baptismal Theology (Klauck, Painter)

**Proposal**: The secessionists placed supreme emphasis on the experience of the Spirit received at baptism, which they believed gave them immunity from sin and direct access to God. For them, the decisive christological event was Jesus's own baptism (when the Spirit descended and "remained" on him — John 1:32–33); Jesus's death was unimportant. Klauck suggests they were also better-off economically, which explains why they provided meeting places and hospitality that could then be withdrawn when they seceded. Painter: the secessionists came from a gentile background shaped by mystery religions and their initiatory experience; what mattered was their participation in the divine *sperma* received at baptism.

**Fit with 1 John**: Explains the Spirit claims (4:1–3; 2:20, 27); explains the sinlessness claims as rooted in baptismal theology; explains the de-emphasis on the cross.

**Objections**: These proposals explain the experiential basis of the secessionists' theology but do not account for the sharp christological denial (that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, come in the flesh) that the author treats as the decisive dividing line.

## Assessment

[[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]]: "It seems most likely that the opponents were Christians who felt that they had moved beyond the elementary stages of orthodox theology to a new position which called orthodox affirmations into question. Under the influence of Docetism they argued against a real incarnation of the Son of God in Jesus, and probably adopted a view like that of Cerinthus or Basilides." He resists a precise identification; a combination of reliance on spiritual experience and a Gnostic-type world-view led to a Christianity in which Jesus Christ no longer occupied a central position.

[[colin-kruse|Kruse]]: Whatever the precise origin (misreading of the Gospel, background in mystery religion, Spirit experience, or external influence), the result was the same — a de-emphasizing of the incarnation and vicarious death of Christ and a concomitant de-emphasizing of the commands of Christ, especially the command to love one another.

The controversy is open. No proposed identification is exact; the secessionists appear to be a transitional phenomenon, not yet a fully developed Gnostic system, but exhibiting the seeds of the second-century Docetic/Gnostic movements that Irenaeus would combat.
