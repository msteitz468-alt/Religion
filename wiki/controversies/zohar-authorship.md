# The Authorship of the Zohar

Who wrote the [[zohar|Zohar]]? The question is among the most consequential in Jewish intellectual history, because the answer determines whether the foundational text of [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] is an ancient, quasi-revealed transmission from the age of the Mishnah or a brilliant medieval pseudepigraphon. The dispute pits the **traditional attribution** to the 2nd-century Tanna [[shimon-bar-yochai|Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai]] against the **critical consensus** identifying [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] (c. 1240–1305) as the author, and it has been live since the Zohar first appeared.

---

## The Positions

### 1. Traditional Attribution — Shim'on bar Yoḥai (2nd century CE)
The Zohar presents itself as the teaching of Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai (Rashbi) and his circle of Companions (*Ḥavrayya*), set in the Galilee of the 2nd century CE. On this view, the mystical wisdom it contains is ancient — received tradition (*qabbalah*) transmitted orally and esoterically until it was written down and "revealed" in 13th-century Spain. This is the position of traditional Kabbalah and remains normative in much of **Orthodox and Ḥaredi Judaism**, where the Zohar is venerated as a holy, authoritative, and effectively ancient text. Some traditionalists concede medieval editing or transmission while maintaining an ancient core.

**Arguments offered**:
- The Zohar's own internal testimony and consistent dramatic setting
- The doctrine of esoteric oral transmission, which explains the absence of earlier written attestation
- The text's saturation in tannaitic and amoraic names, settings, and idioms

### 2. Critical Consensus — Moses de León (late 13th century)
The dominant position in academic Jewish studies, established definitively by **Gershom Scholem**, is that the Zohar was composed in Castile in the last quarter of the 13th century, principally by Moses de León. See [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]].

**Arguments offered**:
- **Linguistic**: the Zohar's Aramaic is an artificial, often grammatically anomalous medieval construction — built largely from the Aramaic of the Babylonian Talmud and Targumim, with errors and Hebraisms no native 2nd-century Aramaic speaker would make
- **Anachronism**: the Zohar quotes or presupposes medieval sources — Rashi, Maimonides, the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, Spanish-Jewish philosophy and Neoplatonism, even medieval realia (e.g., references reflecting medieval customs and ideas)
- **Doctrinal**: the developed doctrine of ten sefirot, the divine feminine ([[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]), and the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] is wholly unattested before the 12th–13th centuries; it is inconceivable as 2nd-century rabbinic teaching
- **The widow's testimony**: the kabbalist **Isaac of Acre**, investigating the Zohar's origin shortly after de León's 1305 death, recorded that de León's widow stated her husband had written the work himself from his own mind and ascribed it to Shim'on bar Yoḥai for prestige and profit
- **Internal parallels**: de León's signed Hebrew works share the Zohar's distinctive vocabulary, doctrines, and even errors

### 3. Circle / Composite Authorship
A refinement of the critical view, associated especially with **Yehuda Liebes** ("How the Zohar Was Written"): the Zohar was not the product of a single pen but of a **circle** of Castilian kabbalists engaged in shared mystical and literary creativity, with de León as the central figure and redactor. This accounts for the work's stylistic unevenness, internal contradictions, and multiple literary strata (e.g., the distinct later units *Idrot*, *Raya Mehemna*, *Tiqqunei ha-Zohar*, which most scholars assign to different, somewhat later hands).

### 4. Ancient-Core / Layered Redaction
An intermediate position (held by some scholars and many traditionalists seeking accommodation): the Zohar preserves genuinely ancient mystical traditions and oral material reaching back to late antiquity, which medieval kabbalists collected, expanded, reworked, and committed to writing. The disagreement then becomes one of proportion — how much is ancient, how much medieval.

## Early Critics

The authorship question is not merely modern. Doubts were raised within a century or two of the Zohar's appearance:
- **Isaac of Acre** (early 14th c.) — conducted the earliest known investigation; his report is the source of the widow's testimony
- **Elijah Delmedigo** (15th c.), in *Beḥinat ha-Dat*, argued on historical grounds that the Zohar could not be ancient
- **Leon of Modena** (17th c.), in *Ari Nohem*, mounted a sustained critique of the Zohar's antiquity and authority
- **Jacob Emden** (18th c.), though himself a kabbalist, in *Mitpaḥat Sefarim* catalogued hundreds of anachronisms and internal problems, concluding much of the Zohar was post-talmudic — a particularly damaging critique because it came from within the traditionalist camp (motivated partly by anti-Sabbatian polemic)

---

## What Is at Stake

The controversy is not a dry question of dating; it touches the foundations of Kabbalistic authority:

- **If ancient** (Shim'on bar Yoḥai): the Zohar carries tannaitic authority, nearly on a par with the Mishnah; its theology of the sefirot, the divine feminine, and theurgy is part of the authentic ancient deposit of Torah
- **If medieval** (de León): the entire theosophical edifice of Kabbalah is a 13th-century innovation — profound and creative, but a human construction presented under a false attribution, raising sharp questions for those who treat it as authoritative revelation

Notably, the critical dating does **not** by itself diminish the Zohar's religious value for many — including scholars like [[daniel-matt|Daniel Matt]] — who regard it as one of the supreme achievements of the Jewish mystical imagination regardless of its date. Pseudepigraphy was an accepted and even pious literary mode (compare the pseudepigraphical attribution of other Jewish works — Enoch, the *Wisdom of Solomon*, much of the Heikhalot literature).

## Positions in Pritzker Edition Volume I Front Matter (Green and Matt)

*Recorded as **positions** stated in [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol1|Pritzker Vol. I]]; no adjudication.*

### Matt’s Translator’s Introduction (partial; defers full debate)

[[daniel-matt|Daniel C. Matt]], in the Translator’s Introduction alone, does **not** name Moses de León or re-argue the full pseudepigraphy dossier. He states that *Sefer ha-Zohar* “emerged mysteriously in medieval Spain (Castile) toward the end of the thirteenth century”; that it was “composed in Castile mostly in Aramaic”; that “author(s)” concocted the unique neo-Aramaic; and that the Zoharic oxymoron “new-ancient words” alludes to secrets “recently composed yet ascribed to ancient sources.” He models textual production as multi-year, piecemeal writing and booklet circulation, with no complete book likely until sixteenth-century print collation. The “complex question of authorship,” together with historical and literary context, he **defers** to [[arthur-green|Arthur Green]]’s introduction in the same volume.

### Green’s introduction — traditional rediscovery claim vs. critical reconstruction

Green frames two simultaneous receptions at first appearance (c. 1300): those who distributed the Zohar “claimed that it was an ancient text they had recently rediscovered,” composed in the circle of Rabbi Shim’on son of Yoḥai and his disciples in second-century land of Israel; while “a few of the more critical spirits in each century doubted the Zohar,” the great majority came to venerate it as holy revelation alongside Bible and Talmud. Green’s own critical reconstruction (as he presents it) includes these claims:

1. **Medieval dating of composition (not antiquity of writing).** Green holds there is “no question that the work was composed in the decades immediately preceding its appearance,” responding to literary works and historical events after **1270**; the **1280s** are “the most likely decade” for the main body, probably preceded by *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* and possibly other sections; composition may still have been ongoing when texts first appeared.

2. **De León as distributor and classic pseudepigrapher (Green’s terms).** Fragments were first distributed by [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]], who claimed they were copied from an ancient manuscript in his possession — “a classic technique of pseudepigraphy.” Green distinguishes: some believed antiquity and the MS literally; some fellow kabbalists may have known de León was the writer and still held the *content* ancient and authentic, joining a “grand literary fiction”; skeptics called the enterprise literary forgery from the start.

3. **Isaac of Acre / widow testimony (as Green retells it).** Isaac of Acre (arrived Castile 1305; account via Zacuto’s *Sefer Yuḥasin*) met de León shortly before death (de León affirmed the ancient MS); then the widow denied any manuscript and reported her husband’s pecuniary motive; others, denying the MS, claimed writing “through the power of the Holy Name.” Green notes opponents (especially **Heinrich Graetz**) used this to dismiss the Zohar as forgery and de León as charlatan, and Green questions taking the widow’s account as simple truth.

4. **Scholem’s single-authorship critical position (Green summarizing).** Green narrates modern scholarship as beginning with young **Gershom Scholem**’s attempt to refute Graetz: initial openness to earlier layers and multi-authorship, then reversal to the conclusion that **the entire Zohar was written by de León**, supported by language, Holy Land geography, relation to philosophy and earlier Kabbalah, historical markers, and especially philological comparison of Zoharic Aramaic quirks with de León’s Hebrew works (a claimed literary fingerprint). Green stresses that Scholem’s agreement with Graetz on *single* authorship did **not** mean agreement with Graetz’s low valuation of the work.

5. **Contemporary circle / multi-generation school (Green summarizing Liebes–Meroz).** Green reports that contemporary scholarship—especially **Yehuda Liebes** and its development by **Ronit Meroz**—has “parted company with Scholem on the question of single authorship.” On this position: de León wrote or edited long sections including the main narrative body, but is not the only writer; multiple literary layers; possible school of practitioners/writers from before 1270 into the early fourteenth century; *Midrash ha-Ne’lam* among oldest strata; *Tiqqunei Zohar* and *Ra’aya Meheimna* as a possible third “generation”; internal commentary and repeated rescensions of stories/homilies as cumulative evidence when joined to sectional and multi-voice factors. Green compares a Castilian multi-generation school to the more conservative three-generation Naḥmanidean kabbalistic school in Catalonia.

6. **Green’s meta-disclaimer.** Green opens the authorship discussion by saying origins have puzzled readers from the first and that “no simple and unequivocal statement as to the question of its authorship can be made even in our own day,” while still affirming medieval composition after 1270 as beyond question *on his critical premises*. The introduction as a whole he presents as a digest of post-Scholem scholarship rather than free invention.

These Green/Matt front-matter positions map onto the numbered positions above (traditional rediscovery claim ≈ §1; Scholem single-author de León ≈ refinement of §2; Liebes–Meroz school ≈ §3) without collapsing them into wiki-voice verdict.

## Body-internal claim — Zohar on Song of Songs (Pritzker Vol. XI)

*Source: [[hecker-zohar-pritzker-vol11|Hecker Pritzker Vol XI]] (standards reingest 2026-07-18). Zohar on Song of Songs body (Rashbi–Elijah dialogue).*

In the literary frame of *Zohar al Shir ha-Shirim*, Elijah states that Rabbi Shim’on’s words are written **above** before the Ancient of Days, while Elijah’s own words are written only **below**, by Rashbi’s hand. This is a **body-internal** hierarchy of authority (speaker claim within the composition), not a modern critical authorship argument. Recorded here as reception/self-authorization data relevant to the pseudepigraphic frame; does not adjudicate de León vs circle vs traditional positions. See [[zohar|The Zohar]] Vol. XI, [[shimon-bar-yochai|Shim’on bar Yoḥai]], [[joel-hecker|Joel Hecker]].

## Resolution Status

**Tradition-specific.** In academic scholarship the de León / Castilian-circle thesis is settled and effectively unanimous. In traditional Orthodox and especially Ḥaredi and Ḥasidic communities, the attribution to Shim'on bar Yoḥai remains affirmed and the Zohar is treated as an ancient, holy text. The two positions reflect fundamentally different epistemologies (historical-critical vs. traditional-authoritative) and are not adjudicable on shared premises — a paradigm case of the wiki's [[contradiction-protocol]] concerning critical-vs-confessional conflicts. Per the wiki's policy, both are recorded without adjudication.
