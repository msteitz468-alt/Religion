# Worldview of Hebrews — Apocalyptic or Neo-Platonic?

## The Question

Hebrews operates with a sustained heavenly/earthly dualism. Christ has entered a "heavenly sanctuary" (8:1–2; 9:11–12); the Levitical Tent was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (8:5); the earthly Tent was made "according to the pattern" (*typos*) shown on the mountain (8:5; cf. Exod 25:40); "the powers of the coming age" are accessible now (6:5); the faithful seek "a better country, that is, a heavenly one" (11:16). What philosophical or theological framework accounts for this structure?

Two background traditions have been proposed: (1) Philo's Platonic idealism — the material world is a changeable, ever-shifting copy of an eternal, unchanging noumenal world of ideas; and (2) Jewish apocalyptic — a concrete heavenly world where God dwells, combined with futuristic two-age eschatology (the present evil age / the coming age of salvation). The question is not merely academic: if Hebrews operates with Platonic dualism, the heavenly world is a timeless realm of ideas, the "rest" (3:7–4:11) may be Platonic repose, and eschatological arrival may occur at death rather than at Christ's return. If Hebrews operates with Jewish apocalyptic, the heavenly world is a concrete spatial realm entered by Christ at his exaltation, and all eschatological language points toward a future historical consummation.

## Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–AD 50)

Philo is the key comparative figure because he was an Alexandrian Jewish philosopher and exegete, roughly contemporary with the author of Hebrews, who interpreted the Hebrew Scriptures through the lens of Middle Platonism. For Philo:

- The **noumenal world** is eternal, changeless, and apprehended by rational contemplation. The **material world** is an ever-changing copy of the noumenal, without beginning or end.
- The **Logos** mediates between the two realms; salvation is the soul's liberation from the flesh and return to the changeless noumenal home.
- Philo affirms the **immortality of the soul**, not bodily resurrection; he explicitly rejects the Stoic doctrine of the world's future conflagration (*Eternity* 75–76).
- OT institutions — the Tent, the priesthood — are **allegorized** as symbols of this Platonic structure.

The overlap with Hebrews is real at the level of vocabulary and imagery: both use sophisticated Greek; both refer to a heavenly world; both interpret OT cultic texts; both share vocabulary items not found elsewhere in the NT. Spicq's magisterial commentary elevated this comparison to paradigmatic status.

## The Neo-Platonic Reading (Spicq, Thompson)

Ceslas Spicq's *L'Épître aux Hébreux* (1952–53) is the classic statement of neo-Platonic influence. On this reading:
- The "true Tent" (8:2; *alēthinos*) is the noumenal ideal, of which the Mosaic Tent is a material copy
- "Shadow" (*skia*, 8:5; 10:1) = the Platonic copy; "form" (*eikōn*, 10:1) = the Platonic ideal
- The Old Covenant is identified with the transitory material world; the New with the transcendent eternal world
- Something is "better" in Hebrews precisely because it belongs to the eternal, changeless heavenly realm

James Thompson's recent NICNT-series commentary carries forward the Spicq tradition with more methodological sophistication, arguing that Hebrews draws on a Jewish Hellenistic synthesis of apocalyptic and Platonic elements. Grässer, Braun, and especially Eisele extend this in increasingly radical directions.

**Eisele's extreme position**: the "rest" of 3:7–4:11 refers to changeless heavenly repose entered at death; the eschatological dimension of Hebrews is systematically subordinated to the spatial; language about Christ's return (9:27–28; 10:25, 36–39; 12:25–29) is reinterpreted or removed.

## The Apocalyptic Reading (Williamson, Hurst, Laansma, Adams, Cockerill)

Ronald Williamson's *Philo and the Epistle to the Hebrews* (1970) initiated the decisive critique of the Spicq reading. His method: to compare not just the shared vocabulary but the *use* of that vocabulary in its actual contexts. His finding: even where Hebrews and Philo use the same or similar terms to describe the heavenly world and related subjects, they invariably use them in different ways.

**Lincoln Hurst** (*The Epistle to the Hebrews: Its Background of Thought*, 1990) extended Williamson's work and argued for Jewish apocalyptic as the primary background frame. The apocalyptic writings (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, Dead Sea Scrolls) routinely combine (1) a present, already-existing heavenly world with (2) futuristic eschatology — God's coming world-shaking intervention at the end of the age. This is precisely Hebrews' structure.

**Jon Laansma** on the "rest" (1997): demonstrated conclusively that the *katapausis* ("rest") in 3:7–4:11 refers to the eschatological destiny of God's people at Christ's return, not to a Platonic state of changeless repose. The argument from Gen 2:2 (Heb 4:4) shows that God's own "rest" is the eschatological Sabbath rest still open for the people of God. The Promised Land under Joshua was not the ultimate rest (4:8); Ps 95:11 was still open "today" when David wrote — which means the true rest has always been eschatological.

**Edward Adams** (*The Stars Will Fall from Heaven*, 2007; and on the heavenly world in Hebrews): the heavenly world is a concrete spatial realm — the actual place where Christ now sits at God's right hand — not a neo-Platonic world of abstract ideas. Christ physically entered it (9:11–12, 24); the faithful will concretely enter it (12:22–24); the Judgment will physically shake both earth and heaven (12:26–28). This is not Platonism; it is Jewish apocalyptic cosmology.

**Cockerill’s structural/dating argument** (R1 L1436–1466): If the old Mosaic order were identified with the Platonic material world, the author would have used the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple (AD 70) as the decisive argument for its demise. Instead he argues entirely from Pentateuchal *typology* — the internal descriptions of the old order show its own inadequacy as a means of salvation. This argument is more appropriate for a situation *before* the Temple’s fall (though Cockerill will not narrow the date beyond AD 50–90 with certainty).

**Cockerill on apocalyptic two-age + heavenly world** (R1 L1416–1422): Hebrews is not an apocalypse but uses futuristic two-age eschatology with NT two-stage intervention (first coming: definitive dealing with sin; second: final salvation, judgment, unshakable kingdom). Interest in heavenly world suggests closer apocalyptic ties (1 Enoch, 2 Baruch, 4 Ezra; DSS), but belief is *Christologically/exegetically* established (Ps 110:1 fulfillment), not mere tradition; no speculative heavenly topography; heavenly world clarifies present access/grace for perseverance until final entrance. **Old Covenant must not be equated with the evil “old age”** — covenantal three ages: anticipation / present drawing near / future entrance; misreading drives errors at 8:13 and 9:8–10 (R1 L1422).

## Key Exegetical Battlegrounds

### Hebrews 8:1–5 — The “True Tent” (not Philo)

*"Now the point in what we are saying is this: we have such a high priest, one who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven, a minister in the holy places, in the true tent that the Lord set up, not man.… They serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things. For when Moses was about to erect the tent, he was instructed by God, saying, 'See that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain.'"*

**Neo-Platonic reading (Spicq, Thompson — kept intact)**: “True tent” (*alēthinos*) = the noumenal ideal. The earthly Tent is a “copy and shadow” (*hypodeigma kai skia*) in the Platonic sense — a material imitation of the changeless heavenly original. The old order thus represents the world of becoming; the new, the world of being. Philo develops the universe as God’s sanctuary accessible to the senses, copy of a heavenly sanctuary accessible only to the mind.

**Apocalyptic reading (Cockerill densified, R7 L3004–3040)**: “Despite many protestations to the contrary, [the pastor] has little in common with Philo. The Sanctuary of Hebrews is not a heavenly sanctuary of eternal ideas perceived only by the mind… The pastor speaks of the ‘place’ of God’s presence already entered by the incarnate and exalted Son… Unlike Philo, Hebrews never refers to the physical world using Tent imagery” (R7 L3004–3006). “True” does not condemn the Mosaic Tent as “false,” nor identify the heavenly Tent with Philo’s invisible sanctuary — true because it is where salvation accomplished by Christ brings eternal fellowship with God (R7 L3012–3016). Sanctuary = Tent = heaven itself / Most Holy Place only — no two-part heavenly temple needed once the Son enters God’s presence (R7 L3008–3010). “Pattern and shadow” underscore derivative, symbolic, *anticipatory* character of earthly Tent — not a pattern of eternal ideas or shadow of a timeless ministry always ongoing in heaven (R7 L3030–3034). Exod 25:40 grounds typology; Philo uses the same verse for sensible copy of noetic pattern *without* antiquating rituals; Hebrews: prefiguration + self-revealed inadequacy; continued participation in old Tent services denies Christ’s sufficiency (R7 L3036–3040).

### Hebrews 10:1 — *Skia* and *Eikōn*

*"For since the law has but a shadow of the good things to come instead of the true form of these realities…"*

**Neo-Platonic reading**: *skia* (shadow) = Platonic copy; *eikōn* (form, image) = Platonic ideal. The law belongs to the world of appearance; Christ’s sacrifice to the world of reality.

**Apocalyptic reading (Cockerill densified, R8 L3330–3332)**: *Skia* is temporal — an intimation/pattern of good things to come (what has come in Christ, 9:11, plus final salvation at second coming, 9:28), not a copy of an eternal original. “The pastor’s use of the terms translated ‘shadow’ and ‘reality’ betrays the distance between his worldview and the Platonic dualism espoused by Philo. Philo uses both words almost synonymously to describe things in the created world as temporal, transitory copies of the eternal world.… The pastor, however, *contrasts* these two terms. The first is not a somewhat deceptive reflection of the eternal but a divinely ordained, though imperfect, anticipation and foreshadowing of God’s full salvation to come” (R8 L3332). The law’s very ineffectiveness is part of how it pointed forward. Philo’s memorial-of-sin teaching (offerings of the unjust) also differs: Hebrews teaches *all* Old Covenant sacrifices were a memorial of sin because of the impotency of the sacrifices themselves (R8 L3340–3342).

### Hebrews 3:7–4:11 — The “Rest”

**Neo-Platonic reading**: The “rest” is changeless heavenly repose, entered at death by those who transcend the world of change. Eisele presses this to the extreme of removing Christ’s return from the passage.

**Apocalyptic reading (Laansma, Cockerill densified R4 L2299–2353)**: Rest is **local and future** — a place of joining God penultimately at death, finally at Judgment; identity with homeland/City (R4 L2301). Continuous present “we are entering” = process of entering, not secured past belief as guarantee (R4 L2315). Two image-fields for one reality: Promised Land language for final perseverance/entrance at return; Most Holy Place language for present draw-near — converge at 12:22–24 (R4 L2299). When the created order passes at Judgment (12:26–27), this rest (City / Unshakable Kingdom) remains — “beyond the created order… a different, that is, ‘eternal,’ dimension of reality” — apocalyptic-concrete destiny, not Platonic abstraction of rest-as-state alone (R4 L2353). God’s Sabbath rest (Gen 2:2) + Ps 95 “today” show the rest was never mere earthly Canaan (Joshua did not give it, 4:8). Explicit horizon: Christ will appear a second time for salvation (9:28).

### Hebrews 9:23–24 — “Better Sacrifices” / Heavenly Cleansing

*"Thus it was necessary for the copies of the heavenly things to be purified with these rites, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these. For Christ has entered, not into holy places made with hands, which are copies of the true things, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf."*

**Neo-Platonic concern**: If the heavenly sanctuary needed purification, does this not imply contamination by material change — and is this not Platonic cosmology?

**Apocalyptic/Cockerill reading (R8 L3282–3286; R1 L1438)**: “Those who contend that Hebrews is dependent on the radical heavenly/earthly dichotomy characteristic of Platonic dualism have the most difficulty explaining how the heavenly world required cleansing.” Defilement = **objective impediment** to access (sins of the people polluted the Mosaic sanctuary; likewise the barrier before God). Christ removes barrier and threat of judgment — not reducible to conscience-cleansing alone or interior metaphor; objective new fellowship possibility (R8 L3282). Sacrifice does **not** take place “in heaven”; the earthly, incarnate, obedient self-offering of the eternal Son (10:5–10) *provides* entrance by purifying the conscience (9:14) and establishing the New Covenant (R1 L1438). Christ entered “heaven itself” to appear before God’s face *on our behalf* (R8 L3284–3286).

## Cockerill’s Positive Framework (densified 2026-07-18)

Hebrews’ worldview, on Cockerill’s reading, is an **apocalyptic two-age + heavenly world** structure, developed Christologically:

1. **Two ages**: Present age (between first and second comings) = Christ’s session and high-priestly intercession; coming age initiated by return in judgment (12:25–29). Foretastes now (6:5; 10:15–18); fullness not yet (R1 L1416).

2. **Heavenly world**: Concrete spatial realm — God’s actual throne room — entered by Christ at exaltation (1:3, 13; 8:1–2; 9:24). “Heavenlyness” is eschatological, not Platonic: dwelling of God toward which pilgrims move. Faithful “have come” to Mount Zion / City of the living God / heavenly Jerusalem (12:22) — present privilege uniting preliminary access and ultimate destiny (R11 L4149–4153). “Spirits of righteous made perfect” = apocalyptic term for dead faithful awaiting resurrection — one people perfected through Christ (R11 L4161).

3. **Three covenantal stages** (Cockerill, R1 L1422): (a) anticipation (Old Covenant typological foreshadowing — *not* the evil old age); (b) present “drawing near” through Christ’s high priesthood; (c) future final entrance at return.

**What “better” does not mean** (R1 L1438): Something is not “better” merely because it is *heavenly* in a Platonic sense. New Covenant better for forgiveness/heart transformation; sacrifice better as incarnate obedient self-offering of the eternal Son; true Tent evidences superior priesthood but is not its *cause*; only the people’s “home” is “better” because heavenly (eternal dwelling of God). Old Covenant/Mosaic Tent as symbols of the transitory physical world has **no textual basis**. “Outside the camp” is not the heavenly world but the place of Christ’s suffering (13:11–13).

### 12:25–29 — Shake / Removal, not Platonic dualism (R11 L4191–4199)

**Neo-Platonic pressure point**: Some argue from Platonic dualism that future Judgment is not primary — contrast “changeability” of created order with unchanging heavenly world entered at death; *metathesis* read as changeability rather than removal.

**Cockerill’s anti-Platonist position for 12:26–27**: The pastor “is neither a Platonist, nor is he indebted to Platonic dualism in this passage.” Usage elsewhere gives no genuine support for “changeability” over **“removal”** (*metathesis*; cf. 7:12 / 7:18 abolishing “change”). Does not acknowledge resurrection if flattened to death-entry only. “The things that can be shaken” are not related to “the things that cannot be shaken” as flesh to spirit — different because of differing relationships to God: God *made* the world in six days, but by entering his *rest* brought into being the eternal City — both beyond creation and creation’s divinely intended goal (R11 L4195–4199). Hag 2:6 paraphrase (not only earth but also heavens) presses absolute finality of Judgment, not Platonic sky/idea dualism (R11 L4191–4193). Sinai/Zion in 12:18–24 are **two present possibilities** (apostasy/exclusion vs faith/blessing), not earthly copy of heavenly or old/new religions or anti-Judaism polemic (R11 L4125).

## The Philo Question

Philo is a legitimate comparandum at the level of **semantics, vocabulary, and rhetorical resources**. Both authors inhabit the Hellenistic world; both use sophisticated philosophical Greek; both refer to a heavenly world using some overlapping terminology. Philo can illuminate what a particular word meant in first-century Alexandrian Greek.

But Philo's *worldview* — the eternity of the material world, the immortality of the soul rather than bodily resurrection, the exclusively contemplative path to God, the absence of any futuristic eschatology, the cosmos as an eternal copy of timeless ideas — is alien to Hebrews on every point. Philo never anticipates a *second coming*, a *final judgment*, or a *new heaven and earth*. Hebrews repeatedly and emphatically does (9:27–28; 10:36–39; 12:25–29). This is the "essential gulf" (Williamson) that the semantic overlap cannot bridge.

## Resolution Status

**Open at the scholarly level, but with significant weight on the apocalyptic side** (as Cockerill and allies argue). Williamson’s philological work (1970) was never adequately answered by neo-Platonic proponents. Laansma on the “rest” and Adams on the heavenly world reinforce the apocalyptic reading at crucial points. Eisele’s extreme position (eliminating Christ’s return) has found little following. Thompson’s nuanced synthesis remains the most sophisticated current neo-Platonic/Jewish-Hellenistic case and continues to have advocates. Spicq’s magisterial commentary remains the classic statement of the neo-Platonic influence thesis. The debate remains active; this page records positions without adjudicating.

Practical interpretive stakes: if the apocalyptic reading is correct, Hebrews’ warnings concern eschatological inheritance *at Christ’s return*, not Platonic repose at death; and the old order is a *typological* anticipation of Christ, not a symbol of the transitory material world. The two families of reading produce substantially different emphases throughout.
