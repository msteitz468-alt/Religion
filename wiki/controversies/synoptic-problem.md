# The Synoptic Problem

The **Synoptic Problem** is the question of how the literary relationship among
[[matthew|Matthew]], [[mark|Mark]], and [[luke|Luke]] is to be explained: the three
gospels share large stretches of common material, often in the same order and sometimes
in near-identical wording, while also diverging in ways no single model of copying
accounts for cleanly.

This page records positions. It does not adjudicate among them.

## Positions Recorded

### Markan priority with a two-document hypothesis

[[rt-france|France]] summarizes the position that held near-unquestioned consensus for
about a century: Mark was written first, and Matthew and Luke each drew on Mark and on a
body of shared non-Marcan tradition conventionally labelled **Q**, often supposed to be a
single lost document. France reports that in recent debate "everything seems, in the view
of some scholars, to be up for grabs again."

### Matthean priority

France reports a **vocal minority** reviving the view held almost without exception by the
church until the mid-nineteenth century — that Matthew was the earliest gospel to be
written. He records this as a live revival without endorsing it.

### France's own position (NICNT *Matthew*, 2007)

France declines to attempt even an overview of the problem, calling it much more complex
than the questions of authorship, provenance, and date, and noting the intensification of
debate since the 1970s. What he does state, he states as his own working stance:

**He retains Markan priority.** France cannot explain how Mark could have been written by
someone with the fuller Matthew in front of him: such a writer would have had to omit the
whole [[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]] while adding inconsequential narrative
detail, and the suggestion that Mark was uninterested in Jesus' teaching he judges "simply
untrue." He concludes, "I continue to believe in the priority of Mark."

His clearest ground for Marcan formative influence is **overall narrative structure**.
Since (on his reading) Mark's single north-to-south journey is an artificial literary
construction — [[gospel-of-john|John]] more faithfully reflecting the actual pattern of
Jesus' movements — France judges it improbable that two or three writers made the same
literary decision independently. Mark must therefore be "the foundation gospel among the
three."

**But he rejects "x-copied-y" models.** France denies that Mark's non-use of Matthew
entails that Matthew "used" Mark in the sense of sitting with a copy and consciously
editing it. The "simple x-copied-y approach," he writes, is "more appropriate to a modern
scholar's study than to the real world of first-century church tradition." He judges that
recent debate has made it impossible to claim the simple two-document hypothesis is a
total solution: the data are too complex and multifaceted.

**He prefers fluid mutual influence.** France inclines to the view promoted by
[REPORTED: E. P. Sanders] and developed by [REPORTED: J. A. T. Robinson] that neat
theories of literary dependence — "even complex ones like that of Boismard" — cannot do
justice to the data, and that we should think instead of a more fluid process of mutual
influence between the various centres of Christian gospel writing as people travelled and
consulted. He proposes seeing the Synoptics as **at least partially parallel developments
of common traditions** rather than as standing in a simple line of dependence. This
coheres with his separate proposal that all three reached final form within the sixties
rather than across twenty-plus years.

**He doubts a unitary Q.** France counts himself among a growing number who find a single
document "Q" an improbably simple hypothesis. He is happy to speak of "**Q tradition**"
(oral or written, not necessarily a single source) but not of Matthew "editing Q" as a
fixed text unavailable to us; and the "even more esoteric and hypothetical debates about
'the Q community' or 'recensions of Q'," he writes, "leave me cold."

**He is reluctant to speak of redaction.** France states his stance toward redaction
criticism explicitly: he is more reluctant than many interpreters to speak simply of how
Matthew "redacted" Mark's material or "adapted" the common tradition in Q material. He
regards the Marcan and Lucan parallels as **other witnesses to the traditions Matthew had
available, not necessarily as his direct sources**; a difference between them may mean
deliberate alteration, or may mean reception of the tradition in a different form. His
commentary, he says, will call attention to Synoptic differences that highlight Matthew's
distinctive contribution without always assuming direct dependence and deliberate
alteration — results that may resemble a rigid x-copied-y approach but "are likely to be
more cautiously expressed."

**Mark earliest is not Mark original.** France regards Mark as the earliest surviving
compilation but denies that this means Mark's version of every saying or event must be the
"original" from which the others deviate.

## Evidence France Adduces

### Matthean abbreviation, quantified

Across the commentary France repeatedly quantifies how drastically Matthew's parallels
compress Mark's:

| Pericope | Matthew | Mark |
|---|---|---|
| Gadarene demoniacs | 135 words | 330 words |
| Woman with the hemorrhage | 48 words | 154 words |
| Jairus's daughter | 90 words | 192 words |
| Death of [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] | 136 words | 249 words |

France notes that Mark's extended description of "Legion" is reduced in Matthew to two
"almost banal words."

### Against the doublet explanation

Against the usual dismissal of Matt 9:27-31 (the two blind men) as "a colorless doublet"
of 20:29-34, France argues at length that it is **more likely an independent tradition
preserved only by Matthew**, given how much of it is not paralleled: the following, the
house, the faith question, the fierce warning, and the disobedience. He reverses the usual
judgment about which pericope deserves the label: it is 9:32-34, he writes, "more
appropriately than in relation to the preceding pericope," that is a Matthean doublet
(with 12:22-24), since little there is distinctive.

### Other data France cites

- Of the nine [[miracles|miracle]] stories in Matt 8-9, six are paralleled in both Mark
  and Luke and one (8:5-13) in Luke only.
- Luke, on France's reading, moved the Nazareth story out of its natural narrative place
  to serve as a "frontispiece" for the Galilean ministry, while Matthew and Mark retain
  its natural position — a redactional judgment he makes about Luke, not Matthew.
- Matthew's version of the unforgivable-sin saying combines elements found separately in
  Mark 3:28-29 and Luke 12:10.
- The parallels to the material in Matthew's five discourses are widely scattered across
  the other Synoptics; since Mark and especially Luke had no obvious reason to dismember
  existing sermons, France's "natural conclusion is that it is Matthew who has collected
  related material together."

## France's Position in the NIGTC Mark (2002; re-ingested in full 2026-07-18)

France's earlier and fuller statement of his stance, from *The Gospel of Mark* (NIGTC, 2002;
see [[france-mark-nigtc]]), is consistent with the NICNT position above and adds detail:

- **The reopened question.** France reports that "the publication of W. R. Farmer's *The
  Synoptic Problem* (1964) marked the first significant reopening of what had been generally
  regarded… as a closed question," resurrecting the Griesbach hypothesis (Matthean priority,
  Mark as mediating gospel); he notes Mann's Anchor Bible commentary interprets Mark as a
  deliberate revision of Matthew.
- **Three post-1964 alternatives.** He surveys Griesbach; Marcan priority without a
  documentary Q (Goulder, or a looser "Q strand"); and the Sanders–Robinson view that gospel
  formation was more complex and fluid than linear dependence — the strand he finds most
  persuasive.
- **Cross-fertilisation with Marcan priority.** France adopts [REPORTED: J. A. T. Robinson]'s
  "cross-fertilisation" model — gospels grew for continuous church use, with traditions shared
  among mobile communities — while maintaining Marcan priority on the ground that Mark's
  privileged Petrine access made his work the most significant single component used by
  Matthew and Luke. Marcan priority does not entail that Mark's version of every tradition is
  earliest: "it does not seem to me necessary to conclude that at all points Mark represents
  an earlier form of the tradition than that preserved in either Matthew or Luke" — he treats
  synoptic versions "as parallel rather than derivative" at times.
- **The signature conclusion.** "In the light of that situation, I do not need a solution to
  the Synoptic Problem."
- **Patristics and priority.** France observes that patristic views of Mark's origins are
  "found guilty by association" with the fathers' mistaken belief in Matthean priority.

### Data points France logs in the Mark commentary

- The dual time expression of Mk 1:32 has been claimed as evidence both for and against
  Markan priority "but cannot ultimately prove anything."
- Matthew and Luke may have omitted Mk 2:27 as "too sweeping and radical in its implications
  for halakhic authority"; both read 2:28 christologically.
- Mark 6:45 begins Luke's "Great Omission" (nothing of Mk 6:45–8:26 in Luke).
- The staff discrepancy (Mark permits, Matthew/Luke forbid, Mk 6:8) is "a genuinely
  unresolved harmonisation test case," of more importance for gospel-harmonisation debates
  than for exegesis.
- Matthew's and Luke's omission of the saliva healings (Mk 7:31-37; 8:22-26) likely reflects
  distaste for their "almost 'magical'" technique — "neither Matthew nor Luke ever mentions
  the use of saliva in healing."
- Mark's "after three days" versus Matthew/Luke's "on the third day" is Jewish idiom
  ("the day after tomorrow"), not a difference of meaning.
- Mark and Matthew disagree on whether the temple charge met the two-witness criterion
  (Mk 14:56-59 vs. Matt 26:60): "there is no way of discovering whether Mark or Matthew is
  historically right."
- On the parable of the tenants, France notes recent scholarship no longer assumes the
  simpler Gospel of Thomas 65 version must be earlier: "The days are long gone when it was
  axiomatic that Jesus never used allegory."
- On the ending of Mark, France's "reasonable guess" is that the lost ending resembled
  Matthew's; that Matthew's ending was actually *based on* Mark's lost ending "is perhaps to
  push conjecture to the limits." See [[mark-ending|The Ending of Mark]].

## Relation to Dating

France's dating proposal and his source-critical stance are mutually supporting rather
than independent. He endorses the proposal that the main period of Synoptic composition
was **the sixties**, taking his cue from the lack of reference in Acts to any events later
than AD 62. He judges that the most influential reason for a late date for Matthew is not
textual argument but "a relative dating scheme which is widely adopted in current
scholarship, but which has few if any fixed points." Recorded as his position; the
majority view (fourth quarter of the first century) is recorded on [[matthew|the Matthew
page]].

## Notes on Evidence Quality

- France's discussion in the NICNT Introduction is explicitly a **statement of conclusions
  without argument**. He refers the argument to his *Matthew: Evangelist and Teacher*
  (1989) and states he will not seek to persuade anyone in the commentary. Citing this
  volume for his *reasons* on Synoptic questions therefore overstates what it contains.
- Named scholars recorded above (Sanders, Robinson, Boismard) are those France names in
  his running text. The ebook conversion from which this material was extracted dropped
  all footnote bodies, so further attributions France may have made in notes are not
  recoverable.
