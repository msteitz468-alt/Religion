# The Tritheist Controversy (sixth century)

The tritheist controversy was a mid-sixth-century dispute, primarily **within the [[oriental-orthodoxy|miaphysite]] communion**, over whether the introduction of "particular natures/substances" into Christology entailed recognizing three substances — and so "three Gods" — in the Trinity. In Johannes Zachhuber's reading ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §5.3), the controversy was "not directly tied to any particular understanding of universals and particulars" but "consisted in the doctrine that the particular substances in the Trinity ought to be numbered as three" — the unavoidable consequence of "the introduction into the classical theory of particular substances which could and arguably should be counted." Chalcedonians watched "mostly as outsiders occasionally moderating."

## Origins

John Ascoutzanges, a student of the philosopher Samuel (or Peter) of Rešaina, first taught tritheism c. 556/557: "I confess natures, substances, and divinities according to the number of hypostases." Condemned, he produced "a book of excerpts" arguing the Fathers had "counted natures and divinities in the Trinity." The label "tritheist" is, Zachhuber stresses, "a term of abuse coined by their opponents" — used by Theodosius as a *reductio ad absurdum*; Ascoutzanges did not initially present his teaching as a confession of three Gods.

## Positions

- **Theodosian anti-tritheism.** Theodosius of Alexandria (*Theological Oration*, after 560) **conceded the factual point** that the Fathers sometimes used *physis*/*ousia* for individual hypostases, but refused to number three substances in the Trinity: "It is not right to introduce a number of natures or substances into the holy and consubstantial Trinity." Zachhuber judges that "the difference between his own position and that of his tritheistic opponents is not particularly robust"; ultimately Theodosius fell back on apophatic appeal — the Trinity "is above all substances and natures."

- **Philoponite tritheism.** [[john-philoponus|John Philoponus]], drawn in c. 567 (a decade late, perhaps deferring while Patriarch Theodosius lived, d. 566), accepted "three individual substances" in the Trinity, homoousios but distinct: "we do not speak of three divinities of different substances... but call those divinities homoousioi." His argument: a common nature is *ipso facto* divisible — "if it was not divisible, it was not common"; and the universal "does not have proper existence... but is either nothing or merely constituted in our mind" (citing Aristotle, *De An.* A.1). God is one only "in mind and reason"; "in his hypostases... there are three substances and three gods." For Zachhuber the decisive shift was Philoponus's "new willingness to speak of 'three substances, natures, and divinities'... as a direct consequence of the Severan postulation of particular natures."

- **Chalcedonian critique.** Anastasius of Antioch, "the most outspoken (known) Chalcedonian opponent," argued that introducing particular substances "led inevitably to tritheism" (πολυθεΐας ἀρχὴ τὸ μερικὰς ὀνομάζειν φύσεις) — the same charge later pressed by [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]], who cited Philoponus's *Arbiter* as evidence.

## Significance and Aftermath

The controversy forced both miaphysite and Chalcedonian thinkers to address what the classical theory had left unspecified — the **individuation of the universal**. Miaphysite anti-tritheists ([[damian-of-alexandria|Damian of Alexandria]], [[peter-of-callinicus|Peter of Callinicus]]) reasserted the classical theory; Chalcedonians ([[pamphilus-the-theologian|Pamphilus]], [[theodore-of-raithu|Theodore of Raïthu]]) avoided "particular natures" by introducing "individual natures" instead, and increasingly drew on [[pseudo-dionysius|pseudo-Dionysius]] for language affirming the supersubstantial oneness of the divine ousia. Per the **Contradiction Protocol**, the wiki records the disputants' positions without adjudicating; tritheism was historically condemned. The early-modern afterlife of the question (Petavius, Bingham, Cudworth) is noted on [[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|the source page]].
