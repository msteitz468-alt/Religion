# The Fadak Inheritance Dispute

The dispute over Fadak (a fertile oasis near Khaibar) and related prophetic properties is one of
the foundational grievances separating [[sunni-islam|Sunni]] and [[shia-islam|Shia]] Islam. It
concerns whether [[muhammad|Muhammad]]'s daughter [[fatima|Fatima]] was entitled to inherit from
his estate, and whether [[abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]]'s refusal was legally correct or a political
injustice.

---

## The Sunni Position (Sahih al-Bukhari)

**Primary hadith** (No. 325, narrated by Aisha): After the Prophet's death, Fatima "asked Abu
Bakr As-Siddiq to give her, her share of inheritance from what Allah's Apostle had left of the
Fai (i.e. booty gained without fighting) which Allah had given him. Abu Bakr said to her,
'Allah's Apostle said, "Our property will not be inherited, whatever we (i.e. prophets) leave
is Sadaqa (to be used for charity)."'"

**The properties at issue**: Khaibar (land), Fadak (the oasis), and the Prophet's charity-
designated property in Medina.

**Abu Bakr's ruling**: All of it is *sadaqa* (charitable property) belonging to the community,
not personal property that can be inherited. Prophets do not leave inheritable estates.

**Fatima's response**: She stopped speaking to Abu Bakr until her death, which came six months
after the Prophet's. "Fatima remained alive for six months after the death of Allah's Apostle."

**The Ali and Abbas arbitration** (No. 326, narrated by Malik bin Aus): Later, both Ali and Abbas
came before Umar disputing the management of the properties. Umar confirmed Abu Bakr's position —
citing the same hadith — and said he would hand over management to them under the same covenant
the Prophet had used (the proceeds administered for the community's welfare), not as personal
inheritance. He assigned the Medina property to Ali and Abbas jointly to manage under these terms.

**Ali's subsequent acknowledgment** (Book 57, No. 60, narrated by Aisha): When Ali came to Abu
Bakr after Fatima's death, he said: "O Abu Bakr! We acknowledge your superiority." This is
narrated as Ali's public deference to Abu Bakr's ruling, though the Shia tradition interprets
this context very differently.

**Prophetic confirmation** (No. 328, Abu Huraira): "My heirs should not take even a single
Dinar… whatever I leave, excluding the expenditure of my wives and my laborers, will be Sadaqa."

---

## The Shia Position

Shia tradition holds that:
1. The prophetic non-inheritance hadith is fabricated or misapplied — Q 27:16 states Solomon
   "inherited" David, and Q 19:6 uses inheritance language for Zechariah and Yahya. These texts
   support the view that prophets do leave inheritable estates.
2. Abu Bakr's refusal was not a legal ruling but a political act depriving Fatima of her due.
3. Fatima's estrangement from Abu Bakr until her death is itself significant: the Prophet had
   said "whoever makes Fatima angry makes me angry" (a hadith also in Bukhari, Book 57 No. 111).
4. Fadak specifically was a *fai* (gift without fighting) that the Prophet had already given
   to Fatima during his lifetime; Abu Bakr's refusal to honor that transfer was an additional
   injustice.

The Shia account is documented in the *Khutbat al-Fadakiyya* (Fatima's Fadak sermon), preserved
in Shia sources, in which she addresses Abu Bakr and Umar directly and cites multiple Quranic
verses in favor of her claim.

---

## Significance

The Fadak dispute is not merely a property disagreement. In Shia theology, it signals:
- The usurpation of the caliphate from Ali
- The deliberate marginalization of the Prophet's family (*Ahl al-Bayt*)
- The beginning of the corruption of the Islamic polity after the Prophet's death

In Sunni theology, Abu Bakr's ruling is a faithful application of an authentic prophetic hadith;
his refusal of Fatima's claim demonstrates his strict adherence to law even under family
pressure.

---

## Textual Note on the Aisha Hadith (Bukhari No. 325)

The main Sunni hadith (No. 325) is transmitted by **Aisha** — a contested source for this
episode, since Aisha and Fatima were rivals in the Prophet's household, and the Shia tradition
regards Aisha's testimony in matters touching Fatima's interests as structurally compromised.
Both the Sunni and Shia traditions are aware of this dynamic and argue it respectively.
