# Ezekiel 20:25–26 — Did Yahweh Give "Laws That Were Not Good"?

In Ezekiel's revisionist history of Israel (ch. 20), Yahweh says of the second wilderness
generation — in Block's translation (NICOT, 1997) — "Indeed, I gave them no-good decrees,
and laws that did not offer them life. I defiled them through their gifts, when they offered
up every firstborn of the womb, so I might devastate them." Block notes that Yahweh as the
subject of *ṭimmēʾ* ("defile," Piel) occurs nowhere else in the OT, and that *haʿăbîr* is a
terminus technicus for child sacrifice. The verse appears to make God the giver of bad laws
and the defiler of his own people — the sharpest theological scandal in the chapter.

## Positions

**Patristic — punitive or restraining legislation.** As surveyed by Block: Justin Martyr read
the laws as punishment for abandoning God; Irenaeus as enslaving legislation imposed after
the golden calf (developed by later "Second Legislation" writers); Chrysostom as "a kind of
rein to hinder their license"; Origen argued disobedience turned good laws bad — the literal
reading kills, the spiritual reading gives life.

**Targumic and rabbinic softenings.** The Targum shifts the agency: the people "obeyed
religious decrees which were not proper." Rabbinic tradition tamed the verse variously —
non-rewarding diaspora rules, a Talmudic melody reading, medieval "ordinances of the
Gentiles" (Block's survey).

**Modern critical solutions.** Block enumerates seven: softening *šimmēm*; reversing the
verse order as blasphemous popular speech; deleting v. 26 as interpolation; reading rhetorical
questions; good laws literally misapplied under Ahaz/Manasseh; an analogy to the hardening of
Pharaoh; and a literal negative canonical judgment on (part of) the Mosaic law (e.g., the
firstborn law of Exod 22:28–29 read as commanding sacrifice).

**Davis/Block — anti-domestication.** Ellen Davis holds that the statement "resists all
attempts at domestication": the bad laws are an "anti-gift" expressing Yahweh's authority
"to determine and interpret the course of human history." Block endorses this and adds that
"any attempt to correlate this statement with a historical event is mistaken" — ch. 20 is
rhetoric, not chronicle; Ezekiel "is a preacher, not a chronicler or a systematic theologian,"
and "the force of Ezekiel's rhetorical strategy lies precisely in its nonconformity with human
reason." He notes textual signals against equating the no-good laws with Sinai: they are given
to the second generation, separated from the life-giving laws, and marked by the anomalous
masculine *ḥuqqîm* against Ezekiel's usual feminine *ḥuqqôt*.

No position is adjudicated here; the dispute is live across traditions and eras.
