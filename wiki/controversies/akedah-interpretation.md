The *Akedah* (עֲקֵדָה, "binding") — the account in [[genesis|Genesis 22]] of God's command to Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac, Abraham's obedient journey to Moriah, and the last-moment intervention of an angel — is among the most theologically dense and controversy-generating passages in the Hebrew Bible. No single interpretation has dominated across traditions; the episode has been read as divine testing, cultic etiology, typological prophecy, existentialist paradigm, and ethical horror.

## The Narrative

God commands Abraham: "Take your son, your only son whom you love — Isaac — and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains that I will show you" (22:2). Abraham obeys, journeys three days, binds Isaac on the altar, raises the knife — and is stopped by an angel, who declares Abraham's fear of God proven. God provides a ram as the substitute. God then reaffirms the covenant promises with an oath: "because you have done this thing and have not withheld your son, your only son, I will greatly bless you..." (22:16-17).

## The Test Interpretation

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] reads the Akedah as **a genuine historical test** whose narrative focus is Abraham's faith and willingness to surrender the one through whom all covenant promises flow. The test is maximally severe: Isaac is the only son through whom the promises can be fulfilled; to surrender Isaac is to place the entire covenant in God's hands. Abraham's obedience demonstrates that he values the Giver over the gift. The appearance of the ram is divine provision, not merely symbolic — God does not ultimately require the sacrifice.

Hamilton explicitly resists making Isaac the narrative's center or Isaac's survival the key point. The Christological application (Isaac as type of Christ) is acknowledged as the NT's use (Heb 11:17-19) but is not treated as the primary interpretive key.

**Conditionality of the covenant**: 22:16-18 introduces a rare conditional note into the Abrahamic covenant — Abraham's obedience is cited as the *basis* for the oath-sworn reaffirmation. Hamilton notes this creates tension with the unconditional character of the covenant in Gen 15. The tension is real: the covenant is both gratuitous grace and demand of obedience.

## The Etiological Reading (Gunkel, tradition criticism)

Form critics read Gen 22 as an **etiology**: the narrative originally explained why Israelites, unlike their Canaanite neighbors, do not sacrifice their firstborn sons to the deity. In its Sitz im Leben, the story was told in cultic settings to justify animal sacrifice as the legitimate substitute for human sacrifice. The angel's intervention is the etiological punch line.

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] rejects this as reductive: the text's present function is not etiological but theological and narrative. The question is why Abraham's test was maximally severe — the etiology theory makes the test's severity incidental.

## The Typological Reading (Christian)

The NT reads the Akedah through a Christological lens:
- **Hebrews 11:17-19**: Abraham reckoned that God could raise Isaac from the dead and so received him back "in a figure" (*en parabolē*)
- **John 3:16**: "God so loved the world that he gave his only Son" — the verbal echo of "your only son whom you love" is widely read as deliberate
- **Romans 8:32**: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" — verbal parallel to Gen 22:16's "you did not spare your son"

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] treats the NT typological application as legitimate canonical reading but does not make it the primary interpretive frame. He notes that the typology focuses on Abraham's faith more than on Isaac's sacrifice as prefiguring Christ's.

## Kierkegaard's Reading: Fear and Trembling

Søren Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843) reads the Akedah as the paradigmatic case of **faith's transcendence of ethics**. Abraham's willingness to kill Isaac is not heroic ethical action (which would be sacrifice for a greater moral good) but a "teleological suspension of the ethical" — faith requires acting *against* universal moral law on the basis of a personal absolute relationship to God. Abraham's greatness is that he cannot explain himself to the ethical order.

This reading has been enormously influential in existentialist and postmodern theology but is not endorsed by biblical commentators working historically.

## The Ethical Critique

Modern and feminist scholars have raised the question of whether God's command is morally defensible. Walter Moberly and others note that the text opens by telling the reader "God was testing Abraham" (22:1) — the reader knows from the start that God does not ultimately intend Isaac's death. This frames the test as purposive, not arbitrary. Others argue the narrative presupposes an ancient religious world in which divine command is its own justification, and that reading it through modern moral categories is anachronistic.

## Jewish Interpretation (Rabbinic and Medieval)

Rabbinic midrash reads the Akedah through the lens of merit (*zekhut*): Abraham's willingness creates an enduring credit for his descendants. Israel can appeal to the *Akedah* in its prayers, invoking Abraham's merit as grounds for divine mercy. This **intercessory merit** reading is absent from the text itself but became foundational in Jewish liturgy (High Holy Day prayers invoke the *Akedah* explicitly).

The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands the narrative, giving Isaac an explicit voice of willing submission that the Hebrew text withholds.

## Kabbalistic Reading (the Zohar, Va-Yera)

The [[zohar|Zohar]] reads the Akedah at the **Sod** level not primarily as a test of faith but as a **cosmic event within the Godhead** — the necessary manifestation and harmonization of divine judgment. The binding stages the emergence of **"water versus fire"**: [[abraham|Abraham]] embodies **Ḥesed** (love, water, the right side), [[isaac|Isaac]] embodies **Gevurah/Din** (judgment, fire, the left side). "Who would have created a compassionate father who turned cruel? It was only so that **division would manifest: water versus fire**… until [[jacob|Jacob]] appeared and everything harmonized — the **triad of patriarchs completed**, above and below arrayed." The Akedah is thus the moment Gevurah is fully revealed and bound, then reconciled to Ḥesed in the central pillar (Tif'eret/Jacob). The Zohar stresses Isaac's age (37) and **willing self-binding** "like a lamb" — Gevurah consenting to its own severity. This relocates the controversy entirely: the question is neither historicity nor ethics but the **structure of the divine attributes**. See [[isaac|Isaac]] (Gevurah), [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]], [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]].

## Islamic Reading (Eid al-Adha)

Islamic tradition (Quran 37:100-112) presents a parallel *Akedah* narrative but does not identify the son by name. Muslim scholars are divided: most classical Islamic authorities identify the son as **Ishmael**, not Isaac (since Ishmael is the son of the covenant in Islamic tradition). The annual festival of Eid al-Adha commemorates the near-sacrifice and celebrates Abraham's submission (*islam*) to God. This tradition puts Judaism and Islam in direct disagreement over the identity of the bound son.
