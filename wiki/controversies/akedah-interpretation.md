The *Akedah* (עֲקֵדָה, "binding") — the account in [[genesis|Genesis 22]] of God's command to Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac, Abraham's obedient journey to Moriah, and the last-moment intervention of an angel — is among the most theologically dense and controversy-generating passages in the Hebrew Bible. No single interpretation has dominated across traditions; the episode has been read as divine testing, cultic etiology, typological prophecy, existentialist paradigm, and ethical horror.

## The Narrative

God commands Abraham: "Take your son, your only son whom you love — Isaac — and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains that I will show you" (22:2). Abraham obeys, journeys three days, binds Isaac on the altar, raises the knife — and is stopped by an angel, who declares Abraham's fear of God proven. God provides a ram as the substitute. God then reaffirms the covenant promises with an oath: "because you have done this thing and have not withheld your son, your only son, I will greatly bless you..." (22:16-17).

## The Test Interpretation (Hamilton)

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] in [[hamilton-genesis-18-50-nicot|Genesis 18–50]] reads the Akedah as **primarily a divine test** (*hāʾĕlōhîm nissâ ʾeṯ-ʾaḇrāhām*), not demonic temptation. Casus-pendens word order and the definite article on Elohim stress that Abraham’s God speaks; closest parallel motif Exod 20:20 (same subject *hāʾĕlōhîm*, verb *nāsâ*, aim = fear of God) — difference: Exodus’ people know they are tested; Abraham does not.

> “Some time later God tested Abraham… The text clearly makes the point that what follows is a divine testing, not a demonic temptation… the reader, but not Abraham, knows that what he is about to hear is a divine test.” (Hamilton; on Gen 22:1)

**Real test = sacrifice of the promise-bearer**, not only a beloved son. Stacked objects after “Take, please” intensify intimacy (// 12:1 leave-country chain):

> “The intensity of the test is magnified by the three direct objects of the imperative: your son, your precious son whom you love, Isaac… The test for Abraham is not primarily whether to sacrifice a beloved son, though that is no doubt involved emotionally. The real test is whether Abraham will sacrifice the one person who can perpetuate the promises of God, and particularly those promises that his posterity should thrive.” (Hamilton; on Gen 22:2)

Contextual link: Ishmael dismissed (21) // Isaac bound (22) — loss of a son, early-morning departure, question whether God can protect the endangered child. Fear of God is defined as “obedience which does not hold back even what is most precious… and commits to God even that future which he himself has promised.” *Yirʾeh* (provides/sees) ↔ *yērēʾ* (fears) is artful wordplay; Yahweh-yireh memorializes **God’s provision**, not Abraham’s obedience:

> “Appropriately Abraham names this place Yahweh-yireh… He does not call this site ‘Abraham-shama’ (‘Abraham obeyed’)… The reader will come away from this story more impressed with God’s faithfulness than with Abraham’s compliance.” (Hamilton; on Gen 22:14)

Ram as “another” ram (retain MT *ʾaḥar*); sheep→ram shift; inner-biblical clustering of burnt offering / appear / ram with priestly ordination (Lev 8–9) and Day of Atonement (Lev 16). On child-sacrifice ethics: focusing only on v. 2 makes God “deceptive, irrational… cruel”; the **whole** narrative (ram, stop-knife command, subsequent promises) yields de Vaux’s mercy-and-obedience reading. Kierkegaard and early Jewish ethical objections are registered; Hamilton refuses command-only framing.

### 22:15–18 integral; novel conditionality

Against near-unanimous critical excision of 22:15–18 as secondary, Hamilton treats the second call from heaven as **climax**, not appendix:

> “Commentators almost unanimously view these four verses as secondary… But these reasons are hardly correct. To have concluded the story at v. 14 would have been to produce a truncated narrative… They shall prosper because Abraham was faithful. That is the ultimate consequence of the divine testing of Abraham, not the sparing of Isaac… The novel element in this catena of promises is the emphasis on conditionality — that human behavior determines God’s response… Every promise to Abraham up to this point has been essentially unconditional. The postponement of the announcement of this cause and effect relationship clearly subordinates performance to promise, works to faith, and merit to grace.” (Hamilton; on Gen 22:15–18)

Unique *bî nišbaʿtî* (“I swear by myself”) and *neʾum YHWH* mark the oath. Conditionality reappears at 26:5 for Isaac. Ultimate outcome of the test = numerous, victorious descendants and nations blessed *because* Abraham obeyed — not merely Isaac’s survival.

### Anti–Isaac-as-Christ-as-center (NT appropriation)

Hamilton explicitly resists making Isaac (or Isaac-Christ typology) the **center** of the narrative or of NT use:

> “To summarize, the NT writers appropriate the story of the offering of Isaac primarily to make a point about Abraham — his faith, his works, his loving obedience. … One major difference between the two stories prevented the NT writers from clearly connecting Christ’s death with Isaac typology: In Gen. 22 the son is spared, but in the Christian story the Son is not spared.” (Hamilton; NT Appropriation of Gen 22)

Detail: Heb 11:17 perfect then imperfect of *prospherō* = attempted but prevented sacrifice (Isaac did not die); *en parabolē* broader than pure christological typology (includes general resurrection hope). Jas 2:21–23: Abraham’s “works” as obedience fulfilling Gen 15:6 ethically. Rom 8:32 “did not spare” ~ Gen 22:16 is possible but not certain. Daly’s “probable/possible” allusions (John 3:16, Mark 1:11, third-day, lamb texts) remain allusory; first clear Isaac-as-type-of-Christ = Barnabas 7:3 (2nd c.). Jewish tradition (e.g. Midrash: Isaac at 37) enlarges Isaac’s agency; Christian reception has centered Abraham’s obedience and God’s faithfulness — a contrast Hamilton reports without collapsing either.

Isaac’s wood-bearing may echo in John 19:17; cooperative faith of Isaac (silence, non-resistance) sits beside Abraham’s obeying faith — still secondary to the test frame. Medieval Jewish “Isaac actually killed and resurrected” opinions (Spiegel) are recorded as polemical/martyrological; Hamilton favors willingness as moral equivalent of outward sacrifice (cf. 1 Sam 15:22).

## The Etiological Reading (Gunkel, tradition criticism)

Form critics read Gen 22 as an **etiology**: the narrative originally explained why Israelites, unlike their Canaanite neighbors, do not sacrifice their firstborn sons to the deity. In its Sitz im Leben, the story was told in cultic settings to justify animal sacrifice as the legitimate substitute for human sacrifice. The angel's intervention is the etiological punch line. (Hamilton’s introduction reports Gunkel’s assignment of Gen 22 to animal-vs-human-sacrifice etiology as a classic form-critical example.)

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] resists reduction of the *present* narrative function to etiology: the test’s severity (promise-bearer, not only beloved son) and the integral oath-promises are load-bearing theology, not incidental decoration of a cultic just-so story.

## The Typological Reading (Christian)

The NT and later Christian reception read the Akedah through a Christological lens:
- **Hebrews 11:17-19**: Abraham reckoned that God could raise Isaac from the dead and so received him back "in a figure" (*en parabolē*)
- **John 3:16**: "God so loved the world that he gave his only Son" — the verbal echo of "your only son whom you love" is widely read as deliberate (Hamilton: allusory, not certain)
- **Romans 8:32**: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" — possible verbal parallel to Gen 22:16's "you did not spare your son"

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] treats the NT material as primarily **about Abraham** (faith, works, loving obedience), not as warrant to make Isaac-Christ typology the primary interpretive key of Gen 22 itself (see densified block above). John 19:17 wood-bearing and priesthood/atonement ram-clusters may support secondary typological extension without displacing the test frame.

## Kierkegaard's Reading: Fear and Trembling

Søren Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843) reads the Akedah as the paradigmatic case of **faith's transcendence of ethics**. Abraham's willingness to kill Isaac is not heroic ethical action (which would be sacrifice for a greater moral good) but a "teleological suspension of the ethical" — faith requires acting *against* universal moral law on the basis of a personal absolute relationship to God. Abraham's greatness is that he cannot explain himself to the ethical order.

This reading has been enormously influential in existentialist and postmodern theology but is not endorsed by biblical commentators working historically.

## The Ethical Critique

Modern and feminist scholars have raised the question of whether God's command is morally defensible. Walter Moberly and others note that the text opens by telling the reader "God was testing Abraham" (22:1) — the reader knows from the start that God does not ultimately intend Isaac's death. This frames the test as purposive, not arbitrary. Others argue the narrative presupposes an ancient religious world in which divine command is its own justification, and that reading it through modern moral categories is anachronistic.

## Jewish Interpretation (Rabbinic and Medieval)

Rabbinic midrash reads the Akedah through the lens of merit (*zekhut*): Abraham's willingness creates an enduring credit for his descendants. Israel can appeal to the *Akedah* in its prayers, invoking Abraham's merit as grounds for divine mercy. This **intercessory merit** reading is absent from the text itself but became foundational in Jewish liturgy (High Holy Day prayers invoke the *Akedah* explicitly).

The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands the narrative, giving Isaac an explicit voice of willing submission that the Hebrew text withholds.

## Kabbalistic Reading (the Zohar, Va-Yera)

The [[zohar|Zohar]] reads the Akedah at the **Sod** level not primarily as a test of faith but as a **cosmic event within the Godhead** — the necessary manifestation and harmonization of divine judgment. The binding stages the emergence of **"water versus fire"**: [[abraham|Abraham]] embodies **Ḥesed** (love, water, the right side), [[isaac|Isaac]] embodies **Gevurah/Din** (judgment, fire, the left side). The Akedah is thus the moment Gevurah is fully revealed and bound, then reconciled to Ḥesed in the central pillar (Tif'eret/Jacob). This relocates the controversy entirely: the question is neither historicity nor ethics but the **structure of the divine attributes**. See [[isaac|Isaac]] (Gevurah), [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]], [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]].

### Position: Zohar, Pritzker Edition Volume II (Va-Yera / Ḥayyei Sarah) — attributed, not adjudicated

Grounded in Matt, *The Zohar: Pritzker Edition*, Volume II (Zohar 1:103a–104a preview; 1:119a–120b full block; 1:123a Sarah-death midrash; 1:133b–134a fire–water gift reverse). Recorded as the Zohar's own position; not ranked against historical-critical, Christian typological, Kierkegaardian, or Islamic readings above.

**1. The test is of Abraham, not Isaac.** *Elohim tested Abraham* — “Abraham, precisely!” Isaac was already thirty-seven; Abraham “had to be encompassed by judgment, since previously Abraham had contained no judgment at all. Now **water was embraced by fire**… His whole life long he had been incomplete until now” (Zohar 1:119b–120a). Water (Abraham’s rung) is completed by fire; fire by water; one is judged, one executes judgment.

**2. Water versus fire; *et Abraham* = Isaac crowned.** *Et Abraham* can designate Isaac, who “dwelled in low power” until bound:

> "As soon as he was bound… crowned in his realm alongside Abraham—fire and water encompassing one another… Then division became apparent: **water versus fire**." (Zohar 1:120a; Pritzker II)

**3. Jacob needed for harmonization.** Division must appear so that the triad can later be completed:

> "It was only so that division would manifest: water versus fire, crowned in their realms, until **Jacob appeared and everything harmonized—triad of patriarchs completed**, above and below arrayed." (Zohar 1:120a; Pritzker II)

Third day of the journey = Jacob / third rung; “the place from afar” = Jacob seen through a dim lens because he is not yet born (1:120a).

**4. Isaac willing at thirty-seven, “like a lamb.”** Preview midrash (Mal 1:6, son honors father): “When he bound him on the altar… Isaac was thirty-seven years old… if he had kicked… he could not have withstood him. Yet he honored his father, allowing himself to be bound **like a lamb**” (Zohar 1:103a–104a). After “my son, not a sheep… *the two of them walked on together*” — willing togetherness (1:120a). Compassion transforms to judgment at the binding: “‘Here I am, my son’… compassion has vanished, transformed into judgment… not appear as a father but rather as his adversary” (1:120a).

**5. Sarah dies when Isaac is bound.** R. Ḥiyya (Ḥayyei Sarah): as soon as Isaac was bound, Sarah died; Abraham came from Moriah; “Those thirty-seven years from the day Isaac was born until… bound were Sarah’s true life, equivalent to the numerical value of ויהיו (*va-yihyu*)” (Zohar 1:123a; Pritzker II).

**6. Later reverse blend; Toledot echo.** After the Akedah, Abraham’s gift of “all” to Isaac is read as the reverse embrace (“fire completed by water… water embraced by fire”) for the “mystery of supernal faith” (1:133b–134a). In Toledot, Isaac’s tremble when Hell accompanies Esau **exceeds** the tremble at the binding knife (1:144a) — a Zoharic ranking of fears that leaves the Akedah as cosmic completion rather than mere terror. Salvation for Jacob depends on Isaac’s salvation: “Once Isaac was saved, this was salvation for Jacob” (1:119a, Ps 44:5 frame). Angels of peace weep for Isaac; sacrifice consummated as incense-ascent of Isaac plus ram of twilight force (1:120a–b). Shekhinah-in-exile coda via Isa 63 / *et* with the patriarchs (SW Abraham, NW Isaac, full coupling Jacob) (1:120b).

**Relation to other positions on this page:** the Zohar does not deny narrative “testing” language (*nissah*); it re-reads the *object* and *purpose* of the test (completion of Ḥesed by Gevurah; preparation of the triad). It does not engage Christian substitution typology, Kierkegaard’s ethical suspension, or the Islamic Ishmael identification. Those remain separate positions; this wiki does not adjudicate among them.

## Islamic Reading (Eid al-Adha)

Islamic tradition ([[quran|Quran]] 37:99–113) presents a parallel *Akedah* narrative but does not identify the son by name. Muslim scholars are divided: although early authorities were split (some named Isaac), **most classical and effectively all later [[islam|Islamic]] authorities identify the son as [[ishmael|Ishmael]]**, not Isaac (Ishmael being the firstborn and, in Islamic tradition, central to the line leading to Muhammad). The annual festival of **Eid al-Adha** commemorates the near-sacrifice and celebrates Abraham's submission (*islam*) to God. This puts Judaism/Christianity and Islam in direct disagreement over the identity of the bound son — one of the cross-tradition cruxes flagged on the [[quran|Qur'an]] and [[islam|Islam]] pages.
