# Was Christianity an Apostasy from Judaism? (the pagan charge)

A recurring argument in pagan criticism of [[christianity|Christianity]] from the second through fourth centuries: Christianity is an illegitimate movement because it abandoned the ancestral Jewish law while simultaneously claiming the prestige of Jewish antiquity. Christians had neither a venerable tradition of their own, nor fidelity to the one they claimed to have inherited. This critique was directed not merely at Christians' behavior but at the structural coherence of the new religion's claim to historical legitimacy.

The argument was most fully developed by [[celsus|Celsus]], and recurred — with variations — in Porphyry and Julian. [**Append points for Porphyry and Julian below.**]

---

## Celsus's Version (c. 170–180 CE)

Source: Wilken, *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, Ch. V, lines 3759–4914 (raw file lines approx. 3671–4914).

Celsus is identified by Wilken as "the first" pagan critic to perceive the significance of the Christianity-Judaism relationship for a systematic attack on Christianity: "not until Celsus had a pagan critic seen the significance of the relation between Christianity and Judaism for criticizing the Christian movement" (l.678–680).

### The Structural Charge

Celsus framed the accusation through a Jewish interlocutor:

> "Why do you [Christians] take your origin from our religion [Judaism], and then, as if you are progressing in knowledge, despise these things, although you cannot name any other origin for your doctrine than our law?" (*c. Cels.* 2.4; l.687–690)

> "What was wrong with you [Christians] that you left the law of our fathers, and being deluded by that man [Jesus] whom we were addressing just now, were quite ludicrously deceived and have deserted us for another name and another life?" (*c. Cels.* 2.1; l.755–760)

Celsus charged that [[christians|Christians]] had "deserted the Jewish Law even though Jesus, the founder of Christianity, was a Jew and Christians claimed to be faithful to the Jewish heritage" (Wilken, l.683–686).

### The Argument from Tradition (*Nomos*)

Celsus's critique was embedded in his broader conception of *Nomos* — the accumulated traditional wisdom of a people — as the criterion of religious truth. Whatever is old is true; whatever is recent is suspect. He identified the "true doctrine" (*alēthēs logos*) with the "ancient doctrine" (*palaios logos*) (l.1026–1027).

From this axiom the charge follows naturally:

- [[judaism|Judaism]] is old and venerable. Jews "observe a worship which may be very peculiar, but it is at least traditional" (*c. Cels.* 5.25; l.1089–1091). Even though Celsus disliked Jewish particulars, he could praise the Jews because they had maintained their customs "over the centuries up to the present time" (l.1093–1094).
- Christianity is recent: "their sect came into existence only recently" (l.1096). Christians "can only point to a shallow and inconsequential past going back a little over a hundred years" (l.1098–1099).
- The appeal to Jewish antiquity fails: "If I ask them 'where they have come from or who is the author of their traditional laws,' they will say: 'Nobody. In fact they themselves originated from Judaism, and they cannot name any other source for their teacher and chorus-leader. Nevertheless they rebelled against the Jews'" (*c. Cels.* 5.33; l.1100–1105).

### The Argument from Visible Jewish Communities

A crucial empirical dimension: had Jewish communities disappeared, Christians could perhaps have claimed undisputed inheritance of the Jewish tradition. But Jewish communities were flourishing throughout the empire. In the second century Jews numbered "four to six million people out of a population of approximately sixty million" (l.708–709). In Palestine, Syria, Egypt, and Asia Minor they comprised an even larger percentage. By the second century "Jewish life was prospering" (l.721); Jews served on city councils and held Roman provincial posts (l.722–724).

This visibility made the Christian assertion implausible:

> "the existence of another religious group, and one that was well known and well established in the cities, made the Christian assertion implausible in the extreme. Why, asks Celsus, did God 'give contradictory laws to this man from Nazareth, his son'? Jesus taught many different things than Moses taught. 'Who is wrong? Moses or Jesus? Or when the Father sent Jesus had he forgotten what commands he gave to Moses? Or did he condemn his own laws and change his mind, and send his messenger for quite the opposite purposes'" (*c. Cels.* 7.18; l.768–775)?

Jews who continued to observe circumcision, food laws, and the Sabbath demonstrated that it was possible to maintain Jewish tradition. Christians did none of this, yet claimed to be the true heirs (l.748–755).

### The Apostasy Framing

Wilken observes that Celsus used the language of **revolt** and **apostasy** throughout. The term "revolution or sedition" (*stasis*) recurs in the *True Doctrine* to describe Christianity: "A revolt against the [Jewish] community led to the introduction of new ideas" (l.903–904). Christianity "encourages the dissolution of the religious Nomos. The cause of its destructive influence lies finally in its unfaithfulness to that historical inheritance with which the various people have been entrusted in their Nomos" (l.991–995; citing Andresen).

### Why This Mattered: The Debate's Three-Way Character

Wilken argues the "significance of Celsus's argument that Christianity was an apostasy from Judaism has seldom been recognized" (l.823–825). The debate between Christianity and Hellenism was not merely two-way:

> "The relation to Judaism is, however, very significant to understanding the development of Christianity in the Roman world. In many cases the Jews participated actively in the debate; but in other cases, as with Celsus's True Doctrine, the simple fact that the Jews were part of the social world changed the terms of the discussion." (l.831–835)

As long as Jewish communities existed alongside Christian ones, critics like Celsus could demonstrate that Christianity was "patently false because, contrary to its own claims, it had deserted Jewish ways" (l.837–838).

Wilken notes that Celsus's Jewish interlocutor is not merely rhetorical: the parallel argument appears in Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho*, where the Jew Trypho charges Christians with "openly despising this covenant, neglect[ing] the [laws] which follow from it" (*Dial.* 10; l.732–737). That "such criticism would come from a pagan critic is another matter" (l.737–738).

### Later Recurrence

Wilken notes that "two centuries later the emperor Julian made the apostasy from Judaism a fundamental objection to Christianity" (l.821–822).

## Porphyry's Version

[[porphyry|Porphyry]] (c. 234–305) reframed the charge as a **double apostasy** — from both the ancestral Greco-Roman religion *and* the Jewish God. In a fragment preserved by Eusebius (*Praep. Evang.* 1.2.1–4), Christians "have not adhered to the God who is honored among the Jews . . . but cut out for themselves a new way," while also having "apostasized from the customs of our fathers, through which every nation and city is sustained." Arnobius confirms the charge of "turning away from the religion of earlier times" (*Adv. Nat.* 2.67). Distinctively, Porphyry's *Philosophy from Oracles* **praises the Jews** for their fidelity to the one high God (the oracle of Apollo in Augustine, *Civ. Dei* 19.22–23), and locates the error not in Jesus but in the **disciples**, who "began a revolutionary movement" by turning their teacher into an object of worship. The apostasy, for Porphyry, is the disciples' innovation against both heritages.

## Julian's Version

The emperor [[julian-the-apostate|Julian]] (r. 361–363), in *Against the Galilaeans* (*Contra Galilaeos*), brought the charge to its fullest and most historically documented form, marshaling **Christian Scripture against Christianity**. He cited Jesus's own words — "I have not come to abolish the Law" (Matt 5:17) — to argue it was the disciples, not Jesus, who apostasized. He pressed the Christians on each abandoned commandment (circumcision against Paul's spiritualizing; sacrifice; dietary law), invoking Moses' declaration that the Law "shall endure forever and that nothing shall be added or taken away from it" (Deut 4:2; cf. *Gal.* 320b: "Nowhere does God announce to the Hebrews a second law"). His conclusion: "Christianity, though it claims Judaism as its origin, has nothing to do with Judaism; it is a new and strange rite . . . with no claim to antiquity"; the Greeks in fact "have more in common with the Jews," who at least keep "temples, sanctuaries, altars, purifications, and precepts" (*Gal.* 306b). Christians, on this view, invented "a new sacrifice, which does not need Jerusalem" (the Eucharist) belonging to neither tradition. Julian's plan to **rebuild the Jerusalem Temple** was the political form of the argument: if the Temple stood again, Christian supersessionist claims would collapse. Wilken: "The Achilles' heel of the Christian tradition was its relation to Judaism. The truth of Christianity seemed to require the demise of Judaism."

---

## Christian Responses

The question was live and acute for Christian apologists. Justin Martyr appealed to Jewish prophecies to substantiate Christianity (l.1045–1047). Origen's response to Celsus engaged at length with the question of Christianity's relation to Judaism. The argument forced Christian thinkers to articulate why observance of Jewish law was no longer required even as the Jewish scriptures remained authoritative.
