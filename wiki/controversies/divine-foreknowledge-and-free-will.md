# Divine Foreknowledge and Free Will

The dispute, set out in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Book V**, over whether God's (or the gods')
foreknowledge of future events is compatible with human free will. [[augustine|Augustine]] frames it
against the Roman philosophers and resolves it in a way that became foundational for Western Christian
theology.

## The Positions

- **Ciceronian** — In *De Divinatione*, Cicero denies divine foreknowledge of contingent future events
  precisely in order to preserve human freedom, judging that certain foreknowledge would impose
  necessity and destroy the will. Augustine's verdict: "wishing to make men free he makes them
  sacrilegious" — and applies Ps. 14:1, "The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God."
- **Stoic** — Fate is the eternal chain of causes; all is determined. Augustine regards this as *closer*
  to the truth than Cicero's denial of foreknowledge, but rejects the astrological/necessitarian sense of
  "fate" ([[fate-moirai]]) and would drop the word for its misleading connotations.
- **Augustinian** — Both are to be affirmed: "we assert both that God knows all things before they come
  to pass, and that we do by our free will whatsoever we… do." The key move: **our wills are themselves
  among the causes** that God foreknows — "human wills are also causes of human actions; and He who
  foreknew all the causes of things would certainly… not have been ignorant of our wills." Foreknowledge
  of a free choice is foreknowledge *of its freedom*, not its abolition.

## Significance

Augustine separates two senses of "fate": stellar/astrological determinism (rejected entirely) and the
providential order of causes willed by God (accepted in substance, [[divine-providence]]). This
compatibilist resolution — God's certain foreknowledge including, not overriding, the freely willing
will — became the standard Western Christian framework and the seed of later debates on grace and
predestination.
