# James and Paul on Justification (James 2:24 vs. Romans 3:28)

The most famous apparent contradiction within the New Testament, and the engine of the Reformation
doubts about the [[james|Letter of James]]:

> **James 2:24** — "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone."
> **Romans 3:28** — "For we maintain that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the
> law."

Both writers appeal to the *same* OT verse — Genesis 15:6 ("Abraham believed God, and it was
credited to him as righteousness") — and the *same* exemplar (Abraham), yet draw apparently
opposite conclusions. The dispute is the reason Luther called James "an epistle of straw." This
page records the positions; [[douglas-moo|Moo]]'s (PNTC James, 2000; [[moo-james-pntc|source page]],
fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18, dense claims on 2:14–26 ring structure, *sōzō*/*dikaioō*, objector
punctuation, Abraham/Rahab) own resolution is presented as one position among several, not as
adjudication.

## The Sharpening of the Tension

The contradiction is real and not dissolved by easy moves:
- **"Works" vs. "works of the law"** is not a sufficient distinction. James says simply *erga*
  ("works," anything done in obedience to God); Paul says *erga nomou* ("works of the law"). But
  Moo (with most contemporary scholars) holds that Paul's "works of the law" is a *subset* of the
  broader category "works" (cf. Rom. 3:28 with 4:1–8), not restricted to ceremonial observances or
  ethnic boundary markers. So at bottom both refer to the same thing. Harmonization cannot rest
  here.
- Both use *dikaioō* ("justify") and *pistis* ("faith") and Gen. 15:6. The collision is head-on.

## The Positions

**1. Irreconcilable contradiction (much modern criticism).** Paul and James present genuinely
contradictory soteriologies; the NT does not teach a single unified view. Often paired with a late
date and pseudepigraphal authorship for James (reacting against a degenerate Paulinism). Moo
rejects this, but only on the basis of a particular doctrine of Scripture (one ultimate author),
which he acknowledges is the deciding premise.

**2. "Ships passing in the night" — misunderstood Paulinism (widely held; Moo agrees on the
historical point).** James is not engaging Paul's actual doctrine but a *slogan* ("justification by
faith alone") that some had detached from Paul's meaning and used to excuse moral laxity (quietism,
antinomianism). On this reading James and Paul never truly join issue. Moo uses this to argue for an
*early date*: the misunderstanding is most intelligible before James and Paul met at the Jerusalem
Council. See [[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]].

**3. Two senses of "justify" (Moo's exegetical solution).** *dikaioō* does not mean the same thing
in the two authors:
- **Paul** — God's *initial, forensic declaration* of the sinner's innocence at the point of faith;
  how one *gets into* relationship with God (Rom. 4:3–5: the *ungodly* are justified, apart from
  works).
- **James** — God's *ultimate vindication in the final judgment*, which *takes works into account*;
  what that relationship must look like to receive God's final approval. Moo grounds this in OT/LXX
  and Jewish usage (e.g., 1 Kgs. 8:32; Mic. 6:11; Matt. 12:37 — "by your words you will be
  justified"), where God vindicates people on the basis of their actual conduct.

Thus: "If a sinner can get into relationship with God only by faith (Paul), the ultimate validation
of that relationship takes into account the works that true faith must inevitably produce (James)."

**4. "Faith alone" = bogus faith (Moo's complementary move).** James's *target* in 2:14–26 is not
Pauline faith but a counterfeit: a word-only profession (v. 14, "claims to have faith"), the "faith"
of demons who believe the Shema and shudder (v. 19), faith that is "dead" (vv. 17, 26) and "useless"
(v. 20). This is *not* what Paul means by faith — Paul's faith is dynamic, obedient, "faith working
through love" (Gal. 5:6), "the obedience of faith" (Rom. 1:5). On the meaning of faith, Moo argues,
James and Paul are in "complete agreement." James is not telling people to *add* works to faith;
he insists that *true saving faith will work*.

**5. "Demonstrate/show to be righteous" (popular evangelical reading — Moo rejects).** *dikaioō* in
James means merely "show to be righteous": Abraham's prior faith-righteousness (Gen. 15:6) was
*demonstrated* by his works, removing the text from the soteriological level. Moo concedes *dikaioō*
can rarely bear this sense (Matt. 11:19) but rejects it here: the meaning is poorly attested, and
the context — "save" (v. 14), the judgment (vv. 12–13) — gives the verb full soteriological force.
This reading, Moo says, "cannot be sustained."

**6. Two justifications (Catholic and some Protestant).** An initial justification by faith (Paul)
and a second/final justification by faith *plus* works (James). Moo declines this: he reserves the
term "justification" for Paul's initial forensic act and classes what James describes under
"judgment." He resists importing synergism into Paul.

## Moo's Theological Integration

Moo situates the question against **covenantal nomism** (E. P. Sanders) — second-temple Judaism as
a religion of grace ("get in" by election/covenant, "stay in" by Torah-obedience). See
[[covenantal-nomism|Covenantal Nomism]]. He argues this Jewish soteriology was, in effect,
*synergistic* about final salvation, and that James 2 looks superficially like it ("get in" by
faith/new birth, 1:18; 2:1; "stay in" by the royal law, 2:8).

But Moo presses for a **monergistic** integration with Paul: read James's "works" as themselves the
*product of God's grace through the Spirit* (cf. Phil. 2:13; the works are "fruit"). T. Laato's
point: James's emphasis on the creative power of the **new birth** (1:18) already signals
monergism. So "what appears on the surface to be synergism in James can... be read in light of the
monergism of Paul." Faith alone brings one into relationship with God; *true faith inevitably
generates the works God takes into account at the final judgment*.

## On Abraham and Rahab (the exegetical battleground)

- **Abraham (2:21–23)**: Moo argues James does *not* contradict Paul's use of Gen. 15:6. Paul seizes
  on its *chronological placement* (faith credited as righteousness *before* circumcision and
  works). James treats it as a "motto" for Abraham's life *as a whole*, "fulfilled" (given its
  ultimate significance, *plēroō*) when Abraham "perfected" his faith by offering Isaac (Gen. 22).
  "Faith was working together with his works, and faith was completed by works" (2:22) — not that
  faith was deficient, but (cf. 1 John 4:12 on love) that it reached its intended goal in action.
  Both retain a *forensic* sense of "righteousness."
- **Rahab (2:25)**: a second, deliberately contrasting exemplar (the Gentile prostitute beside the
  patriarch), formally parallel to v. 21, showing that "justified by works" is a general principle,
  not an Abraham-specific quirk.

## Historical Echoes

Moo notes the dispute recurs across church history: the late-17th/early-18th-c. Anglican
controversies; the 1980s "Lordship salvation" debate (Zane Hodges vs. John MacArthur) over
obedience and assurance. He frames the two emphases as each needed in its moment: **Luther** rightly
preached Paul against a works-heavy medieval theology; **Wesley** rightly preached James against a
church indifferent to moral imperatives. "Paul strikes at legalism; James at quietism. Each message
needs to be heard."
