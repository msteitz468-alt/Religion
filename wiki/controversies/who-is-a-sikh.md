# Who is a Sikh?

A cluster of linked disputes — over the boundary of Sikh identity, the antiquity of Khalsa norms, and
Sikhism's relation to its Hindu and Sant matrix — that runs through **[[mcleod-sikhism|W. H. McLeod's
*Sikhism*]]** and divides historical-critical scholarship from normative Sikh historiography. McLeod's
positions are recorded here **without adjudication**; he is himself a party, and his readings are
"not accepted by most Sikhs."

## The positions in play

- **Tat Khalsa / normative historiography** — Sikhs are *not* Hindus; the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] is the
  Guru Panth and Khalsa membership is the goal of every Sikh; [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]]
  promulgated the Rahit and the [[five-ks|Five Ks]] fully formed in **1699**. This is the view
  institutionalized by the **Singh Sabha** and embodied in the [[rahit-maryada|Sikh Rahit Maryada]].
- **McLeod / historical-critical** — the Five Ks were *formally codified* only in the late 19th
  century (Sainapati's *Gur Sobhā*, c. 1711, attests only uncut hair and the sword); Sikhism emerged
  as a *genuinely separate* religious system only in the Singh Sabha period; the militarization of the
  Panth is best explained by historical-social environment, not solely by a preconceived ideology
  carried from Nanak forward.
- **Sanatan Sikh** — before the Singh Sabha, many Sikhs "regarded their religion as a special branch
  of the Hindu tradition"; ascetic orders ([[udasi|Udasis]], [[nirmala|Nirmalas]]) and saguṇa
  practice were honoured. The Tat Khalsa defeated this party but did not erase it.
- **Sahaj-dhari inclusivism** — non-Khalsa Sikhs who venerate [[guru-nanak|Nanak]] and the
  [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]] without taking the Rahit are fully Sikh; identity is devotion to
  the Gurus and the Name, not the Five Ks.

## The component disputes

1. **The boundary of "Sikh."** Amrit-dhari, Kes-dhari, Sahaj-dhari, and Patit categories; whether the
   Khalsa *is* the Panth or only its "solid core." The **SGPC**'s legal definition (Sikh Gurdwaras
   Act, 1925) gives the question a political edge.
2. **Origin of the Five Ks.** Late-19th-century codification (McLeod) vs. 1699 promulgation
   (tradition) — "a live and sensitive controversy."
3. **The "transformation" of Sikhism.** Interior, pacific [[nam-simaran|nām-simaraṇ]] religion →
   militant Khalsa: ideology-from-the-start vs. environmental process.
4. **Nanak as Sant.** Critical scholarship places Nanak within the **[[sant-tradition|Sant
   tradition]]** on antecedent grounds; devout Sikhs hold that he received direct enlightenment from
   [[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]] and cannot be classed "a Sant."
5. **Doctrine vs. practice on caste and gender.** Doctrinal equality (anti-caste; women's full
   liturgical equality) against the lived reality of [[jats|Jat]] endogamy, land inheritance, and
   male-dominated institutions (see [[sikhs|Sikhs]]).

## The Singh Sabha and the Communal Boundary (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 2)

Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2 adds a historical dimension to the identity question:
the modern boundary between "Sikh" and "Hindu" was, in his account, forged **reactively** by the
[[singh-sabha|Singh Sabha]] in response to the Arya Samaj's absorptionist claim. "The more the Samajists
claimed Sikhism to be a branch of Hinduism, the more the Sikhs insisted that they were a distinct and
separate community" — crystallized in **Kahan Singh Nabha's *Ham Hindu Nahin Hain*** ("We are not
Hindus"). Singh frames this not as a purely internal theological development but as an existential
defence against post-[[annexation-of-the-punjab|annexation]] demographic reabsorption. The boundary was
then legally codified: the **Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925** defined a Sikh as one who believes in the ten
Gurus and the Granth and "is not a *patit* [apostate]" — a definition Singh notes was "particularly
odious to the Hindu members" of the legislature precisely because it drew a hard line against Hinduism.
See [[singh-sabha|Singh Sabha]] and [[akali-movement|the Akali movement]].
