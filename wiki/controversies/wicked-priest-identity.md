# The Identity of the Wicked Priest

The Qumran pesharim — especially the Habakkuk Commentary (1QpHab) — repeatedly refer to a figure called **"the Wicked Priest"** (*ha-Kohen ha-Rasha'*) who was the primary antagonist of the [[teacher-of-righteousness|Teacher of Righteousness]]. The Wicked Priest:
- Rose to power with a (relatively) legitimate reputation
- Grew wealthy and corrupt in office
- Pursued the Teacher "to the house of his exile" on **the Teacher's Day of Atonement** (violating the community's alternative calendar)
- Was subsequently "delivered into the hands of his enemies" and suffered a humiliating death

The identity of this figure is one of the most contested questions in Qumran scholarship.

## Position 1 — Jonathan Maccabaeus (the standard thesis)

**Proponents**: Vermes, Cross, most earlier scholarship  
**Source**: [[dead-sea-scrolls-vermes]]

**Argument**:
- Jonathan Maccabaeus (high priest 153–143 BCE) matches the profile: rose from military leadership to the priesthood (initially plausible), accumulated wealth and power, was eventually captured by the Seleucid general Tryphon and executed. The humiliating death fits.
- Simon Maccabaeus (143–134 BCE) is sometimes proposed as an alternative for some passages.
- The Teacher of Righteousness's floruit (c. 150 BCE) aligns with Jonathan's period of power.
- The "house of his exile" (a reference in 1QpHab) is read as referring to an actual place of exile, which the Teacher may have established after the break with the Jerusalem establishment.

## Position 2 — The Groningen Hypothesis (García Martínez)

**Proponents**: Florentino García Martínez (Univ. of Groningen / Leuven); followed by others  
**Source**: [[dead-sea-scrolls-vanderkam]] (VanderKam's account; he does not endorse the hypothesis)

**Argument**:
The pesher descriptions of the Wicked Priest contain inconsistencies that are difficult to explain if only one individual is meant:
- Some descriptions portray the Wicked Priest as actively persecuting the Teacher during the Teacher's lifetime
- Other descriptions seem to describe events inconsistent with a single individual's career
- The phrase *ha-Kohen ha-Rasha'* ("the Wicked Priest") may function as a **generic title** for the office of the illegitimate Hasmonean high priesthood, not a proper name for one man

García Martínez proposes that **six successive Hasmonean high priests** — Jonathan, Simon, John Hyrcanus, Aristobulus I, Alexander Jannaeus, and Hyrcanus II — each qualify as the Wicked Priest in different passages, because each in turn occupied the condemned office and acted corruptly toward the community.

**VanderKam's critique**: The Habakkuk pesher treats the Wicked Priest in a relatively coherent narrative — rise, corruption, confrontation with the Teacher, defeat, and death. Reading six different persons into this narrative makes it incoherent. The hypothesis solves a real problem (inconsistencies between passages) by introducing a larger problem (the loss of narrative coherence). VanderKam treats the hypothesis as a live scholarly debate but is clearly skeptical.

## Related figure — the Liar / Spouter of Lies

Distinct from the Wicked Priest is the **Liar** or **Man of Lies** (*Ish ha-Kazav*) / **Spouter of Lies** (*Matif ha-Kazav*) — a rival teacher within or adjacent to the community who disputed the Teacher's legal interpretations and drew followers away. The relationship between the Wicked Priest and the Liar is itself debated: are they the same person in different contexts, or two distinct antagonists?

## Impact on the Teacher's Death

Whether the Wicked Priest caused the Teacher's death is disputed. The Commentary on Psalms (4Q171) says the Wicked Priest "watched the Righteous Man in order to put him to death." But CD XIX–XX speaks of a period after the Teacher's death without attributing that death to the Wicked Priest specifically. Some scholars infer a martyrdom; others a natural death.
