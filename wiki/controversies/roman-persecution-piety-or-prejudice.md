# Roman Persecution of Christians — Piety or Prejudice?

A historiographical dispute over *why* Rome persecuted [[christians|Christians]]. The popular and confessional image — a cruel, irreligious, decadent Rome gratuitously martyring the innocent — is challenged by Robert Louis Wilken in [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|*The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*]] (Ch. III, "The Piety of the Persecutors").

## Wilken's Thesis: Persecution Sprang from *Pietas*

Wilken argues that Roman persecutors acted from **genuine religiosity**, not malice. Roman religion was a practice-centered system in which the gods' providence underwrote the welfare of the state (the *pax deorum*); see [[pietas]] and [[roman-religion]]. Christians who refused sacrifice, the games, and civic cult were perceived as genuinely endangering the common good. "The Roman belief in divine providence, in the necessity of religious observance for the well-being of society, and in the efficacy of traditional rites and practices, was no less sincere than the beliefs of the Christians."

- At the trial of the Scillitan martyrs a Roman official could say, in effect, "**we too are a religious people**."
- To call Christianity a *superstitio* "is not a matter of simple bias or the result of ignorance; it expresses a distinct religious sensibility" (see [[superstitio]]).
- Dio Cassius (52.36.2) has Maecenas advise Augustus to punish those who "distort our religion with strange rites," because innovators "persuade many to adopt foreign practices, from which spring up conspiracies, factions and political clubs."
- Galen and others measured Christianity against philosophy and found its self-discipline admirable but its faith "undemonstrated"; Marcus Aurelius dismissed martyr-defiance as mere "obstinacy."

## The Opposing Reading: Roman Religion as Cynical *Utilitas*

[[augustine|Augustine]], in the [[city-of-god|*City of God*]], presented Roman civic religion as a cynical instrument of statecraft — useful fictions (*utilitas*) maintained by elites who did not believe them. On that reading, persecution reflects the hollowness of pagan religion rather than authentic piety. Wilken regards this influential portrait as **anachronistic and distorting**: it reads the genuine religiousness of the Roman world through the lens of a later Christian polemic.

## Significance

The dispute matters for how the entire pagan–Christian encounter is framed. If the critics (Pliny, Celsus, Galen, Porphyry, Julian) were sincere religious thinkers rather than bigots, then their objections — to creation by divine will, bodily resurrection, the incarnation, and Christianity's apostasy from Judaism — demanded, and received, serious Christian answers. Wilken's larger claim is that **pagan criticism helped shape Christian self-understanding and theology** rather than merely opposing it.
