The authority of the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] is the foundational controversy of Jewish religious history. At stake is whether the rabbinic tradition's interpretive supplement to the Written Torah (the Pentateuch) carries divine authority coequal with Scripture, or whether it is a human-constructed body of law and tradition with no special claim to Sinaitic revelation.

## The Pharisaic/Rabbinic Position

The **Pharisees** and their successors, the rabbis, held that at Sinai God gave Moses two Torahs: the Written Torah (*Torah she-bi-khetav*, the Pentateuch) and the Oral Torah (*Torah she-be'al peh*, the interpretive tradition). Both are equally divine in origin. The chain of transmission (*shalshelet ha-kabbalah*) from Moses through Joshua, the Elders, the Prophets, the Men of the Great Assembly, and the rabbinic sages preserves the Oral Torah intact.

This position is foundational for [[rabbinic-judaism|rabbinic Judaism]]: the entire [[mishna|Mishna]], [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]], and subsequent halakhic literature derives its authority from the Sinaitic origin claim. Without this claim, rabbinic law has no more authority than human legislation.

Key biblical support: Deuteronomy 17:8-13 commands Israel to follow the decisions of the priests and judges "in accordance with the instruction they give you" — rabbinics reads this as authorizing the oral interpretive tradition.

## The Sadducean Position

The **Sadducees** (Second Temple period) rejected the Oral Torah. They accepted only the Written Torah as binding. Their interpretations were consistently more literal: "eye for an eye" means bodily injury, not monetary compensation; "burning" as a death penalty means actual fire, not strangulation. They denied bodily resurrection (not found explicitly in the Pentateuch), the existence of angels (similarly), and afterlife rewards.

The Pharisees defeated the Sadducees in the religious-legal arena before the Temple's destruction. After 70 CE, without the Temple and its priestly establishment, Sadducean theology had no institutional base and disappeared.

## The Karaite Position

The **Karaites** (Heb. *Qara'im*, "scripturalists"), founded by Anan ben David (c. 770 CE) in Babylonia, rejected the Talmud and rabbinic tradition entirely. They advocated return to the plain meaning of Scripture (*peshat*) without the rabbinic interpretive apparatus. At their peak (9th–12th centuries) they represented perhaps 10% of world Jewry, with major communities in Palestine, Egypt, and later Crimea.

The Karaite movement generated a significant body of biblical commentary and Hebrew grammatical scholarship. [[maimonides|Maimonides]] wrote polemically against them; [[hai-gaon|Hai Gaon]] composed a work defending the Talmud against Karaite critique.

The Karaite movement was not homogeneous: some Karaites accepted earlier traditions predating the Talmud; others rejected all post-biblical tradition. The movement survives in small communities today.

## The Historical-Critical Position

Modern scholarship does not accept the Sinaitic origin claim. The [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] is read as a historically developing body of legal interpretation, innovation, and community practice that emerged from the Second Temple period through the Tannaitic and Amoraic periods. Key evidence:
- Legal material in the Mishna and Talmud shows historical development, contradiction, and revision — not the characteristics of a fixed transmitted deposit
- Many Talmudic legal positions are demonstrably connected to Greco-Roman legal environments (e.g., in commercial law, family law)
- The disputes between Pharisees and Sadducees suggest the Oral Torah was not a universally acknowledged institution but a contested Pharisaic claim

Scholars like Jacob Neusner have argued that the Mishna reflects the concerns of the Tannaitic period (primarily post-70 CE), not a systematic transmission from Sinai.

## Stakes of the Controversy

This is not merely an academic dispute. The authority of the Oral Torah determines:
- Whether the [[halacha|Halachic]] rulings of the Talmud are binding on Jews or are merely advisory
- Whether the Sadducean/Karaite critiques of rabbinic Judaism are historically vindicated
- Whether "Jewish law" is law in the proper sense (divine command) or human tradition (community agreement)
- How one reads the NT's depictions of Jesus and Paul in conflict with Pharisaic interpretation
