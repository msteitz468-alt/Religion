# The King of Tyre (Ezekiel 28): Human King, Fallen Angel, or Myth?

Ezekiel 28:11–19 laments the "king of Tyre" as a figure in Eden, "the garden of God" — blameless from the day he was created, adorned with gemstones, associated with a guardian cherub, cast down for hubris. Who is this figure? The dispute is one of the oldest sustained interpretive controversies in Ezekiel, documented here from [[block-ezekiel-25-48-nicot|Block, *Ezekiel 25–48* (NICOT)]].

## Positions

**1. The human king of Tyre (historical reading — Block's position).** Block argues the addressee of both panels (28:1–10 *nāgîd*, 28:11–19 *melek*) is the same human ruler (possibly Ethbaal III), on three grounds, including that "in Ezekiel *melek* always refers to an earthly king." He posits a pre-existent tradition of a primeval royal figure, divinely endowed, which the prophet recast into judgment "because a historical figure (the king of Tyre) has perverted what was his by divine gift," and reads the whole as biblical satire exposing the audience's own hedonism. The figure parallels Adam of Genesis 1–3: created, authorized to rule the garden, seeking divinity, punished.

**2. Lucifer/Satan (angelic-fall reading).** Block records that "during the second temple period, the view developed that Ezek. 28 was based on a tradition of an angelic 'fall'… Since the time of Origen many conservative Christians in particular have equated the king of Tyre with Lucifer (= Satan)… mentioned in Isa. 14:12." In this reading the cherub language, Eden setting, and primeval blamelessness describe Satan's prehistory. Block sides against it: "the primary referent within the context is clearly the human king of Tyre," and "a detailed treatment of the origin of the demonic is not to be expected from the OT." The reading remains standard in much conservative Christian reception, paired with the Helel/Lucifer reading of Isaiah 14.

**3. Canaanite/Mesopotamian mythology.** Some scholars read *ʾēl ʾānî* ("I am El/a god," 28:2) as identification with the Canaanite god El, or trace the whole tableau to extrabiblical myth. Block rejects the El identification with five arguments (among them that Tyre's patron was Melkart/Baal Shamem) and, while acknowledging ANE parallels (he quotes a Neo-Babylonian text on the creation of the king, and a Gilgamesh fragment on the jeweled garden of the gods for the "stones of fire"), insists "one should not exaggerate the influence of extrabiblical traditions. Ezekiel's theology is informed primarily by his Yahwistic heritage" — citing Batto (paradise motif as a distinctly Israelite development) and Van Seters (judgment-for-hubris as a prophetic transformation of the primeval-royalty myth).

**4. Veiled oracle against the Israelite high priest.** Because the nine gemstones of 28:13 overlap the high priest's breastpiece stones (Exod. 28; 39), some (Block engages Widengren on royal pectorals) have read the lament as originally aimed at Israel's high priest. Block rejects this with five arguments — e.g., "it is doubtful that either Ezekiel or his audience would have tolerated the image of a pagan king dressed in the most sacred of all Israelite garb" — and suggests the stones were inlaid in the figure itself, like ancient cult images.

## Text-critical entanglements

The positions partly ride on text-critical choices Block discusses: MT's *ʾt* ("you [were] the cherub") vs. a reading that places the cherub *with* the king (LXX of v. 14 suggests a guardian cherub present alongside the man — the angelic reading's anchor; Block calls this reading of Ezekiel "speculative"); and the gemstone list (MT nine stones vs. LXX assimilation toward the high priest's twelve).

## Status

Open and tradition-divided: historical-critical commentary (Block, Zimmerli in different ways) reads a human king; substantial Christian reception since Origen reads Satan's fall; the mythological and high-priestly readings persist as scholarly minority positions. No adjudication here.
