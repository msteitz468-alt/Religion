# Romans 7 — The Identity of the "I" (7:14–25)

Romans 7:14–25 is one of the most intensely debated passages in Pauline scholarship. Paul writes in the first person singular ("I") of a person who wills to do good but cannot: "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing. Now if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me" (7:19–20). The wretched exclamation climaxes in v. 24 ("Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?") and resolves in a christological acclamation (v. 25a) before the contrast is restated (v. 25b). The question is: Who is this "I"?

## The Text

**7:14**: "For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am of the flesh, sold under sin."

**7:15–20**: Extended description of moral impotence — the "I" does not do what it wills and does what it does not will.

**7:21–23**: The "law of my mind" (willing the good) is captive to "the law of sin" operating in "my members."

**7:24–25**: Cry of anguish and christological resolution: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!"

The passage concludes paradoxically (7:25b): "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin."

## The Positions

### 1. Pre-Christian (Unregenerate) Person — Arminian / Wesleyan

John Wesley, C. H. Dodd, Rudolf Bultmann, Werner Georg Kümmel, and most Arminian interpreters: the "I" is a person under the law without the Spirit — either pre-Christian Paul autobiographically, or a generic unregenerate person experiencing the law's revelation of sin without the Spirit's enabling.

**Arguments**:
- Romans 8 describes life "in the Spirit," implicitly contrasting Romans 7's life without the Spirit (7:6 already distinguishes "oldness of letter" from "newness of Spirit")
- A believer indwelt by the Spirit (8:9–11) should not be described as "of the flesh, sold under sin" (7:14) — language used elsewhere only of unregenerate people
- Paul's normal description of the Christian life is freedom from sin's dominion, not bondage (6:14: "sin will have no dominion over you")
- The despair of v. 24 fits an unregenerate person, not one who already has the Spirit

**Difficulty**: The present tenses throughout 7:14–25 (vs. aorist in 7:7–13) suggest current experience. The intensity of the moral struggle (genuinely *wanting* the good) seems inconsistent with a purely unregenerate description of sin's dominion.

### 2. Regenerate Believer — Reformed

Cranfield (ICC), John Murray, [[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (BECNT Romans, 1998): the "I" is a mature Christian — specifically Paul himself — describing the ongoing tension between the Spirit-renewed mind (willing the good) and indwelling sin (the flesh). This is the experience of any believer who has been renewed but not yet glorified.

**Arguments**:
- The intense desire to do good ("I delight in the law of God, in my inner being," 7:22) is hard to attribute to an unregenerate person; Paul's normal description of pre-Christian humanity is that they suppress the truth and do not seek God (1:18–32)
- Augustine came to this position; it is the dominant Reformed reading (Calvin, Owen, Murray, Cranfield)
- Romans 7:25b ("I myself serve the law of God with my mind") presupposes a genuine orientation toward the law's goodness — characteristic of a renewed mind
- The resolution in Romans 8 does not escape the tension of chapter 7; rather, the Spirit enables believers to fulfill the "righteous requirement of the law" (8:4) while still struggling with the flesh (8:10, 13)
- Schreiner: Paul describes the "eschatological existence" of the believer — genuinely renewed, genuinely struggling. The cry "Wretched man!" is the cry of a believer who hates their sin, not an unbeliever comfortable in it.

**Difficulty**: "Sold under sin" (7:14) and "captive to the law of sin" (7:23) use strong bondage language. If believers are no longer "under sin" (6:14) and have been "freed from sin" (6:18), how can they be "sold under sin"?

**Schreiner's response**: The bondage language in 7:14 describes the *situation* apart from the Spirit's deliverance described in ch. 8, not the believer's complete ontological state. The believer still contends with the flesh (8:13) even while the Spirit enables genuine obedience.

### 3. Israel under the Law — Corporate / Redemptive-Historical (Wright)

N. T. Wright and some NPP interpreters: the "I" is not individual autobiography but corporate Israel, or the Adamic human being entering the Mosaic covenant and experiencing the same Adamic pattern (given commandment → sin springs to life → death). Romans 7:7–25 recapitulates the history of Israel under the Torah in the first person singular, just as Paul can speak of the church in the plural first person.

**Arguments**:
- The Exodus motifs (law given at Sinai → sin springs to life → death) are structurally present
- "Sold under sin" echoes the OT language of Israel's slavery (Isa 50:1; 52:3)
- The passage parallels Adam's story (commandment → sin → death, 7:7–11), and Paul may be reading Israel's history through the Adamic lens

**Difficulty**: The present tenses and personal intensity of 7:14–25 resist a purely corporate or historical reading; Paul uses the first person consistently with obvious psychological realism.

### 4. Any Person Attempting the Law without the Spirit (Dunn)

James Dunn (NPP): the "I" represents any person who approaches the Mosaic law from within the "flesh" — the old age, without the Spirit of the new covenant. This is not necessarily pre-Christian autobiography, nor is it specifically Christian experience; it is the human condition under the law prior to Spirit-empowered life in Christ (8:1–4).

## Significance

The interpretation of Romans 7:14–25 shapes:
- **Sanctification theology**: Do believers experience ongoing bondage to sin, or does the Spirit bring effective freedom? Arminian/Wesleyan theologies often connect Romans 8 with an experience of "entire sanctification" that moves beyond Romans 7's struggle.
- **Assessment of human capacity**: Does the pre-Christian person genuinely desire the good (7:15–22) or is this desire possible only for believers?
- **The Christian life**: The "two natures" conflict of Romans 7 (if taken as regenerate) has shaped Reformed accounts of ongoing indwelling sin and the necessity of mortification.

## Resolution Status

**Open / tradition-specific.** The Reformed tradition predominantly holds position 2 (regenerate). Arminian/Wesleyan traditions hold position 1 (pre-Christian). NPP scholars vary. All positions have been maintained by serious exegetes throughout church history.
