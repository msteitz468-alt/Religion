The relationship between [[acts|Acts of the Apostles]] and Paul's letters is among the most contested issues in NT scholarship. Acts and Paul apparently present conflicting pictures of Paul's relationship with the Jerusalem church, his views on circumcision and Torah, and the chronology of his early ministry.

## The Apparent Tensions

**Paul's Jerusalem visits**: Paul states in Galatians 1:17-19 that after his conversion he did not go to Jerusalem for three years, then visited only briefly and saw only Peter and James. Galatians 2:1 describes a second visit "fourteen years later." Acts (9:26-30; 11:29-30; 15:1-29; 21:17) describes multiple Jerusalem visits with different characters and purposes.

**Circumcision**: Paul states in Galatians 2:3 that Titus, a Greek, "was not compelled to be circumcised" at the Jerusalem meeting. Acts 16:3 records that Paul circumcised Timothy (whose mother was Jewish) — apparently contradicting the principle of Galatians. Paul's own letters insist circumcision is theologically irrelevant (Gal 5:6; 6:15); Acts depicts Paul making flexible pragmatic decisions about circumcision.

**Temple participation**: Acts 21:26 depicts Paul purifying himself in the Temple and paying for the Nazirite vows of four men — apparently full participation in Torah observance. Paul's letter theology (the Law is ended for those in Christ; Gal 3:10-13) sits uneasily with this portrait.

**Paul's relationship with Jerusalem leaders**: Paul's letters describe tension with Peter in Antioch (Gal 2:11-14) and apparent independence from Jerusalem authority (Gal 1:12: "I did not receive it from any human source"). Acts presents a largely harmonious relationship between Paul and Jerusalem, with Paul consistently deferring to Jerusalem's authority.

## The Critical Position

Ernst Haenchen and the Dibelius school argue that these tensions reveal Acts as a **tendentious 2nd-generation portrait** of Paul, written by someone who lacked access to Paul's letters or deliberately softened Paul's theology to present a unified early church. On this reading:
- Paul's letters are primary sources; Acts is secondary and unreliable where they conflict
- Acts' Paul is a corrected, domesticated version of the real Paul
- The historical reconstruction of early Christianity must proceed primarily from Paul's letters

## The Evangelical Harmonization ([[darrell-bock|Bock]], Bruce, Marshall)

**Genre accounts for the differences**: Paul's letters are occasional, polemical, and address specific local crises; Acts is biography and ecclesial narrative. They are not competing accounts of the same events but different literary forms with different purposes.

**Acts 11 = Galatians 2** (Bock's minority position): Bock argues that the Acts 11 visit (the famine relief visit, 11:29-30) is the visit Paul describes in Galatians 2, not the Acts 15 council. This means:
- Galatians was written before the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15
- The circumcision question Paul addresses in Galatians is a pre-Council crisis
- The council of Acts 15 settled the question definitively, after which Paul could be more flexible
If correct, this resolves the tension between Galatians 2 and Acts 15 by separating them as different events.

**Timothy's circumcision**: Paul circumcised Timothy (whose mother was Jewish) for missionary strategy — "to the Jews I became a Jew" (1 Cor 9:20). This does not contradict the principle of Galatians because Timothy's circumcision was not coerced and was not undertaken as a requirement for salvation but for missional effectiveness. Cruelty to the principle would be demanding circumcision as necessary for justification — which Paul never countenanced.

**Temple participation**: Paul's 1 Corinthians 9:19-23 principle ("all things to all people") explains his willingness to participate in Temple practices when in Jewish contexts.

## Vielhauer's Four Inconsistencies (and Bock's Replies)

The sharpest form of the "two Pauls" argument is Philipp Vielhauer's (1966) claim of four theological inconsistencies between the Paul of Acts and the Paul of the letters. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats each as a position and rebuts it (siding with Ellis, Bruce, and Fitzmyer, who calls the thesis "clearly exaggerated"):

1. **Natural theology** — Acts 17 (the Areopagus, a positive appeal to pagan religiosity) vs. Romans 1 (pagan idolatry under judgment). Bock: the difference is one of *contextualization*, not contradiction; both affirm creation-knowledge and its inadequacy apart from Christ (see [[natural-theology|natural theology]]).
2. **The Jewish law** — Bock argues "what is explicit in Paul is implicit in Acts": Acts 13:39 ("freed from what the law of Moses could not free you from") already voices a critique of the law's power to justify. He reads Paul's **Pisidian-Antioch statement (13:38–39)** as compressed but genuine [[justification|justification]] language, and answers the Luke-vs-Paul objection with what he calls **Lukan telescoping** — Luke summarizes rather than contradicts.
3. **Christology** — against the charge that Acts is "adoptionist," Bock (with Moule) holds Lukan Christology is "high," Jesus being *shown to be* rather than *becoming* Lord (Rom. 1:2–4).
4. **Eschatology** — Luke does retain end-time expectation (Acts 1:11; 3:18–22; 10:40–42; 17:30–31), so the alleged loss of imminence is overstated.

Behind all four stands the older **Tübingen / F. C. Baur** reconstruction of a Peter–Paul conflict with Luke as a second-century harmonizer — the framework Bock rejects as the root of the skeptical reading.

## Timothy's Circumcision — Bock's Fuller Treatment

On the Timothy problem (Acts 16:3), Bock stresses that Timothy had a **Jewish mother**, so his circumcision regularizes an already-Jewish status for missionary access to synagogues — a matter of strategy ("to the Jews I became a Jew," 1 Cor. 9:20), not of principle. He contrasts this directly with **Titus**, the Greek whom Paul pointedly refused to circumcise (Gal. 2:3): the test case for principle was a Gentile, not a Jew, so the two episodes do not collide. The matrilineal-descent question (Cohen vs. Witherington) is noted as unsettled for the first century.

## Stakes of the Debate

The debate matters for:
- The reliability of Acts as a historical source
- The chronology of Paul's early ministry and the dating of his letters
- Understanding the relationship between Paul's theology and the early Jerusalem church
- Whether the earliest Christianity was unified (Acts) or pluriform and contested (Paul's letters)
