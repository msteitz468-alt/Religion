# Origins of the Vedic People (the "Aryan" Question)

One of the most contested questions in Indian history — **where the Indo-European-speaking people who
composed the [[vedas|Rig Veda]] (c. 1500 BCE) came from**, and how they relate to the earlier
[[indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]]. It is "just as politically driven" as it is
scholarly. Recorded here from [[wendy-doniger|Doniger's]] *[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]* (Ch. 4),
**without adjudication** (Contradiction Protocol).

## A Word on "Aryan"

*Ārya* is the Sanskrit word the Vedic poets used **for themselves** ("Us," the noble/good people) — "long
before anyone had a concept of race." Properly it names a **linguistic** family, not a race: "there are no
Aryan noses, only Aryan verbs." 19th-c. Orientalists gave it a racial, anti-Semitic spin (delighting to
find Sanskrit "older than Hebrew"), and the **Nazis** so poisoned the word that Doniger avoids it,
preferring **"Indo-European speakers"** / "the Vedic people." (The languages are a real family — Sanskrit,
Greek, Latin, Hittite, Celtic, Germanic… from a reconstructed *\*Proto-Indo-European*, the "land East of
the Asterisk"; but "wherever the languages went, there had to be people," so speakers, not a race.)

## The Four Guesses (Doniger's framing)

1. **Aryan invasion of India from \*Indo-Europe.** "Blond-eyed people from the north drove their chariots
   into India and beat the hell out of the dark-skinned people." The 19th-c. **Orientalist/colonial**
   default (a history of invasions conveniently justified the British conquest); often paired with the
   claim that the Vedic people destroyed the Indus cities. Doniger: "one of the great testosterone myths,"
   now "seriously challenged."
2. **Indo-European migration from the Caucasus.** Same mechanism, "invasion" → "migration": a gradual
   infusion of peoples, languages, and burial/pottery cultures. Doniger calls this "quite plausible"
   (citing Martin West's defense), though now deprecated as not "PC."
3. **Indigenous Indian origin ("Out-of-India").** The Vedic people were always *in* India (the Punjab) and
   emigrated *outward* (to Iran, Anatolia, Greece, Italy). "Reasonable in itself," Doniger says, but with
   "considerable evidence against it" — and "susceptible to exploitation by the brand of Hindu nationalism
   that wants the Muslims and Christians to get out of India" ("This land was always ours").
4. **The IVC people composed the Vedas.** The Harappans themselves were Indo-European speakers (the
   "Indus-Sarasvati" thesis). Doniger judges the linguistic/archaeological evidence against this "pretty
   conclusive": the Rig Veda knows none of the Indus's seals, bricks, Great Bath, writing, or
   Mesopotamian trade.

## The Horse Argument

Doniger's decisive evidence against Guess Four: **the horse**. The Rig Veda is "intensely horsey"; the
**Indus seals depict no horses** ("no Indus horse whinnied in the night"), and horses are not native to
India (they must be imported). Since "wherever Indo-European-speaking cultures have been identified,
evidence of horses has been found," the horse-poor IVC was probably **not** Indo-European-speaking — so the
Indus people did not compose the Veda. (She notes the counter-games: a much-touted "horse seal" turned out
to be an upside-down unicorn-bull; and one *could* argue the Vedic people too *imported* their horses,
slightly loosening Guess One.)

## Doniger's Own Resolution: Fusion / Bricolage

She declines all four as exclusive answers. Vedic and Indus cultures were **distinct** (against Guess
Four), but later **Hinduism is a fusion** of "Vedic words and Indus images" plus other non-Vedic
contributions — the "**non-Veda**" (Adivasi, Dravidian, Munda, village traditions) as a partly-uncredited
*fons et origo*. Her governing image is the mutual creation of **Viṣṇu and Brahmā**, each born from the
other: neither Vedic nor non-Vedic can claim priority.

## Status

**Open**, and unlikely to be settled by evidence alone, because the stakes are political: the invasion
theory served colonialism; the indigenous-origin theory serves [[who-defines-hinduism|Hindu
nationalism]]. The wiki records the positions and their tradition-context without adjudicating.
