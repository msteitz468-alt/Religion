# Pastoral Epistles — Pauline or Pseudonymous?

## Historical Overview

**Ancient to 1807**: The PE (1–2 Timothy, Titus) were universally accepted as Pauline from their earliest reception. Polycarp (ca. 69–155) cites all three; Irenaeus quotes them as Pauline; they appear in Marcion's canon (though he may have rejected them), the Muratorian Canon, and all subsequent canonical lists. The church fathers — native Greek speakers closer to Paul's era than any modern scholar — never doubted them.

**J. D. Schleiermacher (1807)**: First serious challenge, targeting only 1 Timothy. Schleiermacher argued its vocabulary and polemic against Gnosticism presuppose a post-Pauline date.

**H. J. Holtzmann (1880)** and **P. N. Harrison (1921)**: Harrison's *The Problem of the Pastoral Epistles* was decisive in crystallizing the pseudonymity consensus. He argued via statistical vocabulary analysis: 175+ words in the PE not found in the undisputed Paulines; 36+ words found in 2nd-century Christian writers but not in earlier Paul. He proposed a fragment hypothesis: genuine Pauline travel notes embedded in otherwise pseudonymous compositions.

**Martin Dibelius and Hans Conzelmann (1966 ET)**: The "bourgeois Christianity" reading — the PE reflect a church that has settled down, accommodated to the world, and replaced pneumatic charisma with institutional order. This has since been shown to be anachronistic (Klinker–De Klerck).

**Current state**: Roughly 80% of NT scholars in Western academia accept pseudonymity; 20% defend Pauline authorship. The figure is reversed in African and Asian Christianity, and likely in non-Western scholarship broadly.

## Main Arguments for Pseudonymity

### 1. Vocabulary and Style

The most frequently cited argument. Harrison counted 175 words in the PE absent from the undisputed Paulines, plus 36 shared with 2nd-century Christians but not with earlier Paul. The PE lack characteristic Pauline particles, connectives, and discourse markers.

**Responses**:
- Harrison's corpus comparison suffers from the law of small samples: the PE (3,484 words combined) are too small for statistically valid stylometric analysis.
- All Pauline letters contain clusters of words not found elsewhere in his corpus. Even undisputed letters like Philippians diverge markedly from Romans.
- The PE were written to specific individuals (not congregations), at a different life stage, in a different sociopolitical setting (post-Acts), addressing different problems — all factors that legitimately alter vocabulary.
- Schlatter catalogued 70+ literal points of contact between the PE and undisputed Paulines (mostly Romans and 1–2 Corinthians), especially in theological and ecclesiological vocabulary.
- Schnabel: "The language of the Pastoral Epistles, despite some distinctive characteristics, renders Pauline authorship neither impossible nor implausible."
- Berger: if divergent vocabulary across letters rules out common authorship, then no genuine Pauline letters exist, since all undisputed letters also diverge substantially from each other.

### 2. Church Organization Too Developed

The PE presuppose distinct offices of overseer (*episkopos*), elder (*presbyteros*), and deacon (*diakonos*). This level of institutional structure, the argument runs, presupposes a generation or more of organizational development post-Paul.

**Responses**:
- Overseers and deacons appear in Phil 1:1 (undisputed). Elders are appointed by Paul and Barnabas on the first missionary journey (Acts 14:23).
- Paul's farewell charge to the Ephesian elders (Acts 20:28) already uses *episkopos* language.
- The PE's structure is less developed, not more, than what we find in Ignatius (early 2nd century) with his three-tiered monarchical episcopate.

### 3. Theological Differences

The PE lack characteristic Pauline theological emphases (justification by faith, the cross as scandal, the body of Christ ecclesiology) and introduce terminology absent from the undisputed letters (*eusebeia*, *hygiainōn didaskalia*, *epiphaneia*).

**Responses**:
- The PE are not systematic theology but pastoral directives. Romans is not replicated in Galatians or Philippians either.
- *Eusebeia*, *hygiainōn*, and *epiphaneia* are all well-attested in contemporaneous Jewish and Hellenistic sources (Josephus, Philo, Epictetus) and reflect the PE's specific apologetic context — not a different theological world.
- The PE do contain Pauline theological motifs: justification (Titus 3:7: "having been justified by his grace"); the cross and atonement (Titus 2:14: "gave himself to redeem us"); election (Titus 1:1: "the faith of God's elect"); the resurrection (2 Tim 2:8: "raised from the dead, descended from David").

### 4. Chronological Difficulty

The journeys implied in the PE cannot be fitted into the Acts chronology.

**Responses**:
- The PE presuppose a post-Acts period: Paul's release from a first Roman imprisonment (which Philippians and Philemon anticipate: Phil 1:25; Phlm 22), followed by renewed travel and a second imprisonment.
- The very difficulty of fitting the PE into Acts actually argues for authenticity (J. A. T. Robinson): a pseudonymous author clever enough to produce the PE would have connected them more obviously to the known Acts/Paulines chronology.
- Eusebius (HE 2.22.1–2) and the Muratorian Canon attest the tradition of Paul's release and further travel.

## The Ethics Argument (Yarbrough)

A distinct argument against pseudonymity not often foregrounded but logically compelling:

1 Tim 2:7: "I am telling the truth, I am not lying." 2 Tim 4:6–8, 17–18: Paul depicts his imminent execution, invoking God's presence in doxology. A pseudonymous author writing these words is not merely employing an accepted literary convention (as defenders of pseudonymity often claim) — there is no documented parallel in Jewish or early Christian literature for a pseudonymous letter that (a) melodramatically depicts imminent martyrdom, (b) explicitly swears to truth-telling, and (c) names numerous real contemporary individuals in personal detail. A forger doing this is not exercising a neutral convention but is engaged in active deception, and the theological stakes (invoking God's name and Christ's glory in the process) are severe.

## The Global Christianity Argument (Yarbrough)

Yarbrough adds a sociological-hermeneutical argument: the pseudonymity consensus is a product of German Enlightenment scholarship that assumed Western academic rationalism was the universal hermeneutical standard. By 2050, 53% of the world's Protestants will be in Africa. The African, Asian, global South, and Eastern European church reads the PE as Pauline — not from ignorance but from a different set of priorities shaped by active persecution and suffering, which makes Paul's pastoral counsel (especially in 2 Timothy) immediately recognizable as authentic voice. The long history of the PE as Pauline — nineteen centuries — should be weighted against a paradigm that became dominant in Western scholarship only in the last two centuries.

## The Fragment Hypothesis

Harrison's own solution was to posit genuine Pauline fragments (especially in 2 Tim 4:9–21) embedded in pseudonymous compositions. This has attracted little following: it requires a pseudonymous author with undetectable access to genuine Pauline letters who then mixed fabrication with authentic material, a sociologically implausible scenario.

## Differentiation Among the Three Letters

Some scholars accept 2 Timothy as authentic while rejecting 1 Timothy and Titus as pseudonymous:
- 2 Timothy's personal details (cloak, parchments, Demas, Luke; 4:13, 11) seem too mundane to be invented.
- 1 Timothy and Titus lack the same degree of personal texture.

Against this, the three letters share vocabulary, style, and theological fingerprints in ways that make individual differentiation awkward.

## Consequences for Interpretation

Whether the PE are Pauline or pseudonymous affects:
- **Authority**: Pseudonymous letters present theological teaching under a false name, which raises canonical questions even for those who accept them as canonical Scripture.
- **Date and setting**: Pauline → ca. AD 62–67, Ephesus and Crete of the Pauline mission era. Pseudonymous → ca. AD 80–100, unknown community, speculative setting.
- **Theology**: Pauline authorship means the PE theology is continuous with (if distinctive from) the undisputed letters. Pseudonymity means the PE represent a distinct, later theological stream.
- **1 Tim 2:12**: Those who accept pseudonymity often argue the passage is *ad hoc* for an unknown community; those who accept Pauline authorship read the creation-order argument as transcontextual.
