# The Arian Controversy and the Nicene Homoousios

The dispute that occasioned the **Council of Nicaea (325 CE)**: was Christ — the
pre-existent Logos/Son — **coeternal with and equal to** God the Father, or a **divine being
who came into existence** ("was begotten") before the universe? [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]
([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) stresses that **both** sides held a
"very high [[incarnation|incarnational]] Christology" — all agreed Christ was a divine being
pre-existing creation — and that [[arius|Arius]]'s view had been "acceptable in the
theological climate of orthodox Christianity" a century earlier (e.g. the subordinationism of
[[justin-martyr|Justin]], [[tertullian|Tertullian]], Novatian, and [[origen|Origen]]).

## Positions

- **Arian subordinationism** ([[arius|Arius]], priest of Alexandria; controversy from 318 CE)
  — God alone is without beginning; the Son is "begotten," "a perfect creature of God, but
  not as one of the creatures," removed from the Father "by an infinity of glories."
  Slogan/anathematized formula: "**There was when he was not.**" Christ is to be worshiped,
  but never as the Father's equal.
- **Nicene homoousios** (Bishop **Alexander of Alexandria**, then **[[athanasius|Athanasius]]**)
  — the Son "has always existed out of the Father"; God cannot have "become" Father, so he
  always had his Son/Wisdom/Word (Dionysius of Rome's earlier argument: if the Word "came into
  being," God once lacked his own Word — "most absurd"). The Creed declares Christ "begotten
  not made, of one substance [***homoousios***] with the Father, true God from true God,"
  anathematizing "there was when he was not."

## Resolution

At Nicaea, of 318 bishops only 3 refused to sign (Arius and two Libyans);
[[constantine|Constantine]] — seeking imperial unity more than doctrinal precision (he called
the dispute "extremely trivial") — pressed the dissenters to acquiesce. The decision did not
end the conflict: Arianism persisted (Jerome, 379: "the world … groaned … to find itself
Arian"), some emperors were Arian, and it was finally condemned at the **Council of
Constantinople (381)**. The settlement generated the [[ortho-paradoxes|Trinitarian
ortho-paradox]] (three persons, one God) and fed directly into the later Christological
definitions ([[chalcedonian-definition|Chalcedon, 451]]).

## Kelly's Narrative (Early Christian Doctrines)

[[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|Kelly]] (chs. IX–X) traces the conflict's roots in two strands of Origenism (the cautious right-wing of Alexander of Alexandria vs. the radical [[subordinationism|subordinationism]] of Eusebius of Caesarea); [[arius|Arius]]'s four propositions (the Son a creature, "there was when He was not," ignorant of the Father, peccable); the original generic sense of *[[homoousion]]*; and the long post-Nicene spectrum of slogans — *homoousios* / *homoiousios* / *homoios* / *anomoios* ([[eunomius|Eunomius]] and the Anomoeans). The resolution came through the [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|one-*ousia*/three-*hypostases*]] formula and the [[cappadocian-fathers|Cappadocian]] synthesis, with [[hilary-of-poitiers|Hilary]] and [[marcellus-of-ancyra|Marcellus]] on the Western side. The parallel struggle over the [[holy-spirit|Spirit]]'s deity (against the Pneumatomachians) was settled at Constantinople (381).

## Ayres's Revisionist Reading (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) sharply qualifies the
traditional narrative above; this wiki records his account alongside Kelly's without
adjudicating between them:

- **"Arianism" is a polemical construct, not a movement.** After Nicaea, "Arius himself" is "of
  little significance" and *homoousios* "is rarely if ever mentioned" in 326–350. The category
  "Arian" was *created* by [[athanasius|Athanasius]] — fully developed in the First *Oration
  Against the Arians* (c. 339–40) — selling "a conspiracy by a group of 'Arians'" where there
  was none. Ayres distinguishes "an attempt to shape ecclesiastical affairs in the light of a
  theological trajectory" from "an overt conspiracy to perpetuate the theology of Arius."
- **Four trajectories, not two parties.** The early controversy is a clash of pre-existing
  **theological trajectories**: Alexander/Athanasius ("theologians of the true Wisdom"); the
  "Eusebians" ("theologians of the one Unbegotten"); the "Marcellan" ([[marcellus-of-ancyra|Marcellus]],
  "theologians of the undivided Monad"); and Western anti-adoptionism. By the 350s these
  crystallized into four positions — [[homoousion|homoousian]], [[homoiousians|homoiousian]],
  [[homoians|homoian]], and [[heteroousians|heteroousian]] (Aetius/Eunomius).
- **Nicaea (325) settled little in 325.** No creed had yet served as a universal standard of
  faith; *homoousios* was chosen mainly because Arius was known to reject it; there was no
  single "Nicene theology," and Western bishops barely knew the creed's text before the 350s.
- **The real resolution is [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene theology]]** (360s–380s), a slow
  *rapprochement* (not Nicene survival), ratified at Constantinople (381) — a council "nobody
  intended… as a replacement for Nicaea." Non-Nicene theology continued into the 420s, and the
  Germanic kingdoms of the West remained [[homoians|Homoian]] into the fifth century.

This deconstruction of "Arianism" and the de-emphasis of the creed mark a real **contradiction**
with the older account; see [[pro-nicene-theology]] and the historiographical dispute at
[[de-regnon-paradigm]].
