# The Chalcedonian Definition as a Crisis of the Classical Theory

The Council of Chalcedon (451) defined Christ as **one *hypostasis* (Person) made known in two natures (*physeis*), without confusion, change, division, or separation** — incorporating the [[double-homoousion|double homoousion]] (consubstantial with the Father in divinity, with us in humanity). This page records Johannes Zachhuber's argument ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]]) that, *whatever* its doctrinal merits, the Chalcedonian formula was **philosophically unsupported by — and arguably incompatible with — the [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|Cappadocian classical theory]]** that all parties took for granted. It thereby triggered the centuries-long realignment of patristic philosophy that is the main subject of Zachhuber's book. (For the *doctrinal* settlement and its scholastic articulation, see [[hypostatic-union|Hypostatic Union]].)

## The Conceptual Problem

The strength of the classical theory was its reciprocal correlation of universal and particular: a nature (*physis*/*ousia*) exists without variation in all its individuals, and exists *only* in them. But this left **no account of individuation** — and Christology is precisely a doctrine about an individual (one divine Person becoming one human individual). Within the binary of the classical theory (statements are about either the universal or the particular, with no third), "the Word became flesh" fits neither cleanly:

- As a truth about **universal natures**, it would apply to all members of the class — but only the Logos, not Father or Spirit, became incarnate.
- As a truth about **hypostases**, it threatens a duality of hypostases in the Incarnate, jeopardizing his personal unity.

Worse, the [[double-homoousion|double homoousion]] implied — by the shared fifth-century logic that **two natures entail two hypostases** (accepted by [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril]] and [[theodoret-of-cyrus|Theodoret]] alike) — that Chalcedon's "two natures in **one** hypostasis" was conceptually unjustifiable on the reigning philosophical paradigm, while being cast in the very vocabulary (physis, hypostasis, homoousios) forged for the Trinity.

## The Three Resulting Positions

The Council did not resolve the philosophy; it set the fault lines along which patristic philosophies (and churches) divided:

1. **Chalcedonian dyophysitism** — two natures in one hypostasis. To defend it, later "neo-Chalcedonians" reduced the classical theory to its **abstract** dimension (Christ shares human properties without a separate human hypostasis) — a move Gregory of Nyssa had implicitly anticipated against [[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinarius]].
2. **Cyrilline / miaphysite** — "one incarnate nature of the Word" (*mia physis*); rejects Chalcedon as crypto-Nestorian. Becomes [[oriental-orthodoxy|Oriental Orthodoxy]] (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian). (Severus of Antioch's philosophy — a later scope.)
3. **Antiochene / "Nestorian"** — accepts that two natures imply **two hypostases**, and modifies the concept of hypostasis to secure the saviour's personal unity. Becomes the Church of the East. (See [[nestorius|Nestorius]], [[theodoret-of-cyrus|Theodoret]].)

## Significance (per Zachhuber)

Chalcedon "energized philosophical debate as no previous doctrinal ruling had done." Because the classical theory was too firmly entrenched to be replaced, the formula's tension with it forced increasingly radical conceptual innovation over the sixth–eighth centuries — the realignment Zachhuber identifies as the genuine "ontological revolution" and the **end of ancient metaphysics**, driven by Christology (the conceptualization of the individual *as individual*), not by the Cappadocian Trinity. *Per the Contradiction Protocol, this page records the positions without adjudicating which Christology is correct.*

## Pelikan: A Mosaic, and an "Agreement to Disagree"

[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 5) stresses that the Definition "is not an original and new creation, but like a **mosaic**… assembled almost entirely from stones that were already available" — Cyril's letters and the Formula of Reunion (433), [[leo-the-great|Leo's Tome]] (the decisive contribution), and Theodoret's "not divided into two persons." Its theological core is really "the theology of preexistence, kenosis, and exaltation" (Leo's Western Christology) in Cyrilline dress. Pelikan's verdict: **"It was, then, an agreement to disagree"** — a deliberate refusal to specify, even, who the subject of the suffering and crucifixion was, leaving the deification-soteriology of the [[hypostatic-union|hypostatic union]] party unsatisfied. Consequently it "settled very little in the East," providing "the terms for subsequent controversies rather than the solution," and alienating churches "even after a millennium and a half… still not reconciled."

His structural thesis: just as the Trinitarian settlement (Nicaea–Constantinople) had *reopened* the Christological question, so Chalcedon **reopened the Trinitarian question** (how one *hypostasis* can bear two natures when the Trinitarian grammar ties hypostasis to a distinct divine subject) — "the controversy had come full circle." The sixth-century resolution was [[neo-chalcedonianism|Neo-Chalcedonianism]] (the Cyrilline re-reading, [[theopaschism]], the Three Chapters, and [[justinian|Justinian's]] Constantinople II of 553), not Chalcedon as the Antiochenes had hoped to read it.
