# Natural Theology — Barth's Rejection and the Liberal Counterposition

## The Issue

Can human beings arrive at genuine knowledge of God apart from, or as a precondition for, the specific revelation of God in Jesus Christ attested in Scripture? This question underlies §6 of [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1|*Church Dogmatics* I.1]] and several passages in §§1–2.

## The Liberal Protestant Position (Schleiermacher and his heirs)

The position Barth calls "Modernist" in *CD* I.1 traces to Schleiermacher, who "for the first time fundamentally relates this newly discovered independent reality of religion to a corresponding possibility that can be generally demonstrated anthropologically" (lines 8810–8831). Two principles:

1. The encounter of man with God is a human religious experience establishable historically and psychologically.
2. This experience is the actualization of a generally demonstrable religious *capacity* of man.

Applied to dogmatics: "the real knowledge of the Word of God is the actualisation of a specific possibility of knowledge peculiar to man as such" (lines 8822–8823). Whether the anthropological framework is Schleiermacher's "feeling of absolute dependence," the "religious a priori" of ca. 1910 (Troeltsch), or Heideggerian existential ontology (Bultmann) is "a secondary matter" (lines 8824–8826) — the structure is the same.

Karl Holl's formulation is quoted as a precise statement of the underlying principle: "Nothing is to be recognised as religiously valid but what can be found in the reality present to us and produced again out of our direct experience" (lines 8951–8954). Barth calls this "the principle of Cartesian thinking, which is quite impossible in theology" (line 8954).

## Indirect Christian Cartesianism (Wobbermin and Schaeder)

Barth distinguishes a subtler form, "indirect Christian Cartesianism" (lines 9729–9751). Wobbermin and Schaeder deny that man has an *a priori* religious capacity *prior to* faith, and they affirm that the order runs from God to man. But in their account, once the grace of the Word has come, a "conjunction or synthesis" takes place in which the gift "passes out of God's hand into the hands of man" (lines 9637–9638): the believer becomes a "*homo capax verbi Dei*" whose "own personal experience of faith" serves as an "opposite pole" and "methodological aid" for knowledge of the Word (lines 9675–9680).

Barth's critique: this eventually forces resort to direct Cartesian self-certainty (lines 9887–9902). Moreover, no certainty can be attained here — the act of acknowledgment observable in the believer is indistinguishable from the acts of "other religions" and of "culture generally" (lines 9862–9886).

## Barth's Position

Knowledge of God's Word is possible only "because and in so far as God wills that they know it ... and because and in so far as there is a revelation of God's will in His Word in which the impotence of disobedience is set aside" (lines 8975–8979). Key theses:

- "We do not find the Word of God in the reality present to us. Rather—and this is something quite different—the Word of God finds us in the reality present to us" (lines 8955–8959).
- The possibility of knowledge "represents an inconceivable *novum* compared to all his ability and capability" (line 8901–8902).
- "The possibility of knowledge of God's Word lies in God's Word and nowhere else. In the absolute sense its reality can only take place, and it can do so only as a miracle" (lines 10131–10135).
- The *imago Dei* was "totally annihilated" by sin; the "point of contact" is the *rectitudo* restored *in Christ*, "not real outside faith; it is real only in faith" (lines 10967–10968).

Barth also rejects the *analogia entis* (Roman Catholic natural theology) in favor of the *analogia fidei* — see [[analogia-fidei]].

## Brunner's Mediating Position

Emil Brunner (cited in *CD* I.1, lines 10952–10954, with reference to *Gott und Mensch*, 1930) argued that sinful man retains "humanity and personality" from creation that can serve as a formal point of contact for revelation. Barth rejects this flatly: "the humanity and personality of sinful man cannot possibly signify conformity to God, a point of contact for the Word of God" (lines 10954–10956). This dispute came to a head in the 1934 public exchange (*Barth: Nein!*) — see [[karl-barth]].

## The Roman Catholic Position

Roman Catholic natural theology (Thomistic *analogia entis*) holds that creaturely participation in being grounds a genuine (if analogical) natural knowledge of God. Barth is "only a hair's breadth from" this position at the point where he acknowledges a genuine "conformity to God" in faith, but insists the difference is decisive: "Not a being which the creature has in common with the Creator for all their dissimilarity, but an act that is inaccessible to any mere theory, i.e., human decision, is in faith similar to the decision of God's grace for all its dissimilarity" (lines 10982–10984).

## Stakes

Barth argues that the choice between these positions shapes everything: the definition of the Church, the method of dogmatics, the authority of Church proclamation, and whether dogmatics works with a criterion ("the self-certainty of God's Word") or with surrogates (lines 9765–9800). "It makes a great difference whether ... we have to say all this [that the criterion is in the believer's consciousness] or whether we are perhaps not to say it at all. Dogmatics will be very different, Church proclamation will be very different and finally the whole Church will be decisively different according to whether this must be said or not" (lines 9774–9777).
