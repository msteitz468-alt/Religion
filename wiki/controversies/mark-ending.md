The ending of the Gospel of Mark is the most celebrated textual problem in NT scholarship. The question is: where did Mark intend his gospel to end? The major options are (1) 16:8 is the intended ending; (2) the original ending has been lost; (3) the "Longer Ending" (16:9-20) is original; (4) the "Shorter Ending" is original.

## The Textual Evidence

The gospel as transmitted exists in three forms:

**1. Ending at 16:8**: The women flee the empty tomb and "said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" (ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ). This is the text found in **Codex Sinaiticus (א)** and **Codex Vaticanus (B)** — the two oldest and most reliable manuscripts of the Greek NT (4th century CE). It is also supported by Clement of Alexandria and Origen, who appear to know no text of Mark beyond 16:8.

**2. The Longer Ending (16:9-20)**: Found in the majority of later manuscripts, including Codex Alexandrinus (A), Codex Bezae (D), the Byzantine manuscript tradition, and most of the Western tradition. It is attested from the early second century (Justin, Tatian's Diatessaron, Irenaeus explicitly cites 16:19). The vocabulary, grammar, and style are markedly different from the rest of Mark. The content appears to draw on material from Matthew, Luke, and Acts: Mary Magdalene's appearance (cf. Luke 8:2; John 20:14-18); the road to Emmaus (cf. Luke 24:13-35); the Great Commission with signs (cf. Matt 28:18-20 + Acts); the ascension (cf. Acts 1:9-11; Luke 24:51).

**3. The Shorter Ending**: A brief alternative ending found in a few manuscripts (usually following 16:8 or between 16:8 and the Longer Ending): "They reported briefly to Peter and those around him all that they had been told. And afterwards Jesus himself sent out through them, from east to west, the sacred and imperishable proclamation of eternal salvation." Almost universally recognized as a later patchwork designed to supply a missing conclusion.

**Critical consensus**: The Longer Ending and Shorter Ending are secondary, composed to supply what was felt to be a deficient conclusion. This is accepted across the denominational spectrum, including the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox scholarly traditions (even though the Longer Ending is included in their canonical texts as Mark 16:9-20). The UBS4 text and Nestle-Aland27 include 16:9-20 but with a double-bracket indicating low confidence in originality.

## Positions on the Original Ending

### Position 1: 16:8 Is the Intended Ending

The majority position in contemporary NT scholarship. The gospel was designed to end on the note of fear, silence, and incomprehension — a literary "open ending" that:
- Reflects Mark's pervasive paradox motif (the disciples never fully understand; even the women fail at the last moment)
- Throws the interpretive responsibility onto the reader (who knows, from the Prologue and the whole narrative, what the empty tomb means)
- Is consistent with Mark's literary technique of leaving loose ends that the reader must resolve
- Makes 16:8 (with its unexplained verb ending the sentence, ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ — literally "for they were afraid," ending with *gar*) into an intentional stylistic shock

Proponents include N. T. Wright, John Dominic Crossan (from different theological angles), and much of the literary-critical guild.

### Position 2: The Original Ending Has Been Lost (France's Position)

France argues 16:8 is *not* the intended ending, on several grounds:

1. **16:7 creates an unfulfilled narrative obligation**: Jesus's promise ("He is going before you to Galilee; there you will see him") requires fulfillment. The entire geographical structure of the gospel has been pointed toward a reunion in Galilee. To narrate the promise and then omit its fulfillment is to leave the drama structurally incomplete.

2. **Ancient biography does not end in failure**: Even an author who valued paradox would not, France argues, end a βίος with the final actors running away in fear. This is "one paradox too far" — it abandons narrative satisfaction entirely.

3. **The Longer Ending confirms awareness of a gap**: The fact that a later author felt the need to supply an ending implies that something was missing. If 16:8 were a satisfying intended ending, the supplemental tradition would not have emerged.

4. **Physical accident explains the data**: The loss of the last leaf of a scroll or the last leaf of a codex gathering is a well-attested phenomenon in ancient manuscript transmission. The uniformity of the gap in all surviving manuscripts requires an early loss — before the manuscript tradition branched — not deliberate truncation.

France's conclusion: the original ending narrated the Galilee appearance(s), consistent with 14:28 and 16:7, and consistent with what Matthew 28:16-20 records. This ending was lost, probably within the first generation of manuscript copying.

### Position 3: The Longer Ending Is Original

A minority position with some pre-critical support (Irenaeus, the Byzantine tradition). Almost no modern critical scholars accept it, given the clear differences in vocabulary and style. The presence of a double bracket in critical editions reflects the near-unanimous rejection.

### Position 4: The Shorter Ending Is Original

Has essentially no scholarly support; the Shorter Ending is universally recognized as secondary.

## Theological Stakes

The controversy matters for several reasons:

**The resurrection appearances**: If the original ending included post-resurrection appearances (France), Mark joins Matthew, Luke, and John in narrating the risen Christ. If 16:8 is the ending (majority), Mark is the only gospel that does *not* include resurrection appearances, leaving the reader with only an empty tomb and an angelic announcement.

**Christological closure**: The Longer Ending's post-resurrection material (including the ascension and the mission command) is doctrinally important in its own right. Denying its originality raises questions about its canonical weight without removing it from the canon.

**The canonical text**: The Roman Catholic Church's declaration at Trent that the Vulgate canon is authoritative implies that 16:9-20, present in the Vulgate, is canonical Scripture. Most Protestant traditions take a more text-critical approach and bracket the Longer Ending's authority.

**Textual criticism and faith**: The controversy illustrates the tension between text-critical method (following the oldest manuscripts) and the long-standing canonical tradition (which has included 16:9-20 in most translations and liturgical readings for 1,800 years).
