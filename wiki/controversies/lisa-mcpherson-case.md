# Lisa McPherson Case

The **death of Lisa McPherson** (December 5, 1995) is one of the most serious controversies surrounding the [[church-of-scientology|Church of Scientology]]. McPherson died while under the Church's care at the Fort Harrison Hotel in Clearwater, Florida, following 17 days of isolation and the administration of the [[l-ron-hubbard|Hubbard]]-developed **Introspection Rundown**.

## Facts (Wright's Account, *Going Clear* Chapter 7)

- **Background**: McPherson (b. 1959) was a longtime Scientologist and Sea Org member.
- **Triggering incident**: Following a minor car accident, McPherson removed her clothing and appeared to be having a psychotic break. She was taken to a local hospital, examined, and released. Scientologists then took her directly to the Fort Harrison Hotel rather than to a psychiatric facility — consistent with Scientology's categorical opposition to psychiatry.
- **The Introspection Rundown**: Hubbard's 1974 program for handling psychotic breaks ("type III PTS"), which involves complete isolation from all external stimulation and specific auditing procedures. McPherson was isolated in a room for 17 days, tended by rotating Scientology caretakers.
- **Death**: McPherson died on December 5, 1995. She was severely emaciated (lost approximately 15 pounds), severely dehydrated, had multiple bruises, and had evidence of insect bites. The medical examiner's initial ruling was **"undetermined" cause of death**, later changed (see below).

## The Cover-Up (Wright's Account)

[[marty-rathbun|Marty Rathbun]] acknowledged in interviews with Wright and in sworn depositions that he:
- Directed the **destruction of log books and other documents** related to McPherson's care
- Organized the campaign to neutralize the Pinellas County medical examiner, Dr. Joan Wood
- Hosted a series of dinners in Clearwater to cultivate favorable contacts in the local political and legal community, including Mayor Iorio and Tom Cruise

Medical examiner Joan Wood initially ruled the death was "undetermined" and was reportedly leaning toward declaring it a homicide. After what Wright describes as sustained Church legal pressure and interference, she changed her ruling to **"accidental"** — a change that led her to eventually resign and retreat from public life. (Andrew Meacham, *Tampa Bay Times*: "Lisa McPherson Scientology Case Drove Joan Wood from Medical Examiner to Recluse.")

## Legal Proceedings

- **Criminal charges**: Filed in 1997; the Pinellas-Pasco State Attorney's office subsequently dropped charges after Wood changed her ruling, on the grounds that the case could not proceed without a viable cause-of-death ruling
- **Civil case**: Settled in 2004 (terms confidential)
- The Church spent an estimated $20–$30 million on the case (Rathbun deposition)

## The Church's Position

The Church maintains that McPherson received appropriate care consistent with her own religious convictions, which included rejection of psychiatric intervention. The Church disputes that evidence was destroyed or that the medical examiner was improperly influenced. Recorded without adjudication.

## Significance

The Lisa McPherson case is central to the legal and ethical arguments against Scientology for several reasons:
1. It illustrates the practical consequences of the Church's absolute anti-psychiatry stance for members experiencing genuine mental health crises
2. The documented evidence destruction and legal maneuvering (per Rathbun's admissions) represents the most credibly documented instance of institutional obstruction in the public record
3. It demonstrates the intersection of celebrity access (Tom Cruise's dinner with the mayor) and institutional self-protection
4. Dr. Wood's decline after changing her ruling is cited by critics as an example of Fair Game tactics applied to a public official
