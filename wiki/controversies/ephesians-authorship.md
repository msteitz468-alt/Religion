# Ephesians — Pauline or Pseudonymous?

Whether Ephesians was written by Paul himself or by a later disciple writing in his name is the most contested introductory question of the letter. It affects how every interpretive decision is made: the historical setting, the theological development, and the relationship to the undisputed Paulines all read differently depending on the answer.

## The Case for Pseudonymity

**Style**: Ephesians is stylistically unlike the undisputed Paulines. Sentences of extreme length and tortured complexity (1:3–14; 1:15–23; 2:1–7; 3:2–13; 3:14–19; 4:11–16; 6:14–20) occur with an unusual concentration. Long compound noun phrases, genitives stacked on genitives, and broken syntax are found throughout. While long sentences appear elsewhere in Paul (2 Thess 1:3–12; 1 Cor 1:4–8), the sustained concentration of this style across an entire short letter is unique. Erasmus noticed the unusual style in the 16th century.

**Relationship to Colossians**: Approximately one-third of Ephesians' vocabulary (155 of 529 words) also appears in Colossians. This includes long stretches of near-identical language — particularly in the *Haustafel* (Eph 5:22–6:9 // Col 3:18–4:1) and the Tychicus commendation (Eph 6:21–22 // Col 4:7–9, 32 words of near-exact agreement). Mitton (1951) argued that this level of literary dependence is more easily explained if Ephesians is a secondary document that "used" Colossians, rather than a genuine letter written contemporaneously. A real letter would not duplicate one-third of a prior letter's content.

**Theological development**: Several theological features appear more "developed" than the undisputed Paulines:
- The identification of apostles and prophets as the "foundation" of the church (2:20; 3:5) looks back on a foundational generation, suggesting the author writes after the apostles' death
- The household code (5:22–6:9) and its domestic conservatism seem to reflect a "settling down" into social structures characteristic of second-generation Christianity ("early catholicism")
- The church as a universal, cosmic entity (rather than the local assembly of 1 Corinthians) suggests a later, institutionalized ecclesiology

**Impersonal character**: Paul spent 2–3 years in Ephesus (Acts 19:10; 20:31). Yet the letter reads as if Paul and his readers are largely strangers: he has "heard" of their faith (1:15), "assumes" they know his apostolic calling (3:2), "assumes" they have been taught about Christ (4:21). This impersonal quality, advocates of pseudonymity argue, is more easily explained if the letter was written after Paul's death to communities who did not know him personally.

**Key advocates**: Andrew Lincoln (ICC, 1990; c. AD 80–90), Margaret MacDonald (Colossians and Ephesians, 2000; Domitian's reign, AD 81–96), Ernst Käsemann, Rudolf Schnackenburg (German Catholic; pseudonymous), C. L. Mitton.

## The Case for Pauline Authorship

**Style is explicable**: [[frank-thielman|Thielman]] argues Paul was a versatile writer. Long, dense sentences appear in 2 Thess 1:3–12 (roughly the same length as Eph 1:3–14, with similar redundancy and emphasis). The broken syntax of Eph 2:1–7 and 3:1–19 is not unlike Rom 5:12–18 and 2 Cor 2:12–7:7. The stylistic concentration in Ephesians is plausibly explained by the conditions of dictation under Roman imprisonment without opportunity for revision. Dionysius of Halicarnassus observed that stylistic variation within and across a writer's works was a mark of education (Comp. 19); Paul's education makes this fully possible.

**The Colossians relationship**: The 32-word near-identical Tychicus commendation is actually stronger evidence for Pauline authorship than pseudonymity. Thielman finds a direct parallel in Ignatius, who used nearly identical commendations for the same courier in two letters written at roughly the same time and sent to different destinations (Ign. Phld. 11.2; Ign. Smyrn. 12.1). This is natural behavior for a letter-writer who dictates one commendation soon after another; it is odd behavior for a pseudonymist who otherwise takes pains to vary his appropriations from Colossians.

**The theological developments**: The "developed" ecclesiology is relative. "Apostles and prophets as foundation" (2:20) does not require retrospective distance — it describes the role that apostles play while they still live. The household code's social conservatism is paralleled in the undisputed Colossians (3:18–4:1). The cosmic ecclesiology is present, in nascent form, in 1 Corinthians (12:12–13, 27) and Romans (12:4–5). Thielman argues that 7+ years of absence from Ephesus during imprisonment is ample time for the conditions that produced Ephesians to develop without requiring post-mortem authorship.

**The impersonal tone**: Thielman explains this by the specific composition circumstances: Paul writing to a community from which he has been absent 7+ years, to house churches that have since diversified, many of whose members would not have been there during his ministry. This is not unlike his relationship to Rome — equally impersonal (Rom 1:13–15) — where Paul felt apostolic responsibility without personal acquaintance.

**Key advocates**: Thielman (BECNT, 2010), Harold Hoehner (Kregel, 2002 — massive defense), Peter O'Brien (Pillar, 1999), Markus Barth (Anchor Bible, 1974), F. F. Bruce.

## The Amanuensis Hypothesis

Some scholars (Erasmus raised it; later T. K. Abbott) propose that Pauline thought is mediated through a secretary who shaped the style significantly. This allows Pauline authority while accounting for stylistic differences. The weakness: it satisfies neither camp and lacks direct evidence; the hypothesis tends to function as a compromise that sacrifices the historical specificity of either position.

## What Hangs on the Answer

- **Dating**: Pauline authorship = c. AD 62; Pseudonymous = c. AD 80–90
- **Historical context**: AD 62 = the Artemis-cult and early imperial-cult pressure of Nero's reign; AD 80–90 = Domitian's reign, more active Jewish tax enforcement, and a different relationship between Christianity and Judaism
- **Theological authority**: For ecclesiastical readers who hold to Pauline authorship as a mark of canonicity, the question has obvious import
- **The Colossians relationship**: If Ephesians is pseudonymous, Colossians is likely the prior letter that the pseudonymist used. If Pauline, both were written simultaneously, explaining the verbal overlaps naturally
- **Ecclesiology**: The "early catholicism" critique depends on reading Ephesians' ecclesiology as an anachronistic development; defenders argue the cosmic ecclesiology has roots in the undisputed letters

## Resolution Status

**Open**. Critical scholarship still divides roughly 60–40 or 70–30 in favor of pseudonymity, with evangelical scholarship running strongly Pauline. No consensus has emerged. The question is methodologically contested at a deep level: how much stylistic variation is credible within a single author? How developed is "too developed"? The evidence is genuinely ambiguous.
