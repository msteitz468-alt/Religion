# The *Pistis Christou* Debate

One of the most contested questions in Pauline Greek since the 1980s: in phrases like *pistis Iēsou Christou* (Gal. 2:16), *pistis Christou* (Gal. 2:16; 3:22; Rom. 3:22), and similar constructions, what relationship does the genitive *Christou* bear to *pistis*?

- **Objective genitive** (traditional): *pistis* is exercised *toward* Christ; English: "faith **in** Jesus Christ." The believer is the one who believes; Christ is the object of that faith.
- **Subjective genitive** (revisionist): *pistis* is exercised *by* Christ; English: "the faithfulness/faith **of** Jesus Christ." Christ himself is the one who exercises faith or fidelity, most likely in his atoning obedience and death.

The grammatical question is in principle determinable, but in practice the genitive construction in Greek is sufficiently flexible that both interpretations are possible syntactically, and the debate has shifted primarily to contextual, theological, and lexical grounds.

## The Key Texts

The following constructions are typically grouped together:

| Text | Greek | Traditional | Revisionist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gal. 2:16a | *dia pisteōs Iēsou Christou* | faith in Jesus Christ | faithfulness of Jesus Christ |
| Gal. 2:16b | *ek pisteōs Christou* | faith in Christ | faithfulness of Christ |
| Gal. 2:20 | *en pistei zō tē tou huiou tou theou* | I live by faith in the Son of God | I live by the faith/faithfulness of the Son of God |
| Gal. 3:22 | *ek pisteōs Iēsou Christou* | by faith in Jesus Christ | by the faithfulness of Jesus Christ |
| Rom. 3:22 | *dia pisteōs Iēsou Christou* | through faith in Jesus Christ | through the faithfulness of Jesus Christ |
| Rom. 3:26 | *ton ek pisteōs Iēsou* | the one who has faith in Jesus | the one who shares the faith of Jesus |
| Phil. 3:9 | *dia pisteōs Christou* | through faith in Christ | through the faithfulness of Christ |

## The Traditional / Objective Genitive Reading

**Historical basis**: The phrase has been translated as "faith in Christ" in virtually every major English translation before 2000 (KJV, RSV, NIV, NASB, ESV). Only the NET and CEB have adopted the subjective genitive reading in their text (though other versions note it in footnotes).

**Grammatical argument**: In forty parallel constructions in the NT where *pistis* is followed by a human name in the genitive, the genitive is almost always subjective or possessive — "the faith of X" = "X's faith" — with *pistis* meaning "faithfulness" (as in 1 Macc. 14:35: "the people saw the faithfulness of Simon"). However, the *divine* name is a crucial distinction. In five of the six NT instances where *pistis* is followed by a divine name in the genitive (Mark 11:22; James 2:1; Rev. 2:13; 14:12; Acts 3:16), the sense is objective ("faith in God/Christ"). This is precisely the most relevant comparison group.

**Verbal parallel**: The verb *pisteuō* never has Christ as subject (with the meaning "believe") in the NT. All 200 NT instances of *pisteuō* have humans (or a group) as subject, with God/Christ as the object (toward whom faith is directed). If *pisteuō* consistently expresses human faith *toward* Christ, the nominalized form *pistis* should follow the same pattern.

**Contextual argument (Moo's strongest)**: In Galatians 3:6–9, Paul's central argument revolves around Abraham's human faith — "He believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness." This human faith is the model for the blessing that comes to "those who believe" (3:9). The "faith" in 3:22 (just a few verses later) must be continuous with this human faith in Abraham's pattern. Moreover, 2:16 pairs *pistis Iēsou Christou* with the verb *episteusamen* ("we believed in Christ Jesus") — the explicit verbal parallel confirms human believing, not Christ's faithfulness, is in view. Fidelity re-ingest loci: Moo prefers objective genitive because "(1) it matches Paul's invariable custom of using the cognate verb to refer to human believing" and because *eis Christon Iēsoun episteusamen* "must be roughly equivalent to πίστις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ" (raw L2589; [[moo-galatians-becnt|source]]). Notes add that early Greek interpreters took the genitive as objective (L15709).

**Prepositional parallels**: When Paul makes the object of faith explicit through a preposition (rather than a genitive), he uses *eis* ("faith into/toward Christ," Acts 20:21; 24:24; 26:18; Col. 2:5) — never Christ as the agent of faith. This makes an objective genitive the natural reading.

## The Subjective / Faith-of-Christ Reading

**Hays's narrative argument**: Richard Hays (*The Faith of Jesus Christ*, 1983, 2002 rev.) argued that underlying Galatians 3:1–4:11 is a "narrative substructure" featuring Jesus Christ as the central protagonist — his obedient trust in God leading him to the cross, his vindication in the resurrection. Within this narrative, *pistis Christou* refers to Christ's own faith or fidelity, and believers "participate" in that faith rather than merely believing "in" Christ as an external object.

**Participatory Christology**: Hays's proposal has been embraced by many who stress participatory categories in Paul — union with Christ, dying and rising with Christ, sharing in Christ's story. On this reading, justification comes not through human believing as an act but through participation in the faith-pattern of Christ himself. This has affinities with Eastern Orthodox anthropology and with more recent "apocalyptic Paul" readings (Martyn, Campbell).

**Campbell's radical form**: Douglas Campbell (*The Deliverance of God*, 2009) goes furthest, arguing that virtually all of Paul's *pistis*-language in the crucial soteriological texts refers to Christ's faithfulness, and that human faith (*pistis*) appears only subsequently as a response to Christ's prior action. This produces a strongly monergistic reading of Pauline soteriology.

**The "coming of faith" argument (Gal. 3:23–25)**: Hays and others note that "faith" appears to *come* as a historical event: "Before faith came, we were held in custody under the law" (3:23); "when faith came, we are no longer under a guardian" (3:25). Human *believing* has always existed (Abraham believed!); what "came" must therefore be something other than human believing — most naturally, Christ's own faith or faithful obedience. This is probably the strongest contextual argument for the subjective reading.

**Moo's rebuttal**: The "faith that came" in 3:23–25 most likely refers back to *tois pisteuousin* ("those who believe") at the end of 3:22, not to *pisteōs Iēsou Christou* earlier in the verse. The article on *tēn pistin* in 3:23 is anaphoric — referring back to the most recent *pistis* reference. More importantly, the *era* of faith (contrasted with the era of the law) is well explained as the era in which Abraham's pattern of faith — response to the grace of the promised Christ — becomes the universal mode of relationship with God.

## Resolution Status

**Open — with objective genitive as the majority scholarly position**, though the subjective genitive view has significant and growing support. The two major English Bibles that adopt the subjective reading (NET, CEB) indicate the debate has influenced translation committees. Most commentaries still translate the key phrases as "faith in Christ" while noting the alternative in footnotes.

The debate also intersects with:
- The NPP debate (Dunn, Wright, in later writings, moved away from the subjective reading or adopted a "both/and" position)
- Participatory vs. forensic accounts of Pauline soteriology
- The degree to which Paul's theology is theocentric vs. anthropocentric
