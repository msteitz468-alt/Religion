# Direction of Influence — Isaiah's Suffering Servant and Job

[[book-of-job|Job]] and the [[servant-songs|Servant Songs]] of [[isaiah|Isaiah]] both present a
righteous figure who suffers without desert. Whether either text depends on the other, and in which
direction, bears directly on the dating of both — and, for Christian readers, on how Job stands to
New Testament Christology.

## Why it matters

The dependence claim is load-bearing rather than decorative. If Job is prior and formative, then a
wisdom text about undeserved individual suffering stands upstream of the Servant Songs, and so
upstream of the Servant's role in the New Testament's interpretation of Jesus' mission. If Isaiah is
prior, Job becomes a wisdom-tradition reflection on a theme already given prophetic form. The
positions also cut against different datings of Job, on which see the text page.

## Position 1: Job is prior and formative (Pfeiffer, Terrien; adopted by Hartley)

[[john-hartley|Hartley]] states the correspondences he takes to be significant. "The most significant
theme common to both books is that of the righteous sufferer." His parallels:

| Motif | Isaiah | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Despised | 53:3 | 19:18 |
| Considered smitten of God | 53:4 | 19:21 |
| Spat upon (*rōq*) | 50:6 | 30:10 |
| Deserted (*ḥdl*) | 53:3 | 19:14 |
| Innocent of violence and deceit | 53:7, 9 | 6:30; 16:17; 17:4 |
| Resting his case with God | 49:4; 50:8–9 | 13:15; 16:19 |

Hartley's conclusion is a claim about preparation rather than quotation: "the message of the book of
Job prepared the people to understand and receive Isaiah's bold new message that God was going to
redeem his people and the world through the innocent suffering of his obedient Servant."

He is careful about the one motif that does *not* transfer cleanly. Vicarious suffering — the
Servant's defining feature — "is merely hinted at in the book of Job, e.g., when God instructs Job to
pray for his comforters when they offer up burnt offerings (42:7–9; cf. 22:30)." That is a hint, not
a parallel, and Hartley does not overstate it.

**The dating consequence, and a tension in Hartley's own account.** This position requires Job to
predate Isaiah. Hartley's stated conclusion elsewhere is a 7th-century date, in Hezekiah's era. But
his Isaiah-priority argument yields a date "no later than the second half of the 8th century." The two
are not reconciled in the commentary; the wiki records both rather than harmonizing them.

## Position 2: Isaiah is prior

The converse dependence, requiring a late-exilic or post-exilic Job. This is the natural companion of
the later datings recorded on the text page — Terrien and Guillaume's 6th century, or Dhorme, Fohrer
and Gordis's 4th–3rd. *No source ingested by this wiki argues the case in detail; the position is
recorded here as the live alternative, not as a reconstructed argument.*

## Position 3: Common tradition, no literary dependence

That both texts draw on a shared Near Eastern and Israelite stock of righteous-sufferer material — of
which Hartley himself catalogues Mesopotamian and Egyptian instances — without either depending on the
other. Hartley's controlling verdict on the ANE comparanda, that influence runs "more in format than
in substance," is the kind of reasoning that supports this option, though he does not apply it to the
Isaiah question.

## Position 4: No significant relation

That the thematic overlap is generic to the problem of innocent suffering and requires no explanation
beyond the shared subject.

## Status

Open. The wiki's coverage is one-sided: it has ingested Hartley arguing Position 1, and Oswalt on
Isaiah, but no source arguing Positions 2–4 at length. Treat the imbalance on this page as a coverage
gap rather than a verdict.
