# The Relationship between 2 Peter and Jude

A large portion of the [[jude|Letter of Jude]] reappears, in roughly the same order, in
[[2-peter|2 Peter]] — the overlap concentrated in **2 Peter 2 ≈ Jude 4–18**, with further parallels
in 2 Peter 1 and 3. The literary relationship is undeniable; its *direction* is one of the classic
source-critical puzzles of the catholic epistles, and it bears directly on the dating and authorship
of both letters. [[gene-green|Gene L. Green]] (BECNT, 2008; [[green-jude-2peter-becnt|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18) adopts the majority view — **2 Peter used
Jude** — and frames the borrowing through the ancient literary category of *imitatio*. Body digests R6–R7 supply concrete adaptation notes (Jude 4/5–8/11–13 reworked; flood added from other tradition; Michael legend dropped; Balaam expanded).

## The Four Options

1. **2 Peter used Jude** (the majority: Bauckham, Kelly, Neyrey, Green, et al.). The dependent
   direction explains the data best (see below).
2. **Jude used 2 Peter** (Bigg, Spitta; Moo). Favored historically by Peter's greater prominence, and
   by the apparent fact that 2 Peter speaks of the false teachers as *coming* (future) while Jude
   treats them as already *present*; Jude 17–18 may echo 2 Pet. 3:2–3.
3. **A common source.** Both drew on a shared tract (as 1 Peter and the Paulines share a household
   code). Explains both similarities and differences, but multiplies unknown entities.
4. **Same author** (J. A. T. Robinson). Both letters by one hand. Almost no adherents — the
   vocabulary and style differ too greatly.

## Green's Case for Jude's Priority

Green (with the majority) judges 2 Peter the borrower:
- **Jude is tightly, exquisitely composed; the parallel sections of 2 Peter are more loosely
  structured** (Bauckham) — easier to explain as 2 Peter reworking a crafted source than as Jude
  distilling 2 Peter.
- **The borrowed material clusters in 2 Pet. 2:1–3:3.** If Jude were excerpting 2 Peter, why ignore
  almost all of 2 Peter 1 and 3?
- **2 Peter is longer and more elaborate**; the early-church tendency was toward *expansion*, not
  abridgment (Kelly).
- **2 Peter suppresses Jude's apocryphal citations** — the 1 Enoch quotation (Jude 14–15) disappears,
  and the Michael–Moses dispute (Jude 9) is dropped while “glories” slander is retained with “they show no fear” (2:10b) — most naturally read as Peter
  editing *out* material he found awkward. See [[jude-1-enoch-canon|Jude's Use of 1 Enoch]].
- **Flood generation (2:5) is not from Jude** (Jude’s catalog omits it); Green places 2:4–8 in the broader Jewish tradition linking Watchers, flood, and Sodom, and notes conceptual handling similar to 1 Pet 3:19–20 (digest R6).
- Against a **common source**: the differences are adequately explained by Peter's adaptation, so
  Ockham's razor favors direct dependence (intro L2298); the *shared ordering* of material is the strongest
  positive evidence for literary (not common-source) dependence. Same-author (Robinson) is least likely given vocabulary/style differences.

Green concedes (with Neyrey) that the case is strong but "by no means proven."

## *Imitatio*, Not Plagiarism — and the Honor Reversal

Green's distinctive framing: 2 Peter's reuse of Jude is ancient ***imitatio* / *mimēsis*** —
the honorable, expected re-use and creative reworking of a respected source (Horace, Seneca,
Pseudo-Longinus, Quintilian) — *not* "theft" (*klopē* / *furtum*, plagiarism), which antiquity also
recognized and condemned. M. Green notes that if Peter used Jude, he changed ~70% of its vocabulary —
exactly what *imitatio* called for (re-moulding, not copying).

This raises a sociological puzzle: *imitatio* implies the **imitated author holds the higher honor**.
Why would Peter, the chief apostle, defer to the lesser-known Jude? Green's answer ties the source
question to the **sociology of the early church**: Jude, as a **brother of the Lord** (a member of
Jesus' family, the *desposynoi*), carried special honor — further evidence of the esteem accorded
Jesus' relatives in the earliest church. The literary relationship is thus "not simply a question of
sources… it also brings us to the core of the sociology of the early church, especially its structures
of honor and authority." See [[jude-brother-of-jesus|Jude, the Brother of Jesus]].

## Why the Direction Matters

- **Dating and authorship of 2 Peter.** If Jude is authentic and early, and 2 Peter depends on it,
  2 Peter can still be Petrine (Green). If instead Jude were *late*, 2 Peter's dependence would push
  it later still — which is how Kümmel argued ("Since Jude belongs to the post-apostolic age, Peter
  cannot have written 2 Peter"). Green breaks that chain by defending Jude's authenticity. See
  [[2-peter-authorship|2 Peter: Authorship]].
- **The opponents are not identical.** Despite the shared language, Green insists the two letters
  counter *different* situations: Jude's intruders pervert **grace** into license (an outside
  incursion); 2 Peter's false teachers are **insiders** who deny the **parousia and judgment**.
  Peter adapted Jude's denunciation to a new target.
