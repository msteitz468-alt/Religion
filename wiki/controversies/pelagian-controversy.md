# The Pelagian Controversy

The early-fifth-century Western dispute over the relation of divine [[grace]] and human [[freedom|free will]], and the reality of [[original-sin|original sin]] — the formative debate of Western soteriology. Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. XIII. Recorded per the Contradiction Protocol without adjudication.

## The Positions

- **Pelagian** ([[pelagius|Pelagius]], Caelestius, Julian of Eclanum): unconditional free will; no inherited guilt (Adam's sin spreads "by custom and example," not physical descent); [[grace]] as external aid ("a grace of knowledge"); sinless perfection (*impeccantia*) is possible. Infant baptism is benedictory, not remissive.
- **Augustinian** ([[augustine|Augustine]]): humanity is a *massa damnata*; all sinned "in Adam" (reading Rom. 5:12 *in quo*, "in whom," with Ambrosiaster); [[grace]] is an interior, prevenient, and (for the elect) efficacious power; double [[predestination]]; the elect are a fixed number.
- **Semi-Pelagian** (John Cassian, the monks of Hadrumetum and South Gaul): a middle way — grace assists rather than implants the will; the first movement of faith is the sinner's own; predestination is *post praevisa merita*. See [[semi-pelagianism]].

## Course and Settlement

Augustine's *Ad Simplicianum* (397) had already set out the "lump of sin" doctrine when Pelagius and Caelestius reached Africa in 409. Caelestius was condemned at Carthage (412); further condemnations followed at Carthage and Milevum (416); the great African council at Carthage (418, confirmed by Pope Zosimus's *Epistula tractoria*) settled the African church on three points (death as penalty of sin, inherited original sin, the indispensability of grace); Pelagianism was anathematised at the Council of [[chalcedonian-definition|Ephesus]] (22 July 431). Semi-Pelagianism was in turn checked at the **Council of Orange (529)**, which affirmed a moderated Augustinianism while condemning predestination to evil.

The **East** was largely untouched: the Greek Fathers affirmed inherited mortality and an inclination (*rhopē*) to sin, but denied participation in Adam's *guilt* — so that [[theodore-of-mopsuestia|Theodore of Mopsuestia]] could be branded "an Eastern Pelagius" (a charge Kelly doubts). See the East/West contrast on [[original-sin]].
