# John and the Synoptic Gospels — Independence or Dependence?

The relationship between the Gospel of John and the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke) is one of the most-discussed questions in Johannine scholarship. John overlaps with the Synoptics in several key traditions while differing from them dramatically in style, structure, and theological accent. The question is whether these similarities reflect direct literary dependence (John knew and used one or more Synoptics as sources) or independent witness to shared oral or pre-literary traditions.

## The Positions

### 1. John Knew All Three Synoptics (Ancient Consensus)

The ancient consensus, represented by Eusebius citing Clement of Alexandria, held that John wrote "last of all" with full knowledge of the other three Gospels, deliberately composing "a spiritual Gospel" to supplement their "outward facts." The canonical placement of John fourth reinforced this assumption: any reader encountering John after the other three would have all three in view.

**Supporting arguments**: The overlap at multiple points (John the Baptizer, feeding of 5000 + walking on water, triumphal entry, anointing at Bethany, passion sequence) is too extensive to be coincidental. John's Gospel assumes reader familiarity with certain traditions.

**Difficulties**: The wording and sequence of overlapping passages differ too consistently for direct literary dependence on the same written texts. Where one would expect verbatim or near-verbatim borrowing (as Matthew and Luke borrow from Mark), John's parallels are loose and often diverge in precisely those places where independent tradition might explain divergence.

### 2. John Knew Mark (20th-Century Majority)

From the late 19th to mid-20th century, many scholars (Streeter and others) held that Markan priority is so strong that John must at minimum have known Mark's Gospel in its final written form.

**Supporting arguments**: The most compelling Johannine parallels are with Mark, not Matthew or Luke; the sequence of certain Markan pericopes reappears in John.

### 3. John Independent of Written Synoptics (Gardner-Smith, Dodd; near-current consensus)

Following P. Gardner-Smith's landmark 1938 monograph and C. H. Dodd's *Historical Tradition in the Fourth Gospel* (1963), the dominant view shifted toward Johannine independence from the *written* Synoptics. John likely knows oral traditions that overlap with — and are intertwined with — the traditions behind each synoptic stratum (Mark, Q, special Matthew, special Luke). But he did not have the Synoptic texts on his desk.

**[[j-ramsey-michaels|Michaels]]'s position**: Independent of the written Gospels, but the traditions are "intertwined" — neither a simple "John corrects the Synoptics" nor a "John supplements the Synoptics" model works. In any given parallel, either version might be more primitive. The pattern of similarities and dissimilarities "remains an enigma."

**Supporting arguments**: If John knew Mark, the divergences — especially in the passion narrative, which both narrate at great length — are harder to explain than convergences. John's independent topographical and chronological precision (Bauckham's argument) is consistent with independent eyewitness tradition rather than creative reworking of synoptic sources.

### 4. John Knew Mark and Luke (Various Scholars)

A middle position holding that John knew at least Mark and Luke is defended by various scholars (e.g., Frans Neirynck). 

## Points of Overlap

The Gospel of John shares the following traditions with the Synoptics, always with significant differences:

| Tradition | Synoptic form | Johannine form |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| Ministry of John the Baptizer | At Jordan; after prologue | At beginning; John frames chs. 1–3 |
| Temple cleansing | Final week (Mk 11:15–17) | Beginning of ministry (2:13–22) |
| Healing of official's son | Healing of centurion's servant (Mt 8/Lk 7) | Royal official (4:46–54) |
| Feeding of 5000 | All four Gospels | 6:1–14 |
| Walking on water | Mark 6 / Matt 14 | 6:16–21 |
| Anointing at Bethany | Mk 14 / Mt 26 (unnamed woman) | 12:1–8 (Mary of Bethany) |
| Triumphal entry | All three Synoptics | 12:12–19 |
| Prediction of betrayal/denial | All three Synoptics | 13:21–38 |
| Passion narrative | All three Synoptics | Chs. 18–19 (diverges on timing, trial, etc.) |

## The Passion Chronology Problem

The most significant point of divergence is the timing of the crucifixion relative to Passover. In the Synoptics, the Last Supper is a Passover meal; in John, the crucifixion occurs when the Passover lambs are being slaughtered in the temple (19:14: "it was the preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour"). Scholars debate which chronology is historically more original and how to harmonize them.

## Temple Cleansing Placement

The Synoptics place the temple cleansing at the end of Jesus' ministry, immediately before the passion. John places it at the beginning (ch. 2). Three explanations: (1) John moved it for theological effect; (2) the Synoptics moved it; (3) there were two separate cleansings. Michaels: John's placement is structural — it introduces the passion theme from the very beginning of the ministry (the body as the destroyed/raised temple, 2:19). Whether it is historically the same event as the Synoptic one is less important than its function in John's narrative.

## Resolution Status

**Open, with near-consensus** toward Johannine independence. Most NT scholars today follow Gardner-Smith/Dodd in holding John independent of the written Synoptics. But the question of whether John knew Mark, or Luke, or both, continues to generate substantial debate. Michaels's "intertwined traditions" model is influential but not universally accepted.
