# Scripture vs. Church as the Criterion of Dogmatics

## The Dispute

What authority stands over the Church's proclamation as its criterion and judge? Three broad positions are in play in modern dogmatics.

## Position 1: Roman Catholic — The Teaching Office as *Regula Proxima Fidei*

According to Roman Catholic teaching as Barth reads it, the *regula proxima fidei* (the nearest immediate rule of faith) is "not the verdict of the Bible but the verdict of the teaching office on the Bible" (*Church Dogmatics* I.1, lines 11831–32). The Church, as both *norma normata* (standardized norm) and *norma normans* (standardizing norm), as both *ecclesia audiens* (listening church) and *ecclesia docens* (teaching church), possesses full authority to determine how Scripture is to be received. Church dogma is defined as *veritas a Deo formaliter revelata et ab Ecclesia . . . definita* — truth formally revealed by God and officially defined by the Church (line 12206, citing Deneffe, Diekamp, Bartmann). In this view, dogmatics is "the systematic exposition of these revealed truths" as the Church has received and defined them.

Roman Catholic theology does not regard this as the Church judging for itself arbitrarily; rather, the teaching office *faithfully transmits* what it has received. The *sensus quem tenuit ac tenet sancta mater Ecclesia* — the meaning which Holy Mother Church has held and holds (Vatican I, cited line 12249) — is what preserves the truth of revelation.

## Position 2: Protestant Modernist — The Christian Spirit/Principle as Norm

Protestant Modernism (in Barth's analysis) reaches a structurally identical conclusion by a different route. P. K. Marheineke's Hegelian theology ascribed to the Christian community "the holy spirit of Jesus Christ" who "not only has knowledge in himself but is knowledge" (line 11867–68), making the Bible merely a stage in the self-movement of this spirit — a stage that "has completely lost . . . the character of a court that is independent of this self-movement" (lines 11883–84). A. E. Biedermann defined dogmatics' task as "logically developing our experiential knowledge of the Christian act of faith" (line 11893). G. Wobbermin professed formally that "Holy Scripture alone must be accepted exclusively and unconditionally as the source of dogmatic work" (lines 11850–52) — yet in Barth's analysis still held the Bible "over it only as it has it in it" (line 11855), i.e., as a resource the Church already possesses rather than a court that stands over against it.

## Position 3: Evangelical/Barthian — The "Free Bible" as External Court

Barth's Evangelical position: "the Bible as the free and supreme criterion of Church proclamation" (lines 12028) stands over the Church, not merely within it. The Church cannot be "judge in its own cause" (line 11862). The difference between the divine word and the human word is not "a distinction within the Church of the present itself" (lines 11921–22) but a genuine external confrontation. Scripture is "not the Bible already dogmatically and historically interpreted by the pope or the professor but the Bible that is not yet interpreted, the free Bible, the Bible that remains free in face of all interpretation" (lines 11924–26).

Barth's defence of this position is distinctive: he refuses to offer a formal proof (which would itself presuppose the standpoint being contested) and instead points to a *sign* — the fact that the Bible has never wholly lost its voice in the Church even under the most domesticating theories (lines 11948–12000). The event of faith — in which the Bible speaks as God's Word and the Church hears — cannot be presupposed or manufactured, only hoped for and pointed to. This is not proof but witness.

## Contested Ground

The dispute turns on several interlocking questions:

- Whether revealed truth has the character of a *command* or a *proposition*. Barth argues the Roman Catholic reduction of dogma to doctrinal proposition permits a "neutral" attitude to it that is incompatible with the nature of revelation (lines 12396–12416).
- Whether the Church's interpretive act and the Bible's witness can be synthesised without remainder, or whether the Bible genuinely remains external to any such synthesis.
- Whether the event of faith is a present *possession* of the Church (Roman Catholic presupposition; Modernist presupposition) or an eschatological promise and hope that the Church lives toward but cannot claim as a standing datum.
