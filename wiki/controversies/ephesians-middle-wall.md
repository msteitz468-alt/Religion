# The "Middle Wall" in Ephesians 2:14

Ephesians 2:14 declares that Christ "has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the middle wall of partition (*to mesotoichon tou phragmou*)." This brief phrase — *mesotoichon* occurs nowhere else in the NT and is rare in Greek literature generally — has generated a disproportionately large interpretive tradition. What the "middle wall" refers to determines the entire logic of the passage: what specifically separated Jews and Gentiles, and in what sense has Christ removed it?

## The Text

> "For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the middle wall of partition (*to mesotoichon tou phragmou*), having abolished the law of commandments expressed in ordinances (*ton nomon tōn entolōn en dogmasin*), that he might create in himself one new human being (*hena kainon anthrōpon*) in place of the two, so making peace." (Eph 2:14–15)

The Greek is compressed. The *mesotoichon* is identified with the *phragmos* (fence or barrier). What follows in 2:15 — "having abolished the law of commandments expressed in ordinances" — either (a) defines what the wall was, or (b) describes a second, accompanying action. This grammatical ambiguity is the crux.

## Position 1: The Jerusalem Temple Balustrade

**The argument**: In Herod's temple, a stone balustrade (*soreg*) enclosed the inner courts, barring Gentiles from proceeding beyond the outer Court of the Gentiles on pain of death. Josephus describes it as a *druphakon* (a barrier of stone or wood, *Ant*. 15.417) and *thrygkos* (cornice/partition, *War* 5.193–194). Inscriptions forbidding Gentile access under penalty of death were posted on the balustrade; two such Greek inscriptions have been found (CIJ 1400; OGIS 598), one reading: "No foreigner is to enter within the enclosure around the temple. Whoever is caught will be responsible for his own ensuing death." Paul himself was accused of bringing Trophimus the Ephesian past this barrier (Acts 21:28–29).

The connection: the most vivid concrete object in the ancient world that physically separated Jews from Gentiles was this balustrade. *Mesotoichon* = the wall; *phragmos* = the barrier/fence; both describe exactly such a structure.

**Difficulties**: (a) *Mesotoichon* is not the vocabulary Josephus or the inscriptions use for the *soreg*; the lexical fit is approximate rather than exact. (b) If Paul is writing to communities in Asia Minor who have never visited Jerusalem, this is an obscure architectural reference. (c) The immediately following phrase — "having abolished the law of commandments expressed in ordinances" (2:15) — does not refer to the balustrade; it refers to the Mosaic law. This creates a non-sequitur if the wall is the balustrade: two distinct things (a physical barrier and a legal code) are collapsed. Advocates of this position often explain 2:15 as describing the *purpose* of the wall's abolition (i.e., the wall was the law's embodiment), which generates additional complications.

**Historical advocates**: Anselm of Laon; some modern commentators.

## Position 2: The Cosmic Barrier (Gnostic/Apocalyptic)

**The argument**: Some early-20th-century scholars (Schlier, *Christus und die Kirche im Epheserbrief*, 1930; taking his cue from gnostic parallels) read the "middle wall" as a mythological cosmic barrier separating heaven from earth, or the realm of the divine from the realm of humanity. The *archōn* (ruler) of the "realm of the air" (2:2) governs this barrier; Christ's incarnation breaks through it. The Jew-Gentile division is then the *earthly* manifestation of a cosmic division.

**Difficulties**: (a) The gnostic texts that describe such a cosmic wall (Valentinian writings, Pistis Sophia) postdate Ephesians by more than a century. Arguing from these texts to Paul's text is methodologically dubious. (b) The context of Ephesians 2:11–22 is unambiguously socio-historical and ecclesiological: the passage is about the actual historical inclusion of Gentiles in the people of God, not cosmological speculation. (c) The specific phrase "law of commandments expressed in ordinances" (2:15) fits neither a cosmic nor a gnostic referent.

**This position is now almost universally abandoned** in scholarly commentary.

## Position 3: The Mosaic Law as Social Barrier

**The argument**: The "middle wall of partition" is the Mosaic law itself — specifically, the system of commandments and ordinances that distinguished Jews from Gentiles and made table fellowship, intermarriage, and social integration impossible. The *phragmos* (fence) had already been used as a metaphor for the law's separating function:

**Letter of Aristeas 139** (c. 2nd century BC; Jewish apologetics): "Now our lawgiver, being a wise man and specially endowed by God to understand all things, took a comprehensive view of each particular, and fenced us round with impenetrable ramparts and walls of iron (*hēmas periphragmos adiakopaistais kai sidēraois teichesi*), that we might not mingle at all with any of the other nations, but remain pure in body and soul, free from all vain imaginations." The law is explicitly the "fence" (*phragmos*) that separates Israel from the nations — the same Greek word *phragmos* that appears in Eph 2:14.

Josephus (*Ant*. 14.228) uses similar language: "the laws that are peculiar to them (*hiera Ioudaika*)" — specifically the Jewish observances — as the concrete marker that distinguished Jews from Gentiles in Asia Minor. In Ephesus itself, the identifying mark of Jewishness was Jewish religious practice, which created a social barrier as real as any physical wall.

On this reading, 2:14–15 is a single coherent movement: Christ broke down the middle wall (the law as social barrier), namely by abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances (2:15) — the participle "having abolished" is epexegetical, defining what the wall's demolition consisted of.

**[[frank-thielman|Thielman]]'s contribution**: Thielman deploys Letter of Aristeas 139 as the most precise verbal parallel to *mesotoichon tou phragmou*. "Walls of iron" + "fence" (*phragmos*) = the Mosaic law as Israel's national boundary marker. This is the Jewish insider's own self-description of what the law did socially. Thielman further argues that in the specific social world of Ephesus — where "the laws peculiar to the Jews" (Josephus) defined Jewish identity — the law functioned *as* the wall between Jew and Gentile.

**Difficulties**: (a) The phrase "law of commandments expressed in ordinances" (*ton nomon tōn entolōn en dogmasin*) sits uncomfortably for those who wish to limit the abolition to ceremonial or ritual aspects; the phrase seems comprehensive. (b) How does "abolishing the law" (2:15) cohere with Paul's use of the Mosaic law as ethical authority elsewhere (6:2–3, citing Deut 5:16; 4:25ff., incorporating decalogue-type commands)? Thielman's answer: Paul in 6:2–3 is citing OT Scripture that he incorporates into his ethical teaching, not enforcing the Mosaic law *qua* Mosaic law. But critics find this distinction strained. See [[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]].

## Position 4: Metaphorical Social Separation (No Single Referent)

Some commentators (Caird, Lincoln to some extent) argue the image is deliberately evocative rather than referential — "the middle wall" gestures toward the whole apparatus of social separation that made Jews and Gentiles two peoples, without specifying any single concrete object. The wall = the total complex of Jewish identity markers (law, circumcision, purity rules, food laws) that functioned to separate. This has the advantage of handling the text's range of reference but loses the verbal precision of Aristeas 139.

## The Phrase *Heis Kainos Anthrōpos* (2:15)

Whatever the middle wall was, the purpose of its demolition is clear: Christ created "one new human being" (*hena kainon anthrōpon*) in place of the two (Jewish humanity and Gentile humanity). This is not the erasure of Jewish identity (Paul himself remains a Jew in the new community) but the creation of a third entity — the new humanity in Christ that transcends the Jew/Gentile binary without abolishing either party. The *kainos anthrōpos* is corporate (singular noun, plural members) and is identified with the church. Its content is unpacked in 2:19–22 (household/temple imagery) and 4:17–5:20 (ethical formation of the new person).

The image draws on [[new-creation|New Creation]] language: the new human being is the beginning of the new creation announced in 2:10 (*epi ergois agathois*) and developed in 4:24 (*ktisthenta kata theon en dikaiosynē*). See also [[heis-kainos-anthropos|One New Human Being (*Heis Kainos Anthrōpos*)]].

## What Hangs on the Answer

- **The extent of the law's abolition**: If the wall is the temple balustrade or a cosmic barrier, the Mosaic law is preserved intact (its ceremonial embodiment was removed). If the wall *is* the Mosaic law (Position 3), then the law as a system has been "abolished" — a claim with significant theological stakes for Jewish-Christian relations, for supersessionism, and for Paul's coherence across letters.
- **The logic of 2:14–15**: Position 3 makes the grammar coherent (the participle is epexegetical); Position 1 creates a two-step sequence that requires bridging.
- **Social vs. metaphysical categories**: The interpretive choice between Positions 1 and 3 reflects a deeper choice about whether the primary barrier between Jews and Gentiles was architectural/liturgical (access to the temple precincts) or social/legal (the law's marking of Israel as a distinct nation).

## Resolution Status

**Open, with Position 3 dominant in evangelical commentary**. Position 2 (gnostic cosmic barrier) is effectively abandoned. Position 1 (temple balustrade) retains historical defenders but faces the objection that it severs 2:14 from 2:15. Position 3 (the Mosaic law as *phragmos*) is exegetically the most coherent reading of the passage and has Thielman, Hoehner, and O'Brien as its major contemporary defenders. Position 4 (metaphorical composite) is an attractive middle ground but sacrifices verbal precision. The question remains contested at the intersection of exegesis and theology.
