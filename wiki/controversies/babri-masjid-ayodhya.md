# Babri Masjid / Ayodhya

The **Babri Masjid** (Babur's Mosque) controversy is the central case study in the **political abuse of
religious history** in modern India — the subject of Doniger's final chapter. It concerns whether a
16th-century mosque built in Ayodhya, Uttar Pradesh, stands on the birthplace of the god [[rama|Rāma]],
and what authority religious mythology can carry in questions of law, property, and violence.

## The Claim and Its History

The Rāmāyaṇa places Rāma's birthplace in **Ayodhyā**. The modern town of Ayodhyā, Uttar Pradesh, makes
this identification. Within Ayodhyā, a mosque built under the Mughal emperor **Babur** (c. 1527) was long
claimed by Hindu organizations to have been constructed by demolishing a temple marking Rāma's exact
birthplace.

During the **1980s**, as the Hindu right rose slowly to power, Hindu organizations — particularly the
**BJP** (Bharatiya Janata Party) and the **RSS** (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh) — began holding rallies
at the site, demanding the "rebuilding" of a Hindu temple there.

The **1987–1988 televising of the Rāmāyaṇa** (78 episodes on Indian national television) dramatically
intensified the mythological atmosphere around the dispute.

## The Destruction (December 6, 1992)

In **1990**, BJP president **L. K. Advani** put on saffron robes and posed with a bow and arrow on a
truck decorated as Rāma's chariot, leading a procession toward Ayodhyā before his arrest.

On **December 6, 1992**, BJP leaders incited a crowd of approximately 200,000. Shouting "Death to the
Muslims!" the mob attacked Babur's Mosque with sledgehammers. **All three domes were smashed to rubble**
— described by historian William Dalrymple as destroying "symbols of India's traditions of tolerance,
democracy, and secularism." The riots that followed killed more than a thousand people, with reactive
riots continuing intermittently and flaring seriously again in 2002.

As of Doniger's writing (2008): nothing but vandalized ruins at the site, intense security, a small
makeshift puja shrine with oleograph pictures of Rāma, and in a nearby BJP tent a model of the temple
they intend to build.

## The Scholarly Response

In 1989, historians at the Centre for Historical Studies at **Jawaharlal Nehru University (JNU)**
published a pamphlet: *The Political Abuse of History: Babri-Masjid-Rama-Janma-Bhumi Dispute*. All
essays argue that the case for a Rama temple under the mosque is **based on myth rather than history**
— no archaeological or inscriptional evidence for a historical Rama at Ayodhyā exists.

Doniger's formulation: **"There is no second Troy here for a Schliemann to come along and discover. Or,
rather, there is a second, and a third, and a nineteenth Troy for anyone to discover"** — Rāma is
claimed by many places across South India as well as North, because the Rāmāyaṇa was retold many times
in many different regional traditions.

The specific claim that a Hindu temple was under the mosque is further undermined by: (a) no evidence
for the pre-mosque structure identified as the temple; (b) no evidence the modern Ayodhyā is the
Rāmāyaṇa's Ayodhyā; (c) the Rāmāyaṇa itself is recognized by literary historians as a work of
fiction composed by human authors, not a historical record.

## The Judicial Sideshow

During a 1989 judicial procedure over Muslim access to the mosque, it was reported that **"a monkey sat
atop the court building and when the order was passed it violently shook the flagstaff."** The monkey
was interpreted by Hindu nationalists as Hanumān, who has become the mascot of the RSS. (Forster, as
early as 1921, referred to Hanumān as "the Monkey God (Hanuman-who-knocks-down-Europeans).")

A judge in Dhanbad separately issued a summons to **Rāma and Hanumān** to appear in court to clarify
ownership of a temple site — the summons was returned as the address was incomplete; a second summons
went out through local newspapers. This is a distinct case but illustrates the broader legal
environment in which divine ownership of sacred sites is treated as justiciable.

## Positions

**BJP/RSS/Hindu-nationalist position**: The mosque was a symbol of conquest; the temple must be
restored; Rāma's birth here is a matter of faith which courts should respect. *Hanumān as RSS mascot*
connects the mythic past directly to present political activism.

**Secular-historical position (JNU historians, Doniger, Romila Thapar)**: The claim is a confection;
there is no evidence; the political abuse of mythology has caused incalculable violence. The myth
"tells us a great deal about attitudes toward women and tribal peoples in the early centuries CE" —
it is not a record of actual events at a recoverable site.

**Indologist position (Doniger specifically)**: The Rāmāyaṇa is a work of **fiction** by human
authors in specific historical periods. Its value lies in what the human imagination did with actual
circumstances — not in any archaeological residue. Using the myth as a property claim or a warrant
for violence is both epistemically and ethically incoherent.

## Broader Pattern: The Ram Setu (Sethusamudram Canal)

A parallel controversy erupted in **September 2007** when the **Sethusamudram Shipping Canal Project**
proposed dredging through the shoals between India and Sri Lanka. Hindu groups argued this would
destroy "**Rāma's Bridge**" (*Rāma Setu* / *Adam's Bridge*), built by Rāma's monkey army (per
Rāmāyaṇa 4.63.17). Mass protests blocked trains across India.

- The Archaeological Survey of India stated the formation is natural, not man-made
- The Indian Supreme Court determined it was not man-made
- The Indian government initially submitted a report questioning Rāma's historical existence; after
  BJP protests about "questioning the faith of a billion," the government **withdrew its own
  archaeological finding**

Historian Romila Thapar pointed out: "All this uncertainty is quite apart from the question of the
technical viability of building a bridge across a wide stretch of sea in the centuries BC."

## Resolution Status

**Open as of 2008.** The Indian Supreme Court subsequently ruled (2019, after Doniger's writing) on
the property question; the political, epistemological, and communal dimensions remain entirely open.
