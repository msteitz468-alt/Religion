# The New Perspective on Paul (NPP)

The "New Perspective on Paul" is a major movement in Pauline scholarship since the 1980s that challenges the Reformation reading of Paul — particularly Paul's polemic against "works of the law" in Galatians and Romans. The NPP argues that the Reformers (especially Luther) misread Paul by projecting the late medieval problem of conscience-stricken works-righteousness onto first-century Judaism. Paul was not arguing against a Jewish religion of self-salvation by accumulated merit; he was dealing with the specific problem of Jewish ethnic boundary markers that excluded Gentiles from the people of God. The debate is exegetically, historically, and theologically consequential: it touches the meaning of justification, the nature of the problem Paul addresses, and the ongoing relevance of his argument for the church.

## The Sanders Revolution (1977)

The NPP was catalyzed by E. P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977), in which Sanders conducted a comprehensive survey of Second Temple Jewish literature (Tannaitic literature, Dead Sea Scrolls, Pseudepigrapha, the Apocrypha) and argued that Jewish soteriology was universally characterized by what he called **covenantal nomism**:

- God graciously elects Israel and establishes a covenant with them (getting *in*)
- Torah observance is the means by which Israel *maintains* covenant status (staying *in*)
- Repentance and atonement are available for failures
- God's final judgment vindicates those who have remained within the covenant

Sanders's conclusion: "Palestinian Judaism was not a religion of merit but a religion of grace." Therefore, Paul was not arguing against a Jewish religion of works-righteousness (which never existed). Paul's critique of Judaism must have a different basis. Sanders's own solution: Paul argues "from solution to plight" — not from the human problem of guilt to Christ as the answer, but from the Christ event backward to the conclusion that Torah must have been insufficient. As Sanders put it in a widely quoted phrase: "In short, this is what Paul finds wrong with Judaism: it is not Christianity."

## Dunn's New Perspective (1982/1983)

James D. G. Dunn coined the phrase "New Perspective on Paul" in a 1982 Manson Memorial Lecture (published 1983) and developed it extensively in his Galatians (1993) and Romans (WBC, 1988) commentaries. Dunn accepted Sanders's covenantal nomism and proposed a more exegetically grounded alternative reading of Paul.

**Key claim**: "Works of the law" (*erga nomou*) in Paul does not refer to works in general or to human achievement as the basis for salvation. It refers specifically to the distinctive practices of Jewish identity — circumcision, food laws, Sabbath observance, purity rules — that functioned as "boundary markers" separating Jews from Gentiles. These are the *works* Paul opposes because they are what prevent the full inclusion of Gentiles in the people of God without requiring them to become ethnically Jewish.

**The logic of Paul's argument (Dunn)**: Paul sets faith in Christ against "works of the law" because:
1. The era of Torah observance as the defining mark of God's people has ended with the coming of the Messiah (salvation-historical argument)
2. Torah observance maintained ethnic boundaries that exclude Gentiles, contradicting God's promise to Abraham that "all nations" would be blessed (ethnic-inclusion argument)

**Wright's contribution**: N. T. Wright (NPP's most prominent contemporary champion) frames Paul's argument within the narrative of God's covenant with Israel: justification is God's declaration that someone belongs to the covenant people. "The righteousness of God" is God's covenant faithfulness — his fulfillment of promises to Abraham. Justification is the verdict that identifies who belongs to the covenant community, and this verdict is given on the basis of faith (the covenant sign of Gentile inclusion) rather than Torah observance (the covenant sign of ethnic Israel). Wright accepts that there is a forensic dimension but argues it has been misread as primarily soteriological when it is primarily ecclesiological.

## The Traditional / Reformation Response

Luther read Galatians as Paul's polemic against a general principle of human merit — works-righteousness as the universal human attempt to secure divine favor through moral achievement. This reading shaped Protestant soteriology: justification by faith alone answers the question "How can a sinner stand before a holy God?" It is the answer to a universal human problem, not merely a first-century Jewish-Gentile problem.

**Post-NPP evangelical defenders** (Schreiner, Moo, Seifrid, Westerholm, Das, Kim) argue:

**1. The "works of the law" are not limited to identity markers.** Paul uses the phrase six times in Galatians and consistently treats the whole Torah as at stake, not merely circumcision or food laws. In 3:10, he quotes Deuteronomy 27:26 about "all that is written in the Law"; in 5:3, he warns that circumcision obligates one to do "the whole law." The phrase consistently refers to Torah observance in its totality, not selected practices. Moo: if Paul were concerned only with ethnic boundary markers, why does he cite Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous will live by faith") and Leviticus 18:5 ("the one who does these things shall live by them")? These are texts about the general principle of doing vs. believing, not about circumcision.

**2. The anthropological dimension is present.** [[douglas-moo|Moo]] argues Gal. 3:10 presupposes human inability in a syllogism: only those who do *everything* in the law escape the curse; no one can do everything; therefore, all who rely on Torah observance are cursed. This assumption is not arbitrary — it is massively attested in the OT (Deuteronomy itself predicts Israel's failure: 31:14–29; Daniel 9:11; Ezekiel 36:24–28 promises what Israel could not deliver for itself). Westerholm's summary: "The fundamental question addressed by Galatians is not 'What is wrong with Judaism?' but 'What is wrong with humanity that Judaism cannot remedy?'"

**3. Sanders's covenantal nomism is partly right but insufficient.** The traditional reading never claimed that Judaism was a *Pelagian* religion of meriting salvation without any covenant. The claim was that the principle of conditioning covenant membership on human doing — even if within a framework of divine grace — creates the problem Paul addresses. The distinction between "getting in by grace" and "staying in by works" was recognized by the Reformers. The real question is whether Paul's argument applies *only* to Torah observance or also (and more fundamentally) to any conditioned human performance as the basis of acceptance before God.

**4. "The righteousness of God" is not reducible to covenant faithfulness.** Against Wright, Schreiner and Moo argue that *dikaiosynē theou* (God's righteousness) draws on a range of OT background (especially Isa. 40–55) and cannot be reduced to "God's faithfulness to his covenant promises." The OT itself locates God's righteousness in his own character (deeper than any covenant), and Paul's use of the phrase in Romans 3:21–26 refers to a saving righteousness that is *given* to believers, not merely a divine attribute.

**5. Justification is about status before God, not merely community membership.** Against Wright's "membership in God's covenant people" as the meaning of justification, Moo argues that *dikaioun* retains its OT forensic sense throughout: it is God's declaration that a person has right status before him. Membership in God's people follows from justification; it is not the same thing as justification. "The content of the doctrine of justification by faith should be distinguished from its scope" (Gathercole).

## Beyond the New Perspective (Barclay, Watson)

More recently, John Barclay (*Paul and the Gift*, 2015) and Francis Watson (*Paul, Judaism, and the Gentiles*, 2007 rev.) have argued that both the traditional and NPP readings are insufficiently grounded in the particularity of the Christ event.

**Barclay**: Paul operates with a concept of grace as "unconditioned gift" — a gift that overrides all prior standards of worth (Jewish, Gentile, moral, or ritual). The problem with Torah is not simply that it is ethnic or that humans can't keep it, but that it "reinstates a standard of value or worth which denied the truth of the Christ-gift as unconditioned gift." This is a third logic, neither the traditional nor the NPP.

**Moo's assessment**: Barclay's insight about the unconditioned nature of God's grace is valuable and convergent with the traditional reading; but his insistence that no abstract principles can be drawn from Paul's particular argument is methodologically illegitimate. "The move to find abstract principles within the particularities of specific texts surely cannot be ruled out in principle."

## Implications for the Wiki

The NPP debate has touched multiple texts already ingested:
- **Galatians** (Moo, BECNT): The primary battleground. Moo's three-level argument (salvation-historical + ethnic + anthropological) is the wiki's most thorough evangelical anti-NPP statement.
- **Romans** ([[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]], BECNT): Schreiner's commentary defends the traditional reading throughout chapters 3–4.
- **2 Corinthians** ([[mark-seifrid|Seifrid]], PNTC): Seifrid is a leading Pauline justification specialist and anti-NPP from the Lutheran angle.
- **1 Corinthians** ([[roy-ciampa-brian-rosner|Ciampa-Rosner]], PNTC): Less directly engaged with the NPP, but their "glory of God" and "OT-primary" method operates independently of the NPP framework.

The NPP debate is the most significant single ongoing controversy that spans multiple ingested sources.
