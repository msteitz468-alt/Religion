# Who Defines Hinduism?

A live dispute over **what "Hinduism" is and who has standing to narrate it** — crystallized by
[[wendy-doniger|Wendy Doniger's]] *[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus: An Alternative History]]* (2009) but
much older than that book. It is as much about **method and authority** as about facts.

## The Stakes (from Doniger, Ch. 25)

Doniger's own framing of the danger is "**the abuse of history**": because the tradition is so vast and
contradictory, "you could easily use history to argue for almost any position in contemporary India" —
that Hindus were vegetarian *and* were not, tolerant *and* were not, oppressors of women *and* their
champions. History thus becomes a weapon in present politics. She juxtaposes **M. S. Golwalkar** (RSS,
1939 — non-Hindus must live "wholly subordinated to the Hindu Nation… not even citizen's rights") with
**Gandhi** (Hinduism "essentially inclusive and ever-growing"), and warns against those who would "set up
a papacy in India, smuggling into Hinduism a Christian idea of orthodoxy."

## The Positions

**1. Doniger's anti-essentialist "alternative history."** There is **no single essence, founder, or
canon** of Hinduism (the "[[hinduism|Zen diagram]]" — overlapping circles with no central ring); Hindus
are "polydox" and orthoprax. The honest history therefore foregrounds the **marginalized** — women, lower
castes, animals — and treats sexuality, myth, and the body frankly. Recorded as a *method*, not endorsed
or refuted here (Contradiction Protocol).

**2. The essentialist / Hindu-nationalist objection.** On this view Hinduism (often *Sanatana Dharma*)
**does** have a coherent core and a dignity that Doniger's margin-centered, sexualized, Freudian,
debunking reading distorts and demeans — an outsider imposing alien (Christian/psychoanalytic) categories.
Doniger documents the political edge of this essentialism in-text: **V. D. Savarkar coined "Hindutva" in
1922**, equating Hinduism with India so as to exclude Muslims and Christians.

**3. The "insider voice / authority" critique — Malhotra's *Being Different* (2011).** A distinct
objection (about *standing* and *method*): that Western academic Indology has spoken *for* Hindus without
their categories. Doniger half-concedes this — calling herself a "**recovering Orientalist**" — while
defending the scholar's right and duty to read texts critically.

[[rajiv-malhotra|Rajiv Malhotra]]'s [[being-different-malhotra|*Being Different: An Indian Challenge to Western Universalism*]] (2011)
constitutes the book-length Hindu rejoinder. Its argument is more structural than Doniger's critics:
Malhotra contends that Western frameworks cannot capture dharmic traditions on their own terms — not
because insiders are infallible, but because **the very categories of Western scholarship are themselves
non-neutral products of a specific civilizational history** (Abrahamic history-centrism, synthetic unity,
Aristotelian binary logic). Key claims:

- Doniger's use of psychoanalytic and feminist frameworks to read Hindu texts is not neutral scholarship
  but an instance of **[[cultural-digestion]]**: dharmic elements dismembered and reassembled within a
  Western meta-framework that erases their original meaning
- The "sameness approach" ("all religions say the same thing") — including perennialist integrations of
  Hinduism into a universal spirituality — is also a form of digestion, eliminating dharmic distinctiveness
  by absorption
- The appropriate response is [[purva-paksha]]: dharmic thinkers should master Western civilization in its
  own terms before engaging it — not to capitulate, but to respond from a position of genuine knowledge
- Malhotra explicitly disagrees with Doniger's reading of Hindu temple reclamation (Ch. 4, n. 41):
  Doniger is accused of "placating the lobby that does not want Hindus to reclaim their temples"

**Malhotra's position (recorded without endorsement per Contradiction Protocol)**: the insider-outsider
distinction is less important than the *framework* distinction. A dharmic insider using psychoanalytic
categories is as problematic as a Western outsider doing so. The key is whether [[sanskriti|Sanskrit
non-translatables]] and dharmic conceptual frameworks are preserved or replaced by Western equivalents.

## Reception (carefully sourced)

- **In Doniger's text (Ch. 25 acknowledgments):** at a **2003 London lecture someone threw an egg** at
  her; **William Dalrymple** stood by her and afterward "threw down a gauntlet… about the need to tell the
  history of Hinduism in a new way" (the book is dedicated partly to him).
- **Outside the ingested text (a later, widely-reported event — 2014):** Penguin India withdrew and
  pulped the book in a legal settlement after a complaint by Dinanath Batra (Shiksha Bachao Andolan) under
  India's law against outraging religious feelings. *This post-dates the 2009 source and is recorded here
  as external context, not as a claim from Doniger's book.*

## Status

**Open.** This is not a dispute that admits a textual resolution: it turns on prior commitments about
whether a religion *has* an essence, and about who may define it. The wiki records the positions and their
tradition-context without adjudicating.

## American Appropriation: A Special Case (Doniger, Ch. 23)

The American context sharpens the question with three nested objections that Doniger identifies and
addresses in her chapter on Hindus in America:

**Objection 1 — Americans got Kali and Tantra all wrong.** American popular culture has seized on
Kali as an archetype (Jungian, feminist, pornographic) and on "Tantra" as exotic sex. Doniger partially
concedes this: much American Tantra is grotesquely distorted. But she notes that "seemingly no limit"
can be placed on the variations Hindus themselves have rung on every aspect of their religion, and
that some aspects of "California Tantra" mirror medieval Indian Tantric antinomianism.

**Objection 2 — Americans have taken up the Wrong Sort of Hinduism.** The Hindu right prefers that
Americans study the Bhagavad Gita and Vedantic philosophy rather than Kali and Tantra. Doniger's
response: the "popular" polytheistic, erotic, violent Hinduism that conservative spokespersons condemn
has been "throughout the history of Hinduism, and remains every bit as real to many Hindus" — the
Brahminical center cannot hold the periphery.

**Objection 3 — Only Hindus may write about Hinduism.** Even correct scholarship by non-Hindus
desecrates the tradition. Doniger's response: "one cannot exploit texts and stories in the same way
that one exploits people — or horses." Stories, "like bhakti in the late Puranic tradition, constitute
a world of unlimited good, an infinitely expansible source of meaning. An American who retells a Hindu
story does not diminish that story within the Hindu world." An American interlocutor can build bridges
that neither insiders constrained by identity politics nor hostile outsiders can build.

The **"inverted pizza effect"**: Indian practitioners take ideas from American Tantra scholars and sell
them to American disciples as authentically Indian — native categories distorted by nonnative
perceptions and then reappropriated, "adding insult to injury."
