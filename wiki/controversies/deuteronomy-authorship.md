The question of Deuteronomy's authorship and date is the most contested issue in Pentateuchal studies. The critical consensus places Deuteronomy in the seventh century BCE, associating it with the Josianic reform; conservative scholarship defends Mosaic origin, using the [[suzerain-vassal-treaty|suzerain-vassal treaty form]] as the primary dating argument.

## The Josianic Reform Hypothesis

The foundational text for critical dating is **2 Kings 22–23**: In the 18th year of King Josiah (c. 621 BCE), a "book of the law" (*sefer ha-torah*) was discovered in the Temple during renovation. When read to Josiah, it provoked intense religious reform — the centralization of worship at Jerusalem, the destruction of local altars (*bamot*), and a sweeping renewal of the Passover. The reform mirrors the demands of Deuteronomy 12–18 (centralization of sacrifice at "the place YHWH will choose") with remarkable precision.

**Critical conclusion**: Deuteronomy *is* the "book of the law" found by Josiah — but it was not discovered; it was composed in this period (or shortly before) and placed in the Temple to authorize Josiah's reforms. The theological program of Deuteronomy (centralization, elimination of competing sanctuaries, prophetic succession, covenant demands) fits the political needs of the Josianic monarchy.

Wellhausen's evolutionary framework read Deuteronomy as evidence that true ethical monotheism and covenant consciousness were products of the prophetic movement of the 8th–7th centuries, not original to Moses.

## The Conservative Response

[[peter-craigie|Craigie]] and M. G. Kline dispute this on formal-comparative grounds:

**The Hittite treaty argument**: Second-millennium Hittite suzerain-vassal treaties contain a **historical prologue** recounting the suzerain's past acts of beneficence. First-millennium Assyrian vassal treaties do not include historical prologues. Deuteronomy contains a historical prologue (chs. 1–3). Therefore Deuteronomy's formal structure fits a second-millennium context, not a first-millennium (Josianic) context. See [[suzerain-vassal-treaty|Suzerain-Vassal Treaty]].

**The Josianic reform as rediscovery**: Craigie reads 2 Kings 22–23 as a **genuine rediscovery** of an ancient document, not a fabrication. The shock and grief of the discovery (Josiah tears his garments, 22:11) is consistent with encounter with an authentic ancient text that reveals how far Israel has strayed — not with the unveiling of a document just written to authorize a predetermined reform.

**Weinfeld's counterargument**: Moshe Weinfeld argued in *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) that the closest formal parallels to Deuteronomy are the **Esarhaddon loyalty oaths** (672 BCE) — an Assyrian suzerain-vassal treaty. On Weinfeld's reading, Deuteronomy's authors consciously adapted the dominant political treaty form of their day (Assyrian imperial administration) as a literary model for YHWH's covenant with Israel. This would support the 7th-century date.

Craigie's response: the overall structural parallels with second-millennium Hittite treaties are more comprehensive; the Esarhaddon parallels are suggestive but less systematic.

## Additional Considerations

**Language**: Deuteronomy's Hebrew is generally later than the Tetrateuch's (Genesis–Numbers), consistent with a 7th-century or later date — though proponents of early dating attribute this to scribal updating.

**The Deuteronomistic History**: Martin Noth (1943) identified Deuteronomy as the theological introduction to a larger historical work (Joshua–Kings), composed by a "Deuteronomistic Historian" during the Babylonian exile (c. 550 BCE). On this view, Deuteronomy is even later than Josiah.

**Internal evidence**: Deuteronomy itself claims Mosaic authorship repeatedly (1:1; 31:9, 22, 24). Whether these claims settle the question depends on one's theological starting point.

## Current Status

The critical consensus (Deuteronomy as a 7th-century or later composition) remains dominant in academic biblical studies. The conservative position (Mosaic origin) is held primarily in evangelical and traditional Jewish contexts. Neither position has been definitively falsified by the evidence available.
