# The de Régnon Paradigm and the Modern Trinitarian Revival

A **historiographical and systematic dispute** over how to read the Trinitarian theology of
the fourth century and its legacy — surfaced sharply by Lewis Ayres
([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004), drawing on Michel Barnes. The
dispute governs how the entire modern secondary literature on the fourth century should be read.

## The Paradigm in Dispute

The **de Régnon paradigm** is the thesis that **Greek/Eastern** theologians begin their
Trinitarian reflection from the **three divine persons** (and must then secure unity), while
**Latin/Western** theologians begin from the **one divine essence** (and must then secure
plurality). The framework derives from Théodore de Régnon's *Études de théologie positive sur
la Sainte Trinité* (1892–98). Originally deployed to assert the superiority of Western
scholastic theology, "in recent writing the same division has been used to highlight the
supposed deficiencies of the west."

The paradigm underwrites the late-20th-century **Trinitarian revival**: theologians including
Moltmann, Rahner, LaCugna, [[gregory-of-nyssa|social-Trinitarian]] readers, Zizioulas, Jenson,
Pannenberg, and Kasper criticized the Western/Augustinian "psychological analogy" tradition as
quasi-unitarian, emphasized the real plurality of persons (often via "social" models), and drew
on Hegelian categories for God's involvement in history. A characteristic move is **"the modern
attack on [[augustine|Augustine]]"** (e.g. Cornelius Plantinga, "Social Trinity and Tritheism,"
1989; Vladimir Lossky, *Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church*, 1957): that Augustine
over-stressed divine unity, founded the Trinity in the unitary essence (the
[[filioque|filioque]] read as symptom), and relied on alien Platonic metaphysics.

## Positions

**(1) The de Régnon / revival narrative.** East and West are divergent Trinitarian types;
Augustine's essence-first, psychological-analogy theology is a Western deviation needing
correction by recovery of the (social, personalist) Greek model. Held across Catholic,
Orthodox, and Protestant systematics.

**(2) The Ayres–Barnes revisionist position.** The east/west typology is historically
unfounded and "inadequate and misleading when used to categorize pro-Nicene theologies." Key
arguments:
- **[[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]]**, the supposed paradigm "eastern" social
  theologian, in *Ad Ablabium* argues *from* the indivisibility of the divine *operation* to
  the oneness of nature (Argument B, which Gregory thinks conclusive) — pointing the reader
  *away* from a "social analogy," not toward one.
- **Augustine** "consistently and specifically rules out the idea that the divine essence is
  prior to the divine persons"; the grammar of [[divine-simplicity|simplicity]] keeps "the
  Father as the personal source," leaving "nothing but the three coeternal and consubstantial
  persons." Augustine's Platonism "serves… to enhance the explanatory power of a fully Nicene
  Trinitarianism" — "virtually the opposite" of the role critics assign it.
- Both belong to one [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene theology]] sharing inseparable operation,
  divine simplicity, and epistemic reserve (see [[opera-trinitatis]]).
- The revival's social/Hegelian moves "systematically undermine" the classical commitments
  (simplicity, inseparable operations, incomprehensibility) they claim to recover. Ayres notes
  the irony, already flagged by Rahner, of asserting Western "functional unitarianism."

Supporting scholarship cited: Michel Barnes, "De Régnon Reconsidered" and "Augustine in
Contemporary Trinitarian Theology" (*Theological Studies* 56, 1995); allied: Bruce Marshall,
Rowan Williams (partially).

## Status

**Open** — an active scholarly dispute cutting across confessional lines. Ayres does not claim
the fourth-century Fathers were free of any ordering: he concedes "the priority of the Father
as cause… is in some sense still a priority," stopping short of denying all hierarchy (a point
also at issue for [[subordinationism]] and on the [[gregory-of-nyssa]] page).
