# The Nature of Brahman and the Self

The defining philosophical controversy of [[hinduism|Vedanta]] — and the wiki's premier example of
**one shared canon read in irreconcilable ways**. All three schools accept the same scripture (the
prasthana-trayi: [[upanishads|Upanishads]], [[bhagavad-gita|Gita]], [[brahma-sutras|Brahma Sutras]]) and
each is founded by a great commentary on the **[[brahma-sutras|Brahma Sutras]]**. They divide entirely
over **what the texts mean** — above all the relation of the self ([[atman|atman]]) to ultimate reality
([[brahman|Brahman]]), and the status of the world ([[maya|maya]]).

## The Test Verse

*Tat tvam asi* — "**that thou art**" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7): the self and Brahman. One sentence,
three readings:

## Positions

- **[[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] (non-dualism — [[shankara|Shankara]], 8th c.)**: literal **identity**.
  Atman *is* Brahman, the sole reality; Brahman is *nirguna* (without qualities); the plural world and
  personal God are [[maya|maya]]. [[moksha|Liberation]] is *jnana* (knowledge). Difference-texts describe
  a lower, conventional level of truth.
- **[[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]] (qualified non-dualism — [[ramanuja|Ramanuja]], 11th–12th
  c.)**: **inseparable dependence**. "That thou art" means the self is the real but dependent *body* of
  the personal God (Vishnu) — unity *and* real distinction. Brahman is *saguna* (personal); maya is God's
  real power, not illusion; liberation is loving communion (*bhakti*).
- **[[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]] (dualism — [[madhva|Madhva]], 13th c.)**: **eternal difference**
  (*pancha-bheda*). God, souls, and matter are forever really distinct; the identity-texts are read
  figuratively (Madhva even parses *atat tvam asi*, "thou art **not** that"). Liberation is graded
  service; some souls are eternally bound.

## Why It Matters

This is the structural heart of the Hindu tradition's internal diversity — and a clean demonstration
that, in the wiki's schema, **`canon_scope` can be identical across sects while interpretation is
opposite**. It also positions Hinduism for cross-tradition comparison: the Advaita pole approaches
monism (cf. [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]]'s [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]]), the Dvaita pole approaches Abrahamic theism
(cf. [[tawhid|tawhid]]), and the maya debate touches Buddhist [[sunyata|emptiness]]. Recorded without
adjudication (Contradiction Protocol).
