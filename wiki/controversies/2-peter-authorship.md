# 2 Peter: Authorship and the Testament Genre

Of all the New Testament writings, 2 Peter's authenticity is the most widely rejected in the academy.
As Bauckham put it, "the pseudepigraphical character of the work [has] come to be almost universally
recognized." The letter claims to be the apostle Peter's (1:1) and his testamentary last word
(1:12–15), but most critical scholars date it pseudonymously to c. AD 80–150. [[gene-green|Gene L.
Green]] (BECNT, 2008; [[green-jude-2peter-becnt|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18) defends authentic **Petrine authorship** — a minority view he shares with Moo,
Schreiner, and others — arguing that none of the standard arguments for pseudonymity is decisive.
This page records the debate; Green's position is one among several.

## The Internal Claims

2 Peter is unusually insistent on its Petrine origin: the self-designation "**Simeon Peter**, slave
and apostle of Jesus Christ" (1:1); the **transfiguration** as eyewitness (1:16–18); a prophecy of
the author's imminent death (1:14, often linked to John 21:18–19); a reference to a **"first
letter"** (3:1, taken to be 1 Peter); and partnership with Paul, whose letters are cited as
"Scripture" (3:15–16). Critics read these as the apparatus of pseudepigraphy; defenders read them as
authentic personal allusions woven into the argument.

## The Arguments for Pseudonymity (and Green's Replies)

1. **Hellenistic vocabulary and ornate style; difference from 1 Peter.** 2 Peter is full of rare
   words and "Hellenistic" terms (e.g., "partakers of the divine nature," 1:4) and differs sharply
   from 1 Peter in style, vocabulary, and key terms (parousia vs. revelation; eternal kingdom vs.
   salvation of souls). Jerome already noted the stylistic gap. *Green's reply*: explicable by a
   **different secretary** (Jerome's own explanation), the well-attested range of secretarial roles
   (dictation → editing → composition); the Petrine corpus is too small to fix what Peter "could"
   write; "Hellenistic" terms were also at home in Hellenistic Judaism.

2. **The testament genre implies pseudonymity (Bauckham's mainstay).** Bauckham argued 2 Peter is a
   "testament of Peter" — a farewell discourse — and since testaments are *always* pseudepigraphic and
   recognizably fictional, contemporary readers would have known it was not by Peter. *Green's reply
   (his most developed counter)*: 2 Peter is **not a testament**. It lacks the genre's salient
   features — no deathbed scene, no account of death/burial, no apocalyptic dreams/visions, no
   blessings and curses. It merely *includes* a testamentary **section** (1:12–15), exactly as
   non-testamentary works do (Paul's farewell in Acts 20:17–38; 2 Tim. 4:1–18; Tob. 14; 1 En. 91).
   Body exposition (≈L2705): “Although this section is surely testamentary since it predicts the forthcoming death of the author and gives moral instruction in light of his demise, we should not thereby conclude that this letter can be classified as a ‘testament’… Major elements of the testamentary genre are lacking while the document before us has all the salient features of an ancient letter.” So the linchpin argument against authenticity fails.

3. **The opponents are second-century gnostics.** This pillar (Käsemann, Kümmel) required a late date.
   *Green's reply*: the opponents are *not* gnostic — no spirit/matter dualism, no salvation-by-*gnōsis*,
   no denial of bodily resurrection; their libertinism is rooted in eschatological skepticism, and
   "knowledge" in 2 Peter is *personal* knowledge of Christ, never opposed to a heretical *gnōsis*.
   See [[2-peter|2 Peter]].

4. **"Early Catholicism" (Käsemann).** 2 Peter supposedly reflects a faded eschatology, a fixed
   deposit of faith, and institutional consolidation typical of the second century. *Green's reply*:
   2 Peter does *not* prescribe the bishop's office against heresy, and the **delay-of-the-parousia**
   problem is demonstrably first-century (Matt. 25; Luke 12; Heb. 10:36–37; James 5:7–8). Its
   "knowledge" is personal, not crystallized orthodoxy.

5. **Historical anachronisms.** "Since the fathers fell asleep" (3:4) supposedly presupposes a dead
   first generation; "your apostles" (3:2) excludes the author from the apostles; Paul's letters as
   "Scripture" (3:16) implies a later canon. *Green's reply*: "the fathers" routinely meant the OT
   ancestors; "your apostles" = those who evangelized the readers; collecting apostolic letters and
   regarding them as Scripture is plausibly first-century (Jesus' words were "Scripture" early).

## Green's Positive Case

- **"Simeon Peter" (1:1)** is a Semitic form found elsewhere in the NT only at Acts 15:14; a
  pseudepigrapher would likely have used the familiar "Simon Peter" — so the unusual form argues
  *for* authenticity.
- The personal allusions are *integral to the argument* (the transfiguration seals the case for the
  parousia, 1:16–18; the death-notice explains the letter's urgency, 1:14), not decorative.
- 2 Peter is **decidedly unlike** the later pseudo-Petrine literature (Acts of Peter, Preaching of
  Peter, the gnostic Apocalypse of Peter), and the early-second-century Apocalypse of Peter *used* 2
  Peter — ruling out a very late date.
- The concerns it addresses fit first-century struggles.

## The Pseudonymity-and-Canon Question

Bauckham held that 2 Peter's pseudonymity is *not fraudulent* but a "transparent" device embodying "a
claim to be a faithful mediator of the apostolic message"; recognizing its canonicity means
recognizing the validity of that claim. Green (with the parallel debate over 1 Peter and the
Pastorals) questions whether a document *known* to be pseudonymous would have been received as
authoritative personal correspondence — the early church censured forged letters when detected (cf.
3 Corinthians). Like Jobes on 1 Peter, he ties the question to **genre**: if 2 Peter is genuine
personal correspondence, the "legitimate pseudonymity" defense is suspect. See
[[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter: Authorship]].

## Assessment

The dispute remains **open** and sharply divides confessional and critical scholarship. Green's
distinctive contribution is to dismantle the *testament-genre* argument (the strongest modern case
for pseudonymity) by showing 2 Peter does not actually belong to that genre, and to neutralize the
remaining arguments as inconclusive. The academic majority is unpersuaded, continuing to regard the
Hellenistic style, the Jude dependence, and the apparent retrospection as marks of a "Petrine circle"
writing after Peter's death. Green's irenic conclusion (echoing the ambiguous verdict of the early
church): authenticity should not be made a test of orthodoxy.
