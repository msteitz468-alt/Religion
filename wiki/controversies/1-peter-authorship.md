# 1 Peter: Authorship, Date, and the Colonization Theory

Did the apostle [[peter-apostle|Simon Peter]] write the letter that bears his name (1:1), or was it
composed pseudonymously by a "Petrine circle" in Rome after his death (c. AD 75–95)? And how did
Christianity reach the remote regions of northern Asia Minor (1:1) in the first place? [[karen-jobes|
Jobes]] in [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|BECNT, 2005]] defends authentic Petrine authorship and an early date,
and proposes a novel historical reconstruction — the **Roman colonization theory** — to explain the
letter's origin. This page records both the authorship debate and her colonization proposal. Positions
are kept in their strongest form; Jobes's quantitative syntax work **does not prove** Petrine
authorship (see below).

## The Critical Case for Pseudonymity (and Jobes's Replies)

The dominant modern view dates 1 Peter to AD 75–95 and attributes it to a Petrine school (e.g. J. H.
Elliott 2000: 127–30). Jobes notes that the sociological plausibility of a Petrine group "does not
address why such a group would write in the specific form and terms found in 1 Peter" (L563). Four
classic arguments, with Jobes's responses:

1. **The Greek is too good for a Galilean fisherman.** *Reply*: polished rhetorical features are real
   (Elliott 2000: 120; Achtemeier 1996: 3), but 1 Peter is "not nearly as rhetorically ornamented as…
   Hebrews," and Latin rhetorical labels do not prove formal training (L574). At the level of
   **syntax** — which "flows almost subconsciously" — the Greek exhibits *bilingual interference*
   consistent with a Semitic L1 author for whom Greek is L2 (preview L576; full method in the end-of-book
   excursus). See **Syntax criticism and S-numbers** below.
2. **The persecution reflects a later era** (Domitian/Trajan). *Reply*: the persecution in 1 Peter is
   *unofficial, local, sporadic social hostility* (slander, ostracism), not state policy — a situation
   that obtained throughout the first two centuries and so is **useless for dating** (L592–L594). The
   "fiery ordeal" (4:12) is OT refining/testing of faith (cf. 1:7; Seneca), not Nero's torches as a
   primary dating clue (L3624–3636).
3. **The church structure is too developed** ("overseeing" elders, 5:2). *Reply*: *presbyteroi* and
   adverbial *episkopountes* refer to the **same office** and do not force a post-apostolic hierarchy;
   early interchangeability (Acts 20; 1 Clem.) supports an *earlier* rather than later date (L3718–
   3720). "Elders **among** you" (*en hymin*, 5:1) suggests fluid organization, not settled
   episcopacy; under Jobes's displacement hypothesis, former elders may be resuming function in a new
   location (L3765–3769).
4. **It depends on Paul** (esp. Romans, Ephesians) and so postdates them. *Reply*: the affinities are
   best explained by *shared early Christian tradition* (perhaps Roman), not literary dependence; and
   if it were by a *Pauline* disciple, attribution to *Peter* is inexplicable (L602–L604).

Two further late-dating arguments: the spread of Christianity to remote Asia Minor would have taken
decades (addressed by the colonization theory, below); and **"Babylon" (5:13)** implies a post-AD-70
apocalyptic code for Rome. *Reply*: modern interpreters almost unanimously take "Babylon" as Rome
*as referent*, but 1 Peter is not apocalyptic and contains nothing overtly subversive about the Roman
state (in fact 2:13–17 urges submission and honor); "Babylon" functions as the closing *inclusio* with
"Diaspora" (1:1) for shared exile status (Rome could be "Babylon" from 63 BC dominance of Palestine)
and may veil Peter's location (L618–L620; L3917–3921). Kelly cautions that if the word is purely
inclusio language, it may not designate a specific locality at all; Jobes notes that a Rome
*composition* site "leans much more heavily on tradition than on exegesis" (L3919–3921).

### Pseudonymity and the Petrine-group fiction

Jobes presses further problems for the late Petrine-circle model: are "Peter, Mark, and Silvanus (1:1;
5:12–13) all part of a pseudonymous fiction?" If Silvanus were the true carrier, how would he represent
the letter knowing Peter had not written it? There is no first-century evidence of such a group with
authority; Mark's Gospel does not write in Peter's name; and Elliott's appeal to Roman church hegemony
"does not explain their specific connection to the regions of Asia Minor addressed" (L565). The
amanuensis theory usually places composition under Peter's direction in his lifetime, but "merely
shades into a pseudonymous author if a close associate composed the letter shortly after Peter's death"
(L567).

## Syntax Criticism and S-Numbers (Excursus)

Jobes's third distinctive contribution (L191) is a **quantitative syntax criticism** of 1 Peter
(adapted from her 1995 LXX dissertation / Martin 1974 criteria; earlier form *BBR* 13.2 [2003]:
159–73). The method produces a graphic profile and an overall **S-number** (normalized average of
criteria scored −1 composition-Greek norm ↔ +1 translation-Greek / Semitic-interference norm)
(L3968–3996).

**Findings Jobes reports** (L4003–4055):
- Only 14 of Martin's 17 criteria occur with sufficient frequency; of those, **nine fall on the
  Semitic side** (prepositions, genitive personal pronouns, attributive-adjective position, dative with
  *en*, among others) and five on the composition side (L4005).
- Relative to Polybius and even Josephus, 1 Peter's syntax is not as "good" if "good" means native
  Classical proficiency (L4024; preview L576). Prepositions in particular are "notoriously the most
  difficult element of a new language to master" (L4005).
- With LXX quotations included, 1 Peter's S-number rises to **0.28** (approaching Greek OT books such
  as Susanna LXX 0.28; Esther LXX 0.33); with quotes removed, Semitic interference remains (L4026).
- **1 Thessalonians** (associated with Silas/Silvanus, 1 Thess 1:1) shows **S-number 0.37** — even more
  Semitic than 1 Peter — with seven of eight comparable criteria at similar frequencies (L4030).
- **Hebrews 5–9** (high-Greek control) shows a **negative** S-number (overall without Semitic
  interference); 1 Peter and 1 Thessalonians are more similar to each other than either is to Hebrews
  (L4040–4042).

**What Jobes claims** (L4053–4055): the extent of Semitic interference indicates an author whose first
language was **not Greek**; the "quality of its Greek ought nevertheless not be exaggerated"
(Achtemeier); the analysis demonstrates the **unlikelihood that the author was a native speaker of
Greek** and opens consideration of Semitic authors for whom Greek was L2 — undercutting Achtemeier's
premise of an "absence of influence by Hebrew or Aramaic."

**What Jobes explicitly does *not* claim** (L3960, L4055):
- The study is "**not an attempt to defend or refute Peter's authorship**" of the epistle.
- Syntax is "**not sufficient for determining who the author of 1 Peter was**."
- It does **not** prove Silvanus wrote 1 Peter, nor that the same author wrote 1 Peter and 1
  Thessalonians (syntax is shared by speakers of equivalent L2 proficiency).
- It does **not** identify any amanuensis.
- A positive S-number / Semitic interference is **not** equivalent to "Peter wrote it" — it only
  *circumscribes* possibilities toward non-native Greek / Semitic L1 authors and undercuts "too good
  for a Semitic speaker" as a blunt weapon.

Critical mainstream still holds that overall Greek quality, Pauline affinities, and the catholic-
epistle profile point to a pseudonymous Petrine circle; Jobes's syntax work is one counter-premise,
not a verdict.

## Silvanus: Courier, Amanuensis, or Fiction? (5:12)

Traditional Petrine defense often made Silvanus the **amanuensis** who produced the "good Greek,"
assuming a Roman citizen could write at that level (Barclay, Cranfield, Davids 1990, Kelly, etc.)
(L3896). Jobes, with Achtemeier, Clowney, Davids 2002, Elliott, Grudem, Michaels, Perkins, E. Richards,
and others, argues that *dia Silouanou … egrapsa* is more likely the **courier** idiom ("to write by
way of someone") — attested in Acts 15:23, first-/second-century papyri, Ignatius, and later Romans
subscripts referring to Phoebe as courier — than a scribal formula (Rom 16:22 uses different language
for a scribe) (L3898–3900). **Even if courier**, Silvanus (or Mark) could still have served as
amanuensis; that question "must be decided on other grounds" (L3900).

Pseudonymous readers treat Silvanus as fictional verisimilitude (Boring 1999: 180). Jobes replies that
such a ruse would preclude actual delivery to an original destination (a pseudonymous bearer spoils the
ruse) and would imply an unknown Asia Minor author presenting a "rediscovered" long-lost letter
(L3902). Silvanus is identified with Paul's companion Silas (Latinized form); Jerusalem leadership;
Roman citizen; co-present with Peter and Mark in two lifetime windows (early 50s; mid-60s Rome)
(L3908; L785).

## The Question of Epistolary Pseudonymity

Jobes presses a methodological point: whether pseudonymity was a legitimate, non-deceptive convention
depends on **genre**. It was tolerated for long-dead figures in *wisdom* and *apocalyptic* (Solomon,
Enoch), but the evidence suggests **forged personal correspondence was condemned when detected** —
the spurious letters to the Laodiceans and 3 Corinthians were rejected and their author (a presbyter)
deposed despite honoring Paul (Tertullian); even the pagan Galen protested works forged in his name;
Metzger's survey of forgery vocabulary and detection practices shows moral offense even in antiquity
(L627–L641). Since 1 Peter bears the form of real personal correspondence (1:1; 5:12–14) with a
concrete audience, "the claim of legitimate pseudonymity becomes quite suspect." (Jobes notes this is
*not* an inerrancy argument — a biblical author could in principle adopt any genuine literary form;
the question is whether *this* genre admitted pseudonymity.) Aland's real-letter vs. epistle
distinction: 1 Peter looks like actual personal correspondence with a coherent epistolary framework.
Schlatter's and Aland's attempts to salvage an "apostolic but pseudonymous" 1 Peter she finds
unpersuasive. History of interpretation: rejection of Petrine authorship is relatively recent
(19th/early 20th c.); earlier support was largely uncontested; late pseudonymy was retained largely
because of the Greek-quality issue — which Jobes's syntax criticism reopens without closing (L663–
L665).

## The Roman Colonization Theory (Jobes's Central Proposal)

The traditional assumption is that 1 Peter's recipients were *indigenous* Asians evangelized in situ
by unknown missionaries (possibly from the Pauline churches). Jobes notes there is **no historical
evidence** of such evangelization, and that 1:12 implies Peter did not first bring them the gospel.
Her alternative:

> The recipients were converted **elsewhere — probably Rome —** and **displaced to Asia Minor**,
> where they found themselves literally "foreigners and resident aliens."

Supporting evidence:
- **Claudius colonized all five regions of 1:1.** Roman colonization (romanizing frontier territory,
  providing military presence, fostering commerce) routinely *populated colonies by relocating people
  from Rome* (freed slaves, volunteers, veterans, sometimes troublemakers; typically ≥300 colonists)
  (L737–L755). The emperor **Claudius** (r. AD 41–54) — "and perhaps only Claudius" — established
  colonies in *each* of the five regions named in 1:1 (L749):
  - **Pontus:** Andrapa → Neoclaudiopolis
  - **Galatia:** Iconium → Claudiconium; Trocmi → Claudiopolis
  - **Cappadocia:** Archelais (colony status)
  - **Asia:** Laodicea → Claudiolaodicea
  - **Bithynia:** Boli → Bithynium-Claudiopolis
- **The Claudian expulsion.** Claudius expelled Jews for disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus"
  (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25; cf. Acts 18:2, Priscilla and Aquila; year debated, ~AD 49 fits Acts;
  Cassius Dio notes assembly ban AD 41). Scramuzza: the late-40s "Jews" expelled were likely prominent
  **Christians** (mass expulsion of ~50,000 Jews is implausible; Christianity still viewed as a Jewish
  sect); Claudius "could have included Christians, but was probably not specifically targeted at
  Christians as Christians" (L765–L769). Such expelled non-citizens (*peregrini*; Greek *parepidēmoi*
  — the very word of 1:1) were the kind of people sent to populate colonies (L787–L789).
- **Peter's Roman association.** Jobes revives the (minority) thesis (Wenham, Thiede, Botermann) that
  Peter's "other place" (Acts 12:17) was an *early* flight to Rome under Claudius (c. AD 42),
  explaining his connection to people who had been in Rome, the later 25-year-episcopate tradition, and
  "Babylon" (5:13) as veiling his location. Against: silence of Acts and Romans; Brown places first
  arrival early–mid 60s; inference from silence is "precarious" (L771–L775). Even if Peter wrote in the
  60s, "the colonization of Roman Christians still provides a motivation for a letter to these remote
  regions" (L799).

### Explanatory Power

The theory, Jobes argues, solves several puzzles at once: how Christianity reached these remote
regions *quickly*; why *no founding evangelist* is named; why Greek-speaking recipients are assumed to
be steeped in the **LXX** (Roman converts, not rural Anatolians who often spoke neither Greek nor knew
the OT); how Peter could write to people he never visited; why he addresses *scattered enclaves* rather
than organized city churches (cf. the oddly tentative elders "among you," 5:1); the *literal* force of
"foreigners and resident aliens" beneath its spiritual meaning; lifestyle evangelism (2:12; 3:15) rather
than overt proselytizing that may have provoked Roman expulsion; and, if early 50s, the gap between
Paul's journeys and his Jewish-mission remit (Gal. 2:8–9) (L799–L809). See
[[christian-exile-diaspora|Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity]].

Jobes also objects to Elliott's *paroikoi* = rural disenfranchised natives: gold-jewelry exhortation
(3:3) is cruel if all are subsistence peasants; rural *paroikoi* may not read Greek; Clowney: one
sociological term cannot fit all churches over a vast area. Elliott is right to highlight historical
setting even if that specific reconstruction fails (L759–L761).

### Weaknesses

Jobes is candid: the surviving evidence is too meager to *confirm* this or any reconstruction, and the
greatest weakness is that **1 Peter never directly mentions such a displacement** — though it mentions
no concrete historical event at all. She offers it as *at least as plausible* as the unargued
assumption of in-situ conversion, and as providing a more specific motivation for the letter than pure
metaphor.

## Date

Jobes concludes the letter could have been written within Peter's lifetime — plausibly the **early
50s** (after an early Roman period, before Paul's later journeys) or the **mid-60s** (with Mark and
Silvanus in Rome, before Peter's Neronian martyrdom c. AD 64–67). Both windows place Peter, Mark, and
Silvanus (5:12–13) in the same location (L785). Persecution profile and elder terminology do not force
a post-70 date (above). The critical majority continues to favor AD 75–95.

## Assessment

The authorship question turns less on any single datum than on whether the cumulative late-dating case
is compelling. Jobes's contribution is to show that each classic argument is *inconclusive* (fitting
any first-century date); to supply a positive historical scenario (colonization) that makes an early,
authentically Petrine letter intelligible; and to show **quantitatively** that the "too good Greek"
premise is overstated — **without claiming that syntax proves Peter wrote the letter**. The critical
mainstream remains unpersuaded, holding that the Greek, the Pauline affinities, and the catholic-
epistle profile point to a pseudonymous Petrine circle. The dispute is **open**.
