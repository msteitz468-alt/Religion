# 1 Peter: Authorship, Date, and the Colonization Theory

Did the apostle [[peter-apostle|Simon Peter]] write the letter that bears his name (1:1), or was it
composed pseudonymously by a "Petrine circle" in Rome after his death (c. AD 75–95)? And how did
Christianity reach the remote regions of northern Asia Minor (1:1) in the first place? [[karen-jobes|
Jobes]] (BECNT, 2005) defends authentic Petrine authorship and an early date, and proposes a novel
historical reconstruction — the **Roman colonization theory** — to explain the letter's origin. This
page records both the authorship debate and her colonization proposal.

## The Critical Case for Pseudonymity (and Jobes's Replies)

The dominant modern view dates 1 Peter to AD 75–95 and attributes it to a Petrine school. Four
arguments, with Jobes's responses:

1. **The Greek is too good for a Galilean fisherman.** *Reply*: the rhetorical structure does not
   require formal training, and at the level of **syntax** the Greek shows *bilingual interference*
   consistent with a Semitic-language author (weaker than Josephus in prepositions, the genitive
   pronoun, adjective position, the dative with *en*). Syntax — flowing "almost subconsciously" — is
   better evidence of native proficiency than polished rhetoric, and it points *away* from a
   Greek/Latin-native author. See [[karen-jobes|Jobes]]'s syntax-criticism method.
2. **The persecution reflects a later era** (Domitian/Trajan). *Reply*: the persecution in 1 Peter is
   *unofficial, local, sporadic social hostility* (slander, ostracism), not state policy — a situation
   that obtained throughout the first two centuries and so is useless for dating. The "fiery ordeal"
   (4:12) is the testing of faith (cf. Seneca), not Nero's fires.
3. **The church structure is too developed** ("overseeing" elders, 5:2). *Reply*: the structure is
   *undeveloped* — only elders, with *episkopountes* in its older general sense, commensurate with
   the early Pauline churches; it points to an *earlier* stage, not a later one.
4. **It depends on Paul** (esp. Romans, Ephesians) and so postdates them. *Reply*: the affinities are
   best explained by *shared early Christian tradition* (perhaps Roman), not literary dependence; and
   if it were by a *Pauline* disciple, attribution to *Peter* is inexplicable.

Two further late-dating arguments: the spread of Christianity to remote Asia Minor would have taken
decades (addressed by the colonization theory, below); and **"Babylon" (5:13)** implies a post-AD-70
apocalyptic code for Rome. *Reply*: 1 Peter is not apocalyptic and is positive toward the state
(2:13–17), so "Babylon" functions as the closing *inclusio* of the Diaspora/exile frame (Rome could be
"Babylon" from 63 BC) and possibly veils Peter's location.

## The Question of Epistolary Pseudonymity

Jobes presses a methodological point: whether pseudonymity was a legitimate, non-deceptive convention
depends on **genre**. It was tolerated for long-dead figures in *wisdom* and *apocalyptic* (Solomon,
Enoch), but the evidence suggests **forged personal correspondence was condemned when detected** —
the spurious letters to the Laodiceans and 3 Corinthians were rejected and their author (a presbyter)
deposed despite honoring Paul; even the pagan Galen protested works forged in his name. Since 1 Peter
bears the form of real personal correspondence (1:1; 5:12–14) with a concrete audience, the appeal to
"legitimate pseudonymity" is suspect. (Jobes notes this is *not* an inerrancy argument — a biblical
author could in principle adopt any genuine literary form; the question is whether *this* genre
admitted pseudonymity.) Schlatter's and Aland's attempts to salvage an "apostolic but pseudonymous" 1
Peter she finds unpersuasive.

## The Roman Colonization Theory (Jobes's Central Proposal)

The traditional assumption is that 1 Peter's recipients were *indigenous* Asians evangelized in situ
by unknown missionaries (possibly from the Pauline churches). Jobes notes there is **no historical
evidence** of such evangelization, and that 1:12 implies Peter did not first bring them the gospel.
Her alternative:

> The recipients were converted **elsewhere — probably Rome —** and **displaced to Asia Minor**,
> where they found themselves literally "foreigners and resident aliens."

Supporting evidence:
- **Claudius colonized all five regions of 1:1.** Roman colonization (romanizing frontier territory,
  providing military presence, fostering commerce) routinely *populated colonies by relocating people
  from Rome*. The emperor **Claudius** (r. AD 41–54) — perhaps uniquely — founded or upgraded colonies
  in *every one* of the five regions named in 1:1 (e.g., Claudiconium, Claudiopolis, Neoclaudiopolis,
  Claudiolaodicea, Bithynium-Claudiopolis).
- **The Claudian expulsion.** Claudius expelled Jews (and likely Christians, then seen as a Jewish
  sect) "at the instigation of Chrestus" from Rome (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25; cf. Acts 18:2, Priscilla
  and Aquila). Such expelled non-citizens (*peregrini*; Greek *parepidēmoi* — the very word of 1:1)
  were exactly the kind of people sent to populate colonies.
- **Peter's Roman association.** Jobes revives the (minority) thesis (Wenham, Thiede, Botermann) that
  Peter's "other place" (Acts 12:17) was an *early* flight to Rome under Claudius (c. AD 42),
  explaining his connection to people who had been in Rome, the later 25-year-episcopate tradition,
  and "Babylon" (5:13) as veiling his location.

### Explanatory Power

The theory, Jobes argues, solves several puzzles at once: how Christianity reached these remote
regions *quickly*; why *no founding evangelist* is named; why Greek-speaking recipients are assumed to
be steeped in the **LXX** (Roman converts, not rural Anatolians who often spoke neither Greek nor knew
the OT); how Peter could write to people he never visited; why he addresses *scattered enclaves* rather
than organized city churches (cf. the oddly tentative elders "among you," 5:1); and the *literal* force
of "foreigners and resident aliens" beneath its spiritual meaning. If Peter wrote in the early 50s, it
also fits the gap between Paul's missionary journeys and his Jewish-mission remit (Gal. 2:8–9). See
[[christian-exile-diaspora|Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity]].

### Weaknesses

Jobes is candid: the surviving evidence is too meager to *confirm* this or any reconstruction, and the
greatest weakness is that **1 Peter never directly mentions such a displacement** — though it mentions
no concrete historical event at all. She offers it as *at least as plausible* as the unargued
assumption of in-situ conversion, and as providing a more specific motivation for the letter than pure
metaphor.

## Date

Jobes concludes the letter could have been written within Peter's lifetime — plausibly the **early
50s** (after an early Roman period, before Paul's later journeys) or the **mid-60s** (with Mark and
Silvanus in Rome, before Peter's Neronian martyrdom c. AD 64–67). Both windows place Peter, Mark, and
Silvanus (5:12–13) in the same location. The critical majority continues to favor AD 75–95.

## Assessment

The authorship question turns less on any single datum than on whether the cumulative late-dating case
is compelling. Jobes's contribution is to show that each classic argument is *inconclusive* (fitting
any first-century date) and to supply a positive historical scenario that makes an early, authentically
Petrine letter intelligible. The critical mainstream remains unpersuaded, holding that the Greek, the
Pauline affinities, and the catholic-epistle profile point to a pseudonymous Petrine circle. The
dispute is **open**.
