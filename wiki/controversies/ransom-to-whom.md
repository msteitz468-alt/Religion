# To Whom Was the Ransom of the Redemption Paid?

If the [[atonement|redemption]] wrought by [[jesus|Christ]] is conceived as the payment of a
**price** (*redemptio*, "buying back"), the question arises: to whom is the price paid?

## The two positions

- **Ransom to the devil.** A strand of patristic thought (e.g. Origen, and elements in Gregory of
  Nyssa) held that fallen humanity was justly held captive by Satan, so that the ransom was in some
  sense paid *to the devil* — sometimes with the image of Christ's humanity as the "bait" by which
  the devil overreached and forfeited his rights.
- **Ransom to God.** [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] III Q48 A4) rejects the
  ransom-to-Satan model: "the price had to be paid **not to the devil, but to God**." The devil held
  humanity only by God's permission, as a jailer, not by any right of his own; by slaying the sinless
  Christ he *exceeded* his permitted power and justly lost his captives (Q49 A2, citing Augustine).
  The redemption is therefore offered to God, satisfying the divine justice, not a transaction with
  Satan.

## Aquinas's resolution

Aquinas absorbs what was true in the older tradition — that humanity was in bondage *to sin and to
the devil's power* — while relocating the "payment" to God, integrating redemption with the other
three modes of the Passion's efficacy (merit, satisfaction, sacrifice; see [[atonement]]). This
became the settled Latin scholastic and Catholic position, and the "ransom to Satan" model receded
in the West (though motifs of it persist in Eastern and patristic-revival theologies).
