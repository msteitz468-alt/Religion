# The Nature of the Resurrection Appearances

Were the post-crucifixion appearances of [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]] **veridical** (Jesus
really appeared) or **non-veridical** (psychologically induced visions / hallucinations)?
[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) makes the
disciples' **belief**, grounded in visionary experiences, the historical engine of
Christology — arguing the historian can establish the belief but not adjudicate the event,
and that his case "does not rise or fall" on whether the visions were veridical. He holds
that it was "visions, and nothing else" — not the [[burial-of-jesus|empty tomb]] — that
first produced resurrection faith (the pre-Pauline creed in 1 Cor 15:3–5 bases belief on
Jesus's *appearing*; even the Gospels show no one believing from the tomb alone).

## Positions

- **Veridical appearance** (Christian mainstream; N. T. Wright, Michael Licona) — Jesus
  really appeared; "mass hallucinations" cannot occur, so the 500 (1 Cor 15:6) saw something
  real.
- **Psychologically induced hallucination** (Gerd Lüdemann) — Peter's and Paul's visions
  were psychological; the body decomposed.
- **Natural supersedes supernatural** (Michael Goulder) — once a natural explanation exists,
  the supernatural is no longer required.
- **Phenomenological agnosticism** (Dale Allison; adopted by Ehrman for historical purposes)
  — the historian need not decide; they were genuine experiences "taken to originate outside"
  the subject.

Ehrman's rebuttals to apologists: those who deny mass visions of Jesus inconsistently affirm
mass Marian apparitions (Fatima, Cairo, Venezuela); a vision need only be **believed**, not
veridical, to transform; first-century Jews could conceive an individual raised before the
general resurrection (Herod on John the Baptist; Nero *redivivus*; Lazarus). He grounds the
account in the documented psychology of **bereavement visions** and "source monitoring"
failure under grief.

## The "Doubt Tradition" (a sub-dispute)

Ehrman emphasizes that the Gospels repeatedly show disciples **doubting or failing to
recognize** the risen Jesus (Matt 28:17 "some doubted"; Luke's Emmaus and broiled-fish
scenes; Thomas; Mary mistaking him for the gardener; Acts 1:3's "many proofs"). Since people
who have visions "tend not to doubt," he infers that only a few (Peter, Paul, Mary Magdalene,
possibly James) had visions and told the rest, the stories later expanding to include all.

## Status

**Open.** Ongoing dispute among believers (veridical), skeptics (hallucinatory), and
historians adopting phenomenological agnosticism. Not adjudicated here.
