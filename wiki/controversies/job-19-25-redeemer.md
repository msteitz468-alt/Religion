# Job 19:25–27 — Who Is the Redeemer, and When Is Job Vindicated?

The most quoted verse in [[book-of-job|Job]] carries two independent disputes: the identity of the
*gōʾēl*, and the timing and mode of the vindication Job expects. They are separable, and this page
keeps them separate.

[[john-hartley|Hartley]]'s translation:

> 25 But I know that my redeemer is living;
>    in the end he will stand on the dust.
> 26 After my skin has been so marred,
>    yet from my flesh I shall see God,
> 27 whom I myself shall behold;
>    my own eyes will see him and no other.

He reads vv. 23–24 (the wish for an inscribed stela as a perpetual witness) as **hypothetical**, and
v. 25 as a break into **conviction**: "Whereas he has just been speaking hypothetically, he now speaks
with assurance." That contrast does work in his argument below.

## Background: the *gōʾēl* as institution

Hartley sets out five duties of the [[kinsman-redeemer|kinsman-redeemer]], each with texts: blood
vengeance (Num. 35:19; Deut. 19:6–12); ransom from bondage (Lev. 25:23–24, 39–55); redemption of land
(Lev. 25:25); levirate marriage (Ruth 2:20; 3:9; 4:1–17); and litigation on a wronged kinsman's behalf
(Ps. 119:154; Jer. 50:34; Lam. 3:58–59; Prov. 23:11). **The fifth is the one he makes operative for
Job** — "In so doing he would restore his kinsman's honor by removing all doubt as to his integrity."
He also notes that *gōʾēl* functions as a divine title rooted in the Exodus (Exod. 6:6; 15:13) and
developed by Isaiah for the return from Babylon (Isa. 41:14; 43:1–7; 44:24; 49:7–9, 26).

## Dispute A — Who is the redeemer?

### Position A1: A third party, a heavenly arbiter

Held by "several modern interpreters," unnamed in Hartley's commentary body. The motive is logical:
they "find intolerable the logic of this interpretation that God will help Job against God," and so
identify the redeemer with the heavenly witness of 16:19 — one other than God who will arise to defend
Job *before* God. Hartley concedes the position has real force: "While this view has much in its
favor, it is not able to account for all the facts."

### Position A2: God himself (the traditional reading; Hartley's own)

Hartley's five arguments:

1. **The title's confessional weight.** *Gōʾēl* "had a special place in Israel's confessional
   theology," so the author's choice of it "must be viewed as designed."
2. **The argument from unused alternatives — his strongest.** "If the author did not want to make this
   identification, he would have chosen another term that would have clearly meant an intermediary
   between God and man, for he has already used many: angel or holy one (5:1), umpire or arbiter
   (9:33–34), witness (16:19), and friend (16:20)."
3. **Human candidates are eliminated** by the text: Job's brothers and kinsmen have forsaken him
   (19:13–14) and his comforters have failed him (6:14–23).
4. **The 9:33 arbiter is eliminated on modal grounds:** there Job "was fully aware that he was
   expressing an unrealistic wish, whereas now he is speaking with conviction."
5. **The adjective *ḥay*, "living."** It stands "in bold relief against Job's fear of dying" — the
   redeemer will survive Job and can restore his honour posthumously — and evokes "the living God"
   (Deut. 5:23; Josh. 3:10; Jer. 10:10). "Therefore, the title 'living redeemer' applies to none other
   than God."

Hartley does not dissolve the resulting paradox; he embraces it: "Job is beseeching the God in whom he
has faith to help him against the God who is punishing him. While this view seems irrational, this
paradox lies at the core of Job's struggle."

## Dispute B — When and how is Job vindicated?

Hartley frames the question as: will Job see God's witness for him before or after his death, and if
after, as a spirit or bodily? He states four prominent interpretations.

### Position B1: Bodily resurrection — **rejected by Hartley**

That God will raise Job from the grave so he experiences vindication before his accusers.

**Reception**, as Hartley reports it: "This view is dominant in the Christian community. It goes back
to the early church fathers, including Clement of Rome and Origen, and it has been popularized by the
major translations in the West." Jerome's Latin, "which even identifies the Redeemer as the Messiah,"
made it traditional; Luther's translation propagated it among Protestants. Chrysostom is named as an
early dissenter who found no resurrection doctrine here.

**Hartley's four counter-arguments, all internal to the book:** Sheol is the land of no return
(7:9; 10:21); the dead have no hope of a better lot (17:15–16); Job has already concluded that
revitalization is possible for a tree but not for a human being (14:10–12), so "the claim that
resurrection is the focal point of this passage seems to be a reading back of the NT into the OT";
and — his strongest — the argument from silence: if Job here proclaimed bodily resurrection, "this
passage would be the climax of the book, bringing the debate to an end," or the theme would
reverberate through the Elihu and Yahweh speeches. It does not.

### Position B2: A disembodied spirit witnessing from Sheol — **rejected by Hartley**

That Job, bodiless, will witness from the grave the occasion when God verifies his innocence before
the local assembly. Hartley traces this to early Jewish interpreters, citing Jubilees 23:30–31.

### Position B3 and B4

Hartley states four views in all; the remaining alternatives concern whether Job expects to see God
*from* his flesh or *apart from* it. He adopts the reading below.

### Position B5: Vindication before death, in the body, at the ash heap — **Hartley's own**

Three translation decisions carry it:

- ***qûm*, "stand,"** is "here a technical legal term meaning 'to stand up' as a witness in court" —
  though Hartley allows a secondary theophanic nuance as authorial double entendre, "subtly point[ing]
  forward to God's appearing out of the storm."
- ***ʾaḥărôn*, "in the end,"** is **not** eschatological: "Job, however, is thinking not of that distant
  future… 'the end' or 'the last' connotes that God will restore Job's honor before he dies."
- ***ʿāp̄ār*, "dust," not "earth"** — an explicit choice against the RSV: "the word dust emphasizes that
  God will appear at the ash heap on which Job sits (2:8)… on the very place of his humiliation."

## A distinction that must not be flattened

After his exegesis Hartley draws a New Testament connection. Per the ingest, that paragraph asserts a
continuity of **logic** — the pattern of faith that trusts God against the evidence — and not a
continuity of **doctrine**. His own reading remains anti-resurrection here. Recording him as a
resurrection reader because he later speaks of Christ would misattribute the position he explicitly
rejects. The Jerome–Clement–Origen–Luther reception line above belongs to Christian reception history,
not to Hartley's exegesis, and is kept in its own register on this page for that reason.
