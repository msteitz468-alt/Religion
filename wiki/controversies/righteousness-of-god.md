# The Righteousness of God (Romans 1:17)

The phrase *dikaiosynē theou* ("righteousness of God") in Romans 1:17 is the thematic key to the entire letter ("the gospel is the power of God... for in it the righteousness of God is revealed"). How to understand the genitive *theou* ("of God") — and what kind of "righteousness" is in view — is one of the most debated questions in Pauline scholarship, with direct implications for the doctrine of justification, the interpretation of Paul's relationship to Judaism, and the nature of the gospel itself.

## The Grammatical Question

*Theou* (of God) is ambiguous in Greek. It can be:
- **Genitive of source**: "righteousness that comes from God" → a gift God gives to the believer
- **Subjective genitive**: "righteousness that belongs to/characterizes God" → a divine attribute or activity
- **Both simultaneously**: the word group covers both in OT background (Ps 98:2–3; Isa 46:13; 51:8 — righteousness and salvation in parallel)

## The Positions

### 1. Forensic Gift (Luther; Traditional Protestant)

Luther (1515 Lectures on Romans) broke from the medieval reading that "the righteousness of God" meant his punitive justice — the standard by which he judged sinners. Luther came to read it as "righteousness that *from God* is given to the believer." *Theou* = genitive of source. The righteousness in view is the believer's *status* before God's law court: declared "not guilty" and credited with Christ's righteousness through faith alone. This is alien righteousness (*iustitia aliena*) — not intrinsic to the believer, not earned, but imputed.

**Strengths**: Fits the forensic context (Romans 8:33; Deut 25:1); consistent with "reckoning" language of Romans 4; explains the gift-character of righteousness (Phil 3:9 — "righteousness from God").

**Weakness**: Does not fully account for the *active* and *eschatological* character of *apokalyptetai* (is being revealed) in 1:17 — revelation suggests a divine saving event, not merely the disclosure of a status.

### 2. Divine Saving Power (Käsemann; Apocalyptic School)

Ernst Käsemann (*New Testament Questions of Today*, 1969) argued that *dikaiosynē theou* is a technical apocalyptic term denoting God's power over all creation — his cosmic sovereignty asserting itself in history through the gospel. *Theou* = subjective genitive. The righteousness is not a gift given to individuals but God's *power* breaking into the present evil age to reclaim his creation. This connects Paul to Jewish apocalyptic texts where God's righteousness is his eschatological saving activity (cf. 1QS 11.2–15; 1QH).

**Strengths**: Accounts for *apokalyptetai* as an eschatological event; connects to the "power of God" language of 1:16; coheres with God's active role throughout Romans.

**Weakness**: Diminishes the personal and forensic dimension; Schreiner: the evidence for *dikaiosynē theou* as a fixed apocalyptic technical term is insufficient; the forensic evidence (Romans 3–4, 8; Phil 3:9) is hard to assimilate.

### 3. Covenant Faithfulness (N. T. Wright; New Perspective on Paul)

N. T. Wright (*What Saint Paul Really Said*, 1997) argues that "the righteousness of God" = God's *covenant faithfulness* — his fidelity to the promises made to Abraham that he would bless all nations through Israel. *Theou* = subjective genitive describing a divine attribute: God's reliability and loyalty to his word. Justification is then the declaration that God has been faithful and that those who believe are marked as members of the Abrahamic covenant people.

**Strengths**: Connects *dikaiosynē* to its OT background in covenant faithfulness (Ps 31:1; 35:24; 98:2–3; Isa 46:13; 51:8, where *ṣĕdāqâ* parallels *yĕšûʿâ*/salvation); coheres with the structure of Romans 9–11 where God's faithfulness to Israel is the issue (3:3–5 links *dikaiosynē*, *pistis*, and *alētheia*).

**Weakness** (Schreiner): "Righteousness" and "covenant" rarely appear together in the OT. Romans 3:3–5 is not a reliable definition-text because the passage is unusually compressed. Phil 3:9 specifically contrasts Paul's *own* righteousness with one that comes *from God* — describing it as God's attribute rather than a gift is strained.

### 4. Forensic + Transformative (Schreiner; Mediating Evangelical Position)

[[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (BECNT Romans, 1998): "the righteousness of God" is *both* a gift (forensic status credited to the believer) *and* a saving power (God's effective activity that transforms). The two cannot be separated. "Those whom God has vindicated he also changes. In my opinion, it is doubtful that the term Paul features in presenting his gospel would contain only a forensic dimension."

Schreiner's formulation: the forensic is the *basis* for the transformative; God's effective declaration of righteousness creates a new ontological reality. This is confirmed by:
- Romans 5:19: the many are "made righteous" (*dikaioi katastathēsontai*) — a constitutive claim, not merely a status
- Romans 6:7: *dikaiōtheis* from sin — "freed/justified from sin" implies real liberation, not just forensic declaration
- Romans 14:17: the "kingdom of God" includes "righteousness" as a present transformative reality

The specific nuance of *dikaiosynē theou* in each context determines whether the forensic or transformative dimension predominates.

**On covenant faithfulness**: Schreiner agrees that God's righteousness *involves* covenant faithfulness (it is the fulfillment of OT saving promises) but denies that it can be *defined* as covenant faithfulness. The ultimate foundation is not the covenant but God's name and glory — *for the sake of his name* (Ps 143:1, 11; Dan 9:17–19; Isa 48:9–11; Ezek 36:22). God's righteousness roots in his desire to glorify his name.

## Significance

The definition of *dikaiosynē theou* determines:
- Whether justification is primarily a *gift of status* (imputed righteousness) or a *divine saving act* or both
- Whether justification is primarily *forensic* or *transformative* or both
- Whether the Reformation doctrine of justification by faith correctly represents Paul
- How to understand the relationship between Romans 1:17 and chapters 5–8

## Resolution Status

**Open / tradition-specific.** The traditional Protestant and Reformed traditions maintain a predominantly forensic reading; Catholic tradition incorporates transformative dimensions into the act of justification itself; NPP scholars emphasize covenant faithfulness; Käsemann's apocalyptic reading continues to influence German Protestant scholarship. Schreiner's mediating position is influential among evangelical scholars.
