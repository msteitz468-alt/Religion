# Colossians: Pauline or Deutero-Pauline?

## The Issue

Colossians bears the name of Paul as author (1:1) and closes with an autograph claim (4:18: "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand"). Despite this, stylistic analysis and comparison with the undisputed Pauline letters have led a significant minority of critical scholars to question or deny Pauline authorship. The debate is structurally parallel to the [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians authorship question]] but is less contested — Colossians is generally accepted as Pauline even among scholars who reject Ephesians. Käsemann's dictum: "If Ephesians is Pauline, so is Colossians; if Colossians is Pauline, Ephesians may not be."

Colossians and Ephesians authorship questions are intertwined. See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]] for the parallel discussion and the verbal relationship between the two letters.

## Positions

### 1. Pauline Authorship (c. AD 52–62)

Paul himself wrote or dictated Colossians, possibly during an Ephesian imprisonment (McKnight: c. 52–57 CE) or the Roman imprisonment of Acts 28 (Thielman, O'Brien: c. AD 62). Timothy is named as co-sender (1:1). The stylistic variations from undisputed Pauline letters are explained by:
- Co-authorship with Timothy (and possibly oral input from others)
- Dictation to a secretary with some compositional latitude
- Different situation (not a crisis letter like Galatians; more meditative) producing different style
- Use of a pre-formed hymnic tradition (1:15–20), which has a distinctive vocabulary

Principal evangelical proponents: [[frank-thielman|Thielman]], Peter O'Brien, Douglas Moo, N. T. Wright.

**McKnight** accepts Pauline authorship but from a distinctive methodological angle: see below.

### 2. Deutero-Pauline ("School of Paul")

Colossians was written by a disciple or community within Paul's circle, either during Paul's lifetime (possibly with his knowledge or approval) or shortly after his death. The author had access to Pauline traditions and genuine letters. This position acknowledges the Pauline connections without requiring Paul's direct composition.

This view is more commonly held for Ephesians; for Colossians it has fewer defenders.

### 3. Pseudonymous Authorship (c. AD 70–90)

A Pauline disciple wrote Colossians in Paul's name after his death, as a legitimate device in the ancient world for advancing a teacher's legacy. Principal proponents: Ernst Käsemann, Eduard Lohse, Petr Pokorný, Andrew Lincoln (who accepts Colossians as Pauline while rejecting Ephesians), some who accept both letters as pseudonymous.

Arguments for pseudonymity:
- *Style*: higher proportion of abstract nouns, longer sentences, more formal diction than Romans/Galatians/1 Corinthians
- *Christology*: the high creator-Christology of 1:15–20 seems advanced even beyond Philippians 2:6–11
- *Eschatology*: more realized eschatology (believers "raised with Christ" in 3:1; "hidden with Christ in God" in 3:3) compared to the future-oriented eschatology of 1 Thessalonians and Romans 8
- *Theology*: the *plērōma* terminology and the body/head ecclesiology feel developed beyond undisputed letters

Arguments against pseudonymity:
- Colossians is the *oldest* NT evidence for the Haustafel tradition (3:18–4:1), predating the parallel forms in Ephesians and 1 Peter — if pseudonymous, it would have to precede these also-debated texts in a complex chain
- The personal details (4:7–18) are specific and cogent with Philemon; forging such detail would be unusual
- The Christology of 1:15–20, while lofty, builds on 1 Cor 8:6, Phil 2:6–11, and the Wisdom tradition — no developmental gap is required

## McKnight's Methodological Challenge

[[scot-mcknight|McKnight]] (2018) makes the most original recent contribution to this debate. He does not merely argue *for* Pauline authorship; he attacks the **epistemological foundations** of the pseudonymous hypothesis — specifically the "known-to-unknown" comparative methodology.

The standard method: take the undisputed letters (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon) as a baseline of "pure Paul," compare Colossians against them, find stylistic and theological divergences, infer a different author.

McKnight's counter: **there is no such thing as "pure Paul."** Every Pauline letter was produced collaboratively:
- Co-workers named as co-senders (Sosthenes in 1 Corinthians, Timothy in Colossians, Philippians, 2 Corinthians, Philemon)
- Secretaries with possible compositional latitude (Tertius in Romans 16:22)
- Oral dictation that varies with emotional register
- Different situations producing dramatically different styles (white-hot Galatians vs. meditative Romans)

If stylistic deviation from a baseline disqualifies a letter, Galatians (so unlike Romans in tone, vocabulary, and argument) would fail the test. Therefore the "known-to-unknown" method establishes only that the letters differ, not that the difference requires different authorship. Colossians is "Pauline as much as Galatians" — not necessarily more, but not less.

This is not simply a defense of Pauline authorship but a challenge to the comparative method itself. McKnight accepts that the question remains open on historical grounds while arguing that the pseudonymous hypothesis has not earned its methodological currency.

## Relationship to the Ephesians Authorship Question

The two letters' authorship questions are intertwined because of their extensive verbal relationship (Mitton: 26.5% of Colossians' vocabulary appears in Ephesians). Positions tend to cluster:
- Both Pauline (evangelical consensus)
- Colossians Pauline, Ephesians pseudonymous (Lincoln, many critical scholars: Ephesians depends on Colossians)
- Both pseudonymous (Käsemann, Lohse, Pokorný)

The relationship is not simple copying; [[frank-thielman|Thielman]]'s "mind saturated" description (following Mitton) is more accurate than literary dependence. See [[ephesians-authorship|Ephesians: Pauline or Pseudonymous?]].

## Resolution Status

**Open.** The majority of modern NT scholars accept Colossians as Pauline or deutero-Pauline (leaning Pauline), while a significant minority hold for pseudonymity. This is notably less contested than the Ephesians authorship question. The debate continues on methodological grounds following McKnight's 2018 challenge to the comparative method.
