# Revelation: Authorship and Date

Who is the "John" of Patmos (Rev. 1:1, 4, 9; 22:8), and when did he write? Both questions have been
disputed since antiquity, and they interlock with the book's interpretation (a Neronian date, for
example, aids preterist readings). [[grant-osborne|Osborne]] (BECNT, 2002) defends authorship by the
**apostle John** and a **Domitianic date (c. AD 95)**, while acknowledging the difficulties on both.

## Authorship

The author names himself simply "John" and calls himself "slave," "prophet," and "brother" — never
"apostle." Candidates proposed across history:
1. **John the apostle** (son of Zebedee) — the early external consensus (Justin Martyr, Irenaeus,
   Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen) and Osborne's position.
2. **"The elder John"** — Eusebius's solution, built on Papias's reference to a second John at
   Ephesus. In the preface to his *Expositions* (Fragment 3.3–4), Papias distinguishes (a) "Andrew,
   or Peter, or Philip, or Thomas, or James, or John or Matthew, or any other of the Lord's
   disciples" in the past tense (= the Apostle John, now dead) from (b) "Aristion and the elder
   John, the Lord's disciples, **were saying**" in the present tense (= still alive in Papias' day).
   Eusebius uses this grammatical observation to argue that two Johns existed in Ephesus — the
   Apostle (who wrote the Gospel and Letters) and the Elder (who wrote Revelation). This allows
   Eusebius to dismiss Revelation's millennialism by attributing it to a lesser figure. Holmes
   (*The Apostolic Fathers*, 3rd ed.) prints and discusses the full fragment; see
   [[fragments-of-papias|Fragments of Papias]] for detailed analysis.
3. **A "Johannine school"/circle** — the Gospel, Epistles, and Apocalypse as products of a prophetic
   circle originating with the apostle (Brown, Culpepper, Schüssler Fiorenza).
4. **An unknown John** (Dionysius of Alexandria, citing two "John" tombs at Ephesus).
5. **Pseudonymous** (a 19th-c. favorite) — but Osborne notes this *fails* the usual apocalyptic
   convention (pseudonymous seers are figures of the distant past, not a recent apostle), and a forger
   would more likely have written "John the apostle" explicitly.
6. Fringe: John Mark, John the Baptist (Ford), the Gnostic Cerinthus (the anti-Montanist Alogoi/Gaius)
   — all dismissed.

### The Difference-from-the-Fourth-Gospel Problem

The main modern argument *against* apostolic authorship is the gulf between Revelation and the Gospel
of John:
- **The Greek**: Revelation's is famously rough and "solecistic"; the Gospel's is smooth. *Osborne's
  reply*: the differences are explicable by **genre and visionary experience** (an amanuensis for the
  Gospel; the ecstatic visions shaping Revelation's style); many solecisms are *deliberate*, flagging
  OT allusions or theology (e.g., the nominative divine title in 1:4). R. H. Charles found as many
  similarities as differences.
- **The theology/tone**: the Gospel's God of love vs. Revelation's God of wrath. *Reply*: a false
  contrast — judgment is central to the Gospel too (5:22–30), and Revelation seeks repentance (9:20–
  21; 14:6–7).
- **Shared distinctives** *for* common authorship: the "oneness motif" Christology (only John and
  Revelation argue Christ's deity this way); *logos* as a title for Christ (only John 1 and Rev.
  19:13); the shared use of *ekkenteō* (Zech. 12:10) in John 19:37 and Rev. 1:7 (found nowhere else
  in the NT/LXX); and overlapping vocabulary (conquer, keep the word, witness, true).

Osborne concludes the internal evidence supports the strong early external witness: **Johannine
(apostolic) authorship "makes the best sense."** See [[john-apostle|John the Apostle]].

## Date

Early Christian writers proposed dates under Claudius, Nero, Domitian, or Trajan; the live modern
options are **Nero (late 60s)** or **Domitian (mid-90s)**. Osborne, having changed his earlier view,
favors **Domitian (c. AD 95)**. The evidence:

- **The imperial cult**: more developed and prominent under Domitian (Asia as its epicenter; the
  competition among cities for imperial temples; Domitian's reported wish to be "lord and god"),
  fitting Revelation's emperor-worship crisis (13:4, 14–17). (L. Thompson argues Domitian was
  maligned by hostile Trajanic-era writers; Osborne, with Beale, thinks this overstated.)
- **Persecution**: the book reflects sporadic, local, mostly *unofficial* hostility (Jewish "synagogues
  of Satan," social/economic pressure to join the guilds and cult), with worse anticipated — fitting
  the 90s; the Neronian persecution (64–68) was severe but limited to *Rome*, with no evidence it
  reached Asia.
- **The seven churches** (the evidence that changed Osborne's mind): Laodicea's *unaided* wealth fits
  its rebuilding after the AD 60 earthquake; "do not harm the oil and wine" (6:6) may reflect
  Domitian's AD 92 edict on vines; the Nero *redivivus* legend behind the beast's "healed wound"
  (13:3) developed in the 80s–90s; Smyrna's church may not have existed in the 60s.

**Arguments for the Neronian date** (and Osborne's answers): the beast's "number" and the "sixth king
who is" (17:9–11) read as Nero/Galba; the temple "still standing" in 11:1–2 (Osborne: 11:1–2 is
*symbolic*, not a literal temple). He concludes the date "must remain uncertain," but Domitian "has
better evidence."

## Why It Matters

- **Interpretation**: a pre-70 date underwrites the *fall-of-Jerusalem preterist* reading (Gentry,
  Chilton); the Domitianic date fits the *Roman-oppression* and *futurist* readings better. See
  [[revelation-interpretive-schools|The Four Interpretive Schools]].
- **The Johannine corpus**: apostolic authorship of Revelation bears on the unity of the
  Gospel–Epistles–Apocalypse and on the identity of "John." See [[john-apostle|John the Apostle]],
  [[beloved-disciple-identity|The Beloved Disciple]].
- **Canon**: doubts about apostolic authorship (Dionysius) and the chiliasm controversy delayed the
  book's acceptance in the East for centuries.
