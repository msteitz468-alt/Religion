# The 1984 Anti-Sikh Violence (November 1984)

The mass killing of [[sikhs|Sikhs]] across northern India in the days after [[indira-gandhi|Indira
Gandhi]] was assassinated by her two Sikh bodyguards on **31 October 1984**. Khushwant Singh (*A History
of the Sikhs* Vol. 2, "Assassination and After") treats it not as a spontaneous riot but as **an
organized pogrom with administrative and political complicity** — and records that, by his 2004 Epilogue,
the guilty remained largely unpunished. Recorded here per the Contradiction Protocol.

## The Assassination

On the morning of 31 October 1984, her guards **Beant Singh** and **Satwant Singh** "opened fire and
pumped eighteen bullets into her frail body"; she had declined advice to remove Sikhs from her guard.
Beant Singh was killed in custody; Satwant Singh and Kehar Singh were hanged on 6 January 1989.

## Singh's Thesis: A Pogrom, Not a Riot

"A certain amount of spontaneous anti-Sikh violence should have been anticipated and easily contained by
swift, stern action by the police. What followed… tells a sordid tale of administrative and political
complicity in a massacre of innocents of dimensions not seen in India since it became an independent
state." Singh documents the mechanism: "Operation 'Teach the Sikhs a lesson' was put in motion on the
night of 31 October"; false rumours (Sikhs celebrating, poisoned water); Congress cadres "commissioned
trucks to bring in villagers… armed them with iron rods and cans of gasoline"; electoral voters' lists
used to target Sikh homes. The violence "was confined to states ruled by the Congress party and
masterminded by local leaders which included some members of Parliament." The killing method: victims
"first bludgeoned with iron rods, then doused with petrol and set alight," sometimes with "a burning
motor tyre lowered around his neck like a flaming garland."

Government inaction is detailed: the army was not deployed until 3 November, "largely to provide security
for the world statesmen who had come to attend Mrs Gandhi's funeral." Rajiv Gandhi's notorious remark on
19 November: "when a mighty banyan tree falls, the earth beneath it is bound to shake."

## Contested Casualty Figures

- **Government-controlled media (initially)**: under 400.
- **Singh's own estimate**: "The figure of 10,000 Sikhs killed (more than half of them in the capital)
  would not be an exaggeration." (His 2004 Epilogue gives "over 5000.")
- **Romesh Thapar**: 6,000–8,000 in Delhi alone, plus "property worth Rs 300 crores looted and burnt."
- Over 50,000 Sikhs sheltered in Delhi refugee camps; 20,000–30,000 families fled to Punjab.

## Naming the Guilty and the Failure of Justice

The civil-liberties report ***Who Are The Guilty?*** (1984) — whose authors included "not one… Sikh" —
named cabinet minister **H. K. L. Bhagat**, MPs **Jagdish Tytler** and **Sajjan Kumar**, 13 police
officers, and 198 others; "not one dared to pick up the gauntlet" of a libel suit. The non-official
**Sikri Commission** found "an avoidable and abysmal failure of the administration and the police"; both
Rajiv Gandhi and Narasimha Rao "refused to receive" it. The official **Mishra Commission** Singh
describes as designed "not to identify the guilty but to cover up their crimes." His damning summary: by
1988 "not one person had been punished," and by 2004 "many have remained unpunished." Rajiv Gandhi's
Congress went on to win 410 of 540 Lok Sabha seats in the 1984 election, "largely in states where
anti-Sikh feeling was strong."
