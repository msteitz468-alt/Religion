# The Anglo-Sikh Wars and British Annexation of the Punjab (1845–1849)

The conquest of the [[punjab|Punjab]] in two wars (1845–46, 1848–49) ended the last independent
Indian power and the Sikh kingdom built by [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]]. Khushwant Singh's account
in *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2 advances a distinct interpretive thesis against the official
British narrative, and is recorded here as a controversy because Singh marshals **British eyewitness
testimony that contradicts the British government's own justification for war**.

## Khushwant Singh's Thesis

**1. The war was deliberately engineered, not provoked.** Singh uses private British correspondence to
argue the decision to take the Punjab predated any Sikh aggression. Lord Ellenborough asked the Duke of
Wellington (Oct 1841) "as to the best mode of attacking the Punjab"; Lord Hardinge wrote in January 1845
"The government of the Punjab must be Sikh or British," and described a strategy of letting "the hills
and the plains weaken each other." By autumn 1845 the British had massed 40,000+ men, 94 guns, and a
fleet of 60 flat-bottomed boats on the Sutlej — "without concealment or mystery."

**2. The "invasion" was contested by British observers themselves.** When the Khalsa crossed the Sutlej
(11 December 1845), Hardinge declared war (13 December) charging an invasion "without a shadow of
provocation." Singh quotes British witnesses who disagreed: Major Carmichael Smyth ("I am decidedly of
the opinion that we did" break friendship first; the bridge of boats "was a *causa belli*"); Sir George
Campbell (the Sikhs "made no attack… What they did do was to cross the river and to entrench themselves
in their own territory"); Robert Cust, who called the British advance "the first British invasion of the
independent kingdom of the Punjab." Even Hardinge privately wondered whether England would accept the war
as justified.

**3. Sikh defeat was caused by treason, not military inferiority.** This is Singh's central and most
insistent claim. The Durbar commanders **Lal Singh** and **Tej Singh** were "in treasonable
communication with the English." Lal Singh wrote to the British asking them to treat him and the *bībī
sāhībā* (Rani Jindan) as friends and to "cut up the *būrchās* (ruffians, i.e. the Khalsa) for them." At
**Ferozeshahr** (21 December 1845) — "that frosty night 'the fate of India trembled in the balance,'"
with British officers preparing to destroy state papers and surrender — Tej Singh arrived with fresh
troops, opened fire, then inexplicably fell silent and retreated. Throughout, Singh pairs the commanders'
treachery with British generals' tributes to the courage of the rank-and-file Khalsa: Lord Gough called
**Sabraon** (10 February 1846, ~10,000 Punjabis dead) "the Waterloo of India" and "could have wept to
have witnessed the fearful slaughter of so devoted a body of men." **Sham Singh Attariwala**'s last
stand at Sabraon became the war's epic.

**4. The Dogras as a fifth column.** Gulab Singh Dogra negotiated with the British before Sabraon, was
made peace-broker afterward, and was "sold" Kashmir for 75 lac rupees by the Treaty of Amritsar (16 March
1846) — Singh quotes the British motive as "to weaken the Punjabis by strengthening the Dogras," and
Gulab Singh's self-description as "*zar k̲h̲arīd* — slave bought by gold." See the Dogra material on
[[duleep-singh|Duleep Singh]].

## The Settlement and the Second War

The Treaties of **Lahore** (March 1846) annexed the Jullundur Doab, imposed a 1½-crore indemnity (Kashmir
ceded in lieu), and slashed the army; the Treaty of **Bhairowal** (December 1846) installed a resident
with "full authority to direct and control all matters in every department of state" — Hardinge calling it
privately "in reality annexation… without entailing upon us the present expense." Singh treats the
**deportation of Rani Jindan** to Benares (May 1848) as the spark of the **Second Anglo-Sikh War**, and
argues Dalhousie deliberately let the Multan rebellion of **Dewan Mulraj** "come to a head" — noting Vans
Agnew himself wrote "I don't think Mulraj has anything to do with it." The war ran through **Ramnagar**,
**Chillianwala** (13 January 1849 — "the worst defeat suffered by the British since their occupation of
India") and **Gujarat** (21 February 1849), ending with the army's surrender and
[[duleep-singh|Duleep Singh]]'s handing over of the Koh-i-noor on 29 March 1849.

## The "Hinduization and Decline" Sub-Thesis

A second strand (Vol. 2, Ch. 5) concerns what annexation did to the Panth. Singh argues the Sikh
nobility had been heavily **Hinduized** under Ranjit Singh (Hindu worship, *sati*, revived female
infanticide), and that after annexation there was mass defection from the Khalsa back to Hinduism —
Dalhousie himself predicted Sikh "disappearance." The irony Singh draws (Ch. 7, *Crescat e Fluviis*) is
that British policies — the "martial race" preference for *kesādhārī* Sikhs in the army and the
prosperity of the **canal colonies** (land rising from Rs 10 to Rs 400 an acre) — **reversed** the
decline Dalhousie had predicted, "staved off by policies initiated by Dalhousie himself." See
[[punjab|Punjab]] and [[sikhs|the Sikhs]].

## Positions Recorded (not adjudicated)

- **British colonial narrative**: a defensive war against Sikh aggression across the Sutlej; annexation a
  reluctant necessity after repeated "rebellion."
- **Khushwant Singh / Sikh national reading**: a premeditated conquest enabled by aristocratic and Dogra
  treason, against the will of a brave Khalsa army and a hostile populace.
- **Decline-and-revival sub-dispute**: whether Sikh identity would have dissolved into Hinduism but for
  British martial-race and colonization policies.
