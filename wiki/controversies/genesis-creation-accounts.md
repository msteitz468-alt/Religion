The relationship between the two creation accounts in [[genesis|Genesis]] — the first (1:1–2:3) and the second (2:4–3:24) — is one of the oldest and most debated hermeneutical problems in biblical scholarship. The two accounts differ in divine name, style, order of events, and theological emphasis, generating fundamental disagreement about composition, historicity, and theological purpose.

## The Apparent Differences

| Feature | Gen 1:1–2:3 | Gen 2:4–3:24 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine name | *Elohim* | *YHWH Elohim* |
| Style | Formal, structured, repetitive | Narrative, intimate, psychological |
| Order of creation | Plants before humans (1:11-12, then 1:26) | Humans before plants (2:7, then 2:9) |
| Creation of humans | Simultaneous (male and female, 1:27) | Sequential (man first, woman from rib, 2:7, 2:22) |
| Setting | Cosmic | Garden of Eden |
| Movement | God creates by word | God forms, plants, breathes, builds |

## The Source-Critical Reading

The [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]] assigns Gen 1:1–2:3 to **P** (Priestly, post-exilic) and Gen 2:4ff. to **J** (Yahwist, c. 850 BCE). On this reading, the accounts are **irreconcilably contradictory** — different authors with different theologies compiled by a redactor who placed them side-by-side. The different divine names are the primary evidence: P's preference for *Elohim*, J's for *YHWH*.

The source-critical position requires that the final redactor's audience would not have noticed or been troubled by the contradictions — the accounts were transmitted as separate traditions before combination.

## The Complementary Reading

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] and most conservative evangelical commentators read the two accounts as **theologically complementary**:
- Gen 1:1–2:3 focuses on the **cosmic** frame: God creates all things in orderly sequence, culminating in humanity as his image-bearers (1:26-27) and the Sabbath rest (2:1-3)
- Gen 2:4ff. focuses on the **human** frame: humanity's creaturely origins (formed from dust), relational existence (*ʿezer kenegdo*), and covenant relationship with God ("you shall not")

Hamilton's formulation: Gen 1 focuses on humanity "created with authority" (1:29: "you shall..."); Gen 2 focuses on humanity "created under authority" (2:17: "you shall not..."). The two are *kenegdo* — complementary pairs, neither more true than the other.

On this reading, the different divine name is not a source indicator but a theologically motivated choice: *Elohim* (the creator God of all) for the cosmic account; *YHWH Elohim* (the covenant God of Israel) for the intimate account of human origins.

**The problem of the plant-and-human order**: Gen 1 places plants (days 3) before humans (day 6); Gen 2:5-7 apparently places the man before plants. Hamilton and others note that Gen 2:5-7 is making a rhetorical point (no plants existed *before* the man to tend them) rather than a sequential statement about creation order.

## The 2:4a Crux

The *toledot* formula in 2:4a ("these are the generations of the heavens and the earth") falls exactly between the two accounts. Is it the conclusion of Gen 1:1–2:3 (reading it as a subscript to Gen 1) or the beginning of 2:4bff. (reading it as a superscript to Gen 2)?

- **Subscript reading** (majority of critical scholars: RSV, JB, NEB): 2:4a concludes Gen 1 and belongs with P. This preserves the cleanest source division.
- **Superscript reading** ([[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]], [[toledot|following the *toledot* formula's standard function]]): 2:4a introduces what follows, linking the cosmic creation to its creaturely result (humanity as the "offspring" of heaven and earth). This is Hamilton's preferred reading.

## Historicity Questions

**For conservative readers**: Both accounts describe real historical events; the different perspectives are complementary, not contradictory. The question of the sequence of events (which came first, plants or humans?) is addressed by harmonization strategies.

**For critical readers**: Neither account is straightforwardly historical narrative. Gen 1 is structured hymnic/liturgical prose (note the seven-day framework); Gen 2-3 displays characteristics of ancient Near Eastern wisdom narrative (*Märchen* genre). Both accounts make theological claims rather than providing historical reportage.

**The Eden narrative (Gen 3) specifically**: Is the Fall a historical event? [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] affirms it as a real event while rejecting the reduction of Gen 3 to mere etiology (explanation for why snakes crawl). Critical scholars (Westermann) read it as a paradigmatic narrative about human nature, not a specific event in time.
