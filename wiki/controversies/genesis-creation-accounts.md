The relationship between the two creation accounts in [[genesis|Genesis]] — the first (1:1–2:3) and the second (2:4–3:24) — is one of the oldest and most debated hermeneutical problems in biblical scholarship. The two accounts differ in divine name, style, order of events, and theological emphasis, generating fundamental disagreement about composition, historicity, and theological purpose.

## The Apparent Differences

| Feature | Gen 1:1–2:3 | Gen 2:4–3:24 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine name | *Elohim* | *YHWH Elohim* |
| Style | Formal, structured, repetitive | Narrative, intimate, psychological |
| Order of creation | Plants before humans (1:11-12, then 1:26) | Humans before plants (2:7, then 2:9) |
| Creation of humans | Simultaneous (male and female, 1:27) | Sequential (man first, woman from side, 2:7, 2:22) |
| Setting | Cosmic | Garden of Eden |
| Movement | God creates by word | God forms, plants, breathes, builds |

## The Source-Critical Reading

The [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]] assigns Gen 1:1–2:3 to **P** (Priestly, post-exilic) and Gen 2:4ff. to **J** (Yahwist, c. 850 BCE). On this reading, the accounts are **irreconcilably contradictory** — different authors with different theologies compiled by a redactor who placed them side-by-side. The different divine names are the primary evidence: P's preference for *Elohim*, J's for *YHWH*. Source critics also appeal to an alleged contrast of creation-by-word (1:1–2:4a, late P) vs. creation-by-action (2:4bff., earlier J).

The source-critical position requires that the final redactor's audience would not have noticed or been troubled by the contradictions — the accounts were transmitted as separate traditions before combination.

## The Complementary Reading (Hamilton)

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] (NICOT; [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot|Genesis 1–17]]) and most conservative evangelical commentators read the two accounts as **theologically complementary**, not as a flat contradiction of incompatible documents. In Hamilton’s formulation:

- **Gen 1** emphasizes creation via the majestic God who speaks and it is done; the generic name *ʾĕlōhîm* fits that emphasis. Humanity appears with **authority** — image, dominion, “subdue.”
- **Gen 2:4ff.** is more personal: not a universe but a garden; man is pictured **under authority**, a vassal in covenant relationship. The compound *Yahweh ʾĕlōhîm* joins sovereign control of the material and moral worlds.

> “In Gen. 1 the emphasis is on creation via the majestic God who speaks and it is done. The more generic name for God—ʾĕlōhîm—fits this emphasis admirably. By contrast, the emphasis in 2:4ff. is more personal. The context here is not a universe but a garden. Also, the picture of man here is not of one with authority but of one under authority, a vassal in a covenant relationship. … The author proceeded, however, to append ʾĕlōhîm to Yahweh to conjoin the concept of a God whose sovereign control extends to both the material and the moral world.” (Hamilton; on Gen 2:4–3:24)

Cross-cutting bridges Hamilton treats as **affirmations**, not contradictions:

- Dust in 2:7 “far from disagreeing with ch. 1, affirms ch. 1’s view of man’s regality.”
- “Subdue the land” (ch. 1) is a semantic parallel to “till and keep the land” (2:5, 15).
- The word/action wedge used by source critics does not hold cleanly: only light is created by pure fiat; other days combine command with subsequent activity — so both chapters use action *and* speech, and neither uses conflict- or birth-modes of ANE cosmogony.

**Plant order (2:5 vs 1:11–12):** Hamilton refuses a flat contradiction. He reads *śîaḥ* and *ʿēśeḇ* in 2:5 as anticipatory of cultivated plants (further explained by 3:18), while Gen 1’s plants are wild seed-plants; neither cultivated type appears until after man and after transgression.

> “It would be premature to say that 2:5 flatly contradicts 1:11-12. … The ‘plants’ referred to in Gen. 1 must be those that grow wild… The plants referred to in Gen. 2 must be those that grow only as a result of human cultivation…” (Hamilton; on Gen 2:5)

**Animal order (2:19 vs 1:24–25):** Hamilton offers either a pluperfect reading or (preferred) a special group of animals formed for naming, not a second first-creation of fauna.

On this reading, the different divine name is not a source indicator but a **theologically motivated** choice; dividing the compound *Yahweh ʾĕlōhîm* into amalgam sources or a post-P redactor’s insertion of *ʾĕlōhîm* is rejected.

## The 2:4a Crux

The *toledot* formula in 2:4a (“these are the generations of the heavens and the earth”) falls exactly between the two accounts. Is it the conclusion of Gen 1:1–2:3 (subscript to Gen 1 / P) or the beginning of 2:4bff. (superscript to Gen 2)?

- **Subscript reading** (majority of critical scholars; many modern versions; BHK/BHS division): 2:4a concludes Gen 1 and belongs with P. This preserves the cleanest source division (P ends at 2:4a; J begins 2:4b).
- **Superscript reading** ([[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]]; [[toledot|standard *tôlĕdôt* function]]): 2:4a introduces what follows. Hamilton’s case:

> “We take the approach that 2:3 concludes the first unit of Genesis and 2:4a begins the second unit. … everywhere else in Genesis the tôleḏôṯ formula functions as a superscription to what follows. … the tôleḏôṯ formula is always followed by the genitive of the progenitor, never of the progeny. Thus the phrase the generations of the heavens and the earth describes not the process by which the heavens and the earth are generated, but rather that which is generated by the heavens and the earth. Quite obviously this would be a most inaccurate description of the process of creation as delineated in 1:1-2:3.” (Hamilton; on Gen 2:4)

He further notes that tacking a Priestly 2:4a onto 1:1–2:3 while calling 2:4bff. Yahwistic creates “an almost insurmountable problem,” because the formula is almost universally a superscript elsewhere; von Rad’s special pleading for a Priestly interpolator is judged “strained.” Dividing 2:4 itself merely to keep a P-formula off J text is, for Hamilton, artificial. See also intro structural discussion in [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot|Hamilton Genesis 1–17]].

## Historicity Questions

**For conservative readers**: Both accounts describe real historical events; the different perspectives are complementary, not contradictory. The question of the sequence of events (which came first, plants or humans?) is addressed by harmonization strategies of the kind Hamilton offers above.

**For critical readers**: Neither account is straightforwardly historical narrative. Gen 1 is structured hymnic/liturgical prose (note the seven-day framework); Gen 2-3 displays characteristics of ancient Near Eastern wisdom narrative (*Märchen* genre). Both accounts make theological claims rather than providing historical reportage.

**The Eden narrative (Gen 3) specifically**: Is the Fall a historical event? [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] treats the Christian “Fall” / “original sin” terminology as traditional extension while noting the OT’s near silence on Adam/Eve after Gen 5 until Paul (Rom 5; 1 Cor 15); he rejects Kantian “rise”/autonomy readings (freedom forfeited, not gained). Critical scholars (Westermann) read Gen 3 as paradigmatic narrative about human nature, not a specific event in time.
