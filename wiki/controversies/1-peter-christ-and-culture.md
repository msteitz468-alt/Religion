# Christ and Culture in 1 Peter (Elliott vs. Balch)

How does 1 Peter want its readers to relate to the surrounding pagan society — by *resisting* it as a
distinct counter-community, or by *accommodating* to it to reduce hostility? The question crystallized
in two influential 1981 studies that reached opposite conclusions about the same letter, and it bears
directly on how to read the **household code** (2:18–3:7) and the **"foreigners and resident aliens"**
motif. [[karen-jobes|Jobes]] (BECNT, 2005), following Miroslav Volf, argues for a third, differentiated
position.

## The Two Classic Positions (Both 1981)

**1. Sectarian resistance (John H. Elliott, *A Home for the Homeless*).** Using sociological method,
Elliott argued that 1 Peter aims to **maintain the distinct identity and cohesion** of the Christian
community *against* assimilation to the surrounding culture. The believers are a sectarian
counter-society — a "home for the homeless." Elliott further read *paroikoi* (2:11) **literally**, as
a socioeconomic descriptor: the recipients were the disenfranchised resident-alien rural poor, and the
letter reinforces their group boundaries.

**2. Acculturation / apologetic (David L. Balch, *Let Wives Be Submissive*).** Studying the household
code (*Haustafel*, 2:18–3:7) against its Greco-Roman background, Balch argued the **opposite**: the
letter urges a degree of **accommodation** to accepted social norms — wives, slaves, and citizens
behaving in culturally honorable ways — precisely to *defuse* hostility and protect the church's
reputation (an apologetic function). The household code adopts the surrounding culture's expectations
about household order.

These two readings became the standard poles of the "Petrine social ethics" debate.

## Jobes's Position: Differentiated, Selective Engagement (with Volf)

Jobes argues that **both positions reduce the complexity of 1 Peter**. Following Miroslav Volf
("Soft Difference," 1994), she holds the letter calls for "either rejecting or accommodating to
particular aspects of the surrounding culture in a piecemeal fashion." 1 Peter offers examples of
**accommodating, rejecting, subverting, and transforming** culture — not a single posture. The
controlling identity ("foreigners and resident aliens," 2:11) means believers keep "a healthy respect
for the society and culture in which they live while at the same time maintaining an appropriate
separation from it" (2:11–12) — abstaining from socially acceptable but soul-warring desires *while*
living visibly good lives among the Gentiles, so that some may be won (2:12). Engagement, not
withdrawal; difference, not assimilation.

## The Household Code (2:18–3:7) in This Light

Jobes reads the code as serving the apostolic concern with the *community's relationship to society*,
not merely domestic order:

- **Slaves (2:18–25)**: told to submit even to harsh masters; the unjustly suffering slave is
  **paradigmatic for all Christians**, who are "slaves of God" — and the section pivots into the
  Christ-hymn (2:21–25), making Christ's unjust suffering the model. Notably, 1 Peter addresses
  *slaves* directly as moral agents (unusual in Greco-Roman codes) and gives *no* reciprocal command
  to masters.
- **Wives (3:1–6)**: submission especially of wives married to unbelieving husbands, that they may be
  "won without a word" by conduct. Jobes (writing as a woman scholar) is emphatic that:
  - the suffering in view is **verbal abuse and loss of social standing**, *not* domestic violence;
  - the passage **neither sanctions abuse nor counsels remaining in an abusive marriage** (even
    Greco-Roman law did not honor a wife-beater, so an abused woman was not thereby "virtuous");
  - the immediately following word to **husbands (3:7)** — to live considerately, honoring the wife
    as co-heir, lest their prayers be hindered — "delicately prohibits" domestic violence and is
    itself a *subversion* of the surrounding patriarchal norm.
- The code thus *accommodates* (honorable household behavior reduces slander), *rejects* (idolatry,
  the old way of life), and *subverts* (addressing slaves and wives as agents; constraining husbands)
  simultaneously.

## Relation to the Wider NT Household-Code Debate

1 Peter's *Haustafel* parallels and differs from those in [[ephesians-household-code|Ephesians
(5:21–6:9)]] and Colossians (3:18–4:1): 1 Peter has no children/fathers pair, foregrounds *slaves* and
*wives of unbelievers*, and frames the whole within suffering and witness rather than "in the Lord"
mutuality. The complementarian/egalitarian arguments that run through the Ephesians code recur here,
but 1 Peter's distinctive contribution is the *missional-apologetic and suffering* framing. See
[[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]] and [[1-tim-2-12-women-teaching|Women,
Teaching, and Authority in 1 Timothy]].

## Why It Matters

The debate is not merely historical: 1 Peter is, for Jobes, the NT's most sustained treatment of
*Christ and culture*, and its differentiated model speaks directly to the global church wherever
Christians are a minority — neither withdrawing into a ghetto nor dissolving into the surrounding
values. The letter's relevance grows, she argues, precisely as Western Christianity becomes more of a
minority and as the faith becomes indigenous in non-Judeo-Christian cultures.
