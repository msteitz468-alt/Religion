# Christ and Culture in 1 Peter (Elliott vs. Balch)

How does 1 Peter want its readers to relate to the surrounding pagan society — by *resisting* it as a
distinct counter-community, or by *accommodating* to it to reduce hostility? The question crystallized
in two influential 1981 studies that reached opposite conclusions about the same letter, and it bears
directly on how to read the **household code** (2:18–3:7) and the **"foreigners and resident aliens"**
motif. [[karen-jobes|Jobes]] in [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|BECNT, 2005]], following Miroslav Volf, argues
for a third, differentiated position. Positions are recorded in their strongest form; no adjudication
in wiki voice.

## The Two Classic Positions (Both 1981)

**1. Sectarian resistance (John H. Elliott, *A Home for the Homeless*).** Using sociological method,
Elliott argued that 1 Peter aims to **maintain the distinct identity and cohesion** of the Christian
community *against* assimilation to the surrounding culture. The believers are a sectarian
counter-society — a "home for the homeless." Elliott further read *paroikoi* (2:11) **literally**, as
a socioeconomic descriptor: the recipients were the disenfranchised resident-alien rural poor, and the
letter reinforces their group boundaries. In later work he takes *paroikoi kai parepidēmoi* as actual
social class — "not simply compared to aliens and strangers but… actually identified as such" (2000:
457) — and reads the household code as building cohesive church identity for mission (2000) (L2384,
L2501).

**2. Acculturation / apologetic (David L. Balch, *Let Wives Be Submissive*).** Studying the household
code (*Haustafel*, 2:18–3:7) against its Greco-Roman background (Plato, Xenophon, Aristotle, Plutarch,
Seneca, Dio — Balch 1981), Balch argued the **opposite**: the letter urges a degree of
**accommodation** to accepted social norms — wives, slaves, and citizens behaving in culturally
honorable ways — precisely to *defuse* hostility and protect the church's reputation (an apologetic
function vs social criticism of Christianity's household effect). Foreign religions were judged by
household compliance (Josephus for Judaism; Isis cult threat for female authority — Balch 1988: 29)
(L2501–2509). Balch later terms this "**selective acculturation**" (1988: 36) (L2533).

These two readings became the standard poles of the "Petrine social ethics" debate.

## Jobes's Position: Differentiated, Selective Engagement (with Volf)

Jobes argues that **both positions reduce the complexity of 1 Peter**. Following Miroslav Volf ("Soft
Difference," 1994), she holds the letter calls for "either rejecting or accommodating to particular
aspects of the surrounding culture in a piecemeal fashion." 1 Peter offers examples of
**accommodating, rejecting, subverting, and transforming** culture — not a single posture (L550). Volf
(1994: 22) via Jobes: "The household codes in 1 Peter are in fact an example of **differentiated
acceptance and rejection of the surrounding culture**"; Balch's "selective acculturation" names a
related insight (L2533).

Further Volf-via-Jobes moves (L2396):
- Living recognizably good lives "presupposes **overlap** between Christian and non-Christian
  constellations of values" (Volf 1994: 25) — self-control, for example, pagans would recognize as
  virtuous.
- Peter "does not seem to be thinking in binary categories that characterize society as evil and the
  Christian community as good"; he "does not condemn all of the values and customs of first-century
  culture… or advise complete withdrawal."
- Volf (1994: 27): "remarkable and refreshing sensibility for the complexity of social realities,
  bursting a black and white way of thinking"; "**no one single proper way** for Christians to relate
  to a given culture as a whole."

The controlling identity ("foreigners and resident aliens," 2:11) means believers keep "a healthy
respect for the society and culture in which they live while at the same time maintaining an
appropriate separation from it" (2:11–12) — abstaining from socially acceptable but soul-warring
desires *while* living visibly good lives among the Gentiles, so that some may be won (2:12). Two
engagement principles Jobes names for the unit: (1) allegiance to God in Christ does not exempt from
submitting to pagan authority; (2) they must maintain holy identity and be prepared to suffer unjustly
without retaliation (L2349). Engagement, not withdrawal; difference, not assimilation. The Christian
community is "a colony in a strange land, an island of one culture in the midst of another" (L558).

## The Household Code (2:18–3:7) in This Light

Jobes reads the code as serving the apostolic concern with the *community's relationship to society*
(not a privatized marriage manual — modern over-attention to wives distorts the first-century public
interest in household order; Balch 1981: 26) (L2511). Peter "both upholds and subverts the social
order" (L2744; also L2550, L2572):

### Slaves as paradigm (2:18–25)

- **Slaves first, as paradigm for all Christians.** Lowest status/least power — fitting if readers face
  loss of status; all Christians live as "slaves of God" (2:16); Jesus is identified with the Isa 53
  Suffering Servant/slave (Jobes: the only NT passage that does so this explicitly and extensively);
  address household slave first, then wife, then husband (L2471, L2540). Actual slave exemplifies all
  as slaves of God (Achtemeier, Campbell, Elliott 1981: 207) (L2538).
- Told to submit even to harsh masters; the unjustly suffering slave is **paradigmatic for all
  Christians**, and the section pivots into the Christ-hymn / Isa 53 exposition (2:21–25), making
  Christ's unjust suffering the model (*hypogrammon* = children's letter-tracing pattern — "closest of
  copies"; "example" is too weak: Christ is **the paradigm**) (L2617). Footsteps lead "to the cross,
  through the grave, and onward to glory."
- **Direct address as free moral agents.** Peter addresses *slaves* directly (unusual in Greco-Roman
  codes: Greek writers did not address slaves as free moral agents — Balch 1988: 33; Aristotle: slave
  as human chattel, incapable of deliberative thinking) and gives *no* reciprocal command to masters
  (L2519–2523, L2540). NT codes thus "actually subverted cultural expectations by elevating the slave
  and the wife with unparalleled dignity" (L2521–2523).
- **Accommodates and subverts:** "Neither he nor any other NT writer mounts a frontal attack on…
  slavery." Volf (1994: 23): following a crucified Messiah is "much more effective" long-run than
  revolution exhortations; "worship of a crucified God… subverts a politics of dominion at its very
  core" (L2550–2552). Calling slave-suffering "unjust" (*adikōs*) implies unprecedented status —
  Aristotle: no true injustice to a slave (Balch 1984: 164; Volf 1994: 23) (L2546–2548). Peter is "not
  optimistic about reforming the world" until the Lord's return; he transforms believers into a
  "colony in this fallen world" (L2556).

### Wives (3:1–6) and husbands (3:7) — what suffering is / is not in view

- **Wives:** submission especially of wives married to unbelieving husbands, that they may be "won
  without a word" by conduct. Direct address affirms moral responsibility "unprecedented in Greek
  thought"; metamessage to the husband: the apostle instructs the wife (the husband's prerogative);
  submission is motivated by Christ, not Greco-Roman philosophy (L2742–2744).
- **Sarah paradigm:** Jewish interpretive tradition (not one Genesis passage alone) replaces Greek
  role models (Plutarch, Xenophon) — another subtle subversion of Greco-Roman culture (L2750–2764).
- **Jobes on abuse (emphatic; writing as a woman scholar):**

  > There is **nothing in this passage of Scripture that would either sanction the abuse of wives or
  > suggest that women should continue to submit themselves to that kind of treatment**. The nature of
  > the suffering that Peter is addressing is **primarily verbal abuse and loss of social standing**.
  > … Because even Greco-Roman statutes did not sanction spousal abuse, a woman who endured domestic
  > violence would not necessarily be viewed as virtuous. (L2766)

  Slaves were commonly beaten as property (faith may have exacerbated hostility; 2:20); that is a
  different social frame from the wives section. Physical domestic abuse is **out of scope** of the
  commanded suffering for wives (L2766).
- **Husbands (3:7):** live considerately ("according to knowledge"), honoring the wife as **coheir** of
  the grace of life, lest their prayers be hindered. "Weaker vessel" primarily physical weakness and
  social empowerment; the exhortation "indirectly addresses the issue of physical abuse" — men whose
  authority "runs roughshod over their women, even with society's full approval, will not be heard by
  God." Jobes: "Peter **delicately prohibits domestic violence** in the Christian household" (L2785–
  2787) — itself a *subversion* of surrounding patriarchal norms.
- Adornment instruction (3:3: gold, elaborate hair) **implies some recipients have wealth** — "contra
  the theory of J. H. Elliott 1981" on poorest/marginalized masses (L2748; also L2435 vs Winter
  benefaction resources).

The code thus *accommodates* (honorable household behavior reduces slander; apologetic in immediate
context, cf. 2:12; 3:15), *rejects* (idolatry, the old way of life; expectation that slave/wife worship
master's/husband's gods — Plutarch Advice 140.19), and *subverts* (addressing slaves and wives as
agents; constraining husbands; Christological re-grounding) simultaneously (L2501, L2525–2529, L2533).

### Application notes Jobes draws (not adjudications)

Reading as a mere marriage manual is shortsighted; first-century slaves/wives would hear an
affirming/empowering word ironically criticized today as oppressive (L2792). Cultural *expressions* of
submission are time-related even if principles transcend (Crouch); modern believers can reject slavery
practice though the NT does not explicitly abolish it, given changed social order and mechanisms for
change (L2804). Churches' public stance on women's roles still has apologetic import; Peter wisely did
not specify the exact look of submission/considerate living — principles left to spouses (L2806–2808).
Peter vs Paul on household: Peter emphasizes evangelistic/apologetic value and addresses the
unbelieving/unworthy-husband case Paul does not; do not conflate (L2796–2800).

## Relation to the Wider NT Household-Code Debate

1 Peter's *Haustafel* parallels and differs from those in [[ephesians-household-code|Ephesians
(5:21–6:9)]] and Colossians (3:18–4:1): 1 Peter has no children/fathers pair, foregrounds *slaves* and
*wives of unbelievers*, and frames the whole within suffering and witness rather than "in the Lord"
mutuality. Sequence/content differences from Eph/Col weaken literary-dependence claims via Haustafeln
(L2540). The complementarian/egalitarian arguments that run through the Ephesians code recur here, but
1 Peter's distinctive contribution is the *missional-apologetic and suffering* framing. See
[[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]] and [[1-tim-2-12-women-teaching|Women,
Teaching, and Authority in 1 Timothy]].

## Why It Matters

The debate is not merely historical: 1 Peter is, for Jobes, the NT's most sustained treatment of
*Christ and culture*, and its differentiated model speaks directly to the global church wherever
Christians are a minority — neither withdrawing into a ghetto nor dissolving into the surrounding
values. The letter's relevance grows, she argues, precisely as Western Christianity becomes more of a
minority and as the faith becomes indigenous in non-Judeo-Christian cultures. McKnight: "Peter intends
his readers to understand who they are before God so that they can be who they are in society" (L544).
