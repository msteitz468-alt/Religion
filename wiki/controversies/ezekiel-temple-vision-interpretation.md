# Ezekiel 40–48: Literal Blueprint, Eschatological Program, or Ideational Vision?

The book of Ezekiel ends with nine chapters of visionary temple architecture, cultic Torah, and ideal geography. What kind of text is this — building instructions, millennial prophecy, constitutional program, or theological vision? Positions documented from [[block-ezekiel-25-48-nicot|Block, *Ezekiel 25–48* (NICOT)]].

## Positions

**1. Literalist / millenarian.** Block notes "a more literalist millenarian understanding has had a long history in Christian circles": the vision describes a future (millennial) temple to be physically built, with reinstituted sacrifices. Block's counterarguments: "the absence of eschatological language" (no "on that day" / "in the latter days"); the *nāśîʾ* "functions primarily, if not exclusively, as a liturgical personage, without a hint of a Davidic connection"; and "contrary to popular opinion, the description of the temple is not presented as a blueprint for some future building… Nowhere is anyone commanded to build it. The man with the measuring line takes Ezekiel on a tour of an existing structure already made." On the cult he adds: "to posit a reinstitution of millennial sacrifices on the basis of Ezekiel's cultic ordinances is unwarranted."

**2. Eschatological-messianic program.** The high mountain is Zion and a new Sinai; the *nāśîʾ* a messianic priest-king. Block resists the messianic identification of the Torah-section prince with the Davidic figure of 34:23–24 and 37:24–25 (four arguments: silence on the Davidic connection; Jerusalem absent; severe restrictions on a supposedly universal sovereign; the "shockingly realistic" portrait), resolving the tension as a genre shift — "the primary concern in this vision is not political but cultic."

**3. Liturgical manifesto (Levenson).** Block cites Levenson's reading of the vision as "'a liturgical manifesto,' a constitution for 'the kingdom of priests and a holy people,'" and calls Levenson "certainly correct in viewing Ezekiel's mountaintop prophetic experience as a programmatic revelation, and the prophet himself as a second Moses" — while resisting a fully eschatologized version.

**4. Failed restoration program.** As a practical postexilic program the vision failed: "the land was never divided among the tribes, no figure like Ezekiel's nāśîʾ emerged… the reconstructed temple fell far short of Ezekiel's plan, and most seriously of all, the kābôd of Yahweh failed to return (cf. Hag. 2:3–9)" (Block). Ezra 3:2's Mosaic altar shows, per Block, that "either the returned exiles were ignorant of Ezekiel's vision, or they rejected it as normative Torah."

**5. Ideational (Block's own position).** "It seems best to interpret chs. 40–48 ideationally. The issue for the prophet is not physical geography but spiritual realities" — on analogy with the dry bones ("not a prophecy of literal individual resurrection"). Summary formula: "Where God is, there is Zion." Block sees this laying "the foundation for the Pauline spiritualization of the temple" (1 Cor 3:16–17; 6:19). The fantastic elements support this: contrived multiples of twenty-five, a perfect-square city, geography-defying allotments — "casting doubt on any interpretation that expects a literal fulfillment."

**6. Christian allegorical reception.** On the river of 47:1–12 Block tabulates Theodoret's full allegory (river = the grace of Christ; the four measurements = the four evangelists; the salt pools = lukewarm Christians), alongside Polychronius, Ephraem, and Jerome — a reception stream Block rejects as exegesis while judging Rev 22:1–3's use of the river "in perfect keeping with the historical interpretation."

## Composition-critical dimension

Behind the hermeneutical dispute sits a literary-historical one: earlier critics denied Ezekiel most of chs. 40–48; Gese argued a multiphase evolution crediting Ezekiel with large portions; holistic readers (Block among them) attribute virtually all of it to the exilic prophet. Zimmerli's "school of Ezekiel" reconstruction of 47:13–48:29 is resisted by Block (with Allen): "there is nothing in this text that could not come from Ezekiel's mouth, if not his pen."

## Status

Open; largely tradition- and method-correlated (dispensational literalism vs. critical-ideational vs. allegorical reception). Not adjudicated here.
