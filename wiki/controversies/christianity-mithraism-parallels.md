# Christianity and Mithraism: Parallels, Dependence, or Common Soil?

## The Issue in Dispute

Early Christianity and the Roman [[mithraism|Mysteries of Mithras]] flourished in the same
centuries, in the same cities, with striking shared features — a savior figure, sacred meals,
initiation/baptism, the date of **Sol Invictus / Dec 25**, moral brotherhood, and the
house-church/[[ostia|mithraeum]] form. What do the parallels mean? The question became central
to debates about Christian origins, and the wiki records the positions **without adjudicating**
(Contradiction Protocol).

## The Methodological Frame (Laeuchli)

Samuel Laeuchli's "Christ and Mithra" essay ([[laeuchli-groh-mithraism-in-ostia|*Mithraism in
Ostia*]]) reframes the older swing between "comparative" scholars (finding parallels, stressing
**proximity**) and the reaction (stressing **remoteness**), insisting the problem is "infinitely
more complex." He proposes a **fourfold distinction**, any or several of which may apply at once:

1. **Direct Mithraic influence on Christianity** — possible "in many instances."
2. **Direct Christian influence on Mithraism** — "not taken seriously enough"; in an age of
   "extreme syncretism" influence "frequently went both ways."
3. **A common root** — elements arising from parallel origins, with no priority of one over the
   other.
4. **Common contemporaneousness** — two religions independently answering the same "Roman
   condition," producing "striking parallels" without contact ("the context changes the
   content").

His cautions: choices are "frequently impossible" (so little pre-Hadrianic Mithraic material);
"more than one possible connection could be involved"; Mithraism of the 2nd c. BCE need not equal
Mithraism of the 3rd c. CE; and one must distinguish conscious, explicit, and implicit contacts.
Yet **cases of dependence do exist** — he names the **4th-century Christmas festival** as one.

## The Points of Contact

- **The sanctuary**: the mithraeum and the Christian **house church** are both a "transmutation
  of the temple religion for the sake of a new type of communal experience" — "the temple became
  house." At Rome the Santa Prisca mithraeum "stood beside a Christian sanctuary until it was
  destroyed by the Christians."
- **The sacred meal**: the Mithraic banquet (bread, water/wine, a *krater*) parallels the
  Eucharist; **Justin Martyr** (*Apol.* 1.66) noted "bread and water" in the mysteries of Mithra;
  **Firmicus Maternus** polemicized that pagan cult food was "poison" against the life-giving
  bread and cup of Christ — while, the liturgy essay argues, the Christian Eucharist was "probably
  influenced also by the sacred meal of Mithra and other contemporary pagan cult meals."
- **The arcanum**: both share a "secrecy which began with social and theological exclusiveness and
  led to the hidden ritual." Laeuchli dissolves the neat "arcane mystery vs. non-arcane
  Christianity" dichotomy into three dimensions (social exclusiveness, arcane theology, arcane
  ritual), all present in degree in both — "gnosis, Qumran, the mystery cult, and the Christian
  church are related in their secrecy."
- **Scale**: a tentative census (~2% Mithraists at Ostia vs. Harnack's ~3% Christians at Rome)
  suggests "a Mithraic impact not too far removed" from the Christian.

## The Essential Differences (Laeuchli's Verdict)

Despite the parallels, Laeuchli argues Christianity and Mithraism diverged at the root, on two
axes:

**The Message.** Mithraism had a "lucid," simple *kerygma* — death and new life, ascent toward
Sol — wholly "subservient to the cult," so "one can ignore theology." Christianity preserved a
"disturbingly pluralistic" body of **texts** that "stood over against all the cultic and social
forms of church life," generating theological drama: "the Johannine literature was a preface to
the Gnostic battle... the Arian fight was nothing but a commentary on the New Testament." The
decisive line: **"Mithra had history, but it had no theological and communal drama which tore
this history apart."**

**The Community.** Mithraic experience was "still mainly vertical," toward the sun and individual
immortality. From its **Hebraic origins** Christianity inherited an "external, even political"
communal function: it **refused the imperial sacrifice** (persecution under Decius), gaining a
"vitally horizontal dimension" — "while the mystery became more and more arcane, the church became
political." The decision "must have been reached in the third century": **Aurelian chose the
Syriac Sol Invictus, not Mithra.** And where Mithraism remained **all-male**, the church included
**women** ("a heritage from the synagogue"), taking earthly community "much more seriously."
Laeuchli even reads the church's **disunity** as paradoxical strength — "the desperate struggle
for concrete communality."

## The Patristic Verdict

The Church Fathers, seeing the resemblances, often explained them as **diabolical counterfeit** —
a "religion devised by Satan to lead souls astray" (so Haywood summarizes the patristic charge in
[[haywood-mithraism-freemasonry-ancient-mysteries|his essay]], adding that "time has proved them
wrong," the two being "as different... as night from day"). Justin and Tertullian deploy the
parallels polemically rather than conceding dependence.

## Status

**Open.** Laeuchli's own conclusion leans toward **fundamental difference with mutual,
multi-directional influence** at the level of form — explicitly refusing the simple
dependence-either-way model. The wiki holds the parallels and the differences side by side,
adjudicating neither; compare the structurally similar
[[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|Zoroastrian-influence]] question and the
[[freemasonry-ancient-mysteries-descent|Freemasonry-descent]] dispute.
