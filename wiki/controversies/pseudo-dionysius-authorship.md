# Pseudo-Dionysius Authorship Controversy

The central controversy surrounding the **Dionysian corpus** — [[on-divine-names|On the
Divine Names]], [[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]], [[celestial-hierarchy|On the
Celestial Hierarchy]], [[ecclesiastical-hierarchy|On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy]], and
the [[letters-of-dionysius|Letters]] — is whether its claimed author, **Dionysius the
Areopagite** (Acts 17:34, the Athenian convert of Paul), actually wrote it, or whether the
texts are a 5th–6th century pseudepigraphon.

## The Two Positions

### Position 1: Traditional Attribution (Apostolic-Era Authorship)
**Held by**: The patristic and medieval church; defended most fully by John Parker (1897–1899)
as one of the last serious modern proponents.

**Key arguments**:
- External testimony from Dionysius of Alexandria (c. 250 CE) affirming the works as
  authentically those of Paul's convert
- Polycarp, Theodore, and others citing the writings as ancient in their own time
- The Council of Nicaea II (787 CE) quoting the *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* as authoritative
- The internal consistency of apostolic-era address (Timothy, Gaius, John the Theologian on
  Patmos) and apparent eyewitness details (presence at the Dormition of the Virgin)
- Parker's argument that Dionysius *preceded* and *influenced* Pantaenus, Ammonius Saccus,
  and Plotinus — inverting the usual account of Neoplatonic dependence

**Parker's distinctive move**: He argued from parallel passages in Pantaenus and Plotinus that
the Alexandrine School drew on Dionysius, rather than Dionysius drawing on Proclus. For
Parker, the similarity to Neoplatonism proves not the corpus is late, but that Neoplatonism
is Christianly derivative. See [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius]] for details.

### Position 2: Fifth-Century Pseudonymous Authorship (Modern Scholarly Consensus)
**Held by**: Virtually all modern scholars since the 19th century; broadly accepted even by
Catholic and Orthodox theologians.

**Key arguments**:
1. **Proclus dependence**: The corpus contains verbal borrowings and structural dependencies
   on Proclus (412–485 CE). Suidas noted this; Professor Stiglmayr (19th c.) filled seven
   pages with parallel passages. Since Proclus died in 485 CE, the corpus must postdate him.
2. **Silence of Eusebius and Jerome**: Neither includes the works in their comprehensive
   catalogues of Christian authors. The first certain citation of the corpus is the 533 CE
   colloquy in Constantinople.
3. **Chalcedonian Christological precision**: The works reflect post-Chalcedonian (451 CE)
   theological formulations that would be anachronistic for a first-century author.
4. **Probable Syrian milieu**: Linguistic and doctrinal features — including Syriac Christian
   parallels — point to a 5th/6th century Syrian author.

**Resolution**: Most scholars date the corpus to c. 485–530 CE and locate the author in a
Syrian Christian environment influenced by Proclus. The identity remains unknown. The
pseudonymous attribution to Paul's disciple gave the works apostolic authority they could
not otherwise claim.

## How the Churches Have Accommodated the Consensus

The modern scholarly consensus does not require theological devaluation of the corpus. The
relevant distinction is between *historical claim* (was this written by Paul's disciple?) and
*theological authority* (is this valuable Christian theology?). Both Eastern Orthodoxy and
Catholicism have effectively separated these:
- The theological content remains authoritative and formative
- The apostolic claim is now treated as a literary convention of pseudonymous writing
- The term "Pseudo-Dionysius" is standard in academic contexts; church use may still say
  "Dionysius"

**Protestant perspective**: The controversy was weaponized by Reformers as evidence that the
medieval church built its theology on a forgery. Luther's remark ("Platonising rather than
Christianising") reflected both suspicion of authenticity and rejection of the corpus's
mystical theology.

## Sharpe's Position (1910): "Not a Forgery"

A. B. Sharpe (*[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True Nature and Value]]*, Ch. XI) is an early
Catholic voice already conceding the modern consensus while resisting the language of "forgery."
On authenticity: "though the Dionysian authorship is not absolutely disproved, the balance of
probability is strongly against it," with the use of Proclus pointing to a date "not earlier than
462." He rehearses both sides — the arguments against (Neoplatonist style; close correspondence
with Proclus's *De Subsistentia Malorum*, identified by **Stiglmayer and Koch**; silence before
the 6th century; anachronisms of monks, post-Nicene *hypostasis*, and the Clement-of-Rome
quotation) and **Monsignor Darboy's** defense (Neoplatonist education; possible reverse
plagiarism by Proclus, per Pachymeres; the anachronisms explained away) — and judges the latter
weaker. He dismisses the Hilduin identification with St Denis of France as having "no historical
value whatever," and notes **Hipler's** 4th-century-theologian theory (1861) as "now generally
rejected."

His distinctive contribution is the argument that pseudonymity here is **not morally culpable
forgery**: "no possible motive can be assigned for a forgery of this kind" — it brought no profit,
and (tellingly) the works "were first quoted in favour of heretics, to whose views they gave no
real support." The author "would seem to have intended to give the Christian rendering of the
philosophico-religious system evolved by Plotinus and later Neoplatonism" (as Aquinas later
Christianised Aristotle), publishing under an honoured name "to gain a hearing." Sharpe compares
the work to historical fiction — *Waverley*, *John Inglesant*, *En Route*: "we have no more right
to fix upon it the moral stigma of forgery." And he grounds the corpus's authority exactly where
the churches later would: "their authority, for us, lies not in their authenticity… but in the
fact that they have been adopted by the Church as truly representative of certain phases of her
doctrine." See [[pseudo-dionysius]] and [[mysticism]].

## Relation to Canon Scope

The authorship controversy affects the status of the Dionysian corpus in different traditions:
- **Catholic/Orthodox**: Authority maintained despite pseudonymity — the corpus is patristic
  theological wisdom regardless of who wrote it
- **Protestant**: The discovery of pseudonymity contributed to Protestant skepticism about
  tradition-based authority; however, Protestant mystical traditions (e.g., some Reformed
  spirituality, Quakerism) have drawn on Dionysian themes indirectly
- The corpus never held *scriptural* status in any tradition; its authority was always as
  patristic theology
