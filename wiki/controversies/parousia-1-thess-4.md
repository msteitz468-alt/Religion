# Parousia and the Fate of the Deceased (1 Thessalonians 4:13–18)

Primary source for densified positions below: [[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]], *1–2 Thessalonians* ([[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|BECNT 2014]]), on 1 Thess 4:13–18 and the related unit 5:1–11.

## The Passage

*"For since we believe that Jesus died and rose, so also, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep. For this we say to you by the word of the Lord, that we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord, will certainly not precede those who have fallen asleep. Because the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a commanding shout, with a voice of an archangel, and with a trumpet call of God, and the dead in Christ will rise first; then we who are alive, who are left, will be snatched up together with them by means of clouds for a reception of the Lord in the air; and so we will always be with the Lord."* (4:14–17)

## Literary Frame (Weima)

Weima treats 4:13–18 as hortatory/paraenetic despite didactic content: teaching aims to replace hopeless grief with comfort (disclosure + purpose clause + closing “comfort one another”). Distinct unit ending at 4:18 (not fused with 5:1–11), though many formal parallels exist. Structure: (1) opening assertion—Christians grieve with hope (13); (2) reason 1—church confession (14); (3) reason 2—“word of the Lord” (15–17: Pauline summary 15b + logion 16–17a + Pauline climax 17b); (4) conclusion—comfort one another (18).

Disclosure form “we do not want you not to know” + brothers + *peri* marks **new, previously untaught** material (contrast repeated “you know / we commanded” in 4:1–12; 5:2). Topic: “those who are asleep” (*koimaō* = death euphemism, not “soul sleep”). Deaths not likely martyrdoms (affliction = social harassment). Source: Timothy’s oral report (3:6), not a written questionnaire.

## The Problem Being Addressed

Weima argues that the passage addresses a *specific* pastoral problem, not general eschatological curiosity. The Thessalonians were grieving over fellow believers who had died before Christ's return, fearing that the deceased would *miss* the parousia or be at some **disadvantage** compared to living believers—a **coordination** problem (how resurrection of the dead coordinates with Christ’s return), not a denial of resurrection itself.

### Six hypotheses surveyed; Weima’s preferred (6)

1. **Gnostic “resurrection already”** (Schmithals; Harnisch)—anachronistic; no strong evidence at Thessalonica; Paul’s response is not aimed at denying a denial of resurrection doctrine.
2. **Paul had not yet taught resurrection** (Marxsen, Lüdemann, Mearns, Nicholl)—incredible given 15+ years of mission, creedal practice, Pharisee background; he can cite “dead in Christ will rise” without defining the body.
3. **Theoretical belief collapses before actual death** (Marshall, Green)—but the focus is coordination with the parousia, not general grief alone.
4. **Abandoned confidence in the second coming** (Hyldahl)—letter thanks for ongoing hope.
5. **Plevnik/Lohfink assumption**: only the living can be assumed to heaven; resurrection restores earthly life so the dead can be assumed with the living—Weima rejects: Gentile church unlikely steeped in that Jewish-apocalyptic assumption literacy; conflicts with *apantēsis* formal-reception image (movement **to earth**, not secret rapture/assumption to heaven).
6. **Preferred (Weima)**: fear that the dead are at a **disadvantage relative to the living at the parousia** (coordination problem).

### Evidence for the disadvantage reconstruction

1. The emphatic future negation "will certainly not precede" (*ou mē phthasōmen*)—“sounds like a denial of an opinion actually held”; *ou mē* + aor. subj. is the strongest negation; rare and emphatic in Paul.
2. The temporal adverbs "first" (*prōton*, v. 16, clause-final) and "then" (*epeita*, v. 17) sequence the dead rising before the living are taken up — correcting the implied concern about the deceased being left out. Dead rise first **to earth**, then with the living are taken up (not privilege of rising straight into air ahead forever).
3. The prepositional phrase in v. 17 ("together with them," *hama syn autois*) is placed early for emphasis: both groups participate equally.
4. Several intertestamental texts express the conviction that the living are at an advantage over the dead at the inauguration of the messianic age (2 Esd [4 Ezra] 13:24; 6:25; Pss. Sol. 17.44).
5. Deep grief is understandable given anticipation of parousia vindication and privileged *apantēsis* escort role.

**Creedal ground (4:14):** “Since we believe that Jesus died and rose” (first-class *ei*) cites a pared creedal formula; Jesus’ resurrection guarantees deceased believers will be resurrected and present so God “will bring with him those who have fallen asleep.” *Dia tou Iēsou* modifies “will bring,” not “those who have fallen asleep.” Locale with *apantēsis*: gathering to escort Christ on the remainder of his journey **to earth**, not heavenly assumption.

**“Word of the Lord” (4:15):** *Touto* cataphoric to 15–17. Weima leans to **Jesus material** (agraphon / Synoptic paraphrase / general end-time teaching; S. Kim), not merely a prophetic “word of the LORD.” Highest authority for the living-not-preceding-dead assertion. “We who are alive” does **not** prove Paul expected to live until the parousia (purpose is equal participation; “we” qualified by participles; both states possible at 5:10).

## Key Terms

### *Parousia* (4:15)

The Greek *parousia* (literally "presence," "arrival") was a technical term in the Hellenistic world for the formal ceremonial visit (*adventus*) of a king or high official to a city. It evoked the elaborate protocols of welcome accorded to visiting dignitaries: delegations going out to meet them, festive decorations, acclamations, and formal escorts. Paul's use of this term — rather than the more generic *erchomai* (coming) or *apokalypsis* (revelation) — deliberately frames Christ's return as a royal *parousia*.

### *Apantēsis* (4:17) — The Central Dispute

The noun *apantēsis* (eis apantēsin, "for a reception") is the exegetical crux of the controversy. It was a technical term for a particular custom: when a visiting king or dignitary was approaching a city, the leading citizens would exit the city gates and go out to meet him (*apantān*), escorting him on the final leg of the journey *into* the city.

This custom is well-documented:
- Cicero uses *apantēsis* without translation when writing about Julius Caesar's and Octavian's triumphal receptions in Italy (Att. 8.16.2; 16.11.6).
- The rabbis used *apantēsis* as a loanword for the same practice (Midr. Tanḥ. 178a).
- Both other NT uses of the identical phrase *eis apantēsin* employ it in exactly this technical sense: Acts 28:15 (Christians in Rome go out to meet Paul at the Appii Forum and escort him into the city); Matt 25:6 (the virgins go out to meet the bridegroom and escort him to the wedding).

**Implication for 4:17**: If *apantēsis* retains its technical sense here, believers go out to meet the descending Christ *in the air* and then escort him on the final descent to earth — the same movement as the Hellenistic custom, only in the vertical dimension. The destination is *earth*, not heaven.

Chrysostom saw this centuries ago:

> "For as when a king ceremoniously entered a city, certain dignitaries and city rulers would go out of the city to meet him; but the guilty and condemned criminals would be guarded within, awaiting the sentence. In the same way, when the Lord comes, those who are confident toward him will meet him in the midst of the air, but the condemned, who are conscious of having committed many sins, will wait below for their judge."

### *Harpazō* (4:17) — "Snatched Up" and the Inversion

The verb *harpazō* (Latin: *rapio*, "rapture") means "snatch, seize" and in 4:17 describes believers being taken up by God (**divine passive**). Stage 3 of Weima’s sequence: living “snatched up” (*harpagēsometha*) **together with** the raised dead (*hama syn autois*—equality stressed). Verb = sudden taking; positive sense of being caught up (cf. 2 Cor 12; apocalyptic ascents).

**Weima’s wordplay inversion (attributed):** Paul’s choice of *harpazō* was intended **not** to teach “a secret rapture of the church” but rather to make a possible wordplay: in Greco-Roman funerary and consolation literature, *harpazō* and cognates were used for how death or Fate “snatches away” the living. Paul inverts this—rather than death snatching the living, God snatches them up into association with the Lord and fellow Christians (Malherbe: “neat twist”—usually separation from the living, here snatching into association). Clouds = theophanic means (Danielic/Son of Man return imagery), not primarily location.

The term does **not** in this context imply stealth, secrecy, or a destination of heaven. The movement is clearly: Lord descends (with three public accompaniments: commanding shout—*keleusma*, most naturally Christ’s, aimed at the dead to rise; voice of an archangel; trumpet of God linked to resurrection, 1 Cor 15:52) → dead rise first → living and dead are snatched up together to meet him in the air → escort him (the *apantēsis*) to earth → “always be with the Lord.”

### Apocalyptic Poetry, Not Blueprint (Weima)

4:16–17 is the most explicit Pauline (and perhaps NT) sequence for the return, but Weima (with Gaventa/Marshall/Calvin) insists on poetic/symbolic apocalyptic language: distinguish meaning and referent; primary pastoral purpose is comfort (4:18), not a detailed predictive timetable. “This passage has more in common with poetry than with blueprints… a misuse of a text whose primary purpose is… to provide pastoral comfort.”

### *Apantēsis* notes densified (R9)

Debate on *eis apantēsin*: Sinai theophany (Dupont) vs Hellenistic formal receptions; Luckensmeyer: not a strict technical term in every use, but Hellenistic reception influence “cannot be rejected.” Luckensmeyer also notes possible counter-imperial use of *keleusma* vs imperial *keleusis*. Chrysostom already: as when a king arrives, the honored go out to meet; the condemned wait within.

## Three Major Interpretive Positions

### Position A: *Apantēsis* as Formal Reception (Weima preferred; critical and most evangelical scholars)

**Representatives**: Weima, Malherbe, Green, Gaventa, Morris, Bruce, Wanamaker, Marshall; most commentators since Peterson (1930) documented the custom.

**Reading (Weima):** Believers are taken up to meet the descending Christ in the air and escort him to earth. The passage's primary purpose is pastoral reassurance: deceased believers will rise first and then participate equally with living believers in this event. The final clause — "so we will always be with the Lord" — is the real climax; the destination is permanent presence with Christ, not a spatial location. Nicholl (as Weima notes): Paul omits a step—resurrection + ascent needed to link Jesus’s death/resurrection to deceased sharing the parousia; transformation of the living is not taught here (drawn from 1 Cor 15; Phil 3:21).

**On the rapture question — Weima’s rejection of secret rapture:** The passage does not teach a "secret pretribulation rapture" because:
1. The event is accompanied by a commanding shout, archangel's voice, and trumpet call of God — maximally *public* (contrast the "secret" rapture of dispensationalism).
2. The *apantēsis* metaphor implies a return to earth, not a departure to heaven.
3. 2 Thess 1:7–10 explicitly describes the revelation of Christ as accompanied by "flaming fire" and visible judgment — not a quiet secretive removal of believers.
4. Fee (as Weima reports): secret rapture cannot be intended—Thessalonians had no prior instruction of such an event “otherwise unknown in the church until the mid-nineteenth century.”

### Position B: Secret Pre-Tribulation Rapture (Dispensationalist — rejected by Weima)

**Representatives**: J. N. Darby (originator, 19th c.), C. I. Scofield, John Walvoord, Thomas, English, and the popular "Left Behind" tradition.

**Reading (strongest form):** 1 Thess 4:13–18 describes a hidden removal of the church before the seven-year tribulation; 2 Thess 2:1–12 describes the *public* revelation of Christ at the end of the tribulation ("the coming of the Lord" in 2:1). These are two separate events. The Holy Spirit and the church are removed from earth (see Spirit/church restrainer view on [[restrainer-2-thess-2]]), allowing the man of lawlessness to appear. Related reading: *apostasia* in 2 Thess 2:3 as “departure/rapture” (English/Wuest/House).

**Exegetical problems** (Weima and most critics — recorded as Weima’s critique, not wiki adjudication):
- The *apantēsis* metaphor implies escort of Christ to earth, not departure to heaven.
- The event in 1 Thess 4 is explicitly public (trumpet call, archangel's voice, shout).
- The separation of 1 Thess 4 from 2 Thess 2 as "two comings" is not stated in either text and requires importing the full dispensationalist framework.
- *Harpazō* does not inherently denote hiddenness; it simply means "snatch" (cf. Acts 8:39: Philip is "snatched" by the Spirit and appears in Azotus — publicly). Weima: intended for possible funerary wordplay inversion, not a secret-rapture doctrine.
- *Apostasia* means religious apostasy or political rebellion in LXX–NT, never positive “departure/rapture,” and is paired with the man of lawlessness (Weima).

The dispensationalist rapture reading is historically recent (19th-century Brethren movement), has no attestation in patristic or medieval interpretation, and requires reading *apantēsis* against its well-documented technical meaning. **Weima’s position is explicit rejection of Position B**, not neutrality between A and B.

### Position C: Public General Resurrection at the Parousia (Non-Dispensationalist Evangelical/Reformed)

**Representatives**: Most Reformed and traditional evangelical commentators who accept the *apantēsis* reading and reject the dispensationalist framework.

**Reading**: The passage describes the single, public return of Christ at the general resurrection. Deceased believers rise; living believers are transformed and taken up; all together meet the descending Lord and accompany him to earth for judgment and consummation. No "secret rapture." No two stages. 1 Thess 4 and 2 Thess 2 and 1 Cor 15 all describe the same single eschatological event from different angles.

This position is functionally identical to Position A in its interpretation of the *apantēsis* metaphor; the difference from Position B is primarily about whether there is a secret prior rapture. Most mainstream evangelical and Reformed commentary supports Position C.

## The Destination of Believers

A distinctive element in Weima's reading: the *apantēsis* metaphor implies that after meeting Christ in the air, believers do not remain in heaven but escort him back to earth — just as the delegation escorted the visiting king *into* the city. This is supported by:
- The *parousia* metaphor throughout, which implies Christ's arrival at a destination (earth)
- 1 Thess 4:14: God will bring the deceased "with him" when Christ returns — not into heaven but at the return to earth
- 2 Thess 1:10: Christ will come "on that day to be glorified in his saints" — a coming to earth
- The closing statement "we will always be with the Lord" (4:17) says nothing about the location; permanent presence with Christ, not a heavenly destination, is the pastoral climax

## Why Did the Thessalonians Grieve?

A closely related crux: why were the Thessalonians' fears intense enough to require this pastoral response?

If they believed in the bodily resurrection (which Paul assumes they did), why grieve so deeply? **Weima’s answer:** they feared not that the deceased would never be raised, but that the deceased would miss or be disadvantaged at the *parousia event itself* — the glorious, visible royal return of Christ, which they anticipated with great longing. The deceased would be raised eventually, perhaps, but would they be present for the moment of Christ's arrival in power?

Paul's correction: No — deceased believers rise *first* (*prōton*), before any other event occurs, so they will be there to participate fully. The eschatological vindication and glory that the Thessalonians so intensely anticipated is not reserved for the living alone.

**Grief with hope, not absolute ban on grief** (Weima with majority from Augustine/Theodoret): Paul does **not** forbid all grief (contra Lightfoot/Milligan/Frame/Best/Malherbe absolute reading of *kathōs*). Support: Phil 2:27; Rom 12:15; death as last enemy; Jesus weeping. Critical difference: outsiders grieve without hope (popular despair on inscriptions; Theocritus, Catullus; Irene’s P.Oxy. 115) vs comfort grounded in resurrection/parousia promises.

## Living Believers at the Day of the Lord (5:1–11) — Related Unit

Weima: while 4:13–18 concerns deceased believers at Jesus’s return, **5:1–11** focuses on living Christians at the same eschatological event—not mere curiosity about timing but anxiety whether they would avoid wrath and obtain salvation/life with Christ. Independent literary unit (*peri de* + “brothers”; return to already-known material; *akribōs* “you know accurately”).

Load-bearing moves for this controversy page:
- **Thief in the night** (5:2–3): sudden/unpredictable for *outsiders*; Jesus tradition (Matt 24:43; Luke 12:39). Believers are “sons of light and sons of day” (5:4–5)—will not be surprised like a thief. This underwrites the authorship-debate rebuttal that 1 Thess teaches “thief without warning” *for believers* (it does not).
- **“Peace and security”** (*eirēnē kai asphaleia*, 5:3): Weima prefers Roman imperial propaganda slogan (Pax/Securitas coins, monuments, Plutarch *Ant.* 40.4) over OT false-“Peace, peace” echo—Gentile audience; pairing of security.
- **Ready and steady** (5:6–8): stay awake / be sober—problem is anxiety-filled fixation, not indifference. Armor: breastplate of faith and love; helmet the hope of salvation (Isa 59:17 adapted).
- **Destined not for wrath but for obtaining salvation** (5:9–10): “whether we are awake or asleep” = alive or dead (not moral vigilance alone)—summarizes fate of both living and deceased; *hama* equal participation; future life with him at the parousia.
- Comfort and build up one another (5:11); later 5:14 “fainthearted” best linked to grief over the dead (4:13–18), “weak” hypothetically to anxiety about status at judgment (5:1–11). Peace benediction 5:23 recapitulates holy living + comfort at the *parousia*.

## Resolution Status

**Open as a multi-tradition dispute**, especially where dispensational pretribulation rapture remains a live confessional option. Within the critical and mainstream evangelical commentary Weima surveys, the *apantēsis*-to-earth / public parousia reading is dominant, and Weima’s own position is clear: reject secret rapture; prefer formal-reception escort; pastoral climax is equal permanent presence with the Lord. This page records positions; it does not adjudicate.
