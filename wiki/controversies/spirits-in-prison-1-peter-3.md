# Christ and the Spirits in Prison (1 Peter 3:18–22; 4:6)

> "For Christ also suffered for sins once for all… being put to death in the flesh but made alive in
> the spirit, in which also he went and preached to the spirits in prison, who formerly did not obey…
> in the days of Noah… Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you… through the resurrection of
> Jesus Christ, who has gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God, with angels, authorities,
> and powers subjected to him." (1 Pet. 3:18–22)

This is one of the most notoriously difficult passages in the New Testament — it lies behind the
creedal clause "he descended into hell" and behind doctrines of a postmortem offer of salvation. The
linked verse 4:6 ("the gospel was preached even to the dead") compounds the puzzle. This page records
the positions; [[karen-jobes|Jobes]]'s reading in [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|BECNT, 2005]] (the Dalton–
Selwyn line) is presented as one option, with her full argumentation and explicit rejections, without
adjudicating the dispute in wiki voice.

## The Interpretive Variables

The passage's difficulty is concentrated in several decisions:
- **When/how did Christ "go and preach"?** Between death and resurrection (a descent), at/after the
  resurrection, or at the ascension?
- **Who are "the spirits in prison"?** Disobedient fallen **angels** (the 1 Enoch / Genesis 6
  tradition), or the **souls of dead humans** (Noah's contemporaries, or all the pre-Christian dead)?
- **What was "preached"** (*kēryssō*, 3:19)? A **proclamation of victory/condemnation**, or an **offer
  of salvation** (the gospel)?
- **Is 4:6 the same event as 3:19?** And does it teach postmortem evangelism?

## The Positions

**1. Ascension proclamation of victory over fallen angels (Dalton, Selwyn, Reicke, France; adopted
by Jobes — post-Dalton 1965 majority).** Jobes follows this line in full. The three participles of
3:18–19 (*thanatōtheis* — *zōopoiētheis* — *poreutheis*) trace a sequence — death, resurrection,
**ascension** — confirmed by the *resumptive* "having gone (*poreutheis*) into heaven" in 3:22
(explicitly the ascension). So the "going and preaching" of 3:19 is Christ's **triumphant
proclamation, in his ascent, of victory** over the powers (L3109, L3259).

Jobes's supporting moves (attributed):
- **Verb is not "descend."** The verb is *poreuomai* ("to go"), not *katabainō*; the text does not
  locate the prison "below"; no Hades, Tartarus, or Sheol appears; *phylakē* is not elsewhere a NT
  term for the place of dead humans (L3111).
- **"Spirits" = fallen angels / demonic spirits**, not unqualified human deceased. In NT and 1 Enoch,
  *pneuma* (esp. plural) overwhelmingly denotes malevolent supernatural beings; deceased souls are
  typically *psychē*. Where 1 Enoch uses *pneuma* for deceased humans it always qualifies (e.g.
  *pneumata nekrōn*); in sections paralleling 3:19–20, unqualified *pneuma* = supernatural beings
  (L3163; cf. L3117). Grudem's lexical counter-examples Jobes finds "arguably ambiguous" (L445 area).
- **1 Enoch / Watchers background (Gen 6:1–4 embellished):** fallen angels abandoned heaven, produced
  giants whose bodies spawned evil spirits, were imprisoned until "the day of the great conclusion"
  (1 En. 12–16). Both traditions: spirits receive a divine proclamation closely associated with Noah.
  Imprisonment = spatial expression of God's restraining power; the message is **victory/condemnation**
  confirming that day is now upon them — Christ's ascension itself may *be* the proclamation of their
  defeat (Bandstra 2003) (L3115–3117, L3132).
- **Allusion without book literacy.** Peter does not mention Enoch; Jobes: only general knowledge of
  evil spirits' role in the flood story is needed (analogy: many know purgatory without citing texts).
  Asia Minor's indigenous flood traditions, Apamea Kibotos (*kibōtos* = ark in LXX Genesis), Sibylline
  Oracles, and later Noah coinage make the Noah tradition prominent even among Gentiles (L3119–3130).
- **Flesh/Spirit (3:18):** not body vs soul (Alexandrian dualism) but spheres or modes — earthly
  limitation/suffering vs eschatological life by the Spirit; Christ *in entirety* put to death and made
  alive; *zōopoieō* elsewhere parallels raising (L3098–3104).
- ***kēryssō* ≠ necessarily gospel offer.** Often gospel preaching in the NT, but also broader
  "proclaim"; notably *euangelizomai* is **not** used of Christ's proclamation in 3:19 (L3161).

The cosmic sweep of Christ's victory (3:22: "angels, authorities, and powers subjected to him")
reassures persecuted readers that the evil forces behind their oppression are already defeated.
**Jobes's verdict on this verse:** "Therefore, the doctrine of the descensus of Christ into hell
cannot be defended on the basis of 1 Pet. 3:19" (L3259). Other usual NT supports (Acts 2:27, 31; Rom.
10:6–8; Eph. 4:8–10; Matt. 27:52) can be read otherwise (L3259).

**2. Descent into hell (*descensus ad inferos*).** Between his death and resurrection Christ
descended to the realm of the dead. The clause grounds the Apostles'/Athanasian creedal "he descended
into hell." Versions differ on what he did there — proclaim victory to the imprisoned, or liberate the
righteous dead ("the harrowing of hell," prominent in patristic and Eastern tradition). Jobes notes:
the creed phrase is not Nicene and not from an early official council; first ~AD 400 in Rufinus's
exposition of the Roman creed, *instead of* "and was buried," so originally graphic for burial/
"descent into the grave" (Westminster Larger Catechism Q&A 50 still so understands); "consensus of
scholarship has largely abandoned the descensus interpretation" of 3:19 (L3065–3067). Historically the
doctrine arose from theological questions (OT saints' redemption; what Jesus did between death and
resurrection; postmortem opportunity) **not** from exegesis of 3:18–22; 1 Peter was only later pressed
into service as biblical defense (Dalton 1965: 16) (L3098, L3139–3141). Loss of 1 Enoch after the 2nd
c. (until Ethiopic rediscovery late 18th c.) left interpreters without the Watchers background and
favored descent readings (L3139).

**Goppelt's contemporary descensus defense** (most detailed): (1) *en hō* simple conjunction, no
temporal force; (2) *kēryssō* implies gospel offer → not fallen angels → human deceased; (3) fallen
angels disobeyed long before Noah → spirits = humans disobeying while the ark was built; (4) 3:19 =
same event as 4:6. Point: gospel to even the most lost humanity (L3159). Jobes rejects each link (see
4:6 below and lexical arguments above). Reicke and A. Hanson allow preaching to **both** souls and
fallen angels; Selwyn allows. Jobes: the question turns on whether the Enoch–Noah tradition is the
background, where *pneuma* clearly = malevolent angelic beings.

**3. Christ preached through Noah (Augustine; Grudem; older Reformed).** The *preincarnate* Christ,
"in the spirit," preached *through Noah* to Noah's unbelieving contemporaries while the ark was being
built; "the spirits now in prison" are those human contemporaries, now imprisoned in death/hell for
having rejected that preaching. Taught by Augustine, repeated by Aquinas, current in the Reformation;
modern defenders include Clark, Erickson, Feinberg, and Grudem — motivated by rejection of postmortem
conversion implied by descensus (L3090, L3149). Accords with 1:11 (Spirit of preincarnate Christ
through prophets) but Jobes's "test of viability" is adequacy to the parallel syntax of 3:18 and the
opening of 3:19: she finds the through-Noah reading does **not** adequately address that syntax
(L3090, L3149, L3281). Augustine had weak Greek, may not have known Enoch, and was more concerned with
theological implications; he rightly held Christ wholly put to death and wholly raised (rejecting
Alexandrian body–soul dichotomy) (L3149).

**Calvin's variant:** troubled by postmortem conversion; reinterprets descensus as final proclamation
of salvation to the godly deceased + damnation to the reprobate (*Institutes* 2.16.8–9); on 3:19 he
reads proclamation of salvation to godly souls before the flood, with "prison" as refuge/watchtower —
forced against the text's "who **disobeyed**" (L3151–3153).

Jobes affirms that Grudem's *pastoral applications* (Noah as a righteous minority amid a hostile world
— a model for Peter's readers) stand independently of his exegesis of 3:19 (L3281–3293).

**4. Postmortem evangelism (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, much of the Eastern church; Goppelt
defends it in detail).** Christ descended and *offered the gospel* to the dead — at minimum Noah's
generation, at most all the pre-Christian dead — opening the possibility of postmortem conversion.
Clement (~150–220) may first use 3:19–20 for postmortem conversion; Origen and Cyril of Alexandria
followed; still used with 4:6 for universal opportunity (Goppelt; Hanson demythologizes but keeps the
claim) (L3143–3145). Jobes's limit critique: the text **specifically limits** preaching to spirits
disobedient **at the time of Noah** — hard biblical basis for broadening to all pre-Christ dead, much
less universalism (L3147).

## The Noah–Baptism Typology (3:20–21)

All readings must account for the pivot to baptism. Peter draws a **typology** between Noah's flood
and Christian baptism: as eight persons were "brought safely through water," so "baptism now saves you
… through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (3:21) — explicitly *not* as a washing of dirt but as "an
appeal [*or* pledge] to God for a good conscience."

**Jobes on *eperōtēma* (3:21):** only here in the NT. Under influence of the verb "ask," many take it
as "request/appeal" for a good conscience — attractive but, she judges, a poorer contextual fit.
Better: **"pledge"** — papyri (P.Cair.Preis. 1.16; Selwyn) at sealing a legal contract = formal
question of willingness + positive response. Second-century lexical evidence: baptism as question about
faith + positive response + water as sacrament of a **pledge of faithfulness**. Facing suffering and
temptation to turn away, the baptized are reminded of that pledge (L3218). Selwyn: not far from Latin
*sacramentum* (military oath) for baptism and Eucharist (L3220). Baptism that saves does **not**
remove moral filth once-for-all as a "ticket to heaven"; objective basis is the resurrection; subjective
basis is the believer's pledged relationship with God resulting in a good conscience (L3204–3222).

Jobes (with Dalton's four-stage scheme, L3271–3279) reads the whole unit as assuring persecuted
believers: (1) by water God saved Noah's family from an evil world under evil spirits; (2) definitive
salvation comes through Christ's passion and resurrection — as risen Lord he proclaims defeat of those
spirits; (3) God now saves the baptized through that same resurrection; (4) therefore the suffering
Christian, though a minority, is secure because the hostile powers are already subjected to Christ.
**Typology does not require** descensus or preaching through Noah (L3281). Baptism is not a digression
and does not support theories that 1 Peter was originally a baptismal homily/liturgy (only one explicit
baptism reference at 3:21; such genre theories largely discarded) (L3174, L3224).

## 1 Peter 4:6 — "The Gospel Preached to the Dead" (Severed from 3:19)

A distinct crux often dragged into the orbit of 3:19. Jobes argues **against** linking the two and
**against** a postmortem-second-chance reading (most contemporary interpreters no longer associate
4:6 with 3:19: Achtemeier, Bandstra, Dalton, Davids, Elliott, Hillyer, Kistemaker, Michaels) (L3414):

- "The dead" in 4:6 must have the same referent as in 4:5 ("the one ready to judge the living and the
  *dead*") — i.e., all humanity subject to judgment; the spiritually-dead-though-alive reading is
  unlikely (L3410).
- The two verses share few real points of contact: different verbs (*kēryssō* in 3:19 vs.
  *euangelizomai* in 4:6), different audiences (*pneumata*, "spirits," vs. *nekroi*, "the dead" — not
  synonymous), different discourse units; in 3:19 Christ *proclaims*, while 4:6 is passive (Christ is
  the *content* preached) (L3410).
- 4:6 speaks of **one group**, not two: the same dead judged by humans *sarki* and alive *pneumati* by
  God — "they therefore do not need an offer of salvation" (L3414).
- The postmortem-conversion reading of 4:6 historically arose from the creedal descensus assumption
  about 3:19 (L3410).

**Jobes's positive reading of 4:6:** "the gospel was preached to the dead, meaning to those who are
now dead (but who heard the gospel while living, as the TNIV makes clear)" (L3416). Immediate point:
"death does not exempt a person from God's coming judgment" (L3404). Physical death nullifies neither
the gospel's promises nor its warnings; Christian dead judged wanting by human standards may still
"live in the spirit by God's standards."

Despite non-identity of the two proclamations, Jobes holds both make universal claims for Christ:
victor over present and ancient evil, full authority over fallen angels and human souls, basis of
God's judgment (L3418).

## Why It Matters

Beyond the exegesis, the passage anchors:
- the creedal **"he descended into hell"** (position 2);
- debates over a **postmortem opportunity for salvation** (position 4) vs. judgment fixed at death
  (Jobes, Reformed/evangelical mainstream);
- a **cosmic Christology** of Christ's enthronement over all spiritual powers (3:22) — Jobes's
  pastoral payoff for a persecuted, minority church: "If Peter's claim is true that Christ's
  resurrection and ascension have dealt with even the primordial evil of fallen angels… then Christ
  is victorious over all evil—even the most depraved—for all time" (L3295).
