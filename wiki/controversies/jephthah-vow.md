# Jephthah's Vow

The fate of Jephthah’s daughter after his vow in Judg 11:30–31 is one of the most contested ethical-interpretive cruxes in the Former Prophets. The vow: if YHWH gives victory over Ammon, “whatever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me… shall be YHWH’s, and I will offer it up as a burnt offering.” His only child comes out with tambourines; after two months bewailing her virginity on the mountains, “he did to her according to his vow” (11:39).

## Positions

### 1. Literal burnt-offering sacrifice (Webb and many readers)

[[barry-webb|Webb]] (NICOT, 2012; [[webb-judges-nicot|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18) holds that the narrator intends **literal sacrifice**. The narrator’s reticence (“he did… his vow” rather than repeating “burnt offering”) still implies the other: “we are clearly meant to understand that Jephthah literally sacrificed his daughter.” The virginity motif is the content of her mourning (dying young, childless, unfulfilled) — **not** a substitute for death.

Webb’s distinctive framing is **characterological**: the vow is not impulsive but “shrewd and calculating,” a **bribe** continuous with Jephthah’s negotiation style — “As the elders once offered inducement to Jephthah, so Jephthah now offers inducement to Yahweh.” Victory would have come anyway (Spirit already on him; battle itinerary resumes pre-vow). Theme of the episode: turning religion into **politics/negotiation** with God instead of submission. If laying his cause before “YHWH the Judge” (11:27) was Jephthah’s greatest act of faith, the vow was his greatest folly.

**Excursus 6 (Webb):** wrong to make the vow (unnecessary bribe; undercuts 11:27 confidence) and wrong to sacrifice (child sacrifice abomination, Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). Law provided paths for rash/unwitting vows (Num 30; Lev 5:4–6) he did not take. Confused religion of the period may explain ignorance without excusing the act.

### 2. Perpetual virginity without death

Some Jewish and Christian apologetic readers argue the vow’s fulfillment was lifelong dedication/celibacy (bewailing virginity; annual custom of the daughters of Israel). This spares the text from depicting a judge performing human sacrifice. Webb **rejects** this as the content of fulfillment while acknowledging virginity as the daughter’s mourned loss.

### 3. Deliberate narrative ambiguity

Some literary critics stress the narrator’s refusal to say “he offered her as a burnt offering” and treat silence as ethical pressure on the reader. Webb agrees the wording is reticent but denies that reticence cancels the implication of sacrifice.

## Tradition-specific notes

- **Jewish reception:** rash-oath warnings; midrashic attempts to mitigate or condemn; daughter sometimes given voice or name in later tradition.
- **Christian reception:** Heb 11:32 lists Jephthah among the faithful without narrating the vow; preaching often treats the episode as tragic warning against bargaining with God; feminist recovery of the daughter’s agency and the annual commemorative custom.
- **Critical scholarship:** diplomatic speech sometimes treated as secondary; Greek tragic parallels (Iphigenia) noted; historicity of the custom debated.

## Resolution status

**Open** across traditions. Within this wiki’s ingested sources, **Webb’s position is firm literal sacrifice + negotiation-religion theme** — not “left open.” Earlier wiki summary (2026-06) wrongly reported that Webb withholds adjudication between death and virginity.
