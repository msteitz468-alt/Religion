# The Partition of the Punjab (1947)

The division of the [[punjab|Punjab]] at Indian independence split the [[sikhs|Sikh]] homeland in two and
produced one of the largest and bloodiest population exchanges in history. Khushwant Singh (*A History of
the Sikhs* Vol. 2, "Civil Strife, Exodus, and Resettlement") treats it as **the central wound of modern
Sikh history**, the trauma generating all subsequent Sikh separatism. The page records his account and
the interpretive tensions within it.

## The Character of the Violence

Singh distinguishes 1946–47 categorically from earlier communal riots: "what took place in 1946 and 1947
can only be described as a general massacre. No one was exempted on grounds of age or sex… masterminded
by politicians and executed by gangs… armed with modern weapons such as stenguns and hand-grenades." The
chain ran from the Muslim League's **Direct Action Day** (Calcutta, 16 August 1946: "5000 killed, 15,000
injured, and 100,000 rendered homeless") through Noakhali and Bihar to the NWFP.

## The Sikh Predicament

The Sikhs were "13 per cent in the Punjab… much the most prosperous section of the Punjab peasantry,"
regarded by Muslims as "an aggressively anti-Muslim element." Rahmat Ali, originator of the Pakistan
idea, had written that Sikhs must be expelled: "they will never be of us." Singh records that the Sikh
"armies" (Akal Fauj, Akal Sena) proved "paper organizations and their leaders paper tigers" in the March
1947 riots; on Baisakhi 1947 [[master-tara-singh|Master Tara Singh]] led 280 *jathedars* in an Akal Takht
vow to defend the community.

## The Division and the "Self-Inflicted Wound"

The **Radcliffe Award** gave Pakistan "62 per cent of the total area of the province and 55 per cent of
the population"; the Sikhs' "richest lands, over 150 historical shrines, and half of their population were
left on the Pakistan side." A boundary at the **Chenab** (the Sikh demand) would have kept "over 90 per
cent of the Sikhs in a compact unit." Crucially, Singh records Mountbatten's point that the partition of
the Punjab was itself a **Sikh/Congress demand**: "it was mainly at the request of the Sikh community that
Congress had put forward the resolution on the partition of the Punjab" — even though "the plan which they
had produced divided their community into two almost equal parts." This is the controversy's sharpest
edge: the Sikhs pressed for a division that cut their own community in half.

## Sikhs as Victims and as Perpetrators (the unadjudicated tension)

Singh holds two pictures together without reconciling them. Sikhs were victims of massacre in West Punjab
and the NWFP; yet "Sikh violence attained its peak in September 1947," with *jathas* swelling "to
thousands for attacks on villages, convoys and trains": "trains were attacked and sent across the borders
with bogies jammed with slaughtered passengers… Never in the history of the world was there a bigger
exchange of populations attended with so much bloodshed." On casualties he cites Penderel Moon's estimate
of "60,000 dead in Pakistan and a little more in east Punjab," calling Moon's headline "total of 200,000
dead on both sides" possibly "somewhat inflated."

## Exodus and Resettlement

Refugee flows: "4,351,477" non-Muslims out of West Punjab/NWFP against "4,286,755" Muslims out of East
Punjab; Hindus and Sikhs left "67 lac acres of the very best agricultural land," Muslims "only 47 lac
acres of comparatively poor soil." Singh notes Sikh dignity in adversity ("seldom… was a Sikh man, woman,
or child seen begging in the streets") and the eventual economic rebound — owner-cultivation, tractors,
tube-wells turned East Punjab "from a deficit to a surplus region." The new capital, **Chandigarh**, was
built by Le Corbusier; see [[punjab|Punjab]].

## The Lasting Significance

Singh's thesis: "The most significant effect of the migration was to create Sikh concentrations in
certain districts of east Punjab… [which] revived the demand for a Sikh state." Partition reduced the
Sikhs "from… the most prosperous community… to the level of other Indian communities" and seeded the
post-1947 [[akali-movement|Punjabi Suba campaign]] and beyond.
