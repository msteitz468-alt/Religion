## Identity and Nomenclature

The Sanskrit word *Jaina* derives from *jina*, "conqueror," an epithet given to a line of human teachers who, having overcome the passions and obtained enlightenment, taught the true doctrine of non-violence and subsequently attained freedom from rebirth. "The Jains are at the most basic level those who credit these spiritual conquerors with total authority and act according to their teaching of the Three Jewels, namely, right knowledge, right faith and right conduct" (Dundas, p. 3–4).

It is not clear when the term "Jain" was first employed to designate an adherent of a specific religious path, "although it was probably in use by the early centuries of the common era" (Dundas, p. 4). The older designation found in the ancient scriptures, *niggantha* ("free from bonds"), was employed to describe only members of the ascetic community and the teaching associated with them. The "gradual emergence of a self-aware laity supporting the bondless ascetics… led to 'Jain' eventually becoming current for both the teachings of the religion and those who followed them" (Dundas, p. 4).

Demographically, Jains form a tiny minority within India. The 1991 Census of India recorded approximately 3.35 million Jains out of a total population of just over 838 million (0.41 per cent), with largest concentrations in Gujarat (491,331), Karnataka (326,114), Madhya Pradesh (490,324), Maharashtra (965,840), and Rajasthan (562,806). There is nothing in terms of language or physical appearance which renders Jains distinct from the broader Indian social world in which they live (Dundas, p. 4–5).

## Primary Textual Appearances

The Jain community as a corporate actor appears throughout the [[jain-agamas|Jain scriptural canon]], especially in texts like the [[kalpasutra|Kalpasūtra]], the Ācārāṅga, and the Sūtrakṛtāṅga. These texts describe both the ascetic community and, somewhat less prominently, the lay community (śrāvaka/śrāvikā) that supports it. The oldest texts focus on the ascetic ideal; the laity emerges more clearly in later strata.

## The Question of Jain Identity

The most persistent difficulty in defining the Jains as a distinct group is the contested relationship between [[jainism|Jainism]] and [[hinduism|Hinduism]]. Article 25 of the Indian Constitution states that the terms "Hindu" and "Hinduism" are to be taken to include the Jains, along with Sikhs and Buddhists (Dundas, p. 5). Legal rulings on this question have oscillated:

- An 1847 British judicial statement held that Jains had "nothing or next to nothing in common with brahmanical worship."
- A 1874 ruling argued Jains could not be subject to Hindu law since they fall outside the purview of the shastras.
- The 1921 Privy Council stated the Jains were of Hindu origin and were Hindu dissenters.
- Six years later, the Chief Justice of the Madras High Court insisted scholarly research had shown Jains were not Hindu dissenters.
- After independence, the Hindu Law Committee decreed Jains should be subject to Hindu law for purposes of personal law; confirmed by the High Court of India in 1971. (Dundas, pp. 5–7)

This ambiguity was exacerbated by historical fluidity of identity. The 1921 Census of the Punjab allowed individuals to register as "Jain-Hindus" because "large numbers of Jains and Sikhs were unwilling to be classed separately from Hindus" (Dundas, p. 6). The autobiography of Ganeśprasad Varṇī describes how his Hindu family naturally adopted Jain customs because Jains predominated in their village. Conversely, the prominent Jain merchant house of Jagat Seth returned to Vaiṣṇava Hinduism in the nineteenth century without any dramatic apostasy, "a simply effected reorientation of social and religious preference" (Dundas, p. 5).

Regional variation in self-identification persists today: northern Digambaras may accept the term "Hindu" in an encompassing sense, while Śvetāmbaras in Gujarat and Digambaras in Karnataka would "be unlikely to call themselves anything other than Jain" (Dundas, p. 7). In Rajasthan, many Jain merchants subsume their identity within the broader caste category *mahājan* (applying to both Jains and Hindus), while others insist on their exclusive Jain identity (Dundas, p. 7–8).

Dundas's own position: Jainism is better understood as representing *saṃskṛti* ("culture," "civilisation"), "a specifically Jain mode of life which is independent, coherent and self-contained and yet at times can also intersect with the conceptual world which surrounds it" (Dundas, p. 9). A Jain lay person who occasionally worships a Hindu deity such as Hanuman or Bhairunji does not thereby abandon Jainism but participates "within a South Asian religious world richly populated with figures redolent of power, prosperity and transcendence" (Dundas, p. 8).

## Sect Structure

[[jainism|Jainism]] is internally differentiated. The two major sects are the [[digambara|Digāmbaras]] ("Sky-clad"), whose fully initiated male ascetics renounce clothing, and the [[svetambara|Śvetāmbaras]] ("White-clad"), whose male and female ascetics wear white robes. The Śvetāmbara sect further subdivided in more recent times on the basis of adherence to or rejection of image worship. "All Jain sects and their internal subdivisions have their own histories, traditions and ways of interpreting aspects of Jainism which often provoked intense disagreement amongst themselves" (Dundas, p. 2).

A fundamental social distinction within the community is between the ascetic community (*śramaṇa*) and the lay community. In numerical terms, "to be a Jain today effectively means to be a layman or a laywoman, since the ascetic community is now relatively very small" (Dundas, p. 11). The lay community has throughout Jain history constituted "by far the more substantial proportion of the community" (Dundas, p. 12).

## Western Views and Stereotypes

The stereotype of the Jains in Western scholarship was shaped in part by Daṇḍin's seventh-century Sanskrit novel *Deeds of the Ten Princes* (DKC p. 47), which presents a naked Jain monk named Vīrpaka ("Ugly") who converted through misery and humiliation, and whose reflections frame Jainism as a "heretical path" opposed to brahmanical norms (Dundas, pp. 1–2). Dundas notes this contains a "degree of truth" (Jain ascetics in classical times were enjoined not to wash, and lack of concern for outward appearance was admired as an index of inner purity), but it is an "inadequate and limited" caricature (Dundas, p. 2–3).

Europeans became aware of the Jains from the beginning of the sixteenth century, describing them as *baniyā* ("merchant"), "a term which can be used of Hindu traders also" (Dundas, p. 9). Western travellers were more interested in outward appearances than doctrine; most observations were made in Gujarat (Śvetāmbara territory), so little was said about Digambaras.

German-speaking Indologists provided the first scholarly engagement. Albrecht Weber (1825–1901) performed the "prodigious task of sifting through and analysing the contents of the huge body of Śvetāmbara scriptures" from manuscript material sent to Berlin (Dundas, p. 10). Hermann Jacobi, in a famous 1874 paper, "authoritatively established that Jainism was not merely an offshoot of Buddhism… but an independent religious and intellectual tradition" (Dundas, p. 10).

Negative scholarly attitudes included L. D. Barnett's active dislike of the Jain scriptures and his "denunciation of Jainism in general and what he described as its 'grim ideal' and 'morbid' view of life" (Dundas, p. 11). The missionary Mrs. Sinclair Stevenson's *The Heart of Jainism* (1915) offered "a mixture of accurate and inaccurate information, leavened with a lofty disdain for its subject matter" (Dundas, p. 11). Dundas notes that it "effectively took over sixty years" before P. S. Jainī's *The Jaina Path of Purification* gave English readers "an accurate sense of what Jainism involves" (Dundas, p. 11).

A bias toward ascetic literature has caused Western scholarship to present the "typical Jain" as a monk and Jain practice as "totally orientated towards the gaining of spiritual liberation (*mokṣa*), while the rituals and attitudes of the lay community, which pertain to the this-worldly realm of auspiciousness and well-being… have until very recently been occluded from the scholarly perspective" (Dundas, p. 12).

## Historical and Archaeological Context

Excavation of the funerary monument (*stūpa*) at Mathura and examination of its inscriptional evidence (carried out by the Austrian scholar Bühler in the late nineteenth century) confirmed both that ascetic lineages described in ancient texts had a genuine basis in reality and that Jain ascetics formed a religious community prior to the common era. Since Jain ascetics are not allowed to erect buildings, only a "sizeable and thriving lay community… near to the very beginning of Jainism" could have been responsible for such an undertaking (Dundas, p. 12).
