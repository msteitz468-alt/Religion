# Pharisees

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Pharisee" derives from Hebrew *parush* ("separated one"). The etymology is disputed — possibly referring to separation from impurity, from Gentiles, from other Jews insufficiently observant of the law, or from those who compromised with the Hasmonean establishment. The Pharisees were one of the major Jewish religious parties of the Second Temple period, alongside the Sadducees, Essenes (associated with Qumran), and later the Zealots.

In the NT, "Pharisee" often functions as an epithet for Jewish opposition to Jesus and later to Paul. This usage is historically complex and requires careful handling — see **Controversies** below.

## Primary Textual Appearances

### Gospel Texts
In the Synoptic Gospels, the Pharisees are the most frequent Jewish interlocutors with Jesus:
- **Matthew**: Extended confrontations over Sabbath (12:1-14), purity (15:1-9), divorce (19:3-9), taxes (22:15-22), the resurrection (22:23-33), and the extended "Woes" discourse (23:1-39).
- **Mark**: Similar pattern; conflict over Sabbath healing (3:1-6), hand-washing (7:1-23), divorce (10:2-12), the Beelzebul controversy (3:22).
- **Luke**: Also portrays Pharisees dining with Jesus (7:36-50; 11:37-52; 14:1-24), suggesting a more complex relationship.
- **John**: Pharisees appear less prominently; often linked with "chief priests" as opponents.

### Pauline Texts
Paul identifies himself as a former Pharisee, providing rare first-person insider evidence:
- **Philippians 3:5-6**: "as to the law, a Pharisee; as to zeal, a persecutor of the church; as to righteousness under the law, blameless."
- **Galatians 1:13-14**: His "earlier life in Judaism," his "extreme zeal for the traditions of my ancestors."
- **Acts 22:3; 26:5**: Paul identifies himself as trained under Gamaliel, a Pharisee (historical accuracy debated).

### Historical Sources (Josephus)
Josephus (*Jewish Antiquities*, *Jewish War*) describes the Pharisees as one of three main Jewish "philosophies" (alongside Sadducees and Essenes). He portrays them as having influence with the people, accepting both written Torah and oral traditions (*paradosis*), and believing in resurrection. His depictions are complex and somewhat favorable.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### Jewish Portrait
The Pharisees are the direct ancestors of Rabbinic Judaism. After 70 AD, when the Temple was destroyed and both the priestly Sadducean establishment and the Essene community at Qumran ceased to exist, the Pharisaic tradition — with its emphasis on Torah study, oral tradition, and synagogue-centered practice — proved the most adaptable form of Judaism for life without the Temple. The rabbis who compiled the Mishna (c. 200 AD) stood in continuity with the Pharisaic tradition, though the relationship is not one of simple identity.

Key Pharisaic emphases:
- The authority of *oral Torah* alongside written Torah
- The synagogue as a locus of worship and study (not just the Temple)
- Resurrection of the dead (a point of contrast with the Sadducees)
- Scrupulous observance of purity laws and Sabbath regulations
- The democratization of holiness — not just priests but all Israel could and should observe rigorous purity

### Christian Portrait (NT and Patristic)
The NT Gospels often present Pharisees as hypocrites, focused on external observance at the expense of internal piety (Matt 23 "Woes"), and as the primary antagonists of Jesus. This portrait served the early church's self-definition vis-à-vis Judaism and has been enormously influential in Western culture, where "Pharisee" became a byword for hypocritical legalism.

Post-NT patristic literature deepened this polemic. The irony is that early Christianity and Pharisaic Judaism shared a number of features: both emphasized Scripture study, oral tradition (apostolic and rabbinic respectively), community discipline, and eschatological hope including resurrection.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

### Identity
The Pharisees' origins are placed in the 2nd century BC, possibly in response to the Maccabean crisis (c. 167-164 BC) and the Hasmonean monarchy that followed. Unlike the Sadducees (who were largely priestly aristocracy) and the Essenes (a separatist sect), the Pharisees were a movement of laypeople committed to extending the priestly holiness code to all of Israel's daily life.

Their actual numbers at any given time were probably not large — Josephus gives a figure of about 6,000, though this is disputed. Their influence, however, was disproportionate to their numbers, particularly with the common people who respected their piety and knowledge of the law.

### The Oral Torah
A defining Pharisaic commitment was the authority of oral legal tradition (*paradosis*, "that which is handed down") alongside written Scripture. The Sadducees rejected this, accepting only the Pentateuch as authoritative. The Pharisees held that the oral tradition was also given at Sinai and constituted a fence around the written Torah. This principle became the foundation of Rabbinic Judaism's entire edifice.

## Unterman's Portrait (Internal Jewish Perspective)

[[unterman-talmud|Unterman]] (Ch. IX) offers a detailed internal Jewish characterization
contrasting the three parties as socio-political as well as theological factions:

### The Pharisees (Unterman)

The Pharisees were the **democratic party of common people and Levites** — not priests or
aristocrats. Their theology directly challenged Sadducean privilege:

- **Oral Torah**: The Pharisees held that an Oral Torah was given alongside the Written Torah
  at Sinai, and that this living, interpretive tradition was authoritative. This democratized
  Torah study — anyone could be a scholar, not just the priestly class.
- **Free will AND divine providence**: Unlike the Sadducees (no providence) or the Essenes
  (deterministic predestination), the Pharisees maintained both: man has free will AND God
  guides history. A difficult theological balance, but one that made the Pharisaic tradition
  intellectually dynamic.
- **Reward and punishment; resurrection**: The Pharisees affirmed reward/punishment in the
  afterlife and bodily resurrection — both denied by the Sadducees.
- **Progressive methodology**: Hillel's seven hermeneutical rules; case-by-case legal
  reasoning; willingness to adapt the law to new circumstances (e.g. Hillel's *Prosbul*).
- **Socio-political dimension**: The Pharisees arose partly in response to the aristocratic,
  priestly monopoly on religious authority. Their synagogue-centered practice placed Torah
  scholarship above Temple privilege.

### Contrast: Sadducees and Essenes (Unterman)

**Sadducees**: Aristocratic, priestly, wealthy. Accepted only the Written Torah (Pentateuch)
— no Oral Torah, no resurrection, no divine providence in daily affairs. Their power base
depended on the Temple and the Roman alliance. When the Temple fell (70 CE), they vanished.

**Essenes**: Ascetic, communal, pacifist. Withdrew from public life into desert communities
(identified by Unterman as the authors of the Dead Sea Scrolls). Vegetarian. Held property
in common. Contemplative, deterministic.

Unterman's class analysis: The Pharisee/Sadducee split maps onto a democratic/aristocratic
political divide. The Pharisees' survival and the emergence of Rabbinic Judaism is partly
explained by their non-Temple, non-priestly institutional base (the synagogue and the
yeshiva).

## Hurtado's Specific Contribution

[[larry-hurtado|Hurtado]] (*Destroyer of the Gods*) uses the Pharisees primarily in two ways:

**1. Paul's pre-conversion stance**: Hurtado argues that Paul's self-identification as a zealous Pharisee (Phil 3:6) and his use of "zeal" in describing his persecution of the church places him within the **Phinehas tradition** — a strand of Jewish thought that justified vigilante violence against fellow Jews guilty of publicly shaming God's covenant (Num 25:1-15; 1 Macc 2:26; Philo *On the Special Laws* 3). Paul likely saw the early Jesus-movement's extraordinarily elevated devotion to the crucified Jesus as a blasphemous violation of Jewish monotheism — not merely a legal deviation.

**2. The Pharisees as a religious party, not a sect**: Hurtado notes that the Pharisees were a Jewish *party* (a subset of Jews advocating rigorous observance) but not an *exclusivist sect* in the manner of the Qumran community. They wished all Israel would observe the law more scrupulously, but they did not typically claim to be the only true elect or regard non-Pharisees as outside the covenant. This distinguishes them from the early Jesus-movement, which did make exclusive claims (Jesus is the one Lord of all, both Jews and Gentiles must confess him). The Jesus-movement resembled sectarian Judaism (like Qumran) in its exclusivism more than it resembled the Pharisaic reform party — a significant element of Hurtado's argument that the Jesus-movement was genuinely new within Judaism.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Pharisees function in the NT narrative as the primary representatives of Jewish religious authority and scrupulous observance against which Jesus' teaching and person is contrasted. They appear as serious, sincere religious people (the contrast with Sadducees in Acts 23 is revealing) but as ones who have missed the heart of the law and are now missing the Messiah.

For Paul, his Pharisaic past is both the background for his pre-conversion opposition to Christianity and the theological reservoir from which he draws his understanding of the law, the prophets, resurrection, and eschatology. His new perspective on the law in light of Christ is worked out against this Pharisaic formation.

## Controversies

### "Pharisee" as Epithet
Modern scholarship has extensively debated the NT's portrait of the Pharisees. The consensus view holds that the Gospel portrayals are shaped by post-70 polemical context (the period when Pharisaic/Rabbinic Judaism and nascent Christianity were competing as successor communities after the Temple's destruction) and should not be read as straightforward historical reporting about 30s-40s AD Pharisaism. The historical Pharisees were probably more diverse and more positively regarded by the Jewish population than the NT portrait suggests. Christian use of "Pharisee" as an insult throughout history is considered by scholars an instance of problematic anti-Jewish polemic.

### Paul and Torah
The question of how Paul's Pharisaic background relates to his NT theology — and whether he "left" Judaism or remained within it in a transformed mode — is central to the [[new-perspective-on-paul|New Perspective on Paul]].

## France on the Pharisees in Matthew (NICNT Matthew, 2007)

*Added 2026-07-18. All interpretive claims in this section are [[rt-france|France]]'s own
unless otherwise attributed.*

### Scribes and Pharisees are distinct categories

France distinguishes the terms that Matthew habitually pairs: scribes were professional students
and teachers of halakhah; "Pharisee" named a voluntary reformist movement devoted to meticulous
law-practice (purity, tithing, sabbath). "**The two terms thus represent distinct categories**,"
though in practice they overlapped heavily.

### The surpassing righteousness of 5:20 — a different concept, not a higher score

France's most load-bearing claim about the Pharisees in Matthew, and one that cuts against the
common reading of 5:20 as a demand to out-perform them:

> "Jesus is not talking about beating the scribes and Pharisees at their own game, but about **a
> different level or concept of righteousness altogether**."

The demand, he argues, "makes sense only if their basic premise as to what 'righteousness'
consists of is put in question." He states the programmatic thesis for his whole reading of
ch. 5: "The essence of life in the kingdom of heaven is in fact the antithesis of a legalistic
code, as 5:20 will state and 5:21-48 will repeatedly illustrate." The commandments function
"not as simple rules of conduct but as pointers to a 'greater righteousness' which Jesus has
brought into being and which supersedes the old type of lawkeeping," and Jesus' illustrations
"are not to be treated as if they were a new set of literal regulations to replace those of the
scribes and Pharisees."

Notably, **France generalizes the critique beyond Judaism**: the greater righteousness is in a
different league from that of the scribes and Pharisees "and of any other religious traditions
which understand the will of God in terms of the punctilious observance of rules." He also
allows that the charge against Jesus was not manufactured: "it is not unlikely that there were
in fact some who did suppose that Jesus was against the law," on the evidence of the sabbath
disputes (12:1-14) and the persistence of the charge against his followers (Acts 6:11, 13-14;
21:28).

### Purity as the centre of Pharisaic ideology

On the purity dispute (15:1-20) France states that "**it is hard to exaggerate the significance
of ritual purity for the Pharisaic ideology**," which most scribes supported and which became
the basis of later rabbinic orthodoxy; a large proportion of the halakhic tradition later
codified in the Mishnah focuses on it, especially the division *Ṭeharot*. He establishes that
handwashing itself was not an OT legal requirement for ordinary Jews — "the only regular ritual
handwashing required in the OT law is that of the priests before undertaking their cultic
duties" (Exod 30:18-21; 40:30-32) or when eating sacrificial food (Lev 22:4-7).

He notes the purity question had already been latent throughout the ministry — Jesus touching a
leper, contacting Gentiles, visiting pig-keeping territory, being touched by a woman with a
menstrual disorder, touching a corpse, eating with tax collectors and sinners. And he draws out
the social consequence: because the purity of God's people separated them from all others, "the
food laws thus became a barrier to social intercourse between Jew and Gentile" — the cause of
the long, painful early-church dispute (Acts 10:9-48).

France marks 15:1-20 as the **climactic** episode of the Galilean public ministry, both because
the opponents are a Jerusalem delegation and because Jesus' pronouncement "in effect undermines
a significant principle of the Mosaic law itself." After it, on his reading, the breach with the
scribal establishment is irreparable. Against the common suggestion that Matthew draws back from
Mark here, France holds that Matthew records the key pronouncement (v. 11) and its commentary
(vv. 18-19) "**no less explicitly than Mark**," and that Matthew "can hardly have been unaware
of the radical significance of the principle that defilement comes from inside, not from
outside."

### Sabbath: restoring rest, not abolishing law

France frames Jesus' sabbath arguments as attempting to **restore** the "rest" that scribal
elaboration "was in danger of becoming … more a burden than a blessing" — bridging directly from
11:28-30, whose easy yoke sets up the sabbath conflicts of 12:1-14 as "a striking example" of
two contrasting ways of understanding the rest the sabbath was meant to provide.

On 12:6 he stresses the shock: "It is hard to overestimate the shock value of this
pronouncement." The temple was the symbol of nationhood and its priestly establishment the
nearest thing to Israel's own government; to threaten it "was to commit unpardonable treason."

### Beelzebul and the unforgivable sin

France observes that neither accusation directly calls Jesus Beelzebul, "but we are dealing here
with crude invective, not with sophisticated theological reasoning. Cf. 16:23, where Jesus
actually calls [[peter-apostle|Peter]] 'Satan'!" He holds that the leaders' failure to recognize
the new phase leaves them "perilously close to that fundamental rebellion against God which
cannot be forgiven," and makes the contextual limit explicit: "The opening 'therefore' indicates
that in this context blasphemy against the Holy Spirit … is to be understood in terms of the
Pharisees' charge in v. 24." He locates the unforgivability precisely in "this diametrical
opposition to the good purpose of God" — "to 'call evil good and good evil' (Isa 5:20)" — and
argues that whereas one might speak against the Son of Man in ignorance, "the significance of
Jesus' exorcisms was plain for all to see (v. 28); there could be no excuse for misinterpreting
this work of the Holy Spirit and attributing it to Beelzebul."

France attaches an unusual **pastoral qualification**: the saying "has often been
inappropriately, and sometimes disastrously, applied to contexts which have little to do with its
original setting," such that "serious pastoral damage has thus been caused." His formula: "This
saying is a wake-up call to the arrogant, not a bogey to frighten those of tender conscience."

### The sign demand and the leaven (16:1-12)

France marks 16:1-4 as Jesus' last meeting with Galilean opponents and "the end of the mission
to Galilee, until it will be relaunched dramatically in the Galilean hills by the risen Jesus"
(28:16-20). He flags the contrast between this sceptical demand for a sign and "the enthusiastic
response of the Gentile crowd to the 'signs' in 15:29-31," and reads the closing "he left them
and went away" as more than an editorial transition — "his abandoning them and going away marks
a decisive break," comparable to the departure from the temple at 24:1. He treats 16:2b-3 (the
weather signs) as textually uncertain and discusses it only conditionally.

On the pairing "**Pharisees and Sadducees**" — found only in Matthew, here and at 3:7 — France
calls it a surprising combination, probably a "cross-party delegation." Since the
[[sadducees|Sadducees]]' power base was Jerusalem, he takes the group to represent Jerusalem
establishment interest rather than local Galilean opposition. Given the sharp ideological
differences between the two parties, he judges "the most likely matter on which their 'teaching'
might have been at one would be in their common rejection of Jesus" — comparing the equally
ill-matched "chief priests and scribes."

On the leaven (16:5-12), France notes Mark has "the leaven of Herod," which Matthew replaces
with "the Pharisees and Sadducees," linking the warning to the sign-demand. Leaven here, in
contrast to 13:33, "is clearly a symbol for the pervasiveness of something bad" (cf. 1 Cor
5:6-8; Gal 5:9), imagery derived from removing leaven before Passover. He cites [REPORTED:
Keener] (422) for describing the opponents' stance as "**toxic cynicism**" — ideological
opposition that must be resisted lest it influence others. France is candid that the pericope
resists resolution: "The whole pericope remains at the level of a parable without an
explanation!"

### Incidental note

France classifies Antipas's belief that Jesus is the risen [[john-the-baptist|John]] as "popular
superstition rather than a worked-out theology of resurrection **such as that of the
Pharisees**" — an incidental confirmation that he treats Pharisaic resurrection belief as the
serious, articulated position of the period.

## France on the Pharisees in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], [[france-mark-nigtc|*The Gospel
of Mark* (NIGTC)]]. All interpretive claims are France's unless another scholar is named.*

### Genuine core opposition — against revisionist harmonisation

France argues that all four gospels agree the Pharisees were **the core opposition during
Jesus' active ministry**, and that portraying Jesus as an inoffensive orthodox Pharisee "must
thus depend on a wholesale rejection of the gospel accounts"; he sees continuity with later
rabbinic references to Jesus as a *mesith* (enticer) as supporting the historical probability of
the confrontation. At the same time he insists Christian tradition has "unjustifiably made
'Pharisee' into a pejorative term, in defiance of the high regard in which Pharisees were
clearly held in Jewish circles at the time" — "It is not necessary, in order to rebut that
calumny, to pretend that therefore Jesus saw eye to eye with the Pharisees." This position sits
alongside (and in tension with) the post-70-polemic consensus recorded under **Controversies**
above; both registers are preserved.

### The Marcan conflict cycle (Mark 2:1–3:6; 7:1-23)

- On the alienation itself: it "derives directly from his own deliberately chosen stance," not
  from misunderstanding — "a fundamental incompatibility between his agenda and theirs."
  Engaging E. P. Sanders, France rejects the thesis that Jesus' offence was summoning sinners
  without repentance; the offence lay in "the breach of social and religious convention into
  which that mission led Jesus."
- On the sabbath disputes, Jesus "did not debate, but simply brushed aside the whole complex of
  sabbath prohibitions with sweeping generalisations," asserting a halakhic authority of his
  own while rejecting neither the sabbath law nor its rest principle; his positive principle
  (2:27; 3:4) "leaves no scope for the rabbinic enterprise of building a fence around the
  sabbath law."
- The **Pharisee–Herodian coalition of 3:6** is unsurprising once a modern religion/politics
  separation is discarded (France notes the plausible suggestion that the Herodians were the
  Boethusians), and 3:6 records agreement in principle — not yet a formulated strategy — that
  Jesus must be silenced.
- On 7:1-23 France questions "the automatic assumption by the Pharisees and scribes that there
  is authority inherent in tradition as such," and (endnote) reports Booth's proposal that the
  Pharisees of 7:2 were *ḥaberim* practising supererogatory hand washing, with Sanders placing
  Pharisaic purity aspiration "above the ordinary, but below that of priests… well below that
  of the Qumran sect."
- The refusal of a sign (8:11-13) France reads as deliberate **abandonment** of the Pharisees
  and the termination of the public Galilean ministry; the "yeast of the Pharisees and of
  Herod" (8:15) is focused on those who "constitute a real threat to Jesus' life" (the
  3:6/12:13 alliance) rather than on any specific connotation of the yeast metaphor.

### Jerusalem controversies

- In the tribute question (12:13-17) the Pharisees–Herodians coalition returns as a trap; Jesus'
  answer, read against Judas of Galilee's theology (via Josephus), "is an answer no Zealot
  could have given," and the request for a denarius wrong-footed the questioners, who — not
  Jesus — were carrying the "idolatrous" imperial coin.
- In the Sadducees' resurrection challenge (12:18-27) France notes Jesus' support for the
  "Pharisaic" view of resurrection is "unequivocal" — the only pericope where Jesus directly
  addresses the afterlife (see [[sadducees|Sadducees]]).
- In the passion predictions and hearings France observes the opposition shifts: the
  elders–chief priests–scribes list (8:31; 11:27; 14:43, 53; 15:1) names the three Sanhedrin
  power groups — "a comprehensive rejection of Jesus by all the leading representatives of
  God's people Israel" — with the Pharisees as such largely absent from the Jerusalem trial
  narrative.
