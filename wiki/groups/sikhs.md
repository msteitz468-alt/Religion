# Sikhs (the Panth)

The community of [[sikhism|Sikhism]] — the **Panth** (lit. "path / road," the body of those who
follow the Gurus' way) — treated here as a social and historical collective. A *Sikh* is a "learner /
disciple" (Skt. *śiṣya*). The community began as the **[[guru-nanak|Nanak]]-panth** and was
reconstituted as the **[[khalsa|Khalsa]] Panth** in 1699. (For the Khalsa as a religious *system* —
its canon, Rahit, and hermeneutics — see the [[khalsa|Khalsa sect page]]; this page treats the Sikhs
as a people.)

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Chs 2, 10–13.)*

## Identity and Nomenclature

A *Sikh* is a "learner / disciple." The community began as the **Nanak-panth** — in its own day "one
panth among many," with no festivals or practices distinguishing it from other Punjabis — and was
reconstituted as the **[[khalsa|Khalsa]] Panth** in 1699. McLeod's taxonomy of who counts (Amrit-dhari
/ Kes-dhari / Sahaj-dhari / Patit, plus the pejorative *Mona* Sikh for a shorn Sikh of Khalsa
background) is the substance of the **[[who-is-a-sikh|"Who is a Sikh?"]]** question.

## Historical and Social Profile

**Caste.** Doctrinally the Gurus rejected the vertical caste hierarchy, but the Panth "implicitly
recognizes" horizontal (marriage) caste linkages — "every Sikh knows the caste into which he or she was
born." The dominant constituency is the **[[jats|Jats]]** (>60% — rural landowners, numerically and
politically dominant though low in the classical varṇa order); the Gurus themselves were all **Khatris**
(the urban mercantile caste, 2–3%); other *zats* include Aroras, the distinctively Sikh artisan
**Ramgarhias**, the small hawker-caste **Bhatras**, and the Outcaste Mazhabi/Ramdasia/Chamar groups
(agents 5–6).

**Diaspora.** Of ~16 million Sikhs, roughly a million live outside India; the UK hosts the largest
overseas community, with substantial populations in Canada, the USA, East Africa, and Southeast Asia.
The migration heartland is the **eastern Doaba** (between the Beas and Satluj). McLeod argues migrants
were not the poorest but an intermediate stratum pressed by a falling water-table and the **Jat custom
of dividing land equally among sons**; the overriding motive was *izzat* (family honour). The
**Komagata Maru** affair (Vancouver, 1914) remains a defining memory. The overseas
[[gurdwara|gurdwara]], with its weekly [[langar|langar]], anchors community life.

**Gender.** Sikh teaching affirms women's full spiritual and liturgical equality — a woman may read
the Guru Granth Sahib publicly, receive initiation, and serve among the Pañj Piāre; Guru Nanak's
shalok asks "why should she be called weak?" Yet "all the Gurus were men," and McLeod judges that
their gift was *equal access to liberation*, not social equality — patriarchy "deprived of its
domineering aspects" but "still intact," reinforced by Jat land inheritance. See
[[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]] for the doctrine-vs-practice tension.

## The Panth as a Political People (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 1)

Where McLeod profiles the Panth sociologically, Khushwant Singh's *History of the Sikhs* narrates its
transformation into a corporate political and military actor. The persecution after [[banda-bahadur|Banda
Bahadur]] "uproot[ed] a large number of peasants from the land and convert[ed] them into professional
soldiers"; the Panth governed itself through the **[[misls|Sarbat Khalsa]]** and fought as the Dal Khalsa,
so that "the rise of militant Sikhism became the rise of **Jat** power in the Punjab." Singh credits the
[[misls|misls]] with "the resurrection of the spirit of Punjabi nationalism" and with winning over the
Muslim peasantry — a base that [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] consolidated into the multi-confessional
**Sarkar Khalsa** state. He also notes a doctrinal cost: under the misls, mass Hindu conversion to Sikhism
re-introduced caste and Brahmin influence into the Sikh aristocracy, against the Gurus' intent.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

After 1708 the Panth is itself a locus of the Guru's authority — the **Guru Panth** alongside the
**Guru Granth** — though whether "Panth" means the whole community or specifically the
[[khalsa|Khalsa]] has "caused considerable debate" (agent 1 ~L344–354). The three bonds McLeod names as
holding the Panth together are faith in the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]], the gurdwara, and
the [[rahit-maryada|Rahit]].

## The Panth in the Modern Era (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 2)

Khushwant Singh's Vol. 2 narrates the Panth's nineteenth- and twentieth-century transformation. After the
[[annexation-of-the-punjab|British annexation]], he argues, the heavily-Hinduized community faced
absorption (Dalhousie predicted Sikh "disappearance"), reversed by British martial-race recruitment and
the [[singh-sabha|Singh Sabha]]'s redefinition of Sikhs as a distinct community — crystallized in Kahan
Singh's *Ham Hindu Nahin Hain* (see [[who-is-a-sikh|"Who is a Sikh?"]]). The **Land Alienation Act
(1900)**, Singh notes, split the community along caste lines so that "race came to matter more than
religion." Sikh emigration met racial discrimination abroad, breeding the **Ghadr** movement (which Singh
calls "the first secular movement" for Indian liberation, Sikh in rank-and-file but not religious in aim)
and a later turn to Marxism (the Kirti Kisan party). The [[akali-movement|Akali movement]] then seized the
shrines, and [[partition-of-punjab-1947|Partition]] reduced the Sikhs "from… the most prosperous
community… to the level of other Indian communities," even as leadership passed from Khatri traders to
land-based **Jat Sikhs** of Malwa.

Singh's 2004 balance-sheet: ~18 million Sikhs, 80% in Punjab; the lowest poverty rate of any community
(6.16% vs. 26.10% national); and two Sikhs (Manmohan Singh as PM, Montek Singh Ahluwalia) at the helm of
India's government — "a notional fulfilment of the prophecy *Raj Karega Khalsa*." Yet his "Summing Up" is
unsparingly self-critical of the community's leadership decline and "display of religiosity," ending on
the bleak suggestion that "perhaps the Khalsa have run the course of history prescribed for them."
