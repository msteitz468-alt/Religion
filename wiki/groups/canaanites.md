# Canaanites

## Identity and Nomenclature

In the second millennium BCE "Canaan" most narrowly designated western Palestine
(west of the Jordan), with a northern boundary fluctuating in Lebanon. Modern
scholars use **Canaanite** for the wider cultural continuum spanning western
Palestine, most of Lebanon, and coastal Syria as far north as [[ugarit]] — a region
that its ancient inhabitants never treated as a political or cultural unit (Pitard,
Coogan Ch. 1). The same culture, on the coast, is what the Greeks later called
**Phoenician** (see [[phoenicians]]).

Crucially, Coogan's volume stresses that **early Israel was itself a subset of
Canaanite culture** — sharing language (Hebrew is a Canaanite dialect), material
culture, dress, and coiffure — rather than an alien intrusion. The biblical
opposition "Israelite vs. Canaanite" is in significant part a *retrospective*
self-definition. See [[israelites]].

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Genesis–Joshua**: Canaan as the land promised to [[abraham]] and conquered under
  [[joshua]]; the patriarchs are pastoralists camped among Canaanite towns.
- **The Amarna letters** (14th c. BCE): a "mosaic" of squabbling Canaanite
  city-states — [[jerusalem]] (Abdi-Hepa), Gezer, [[shechem]] (Labayu), [[megiddo]] —
  pleading with the pharaoh against rivals and the [[apiru-habiru]]. Coogan notes this
  Canaan "corresponds well" to the land of Genesis, Numbers, Joshua, and Judges.
- **The conquest narratives** (Joshua 6–12): thirty-one cities "taken"; see
  [[herem-canaanite-conquest]] and [[jericho]] for the archaeological tension.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Israelite/Jewish**: the Canaanites are the paradigm of the doomed, idolatrous
  inhabitant — vehicle of the ḥerem (devotion to destruction) and of the warning
  against Canaanite religion (Exod. 34.13; Deut. 7.5; 12.2–4). Yet the same texts
  preserve deep Canaanite inheritance (the god El; covenant forms; poetic style).
- **Christian**: typological — Canaan as "the world" or "the flesh" to be conquered;
  Rahab the Canaanite as a type of faith/the Gentile church.
- **Critical scholarship**: rejects the "outside invader" model; the Canaanites are
  largely the *ancestors* of Israel, not merely its victims.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

Canaanite urban culture took shape in the Middle Bronze Age (2000–1550 BCE), with
massive rampart-fortified towns — [[hazor]] the largest (72 ha), larger than
[[ugarit|Ebla]]. Most towns were under 20 ha. Around 1700 BCE someone in Canaan
invented the **alphabet** (the ancestor of the Phoenician and all Western alphabets)
— "one of the most important innovations in the history of civilization" (Pitard).
Canaan lay under Egyptian imperial control through the Late Bronze Age (see
[[egypt-and-the-nile-delta|Egypt]] context in [[hyksos]] and [[sea-peoples]]). The
Late Bronze collapse (c. 1200 BCE) eclipsed classical Canaanite civilization on the
plains and opened the highlands to the new polities of Israel, Moab, Ammon, and Edom.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Canaanites function as the **corporate foil** against whom Israel's covenant
identity is defined: the land must be cleared, the gods refused, the customs avoided.
But the religion uncovered at [[ugarit]] shows how much Israel *shared* — El as
creator and king of the gods (the patriarchs' deity; cf. El Shadday, El Elyon, El
Olam, and the name Yisra-**el**), the storm-god imagery later transferred to Yahweh
(see [[divine-name]]), and the Baal/Yamm combat myth echoed in Israel's traditions
(Ps. 74). Baal becomes Yahweh's chief first-millennium rival.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The "Canaanite conquest" is among the most ethically contested biblical traditions —
the command to exterminate whole peoples. Recorded at [[herem-canaanite-conquest]].
"Canaanite" has also been redeployed in later religious-political discourse (conquest
ethics, settler theology, "Amalek" rhetoric). The volume's claim that Israel
*emerged from* Canaan rather than annihilating it reframes the whole dispute.
