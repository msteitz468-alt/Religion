# Persians (Achaemenid Empire)

## Identity and Nomenclature

The **Persians** were an Indo-European people of **Parsa** (Persis/Fars), vassals of
the Medes until **Cyrus II ("the Great")** overthrew Astyages (550 BCE) and built the
**Achaemenid Empire**. Cyrus's capture of **[[babylon|Babylon]] (539 BCE)** brought
Syria-Palestine into the empire as part of the fifth satrapy, **Abar Nahara**
("Across the [Euphrates] River"). The period (539–332 BCE) ends with **Alexander the
Great** (Leith, Coogan Ch. 8).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Isaiah 40–55 (Second Isaiah)**: astonishingly hails **Cyrus as Yahweh's "messiah"**
  (Isa. 45.1) — the only non-Jew so titled — and as the instrument who will free the
  exiles and rebuild Jerusalem.
- **Ezra-Nehemiah / 2 Chronicles 36**: the **decree of Cyrus** (538) authorizing the
  return and Temple rebuilding; **Darius I** confirms it; later kings sponsor **Ezra**
  and **Nehemiah** (mid-5th c.). The official Persian documents are quoted in **Aramaic**
  (Ezra 4–6; 7).
- **Haggai/Zechariah**: the Temple rebuilt under **Zerubbabel** and the high priest
  **Joshua**, completed c. 516/515 BCE, in the reign of Darius I.
- **Esther / Daniel**: stories set in the Persian court (Ahasuerus/Xerxes; "the laws of
  the Medes and Persians").

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Israelite/Jewish**: the **benevolent liberator** — Cyrus the anointed deliverer,
  Persia the sponsor of restoration and (eventually) of the Torah's authority. Yet
  Coogan's volume cautions that this "tolerant Persian" image is itself **Persian
  propaganda** (the Cyrus Cylinder, the Verse Account of Nabonidus). Cyrus appeals to
  *Marduk* in Babylon, to *Yahweh* in Jerusalem, to be *pharaoh* in Egypt — a calculated
  imperial strategy of co-opting local religion. Persian "tolerance" was **enlightened
  self-interest**, and temples now had to **pay taxes** to the crown.

## Historical and Administrative Context

**Darius I** systematized the empire into satrapies, introduced imperial coinage, a
postal system, and the royal roads, and built **Persepolis**. **Yehud** (Judah) was a
small, poor, sparsely populated subprovince — its population fell to perhaps **~10,850
in Persian I**, [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] holding only a few hundred — governed by a
series of governors (Sheshbazzar, Zerubbabel, Nehemiah) under a high priest. Cyrus's
son **Cambyses** conquered Egypt (525). On the wider Persian world the Bible is largely
silent after Nehemiah.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Persian period is, per Coogan Ch. 8, when **"Israel" is redefined** — no longer a
geopolitical nation but a **people identified by ethnicity and religion**
(circumcision, Sabbath, Passover, Temple, endogamy), the matrix of Second Temple
Judaism. The canon (Torah, Prophets, Psalms) approaches its final shape; Ezra reads the
Law aloud at Jerusalem's gates. The roots of later Jewish sectarianism — including the
[[samaritans|Samaritans]] — lie here.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

Cyrus-as-messiah became a major theme in Jewish and later Christian readings of divine
providence working through pagan rulers. The **Ezra/Nehemiah marriage reforms** and the
exclusion of "the peoples of the land" feed the controversy over **[[intermarriage-ezra-nehemiah|
intermarriage in Ezra-Nehemiah]]** and the boundaries of "true Israel." Related:
[[babylonians]], [[samaritans]], [[edomites]], [[phoenicians]], [[ezra-nehemiah]],
[[esther]], [[babylon]], [[jerusalem]].
