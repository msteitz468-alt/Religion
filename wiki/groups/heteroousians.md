## Identity and Nomenclature

Ayres deliberately uses the term **"Heteroousian"** rather than the more familiar "Anomoean" or "neo-Arian": "I have thus opted to use the term 'Heterousian' because of its precision in indicating exactly where they saw the key difference between Father and Son". The term "Anomoians" (those who teach the 'unlikeness' of Father and Son) "was coined by their opponents and both [Aetius and Eunomius] were keen to defend themselves against it, insisting that their concern was to teach 'unlikeness *according to essence*': there were many other ways in which Father and Son were alike".

On the "neo-Arian" label, Ayres is equally direct: "The habit is still widespread of calling this movement 'neo-Arian'. There are, however, significant differences between Arius' theology and that of Aetius and Eunomius, and neither ever appears to have made any claim on Arius' legacy". Their main opponents, the Cappadocians, "do not engage in an 'Athanasian'-style attempt to cast Aetius and Eunomius as new Ariuses". The "Eunomian" label (also used in Ayres) reflects the later dominance of [[eunomius|Eunomius]] in giving the movement institutional form. See [[arianism]] for Ayres's general caution about applying Arian genealogies.

The theological slogan of the Heteroousians: the Father and Son are **unlike in essence** (*heteroousios*), with the Son generated by the Father's *will* rather than from the Father's *essence*.

## Key Proponents

- **[[aetius|Aetius of Antioch]]**: the theological architect; his *Syntagmation* (preserved at Epiphanius, *Panarion* 76.11.1ff) is the foundational text. Educated by supporters of Arius in the 320s; known for dense syllogistic argument; active in Antioch under Eudoxius from 357; exiled by Constantius in 358.
- **[[eunomius|Eunomius of Cyzicus]]** (younger than Aetius; secretary to Aetius from c.355; bishop of Cyzicus 360, d. 394/395 at Dakora near Cappadocian Caesarea): author of the *Apology* and the main literary voice of the movement; developed Aetius's positions on *ingeneracy*, names, and the causal hierarchy of essence–activity–product.

"These two were prominent for Heterousians in a way that no individual figures were for Homoians".

## Theological Position

The central claim is that God's defining attribute is **ingeneracy** (*to agennēton*): "the essence of God lies in being ingenerate... which also involves not consisting in a compound essence". Key logical moves:

1. **God is simple and uncomposite**: an uncomposite essence cannot generate from itself, since any generation from essence would require the generator to be compound.
2. **Therefore the Son is generated by will, not essence**: "God, not being compound, cannot generate in this way, but only by God's will or authority. The Son is thus the product of God's will" (Aetius, *Synt.* 5â7).
3. **The Son is unlike the Father in essence**: since the Son is generated (and thus *not* ingenerate), his essence differs fundamentally from the Father's.
4. **Names reveal essence by nature, not convention** (Eunomius): names are given by God, not by human convention; "ingenerate" therefore provides genuine knowledge of God's essence. Ayres notes this "served the function of allowing him to critique those who emphasized the incomprehensibility of the divine nature".
5. **Essence–activity–product hierarchy** (Eunomius): "We can schematize [the relationships] as essence—activity—product. In this sequence Eunomius is at pains to argue that activity is not coterminus with essence: an activity is distinct from an essence and is temporary". The Son is a product of the Father's will and is "the image of the Father's will". The Spirit is similarly a product of the divine will, "created through the Son and inferior to the Son".
6. **The Son is distinguished from creation** despite being its maker: Eunomius "is concerned to show that the Son is distinct from the creation we inhabit: the Son is a product unlike other products and stands in the relationship of maker to all other things".
7. **Soteriological stake**: Ayres follows Vaggione and Wiles in noting a soteriological motivation — "ingenerate" and associated terms provide "a dependable knowledge on which we can base our discussion of God", which may itself be necessary for salvation.

Ayres notes a development from the Homoian position: "Although [Homoians] also would have spoken of the Father as the only ungenerate, the term had a philosophical significance and range of implication for Eunomius that were very much his and Aetius'".

## Relation to the Other Trajectories

- **vs. Homoians**: The Heteroousians were initially housed within the broad Homoian alliance; Eudoxius supported Aetius before breaking with him. Their emergence "was a significant factor in prompting some to reject [the Homoian alliance]"; George of Laodicea describes their position as "like in will, unlike in essence" and charges it is logically inconsistent with signing a creed affirming "like the Father in all things".
- **vs. Homoiousians**: The 358 council at Ancyra was directly "prompted by the teaching of Aetius in Antioch". The [[homoiousians|Homoiousians]] explicitly targeted the Heteroousian extreme.
- **vs. pro-Nicenes**: The Cappadocians wrote their major refutations against Eunomius specifically. Ayres notes that the emergence of clearly anti-Nicene accounts in the 350s helped clarify what pro-Nicene theology needed to be.

## Historical Rise and Decline

The Heteroousians rose within the Homoian umbrella in the 350s. Aetius taught in Antioch under Eudoxius's patronage from 357. At the peak of Homoiousian influence, Constantius condemned Aetius, Eudoxius, and Eunomius to exile. After the Homoian settlement of 360, Eunomius was made bishop of Cyzicus, apparently through Eudoxius's influence, before falling out with Eudoxius over the latter's failure to support Aetius, and eventually abandoning his bishopric. In the following decade Aetius and Eunomius together established an alternative "Eunomian" episcopate, which "survived well into the next century".

## Influence and Legacy

The Heteroousians had a paradoxical dual effect: their radicalism accelerated the crystallization of the [[homoiousians|Homoiousian]] party *and* the [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene]] coalition, by making the subordinationist implications of Homoian theology explicit and impossible to ignore. Eunomius's *Apology* became the primary target of Cappadocian theology. Ayres also notes that Eunomius may have preserved and retrieved earlier traditions (Dionysius of Alexandria on ingeneracy; the Eusebian essence–activity distinction) rather than innovating ex nihilo.
