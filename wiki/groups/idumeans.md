# Idumeans

## Identity and Nomenclature

**Idumea** is the **Greco-Roman name for the region immediately south of Judea**, and the
**Idumeans** are the people who lived there — descended largely from the **[[edomites|Edomites]]**
who, after the fall of Jerusalem (586 BCE), drifted **westward and northward** out of their
old Transjordanian heartland into the southern hill country, Shephelah, and Negeb as **Arab
([[kedarites|Kedarite]]) tribes pressed them from the east** (Leith, Coogan Ch. 8). This
page covers the **later, post-exilic-through-Roman** phase; for the Iron Age kingdom of
Edom/Seir see **[[edomites|Edomites]]**.

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Persian period (Coogan Ch. 8)**: with **Hebron now "in Edomite territory,"** Idumea
  forms a distinct region south of [[jerusalem|Judah]], **settled primarily by Edomites but
  perhaps also some Judeans** (Neh. 11); its capital is unknown. Beyond it the
  **[[kedarites|Kedarite]] Arabs** under **Geshem** controlled the deeper south.
- **Hasmonean period (1 Maccabees, Josephus)**: **John Hyrcanus** conquers Idumea (late
  2nd c. BCE) and **forcibly converts the Idumeans to Judaism** (circumcision, Torah).
- **Herodian/Roman period (Josephus, NT background)**: the Idumeans are now reckoned among
  the Jews; **[[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]]** is of Idumean descent.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Jewish (Second Temple)**: the Idumeans are the assimilated descendants of the old
  "brother-enemy" Edom — now **forcibly incorporated** into the Judean people, yet their
  origin is used to question the legitimacy of Herod as a "half-Jew" king.
- **Critical/historical**: Idumea is a real Persian-through-Roman administrative region,
  attested in **Aramaic ostraca** (e.g. Maresha/Marisa) showing a mixed Edomite, Arab,
  Judean, and Phoenician population worshiping the Edomite god **Qos** alongside others.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

The displacement that created Idumea was driven by **Kedarite Arab** expansion into former
Edom (Leith, Coogan Ch. 8). Hellenistic Idumea centered on towns like **Maresha (Marisa)**
and **Adora**; its forced Judaization under Hyrcanus is a landmark case of religious
assimilation by conquest. In the **First Jewish Revolt (66–70 CE)**, Josephus records
Idumean fighters entering Jerusalem.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Idumea closes the long biblical arc of **Edom**, the kin-enemy: the people once cursed for
betraying Jerusalem (Obadiah, Ps. 137) are, centuries later, **absorbed into Israel itself**
— and then produce, in Herod, a king "of the Jews" whose Idumean blood is thrown back at him.
It thus crystallizes Second Temple anxieties about **who counts as a Jew** and the
boundaries of forced conversion.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The Idumean origin of **Herod** colors Gospel and rabbinic hostility; "Edom" remains in
rabbinic usage a cipher for **Rome**. Idumea is a key case study in conversion, ethnicity,
and political legitimacy. Related: [[edomites]], [[kedarites]], [[israelites]], [[jerusalem]],
[[persians]], [[pharisees]].
