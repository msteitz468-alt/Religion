## Identity and Nomenclature

The Homoiousians are the party associated with the formula *homoiousios* — "like in substance/essence" — as the appropriate description of the Father-Son relationship. Ayres is careful to correct the label: "Homoiousian theology has also frequently been termed 'semi-Arian', again indicating that Basil should be read mainly as a compromise figure in the 350s rather than as a representative of a significant and persistent strand in earlier eastern theology" (cache l.853–857). He also notes strikingly that "the term *homoiousios* plays no role in Basil's surviving texts" (l.857–858), although its condemnation in the 357 Sirmium manifesto and its presence in other Homoiousian texts show it was in circulation.

Ayres traces Homoiousian theology not to a new invention but to "a tradition in eastern theology that strongly emphasized the Son's nature as image and revealer of the Father" (l.843–847). Their position was clarified *reactively* by the emergence of radical Homoians and then Heteroousians: "the emergence of radical Homoians . . . forced upon the 'Homoiousians' the need to identify their own position far more clearly" (l.839–841).

## Key Proponents and Documents

- **Basil of Ancyra**: The central figure. He had succeeded Marcellus as bishop of Ancyra in 336, prosecuted Photinus at the Sirmium 351 council, and led the 358 Ancyra council. The principal surviving Homoiousian text is "at least one extensive letter" from that council, "written by Basil of Ancyra himself" (l.843), preserved in Epiphanius's *Panarion* 73.2.1ff.
- **George of Laodicea**: Co-author of a letter (preserved at Epiphanius, *Panarion* 73.12.1ff) written between the Dated creed and the Council of Seleucia (autumn 359); extensively defends essence language and attacks the Heteroousians as "like in will, unlike in essence" (l.1259–1260).
- **Eleusis of Cyzicus**: A prominent Homoiousian bishop at the Council of Seleucia (l.1459).

## Theological Position

Basil of Ancyra's approach to theology is best understood through his epistemology: "Basil frequently links the process of doctrinal formulation with the formation of appropriate 'concepts' (*epinoiai*)" (l.880–882). Proper doctrinal speech requires selecting the right scriptural terms and grasping them "apart from any materialistic or temporal connotations" (l.884–885).

The core argument: "Once we remove the corporeal connotations of the Father/Son relationship then we are left with 'only the generation of a living being like in essence'" (l.893–896). Father/Son language implies something distinct from Creator/creature language — and this distinction can only be adequately expressed through essence (*ousia*) language: "we must confess Father and Son to be like according to essence if we are not to mistake the Father/Son relationship for a Creator/creature relationship" (l.895–900). Crucially, this does not presuppose comprehension of God's essence: "talk of likeness in essence is necessary to indicate the closeness that Father/Son language must indicate in a non-material context. 'Essence' language is thus the necessary way of symbolizing a relationship that remains unknown" (l.903–906).

Basil also uses *energeia* (energy/activity) language: "likeness in activities indicates likeness in essences" (l.937–938), paralleling the argument of [[athanasius|Athanasius]] in his *Orations*. However, Ayres notes Basil "does not yet speak of common activities revealing the same essence" (l.945–947) — a distinction that would become important in Cappadocian pro-Nicene theology.

## Relation to the Other Trajectories

- **vs. Homoians**: The Homoiousians charged that Homoian "like according to the Scriptures" without essence language was theologically insufficient. Basil had found Homoian positions "acceptable partners in anti-Marcellan contexts but theologically insufficient in themselves" (l.848–850).
- **vs. Heteroousians**: George of Laodicea's letter attacks the [[heteroousians|Heteroousians]] as treating "like in will, unlike in essence" as their principle and as seeking to remove *ousia* language from discussion (l.1257–1260). The Homoiousians saw their own theology as refuting this position by insisting on the Father's real self-gift in generating the Son.
- **vs. pro-Nicenes**: The Homoiousians could not accept *homoousios* as a term, partly because "spiritual substances could only be *homoios*, not homoousios" — Sozomen reports that they saw *homoousios* as a term appropriate to material beings of the same class (footnote 382). They were also worried that *homoousios* implied Marcellan modalism. Ayres notes that [[athanasius|Athanasius]] began to court them in his *De synodis*, presenting them as people who could accept Nicaea apart from a "misguided reservation about *homoousios*" (l.1483–1486).

## Historical Rise and Fall

The Homoiousian moment crystallized around the Ancyra council of 358, which was "most immediately . . . prompted by the teaching of Aetius in Antioch" (l.828–830). Basil's delegation successfully reached Constantius at Sirmium, and for a brief period (358–359) the Emperor appeared sympathetic, writing to condemn Eudoxius for "promoting Aetius and his teaching" and using the phrase "like in substance with the Father" (Sozomen, *Hist. eccl.* 4.14; l.983–986).

Their influence was brief. The Dated creed of 359 — which Basil himself signed only by adding "according to being" after his name (l.1238–1240) — represented a compromise that the radical Homoians soon outmanoeuvred. At the Council of Seleucia (September 359), the Homoiousians initially prevailed in the vote and deposed Acacius's party, but were eventually pressured into accepting the modified Dated creed on the last night of 359 (l.1529–1532). At Constantinople 360, under Acacius's presidency, leading Homoiousians including Basil were deposed. Ayres summarizes: "Basil's influence was at its height" after 358 (l.1003), but "he never seems fully to have overcome long-standing Homoian influence at court" (l.1243–1244).

## Influence and Legacy

Despite their political defeat in 360, Ayres suggests the Homoiousians are poorly served by the labels "semi-Arian" or mere "compromise" party. George of Laodicea's defence of "three hypostases" and "one divinity/kingship/principle" (l.1299–1304) anticipates the language later developed by Basil of Caesarea. Athanasius's *De synodis* (discussed in the next chapter) sought to bridge the gap between Nicene and Homoiousian theologies, and some Homoiousians were drawn toward the emerging pro-Nicene coalition in the 360s.
