# Babylonians (Neo-Babylonians / Chaldeans)

## Identity and Nomenclature

The **Neo-Babylonian** (or **Chaldean**) Empire arose under **Nabopolassar**, who with
the Medes destroyed Assyria (Nineveh fell 612 BCE), and reached its height under his
son **[[nebuchadnezzar|Nebuchadrezzar II]]** (the spelling closer to Akkadian
*Nabu-kudurri-uṣur*; the Bible also has Nebuchadnezzar). The Bible's "**Chaldeans**"
(*Kasdim*) names the ruling group. Their capital was **[[babylon|Babylon]]**. The last
king was **Nabonidus**, absent in Tema while his son Belshazzar held the city
(Cogan, Coogan Ch. 7; Leith, Ch. 8).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **2 Kings 24–25 / Jeremiah / Ezekiel**: Nebuchadrezzar's defeat of Egypt at
  **Carchemish (605)**; the **first deportation (597)** of King Jehoiachin and the
  elite; the **destruction of [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] and the Temple (586)** under
  Zedekiah, who is blinded and exiled.
- **Lamentations**: the dirge over the fallen city ("How lonely sits the city," Lam.
  1.1).
- **Psalm 137**: "By the rivers of Babylon — there we sat down and wept."
- **Daniel**: the court tales (Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar) set in Babylon.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Israelite/Jewish**: the agent of the catastrophe of 586, yet **Jeremiah** urges
  *submission* to Babylon as God's appointed yoke and even counsels the exiles to "seek
  the welfare of the city" (Jer. 29). Babylon is judgment, but also the place of a
  *flourishing* exilic community.
- **Babylonian self-record**: the **Babylonian Chronicle** is notably evenhanded —
  unlike the Assyrian annals, it can report the king's defeats without apology (Cogan).

## Deportation Policy — Contrast with Assyria

Cogan stresses a key difference: the **Babylonians did *not* practice two-way
population exchange**. They deported Judah's elite but brought no foreigners in, and
they let the exiles live in **ethnically organized, self-governing communities**
(Tel-abib, etc.) where Judeans kept genealogies, elders, and identity intact — the
seedbed of post-exilic Judaism. Those left in the land were placed under the Judean
governor **Gedaliah** at Mizpah (soon assassinated). See [[babylon]],
[[ezra-nehemiah]].

## The Babylonian Exile: Continuity and Change

The exile transformed Judean culture: **Aramaic** displaced Hebrew in daily life and
the **Aramaic script** replaced palaeo-Hebrew; **Babylonian month-names** and even
**Babylonian personal names** (Zerubbabel, "seed of Babylon"; Sheshbazzar) entered use;
and the **lack of a temple** forced new, non-sacrificial forms of piety (Sabbath,
prayer, study) and accelerated the formation of scripture. The empire fell to **Cyrus
the Persian in 539**, opening the return (see [[persians]]).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Babylon is the great theological cipher of judgment and exile in the Hebrew Bible — the
end of the Davidic monarchy, the loss of land and Temple, and the crucible in which
Judaism as a portable, text-centered faith began to take shape. The prophetic theology
(Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Second Isaiah) wrestles God's justice and future hope out of the
disaster.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

"**Babylon**" becomes the archetype of oppressive worldly power, reused in apocalyptic
(Daniel; "Babylon the great" in Revelation; cf. 1 Peter 5.13 "Babylon" = Rome). Related:
[[assyrians]], [[persians]], [[nebuchadnezzar]], [[babylon]], [[jerusalem]],
[[lamentations]], [[ezra-nehemiah]], [[samaritans]].

## Isaiah 13–14, 21, 39 (Oswalt, NICOT — 2026-07-18 ingest)

Oswalt distinguishes two Babylons in Isaiah 1–39. In chs. 13–14 Babylon is "clearly an
attack not so much upon the historical Babylon (cf. 21:10 for that) as… upon that which
Babylon has always represented: human glory" — already in the late 700s Babylon was the
culture-center courting allies (Merodach-baladan, 721–710 and 705–703; cf. Isa 39:1), so
Isaianic authorship needs no exilic setting; the absence of the Persians from ch. 13 is, he
argues, "unaccountable" for a sixth-century author. The king of Babylon in the taunt-song
(14:4b–21) is representative: "the attempt to identify a precise historical figure is
probably futile." In 21:1–10 by contrast Babylon is "a flesh-and-blood kingdom" whose fall
elicits prophetic anguish ("No empathy is possible with a figure of speech"), read by Oswalt
as a two-layered oracle spanning the eighth-century and 539 horizons. Ch. 39's embassy is
"not causal but typical": Hezekiah's pride prefigures the national pride that produced 587.
Sennacherib destroyed the city in 689. See [[sheol|Sheol]] for the 14:9–21 underworld scene
and [[day-of-the-lord|Day of the LORD]] for 13:6–18.
