# The Misls

The **misls** were the eighteenth-century Sikh military confederacies — the corporate form the
[[khalsa|Khalsa]] took as a fighting and land-holding power between the execution of
[[banda-bahadur|Banda Bahadur]] (1716) and the unification of the Punjab under
[[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] (from 1799). This page draws on **Khushwant Singh, *A History of the Sikhs*,
Vol. 1, Chs. 7–10**.

## Identity and Nomenclature (Khushwant Singh)

"The Arabic word *misl* means 'like'. The Sikh misls were 'alike' in the sense that they were considered
equals" (Singh, *History of the Sikhs* Vol. 1, fn.2). Membership and command were voluntary and federal:
"Every Sikh was free to join any misl he chose, and every misl was free to act in any way it wished in the
area under its control. Only in matters affecting the community as a whole were they to take orders from
the Supremo, [[jassa-singh-ahluwalia|Jassa Singh Ahluwalia]]." Singh judges the system "ideally suited to
the conditions of the time… it combined freedom of action with the discipline of a unified command" —
while noting its fatal weakness: once external pressure relaxed, the misls turned on one another and "the
Sarbat Khalsa became a snake with many heads."

## Governing Institutions

- **Sarbat Khalsa** — the biennial general assembly of the whole Khalsa at [[amritsar|Amritsar]], held on
  the 1st of Baisakh and at Divali, which arose after Banda's execution left the Sikhs "without a personal
  leader." It appointed *jathedārs*, chose agents, and authorized negotiation on the community's behalf
  (Singh, ch. 7). Procedure: opened with *ardas*, the matter debated clamorously, the senior sardars
  guiding the assembly to consensus, ratified by shouts of *sat srī akāl*.
- **Gurmatā** — "a resolution passed by it [the Sarbat Khalsa] became a *gurmatā* (decree of the Guru)":
  the assembly's collective decision carried the authority of the Guru himself (see [[the-guru|the Guru]]).
- **Dal Khalsa** — the unified army. At a Sarbat Khalsa following Abdali's first invasion (1748) "it was
  resolved to merge the independent *jathās* into one army, the Dal Khalsa," divided into eleven misls
  (twelve counting the Phulkian, which stood outside it), with Jassa Singh Ahluwalia as supreme commander
  and a total strength of "about seventy thousand horse and foot." It descended from the earlier **Buḍhā
  Dal** (veterans, under **Nawab Kapur Singh**) and **Taruṇā Dal** (younger soldiers).
- **Rākhī** — the protection system that turned raiders into rulers: "Instead of simply robbing the people…
  they offered them protection (*rākhī*) on payment of one-fifth of their takings at the end of each
  harvest." Each misl held "a certain well-known beat or circle" for collection; the right was extended
  east as far as the upper Gangetic Doab.

## The Twelve Misls (Khushwant Singh's enumeration)

1. **Ahluwalia** — under [[jassa-singh-ahluwalia|Jassa Singh Ahluwalia]], also supreme commander of the Dal Khalsa
2. **Fyzullapuria / Singhpuria** — under **Nawab Kapur Singh**
3. **Sukerchakia** — Naudh Singh → Charhat Singh → Maha Singh → [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] (of Gujranwala)
4. **Nishanwalia** — under Dasaundha Singh, standard-bearer of the Dal Khalsa
5. **Bhangi** — under Hari Singh; the largest misl, "more than ten thousand soldiers"
6. **Kanhaya** — under Jai Singh; later led by Sada Kaur, Ranjit Singh's mother-in-law
7. **Nakkai** — under Hira Singh, in the Nakka tract near Lahore
8. **Dallewalia** — under Gulab Singh
9. **Shaheed** — under [[deep-singh|Baba Deep Singh]]; "the name *śahīd* (martyr) was taken after the death of the leader"
10. **Karora Singhia** — under Karora Singh
11. **Ramgarhia** — under (Jassa Singh) Nand Singh's line
12. **Phulkian** — under Ala Singh of Patiala; not part of the Dal Khalsa (the later cis-Sutlej states)

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The misl era is where the Khalsa's *dharam yudh* ideal became territorial power. Singh's balance-sheet
(ch. 9) credits the misls with "the resurrection of the spirit of Punjabi nationalism" and with winning
back the confidence of the Muslim peasantry — Afghan eyewitness Nur Mohammed conceded "there is no
adultery among these dogs… nor is there any house-breaker born among these miscreants" — while debiting
them with "the degradation of the misls from contingents of freedom fighters to bands of robbers and the
anarchy they let loose when they turned against each other." That anarchy is the precondition Ranjit
Singh exploited to absorb the misls into a single state.
