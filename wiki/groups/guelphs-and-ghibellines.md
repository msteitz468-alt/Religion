# Guelphs and Ghibellines

The two rival factions of late-medieval Italian politics — broadly, the **Guelphs**
aligned with the papacy and the **Ghibellines** with the Holy Roman Emperor — whose
conflict pervades the [[divine-comedy|*Commedia*]] and frames [[dante-alighieri|Dante]]'s
own life. Dante was a Guelph; after the Guelphs' victory the party split in
[[florence|Florence]] into **White** and **Black** factions, and the Blacks' seizure
of power in 1302 sent Dante into permanent exile.

## In the Divine Comedy

The factions surface from Ciacco's prophecy (Inf. 6) of how the Whites ("Wildwood
boys") will drive out the Blacks, who will then return "spurred on by one who now
just coasts between" (Boniface VIII). [[farinata-degli-uberti|Farinata]] is the
great Ghibelline of the poem (Inf. 10), defending Florence after the Ghibelline
victory at **Montaperti** (1260); Bocca degli Abati, who cut the Florentine
standard at that battle, lies among the traitors in Cocytus (Inf. 32). The Guelph
nobles of Inferno 16 (Guido Guerra, Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, Iacopo Rusticucci) and
countless others tie the afterlife to factional Florence.

## Significance

Dante uses the factions to dramatize how civic discord destroys the rational order
a city exists to embody (he records [[brunetto-latini|Brunetto Latini]]'s
definition of a city as "a coming-together of persons… to live according to
reason"). His mature political theology — Rome and the Empire as the providential
guarantors of universal justice (*De Monarchia*) — is in part a response to the
ruin he saw faction produce.
