# Gnostics

> **Group page** — the Gnostics as a social/historical actor. For Gnosticism as a *system*
> (canon, doctrine, hermeneutics, relationship to orthodoxy), see the sect page
> [[gnosticism|Gnosticism]] and its sub-schools [[valentinianism|Valentinianism]] and
> [[sethianism|Sethianism]].

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Gnosticism" is a modern scholarly category applied to a loose family of ancient movements that share certain structural features but were diverse and not self-described as a unified movement. The ancient writers who "knew" these groups (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Epiphanius, Clement) typically called them by specific names — "Valentinians," "Sethians," "Ophites," "Basilideans" — not as a single movement. The word *gnostikos* ("one who knows") was used as a self-description by some groups.

Michael Williams (*Rethinking "Gnosticism"*, 1996) argued the category is so imprecise it should be abandoned; Karen King (*What is Gnosticism?*, 2003) made similar arguments. Despite these challenges, "gnosticism" remains in widespread use as a convenience category for movements sharing: (1) a sharp spirit-matter dualism; (2) a demiurgic cosmology (the material world created by an inferior being); (3) a soteriology centered on *gnosis* (revealed knowledge) rather than faith; (4) an esoteric epistemology.

The Nag Hammadi discovery (1945) — 52 texts in Coptic found in upper Egypt — provided primary sources for gnostic thought, substantially expanding knowledge beyond patristic secondhand accounts. While patristic accounts have been shown to be generally reliable (especially Irenaeus on Valentinianism), the Nag Hammadi texts reveal greater diversity and theological sophistication than heresiologists suggested.

## Primary Textual Appearances

Gnostic groups do not appear by name in the NT, but patristic writers identified proto-gnostic tendencies in NT texts:
- 1 John 4:2 — the antichrist "who denies that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" — read as addressing proto-Docetism
- Col 2:8-23 — warning against "philosophy" and "elementary principles of the world" — read as addressing proto-gnostic cosmological speculation
- 1 Tim 6:20 — "knowledge falsely so called" (*pseudōnymos gnōsis*) — most explicit near-terminological reference
- Revelation 2:6, 15 — "Nicolaitans" — an obscure group Irenaeus associated with early gnosticism

The major flowering of gnostic Christianity was in the second century CE, particularly in Alexandria, Syria, and Rome.

## Major Sub-Groups and Figures

### Valentinianism

The most sophisticated and numerically significant gnostic school. Founded by [[valentinus|Valentinus]] (Alexandria → Rome, c.130-165 CE). Key features: elaborated Pleroma cosmology (30 Aeons); three-class anthropology (pneumatics, psychics, hylics); Sophia myth as origin of creation; sophisticated biblical interpretation. Sub-figures: Ptolemy (*Letter to Flora*); Heracleon (first NT commentary, on John); Marcus (numerological gnostic); Theodotus (Eastern school, quoted by Clement). See [[valentinus|Valentinus]].

### Basilideanism

Basilides was active in Alexandria during the reign of Hadrian (117-138 CE). His system featured: 365 heavenly aeons; the OT God as the lowest of these; a Docetic Christology (Simon of Cyrene crucified in Jesus' place — Irenaeus, *Haer.* 1.24.4); salvation as *gnosis* of the highest unnamed God. Basilides reportedly authored a 24-book commentary on the Gospel. His son Isidore continued the school.

### Sethianism

A distinct gnostic strand identified in several Nag Hammadi texts (*Apocryphon of John*, *Gospel of the Egyptians*, *Trimorphic Protennoia*). Centered on Seth (third son of Adam) as the bearer of the divine seed; features: Barbelo (the divine Mother); Yaldabaoth as the Demiurge (explicitly identified with the OT God); a fourfold light-world. Sethian texts show strong Jewish apocalyptic influence and may predate explicitly Christian forms of gnosticism.

### Cerinthus

Active in Asia Minor at the turn of the 2nd century; Irenaeus places him as a contemporary of John the Apostle (with the famous story of John fleeing the bathhouse when Cerinthus entered). Distinctive teachings: Jesus was a natural man on whom the heavenly Christ descended at baptism and departed before the crucifixion (a "Separationist" Christology). This early Separationism influenced later gnostic Christologies.

### Ophites / Naassenes

Named for the serpent (*ophis* / Hebrew *nāḥāš*) whom they venerated as the bearer of *gnosis* in the Genesis garden narrative — a radical revaluation of the OT text. The OT God (who forbade eating from the tree of knowledge) is the villain; the serpent is the hero. Several Nag Hammadi texts show Ophite or Sethian connections (*Hypostasis of the Archons*, *On the Origin of the World*).

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### Proto-Orthodox / Catholic Reception

Gnostics are the primary target of the great anti-heretical works:
- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* (c.180) — most extensive ancient source
- Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* (early 3rd c.) — 10 books; more philosophical account
- Tertullian, *Against Valentinians* and *Prescription against Heretics*
- Origen, *Against Celsus*; *Commentary on John* (against Heracleon)
- Epiphanius, *Panarion* (c.375) — comprehensive heresy catalogue

The orthodox narrative: gnostics corrupted authentic apostolic Christianity with Platonic and Oriental mythology; their denial of the goodness of creation and the real humanity of Christ constitutes an apostasy from the faith.

### Stark's Sociological Argument: Gnosticism as Christian Heresy (Harnack Vindicated)

Rodney Stark (*Rise of Christianity*, Ch. 6) offers a quantitative argument supporting Adolf von Harnack's original position that Gnosticism was **a Christian heresy, not a parallel Jewish movement**.

**The debate**: Harnack (*History of Dogma*, 1886–89) argued Gnosticism was an extreme Hellenization of Christianity — a Christian movement that absorbed too much Greek cosmological speculation. Against this, Birger Pearson and others argued for a **"Jewish heresy" thesis**: that Gnosticism had Jewish roots independent of Christianity, and that Sethian Gnosticism in particular may have preceded Christian forms.

**Stark's statistical test**: Using his 22-city dataset, Stark performed a regression analysis with Gnostic presence as the dependent variable. The key finding:

| Predictor | Effect on Gnostic Presence |
|---|---|
| Christianization score | Significant positive effect |
| Synagogue presence (controlling for Christianization) | No independent effect |

When Christianization is held constant, synagogue presence has no additional predictive power for explaining where Gnostic groups appear. If Gnosticism had independent Jewish roots, synagogue presence should predict it independently of Christian presence — but it does not. Gnostic groups show up where Christianity was already established, not where Judaism was established independently of Christianity.

**Interpretation**: This supports the Harnack/orthodox position that Gnosticism was an internal mutation of Christianity rather than a parallel Jewish development. Gnostics were ex-Christians (or competing Christians), not ex-Jews who independently developed a gnostic system and then drew on Christian materials.

**Caveat**: Stark's dataset is small (22 cities) and the variables are crude proxies. The argument is suggestive but cannot be treated as definitive historical proof. The debate over Sethian Gnosticism's pre-Christian Jewish strata continues in specialist scholarship.

### Modern Academic Reception

Since Nag Hammadi:
- Elaine Pagels (*The Gnostic Gospels*, 1979) — argued gnostic texts represent genuine early Christian diversity suppressed by orthodoxy; widely influential popular work
- Michael Williams (*Rethinking "Gnosticism"*, 1996) — critiqued the category
- Bentley Layton (*The Gnostic Scriptures*, 1987) — standard critical edition; refined the typology
- David Brakke (*The Gnostics*, 2010) — recent synthetic account

### Rudolph's Phenomenological and Historical Synthesis

Kurt Rudolph's *Gnosis: The Nature and History of Gnosticism* (German 1977/1980; English 1987) provides the standard modern phenomenological description of Gnosis as a coherent *late antique religion* (*spätantike Religion*) alongside its historical development. See [[rudolph-gnosis-the-nature-and-history-of-gnosticism|Rudolph, *Gnosis*]] (full scholarship summary).

**Nature / Phenomenology (Main Features of Ideology and Mythology)**: Rudolph structures the "nature" of Gnosis around:
- Radical **dualism** (spirit/light vs. matter/darkness; the transcendent alien God vs. this world).
- **Cosmology and cosmogony**: The Pleroma (fullness) of aeons emanated in syzygies from the unknowable Father; the fall or passion of Sophia (Wisdom) produces deficiency and the Demiurge (inferior, ignorant creator, often identified with the OT God/Yaldabaoth), who fashions the material cosmos and the human body as a prison for the divine spark (*pneuma*).
- **Anthropology**: Humans are tripartite or composite (hylic/body from the Demiurge, psychic/soul, pneumatic/divine spark from the higher world). The "pneumatic race" (often linked to Seth or the "perfect") carries the potential for awakening.
- **Soteriology of gnosis**: Salvation is revealed esoteric *knowledge* (of origin, the true God, the structure of the cosmos, and the path of ascent) granted by a heavenly revealer/redeemer. This awakens the sleeper, discloses the Demiurge's inferiority, and enables the soul's ascent through the archons (often with passwords/seals) back to the Pleroma. The material body and this-worldly creation are of limited or negative value.
- **Community, cult, and ethics**: Gnostic groups had organized communities, varied ethics (ascetic world-rejection alongside some libertine interpretations), and sacraments (baptism, chrism, Eucharist, "redemption," and the Valentinian "bridal chamber" symbolizing restoration to the Pleroma). Not purely intellectual or mythic; had lived cultic and social expression.

**History**: Rudolph surveys the "problem of origins" (no single cause; multiple presuppositions in Jewish apocalyptic/Genesis exegesis, Hellenistic Platonism and mystery cults, Iranian dualism, and Christian motifs reinterpreted). Early schools (Simonians, Basilideans, Ophites/Naassenes); the "great systems" of the 2nd century (Valentinus and Valentinianism as the most elaborate and influential, with Ptolemy, Heracleon, Marcus, Theodotus); Manichaeism as Gnosis "in a new, world-historical form" (Mani's systematization, missionary spread, extensive canon and church); the Mandaeans as the only surviving Gnostic community (living relic with rich mythology and repeated baptismal rites preserving ancient structures).

**Sources**: Heresiologists (Irenaeus especially reliable on Valentinianism; Hippolytus, Tertullian, Epiphanius, Clement as preserver of fragments) remain essential but are now supplemented and corrected by the Nag Hammadi primary texts (the "Nag Hammadi discovery and its significance" is a pivotal chapter for Rudolph). The 1945 find (13 codices, 52+ texts, buried c. 367 CE likely by Pachomian monks) allows reconstruction of Gnostic self-understanding rather than only polemical portraits.

**Tradition-Specific Portraits and Self-Understanding**: Rudolph presents Gnostics not merely as "heretics" or "corrupters" but as participants in a serious religious movement with its own claim to possess the true *gnosis* of the alien God and the path of liberation. Valentinians in particular used mainstream Christian vocabulary and scriptures (Heracleon wrote the first known NT commentary, on John) while operating with a fundamentally different cosmology. Sethians show stronger Jewish-apocalyptic coloring in their reinterpretation of Genesis. This complements (and in some respects challenges) the patristic framing in [[kruger-christianity-at-the-crossroads|Kruger]] and the sociological "Christian heresy" argument in [[stark-rise-of-christianity|Stark]] by giving fuller weight to structural independence, syncretistic roots, and primary voices.

**Influence and Epilogue**: Rudolph's epilogue surveys later consequences (structural parallels in medieval dualist movements such as the Cathars; persistence in esoteric traditions; the modern scholarly recovery itself). The use of "gnostic" as a pejorative for anti-cosmic, esoteric religion continues, often imprecisely.

This synthesis deepens the wiki's existing coverage (built primarily on patristic and sociological sources) by supplying the unified phenomenological account of Gnosis' "nature," the social/ethical/cultic dimensions, the full historical arc through Manichaeism and the Mandaean survival, and explicit engagement with the Nag Hammadi revolution.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

The Nag Hammadi Library (1945) is the primary archaeological find: 52 Coptic texts, probably buried by monks from the nearby Pachomian monastery c.367 CE following Athanasius' Easter letter listing the NT canon — suggesting the texts were previously in use in monastic contexts before being suppressed. Some may be Coptic translations of 2nd-century Greek originals.

Other important texts: Berlin Gnostic Codex (including *Gospel of Mary*, *Apocryphon of John*); Tchacos Codex (*Gospel of Judas*, 2006).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Gnosticism forced proto-orthodox Christianity to articulate its theology more precisely:

1. **Creation theology**: Against the dualism of matter/spirit, orthodoxy affirmed the goodness of material creation (Genesis 1; Irenaeus' *recapitulatio*; the incarnation as God entering the material world)
2. **Christology**: Against Docetism and Separationism, orthodoxy insisted on the real humanity of Christ — real birth, real suffering, real death, real bodily resurrection
3. **Soteriology**: Against *gnosis* as the saving element, orthodoxy (especially Paul's heritage) centered salvation on the death and resurrection of Christ received through faith
4. **Anthropology**: Against the tripartite hierarchy (pneumatics/psychics/hylics), orthodoxy affirmed that all humans bear God's image and all are called to salvation
5. **Canon**: Gnostic groups produced their own gospels and texts; the orthodox response accelerated the identification of the authoritative apostolic corpus

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The gnostic/orthodox debate resurfaced in:
- **Manichaeism** (3rd-4th c.) — a new dualistic religion showing gnostic influence
- **Medieval heresies**: Catharism (12th-13th c.) shares structural similarities with gnostic dualism; scholars debate direct historical connection
- **Modern spirituality**: 20th-21st c. "New Age" and esoteric movements have drawn selectively on gnostic themes; the *Da Vinci Code* popularized a distorted version of the Nag Hammadi findings

The use of "gnostic" as a general pejorative for anti-body, anti-world, esoteric religion continues in theological discourse, often imprecisely. The modern recovery of gnostic primary texts has complicated the polemical picture without vindicating the Bauer thesis that gnostics were the numerical majority. See [[bauer-thesis|Bauer Thesis]] for the gnostics' own admission that they were "the few."

## Separationist Christology ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) describes the
typical Gnostic Christology as **separationist** (distinct from [[docetism|docetism]]): "a
human Jesus who was temporarily inhabited by a divine being," the Christ entering at the baptism
and departing before the crucifixion (a literal reading of "why have you forsaken me"). In the
*Coptic Apocalypse of Peter* the "living Jesus" laughs above the cross while only the "clay
vessel" is nailed; salvation comes through saving *gnosis*, not Christ's death. This is the third
"Christological dead end" — affirming Christ's divinity and humanity but denying he was a single
being. See [[docetism]], [[ortho-paradoxes]], [[bodily-resurrection]].
