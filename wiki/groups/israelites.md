# Israelites (Emergence and Tribal Israel)

> This page treats Israel as an **emerging people/group** in Iron Age Canaan — its
> origins, ethnogenesis, and tribal structure — per Coogan Ch. 3 (Stager). For
> theological constructs of "Israel," see related concept pages; for the divided
> monarchy's kingdoms, see [[samaria]] and [[jerusalem]].

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Israel" first appears in history on the **Merneptah ("Israel") Stela** (erected
c. 1209 BCE), where Egyptian hieroglyphs mark it with the determinative for a
**people** (not a city-state, unlike Ashkelon, Gezer, Yanoam) — a rural or tribal
entity, masculine collective, "probably indicating its identity with an eponymous
patriarchal ancestor" ([[abraham|Jacob/Israel]]). Ironically, the first mention of
Israel proclaims its destruction: "Israel is laid waste and his seed is not."

The earliest *self*-portrait is the 12th-century **Song of Deborah** (Judg. 5), which
calls Israel the **"people/kindred of Yahweh"** (ʿam YHWH) — a confederation of **ten**
(not twelve) tribes occupying varied ecological niches on both sides of the Jordan.

## Origins: Four Models (Coogan/Stager)

1. **Conquest** (Albright, Wright, Yadin): Israel as outside invader (book of Joshua).
   Fails archaeologically — Jericho and Ai unoccupied; only [[hazor]] and Bethel fit.
2. **Pastoral nomad / sedentarization** (Alt, Finkelstein): peaceful infiltration of
   herders settling down. Cannot alone account for ~600 new highland villages.
3. **Peasants' revolt** (Mendenhall, Gottwald): oppressed Canaanite peasants withdrawing
   from the city-states (the [[apiru-habiru|Apiru]] phenomenon), with Yahwism as
   catalyst/product. Only partial — the same "egalitarian" villages appear outside
   Israel (Moab, Ammon, Edom).
4. **Ruralization** (Stager's preferred synthesis): a *general* phenomenon —
   decentralization and ruralization as Late Bronze city-states and Egyptian control
   declined — produced highland villages from mixed sources (pastoralists, displaced
   peasants, [[canaanites|Canaanites]] squeezed off the plains). Early Israel was a
   **rural subset of Canaanite culture**, largely indistinguishable in language,
   dress, and material culture (collared-rim store jars, pillared houses, terraces,
   cisterns, **no pig bones**).

The decisive difference was not ethnicity but **religious identity**: a "supertribe"
united by devotion to **Yahweh**, revealed (per the tradition) to [[moses|Moses]]. See
[[midianites]] for the proposed Midianite/Kenite origin of Yahwism and [[divine-name]].

## Tribes and Tribalism

Iron I Israel was kin-structured: the **"house of the father" (bêt ʾāb)**, lineage,
clan, tribe. Genealogies functioned as **social/political charters**, not literal
family trees ("Israel" as descendants of one couple is a unifying theological claim,
historically inaccurate per Coogan). Commitment to the ʿam of Yahweh ranked above
tribal affiliation — Stager compares the Israelite ʿam to the Islamic *ʾumma*. Central
sanctuaries at [[shechem]] and Shiloh reinforced covenant unity (Josh. 24). The tribes
persisted into the monarchy (the 8th-c. **Samaria ostraca** still reflect intact clan
districts — see [[samaria]]).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The constitution of a "people directly under the patrimonial authority of Yahweh"
(Voegelin: a "spiritual exodus from the cosmological form of imperial rule") is, for
the tradition, the birth of the covenant nation — the whole framework of Genesis–Kings.
Neighboring peoples are cast as kin: Moab and Ammon from Lot (Gen. 19), Edom from Esau,
Midian from Keturah — etiologies expressing first-millennium political relations.

## The Exodus Formation Narrative (Bruckner, UBCS Exodus — reingest 2026-07-18)

*Attributed layer: how the book of Exodus itself narrates the people's formation, per
[[james-bruckner|Bruckner]] ([[bruckner-exodus-ubcs|source page]]) — a canonical-theological
portrait, distinct from the archaeological ethnogenesis models above.*

Bruckner prefers "children of Israel" over "Israelites": "They began and continued on this
journey as 'children.' Too much is lost when this word is replaced with a more abstract word."
He distinguishes the terms: "God is forming the nation Israel from the ethnic group known as
the Hebrews. This group may be related, but it is not identical to... ḫapiru... after the
Babylonian exile... they would begin to refer to themselves as Jews." Key formation claims:
Exod 1:7's five verbs of increase echo Gen 1:28 — fecundity under oppression "introduces the
central theme of the power of God's creation in Exodus"; the broken slaves of 6:9 could not
even listen — "Physical deliverance was necessary before they could hear or understand. This
is the 'gospel' of the exodus"; on the slave mentality, "quick physical liberation does not
necessarily result in the liberation of one's self-perception." At Sinai the vocation "kingdom
of priests and a holy nation" (19:5–6) means mediating "the word and life of God to the world"
(with Fretheim: Israel "not seen over against less honored nations"). Against Brueggemann and
Durham, who see Sinai as Israel's birth, Bruckner argues they were born in Egypt and at the
sea; Sinai "is the beginning of their responsible adulthood, not their birth." After the
golden calf, "the people's creation of the tabernacle demonstrated their true repentance"
(36:3–7) — the freewill offering had to be stopped for excess.
