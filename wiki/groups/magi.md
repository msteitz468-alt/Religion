# The Magi (Zoroastrian Priesthood)

The **magi** are the **priestly class of [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]** — the reciters of the
**Avesta**, ritual specialists, and (in the Sasanian Empire) administrators of law and purity. This page
treats them as they function in and against the **[[talmud-bavli|Babylonian Talmud]]**: the rabbis'
**"significant Other,"** a neighboring religious elite both vilified and, paradoxically, mirrored. It is
seeded from [[shai-secunda|Shai Secunda]]'s [[secunda-the-iranian-talmud|*The Iranian Talmud*]] (2014).

## Identity and Nomenclature

The English **"magic"** descends, via Greek, from the Old Persian *maguš*; Greek and Latin writers
already linked the magi to esoteric arts (Herodotus's Median priestly tribe). In **Babylonian Jewish
Aramaic** the word appears as *amgusha* / *magosha* (and the abstract *amgushta*, "Magianism"), while the
rabbis also call Zoroastrian priests ***ḥabar*** / ***ḥabarin*** (and *amgusha*); Middle Persian
distinguishes priestly ranks such as *mōbed* and the scholar-priest *hērbed*. Secunda cautions that
"Persian/Iranian" ethnicity, "Zoroastrian" religion, and "magus" priestly office do not map neatly onto
one another, and that applying the modern category "religion" to these terms is anachronistic.

## Primary Textual Appearances (in the Bavli)

- **"He who learns something from a *magus* is worthy of death"** (b. Shabbat 75a, in Rav's name). The
  appended debate between **Rav and Shmuel** over whether "Magianism" (*amgushta*) is **sorcery** or
  **blasphemy** is, Secunda argues, a debate over the **status of Zoroastrian recitation** (of the
  Avesta) in rabbinic law — and, read negatively, evidence that some Jews *were* studying with the magi,
  perhaps for the mantric power attributed to their texts.
- **"The magus mumbles (*ratin magosha*) and knows not what he says; the (rabbinic) reciter recites
  (*tani tanna*) and knows not what he says."** A popular saying (cited by Rav Naḥman) deriding rote
  recitation — and pointedly **pairing** the Zoroastrian priest with the rabbinic *tanna*-reciter.
- **"Three hate each other: dogs, fowl, and Zoroastrian priests (*ḥabarin*)… and some say even rabbinic
  scholars in Babylonia."** The magi and the Babylonian rabbis appear in the *same* list of the
  internally quarrelsome.
- **The "destroyers" exchange**: asked to describe the communities, Levi calls the Babylonian rabbis
  "**ministering angels**" and the magi "**destroying angels** (*malakhei ḥabala*)" — opposites that, by
  requiring a common plane of comparison, betray a perceived **parity**.
- **Amemar and the magus** (urination across the Ohrmazd/Ahreman body-divide): a set-piece in which the
  Jewish **monotheist** bests the Zoroastrian **dualist** with bathroom logic.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

For the **rabbis**, the magi are simultaneously (a) practitioners of forbidden, dangerous *foreign* lore
("Magianism" as sorcery/blasphemy), (b) mindless reciters, and (c) — in the "familiarizing" texts of
Secunda's Chapter 4 — uncomfortably **like the rabbis themselves**: a learned, recitation-based, internally
competitive priestly class. Moulie Vidas notes that the Zoroastrian tension between **scholar-priests**
and professional **liturgical reciters** mirrors the rabbinic friction between scholastic *amoraim* and
the *tannaim* (reciters). From the **Zoroastrian** side, the *Hērbedestān* governs priestly study (chiefly
accurate **memorization and recitation**) and the rules for teaching — including whether one may teach a
non-Zoroastrian (a *daēuuaiiasna*) — the mirror image of the rabbinic ban on learning from a magus.

## Historical Context

Under the Sasanians the priesthood was increasingly **institutionalized**, breeding the rivalry the Bavli
caricatures. Zoroastrians formed a **ruling minority** in largely Aramaic-speaking Mesopotamia (no fire-
temple remains have yet been excavated there), and Zoroastrian officials administered purity and law —
the setting for talmudic anecdotes of rabbis confronting magian power (e.g. interference with Jewish
burial, lamps, and ritual baths). The **Aramaic incantation bowls**, many written by Jews for clients
with Persian/Zoroastrian theophoric names, attest a shared "magic trade" cutting across the communal
boundaries the magi and rabbis tried to police.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The magi are the concrete face of the **dualist** religion against which the rabbis define their
**monotheism** (Amemar's quip), and the chief vehicle of the Bavli's **"discourse of the Other."** Yet
their function is double: the very intensity of the polemic, and the recurring slippage by which rabbi and
magus trade epithets ("injurers," "destroyers," "mumblers"), is — on Secunda's reading — evidence of how
**entangled** the two priestly cultures were in Sasanian Iran.
