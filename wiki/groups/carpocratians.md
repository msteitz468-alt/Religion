# Carpocratians

A second-century [[gnostics|Gnostic]] sect founded by **Carpocrates** of Alexandria and developed by
his son **Epiphanes**, known chiefly as the antinomian foil against whom [[clement-of-alexandria|
Clement of Alexandria]] argues in Book III of the [[stromata|Stromata]]. As a *community* of historical
actors they are treated here; for the broader Gnostic milieu see [[gnostics|Gnostics]].

## What Clement Reports

Clement places the Carpocratians among the heresies that "teach that one ought to live on the principle
that it is a matter of indifference whether one does right or wrong" — the libertine wing of his
two-front polemic (against both libertines and encratites). His specific charges:

- **Community of wives.** "The followers of Carpocrates and Epiphanes think that wives should be
  common property. Through them the worst calumny has become current against the Christian name."
- **Epiphanes' *Concerning Righteousness*.** Clement quotes this work at length. Epiphanes — son of
  Carpocrates, who died at seventeen and was honoured as a god in Cephallenia — argued that God's
  righteousness is "a kind of universal fairness and equality"; that the sun shines on all equally and
  nature gives all things in common; and that human laws of **private property and monogamy** therefore
  violate the divine law of equality. He treated the commandment "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's
  wife" as a ludicrous contradiction.
- **Their love-feasts.** Clement describes (with hostility) gatherings where "they overturn the lamps
  and so extinguish the light... and they have intercourse where they will and with whom they will."

Clement's refutation argues that Carpocrates and Epiphanes **misread Plato's *Republic*** on the
community of wives, mistaking a political proposal about the guardian class for a universal sexual
licence, and that "equality" rightly understood does not abolish marriage. He thus uses the sect as the
extreme case proving that [[gnosis|true knowledge]] issues in self-mastery, not licence.

## Wider Tradition

Irenaeus likewise describes the Carpocratians as libertine Gnostics who held that the soul must pass
through (and exhaust) every kind of experience to escape the cycle of reincarnation, and who venerated
images of Christ alongside Pythagoras and Plato. The sect is frequently cited in later heresiology as
the paradigm of Gnostic antinomianism. The accusation of ritual licence became part of the pagan and
intra-Christian polemic against "the Christian name" generally — a reception fact Clement himself
laments.
