# Sea Peoples

## Identity and Nomenclature

The **Sea Peoples** are a modern label for a **shifting coalition of diverse
population elements, probably originating in Anatolia and the Aegean**, who moved
through the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age. Egyptian records
name some fourteen component groups; the best-attested are the **Peleset
([[philistines|Philistines]])**, **Tjeker/Sikils**, **Shekelesh**, **Denyen**,
**Weshesh**, and **Sherden** (Stager, Coogan Ch. 3; Redmount, Ch. 2). As Coogan's
contributors quip, they were "neither a people nor, strictly speaking, entirely from
the sea."

## Primary Textual and Iconographic Appearances

- **Merneptah** (c. 1209 BCE): a first wave of Sea Peoples allied with Libyans against
  Egypt (the Philistines not yet among them).
- **Rameses III at Medinet Habu** (c. 1177 BCE, his 8th year): the great land-and-sea
  battles; the inscription names the confederation — "Philistines, Tjeker, Shekelesh,
  Denyen, and Weshesh, lands united" — who had already sacked Hatti, Cilicia,
  Carchemish, Arzawa, Cyprus, and Amurru.
- **[[ugarit|Ugarit]]'s last letters**: the king Ammurapi reports "enemy ships" burning
  his towns while his fleet is away — a vivid record of the city's fall (c. 1185 BCE).
- **The tale of Wen-Amun** (11th c.): the **Sikils** ruling [[byblos|Dor]] on the
  Canaanite coast.

## Renaming of Territories

The Sea Peoples left their names on the map: the **Peleset → Philistia → Palestine**;
the **Sikils** (who settled Dor) → **Sicily**; the **Sherden** → **Sardinia**; and
Cyprus's name shifted from Alashiya to *Yadanana*, "the isle of the Denyen/Danaoi."

## Historical and Archaeological Context

Their migration is tracked archaeologically by **locally made Mycenaean IIIC pottery**
appearing in destruction-and-rebuild sequences from Tarsus to [[ashkelon]] and across
Cyprus — proof of a genuine **seaborne migration**, families and livestock aboard
oared galleys with bird-head finials (matching Aegean ship paintings). Coogan's
volume links the whole movement to the collapse of the Aegean–Anatolian "palace
economies," possibly triggered by severe droughts and the rupture of trade networks.
The disruptions contributed to the fall of Hatti, the decline of Egypt, and the
eclipse of Canaanite civilization — clearing the stage for [[israelites|Israel]],
Moab, Ammon, and Edom.

## Significance for the Biblical World

Only the **Philistines** (and the Sikils at Dor) enter the biblical narrative
directly, but the Sea Peoples' migration is one half of the twin upheaval — alongside
the highland settlement of early Israel — that defines the Iron Age I world of Judges
and 1 Samuel. The pressure of Philistine expansion is what drives Israel to demand a
king (see [[philistines]], [[israelites]], [[monarchy-request]]).

## Controversies

The Egyptian "great land-and-sea battle" reconstruction (a long overland trek through
Anatolia) is challenged: Coogan's contributors argue the Philistines came **by sea**
and were already settled in southern Canaan before Rameses III's Nile-Delta battle.
