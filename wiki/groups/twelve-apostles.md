# The Twelve Apostles

The Twelve are the named group summoned and commissioned at [[matthew|Matthew]] 10:1-4, listed
there in pairs and sent out with the instructions of the mission discourse (10:5–11:1). This page
records the treatment of the group in R. T. France, *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the
wiki's sole ingested source on it; interpretive claims are attributed to [[rt-france|France]]
throughout.

## Identity and Nomenclature

Matthew 10:2 contains the only occurrence of the word "apostle" in the gospel. France suggests
this may indicate Matthew's awareness of a difference in function between the "apostles" as
church leaders in his own day and the role of the Twelve as companions of [[jesus|Jesus]]. He
insists that 10:1-4 is not the calling or first selection of the group: Matthew has no parallel
to Mark 3:13-15 or Luke 6:13, and "they are summoned as an existing group, 'his twelve
disciples.'"

France identifies two distinctive features of Matthew's list: it is arranged in pairs, "perhaps
reflecting the tradition that they were sent out in pairs, Mark 6:7," and it explicitly
designates Simon [[peter-apostle|Peter]] as "first" though no further numbering follows. He holds
that apart from Matthew the tax collector and Judas, the list consists of men mentioned nowhere
else in the gospel, and concludes: "The corporate role of the Twelve was obviously more important
and more remembered than the individual contribution of most of the members."

Two names in the list receive extended comment. On Simon the Cananaean, France argues that
"Zealot" did not become a technical term for members of the revolutionary party until the time of
the Jewish War, so the nickname "may derive more from his reputation for religious zeal" — though
he allows that Matthew's readers might well have taken it in the later sense. On Judas Iscariot,
France surveys the proposed derivations, including *sicarios*, and prefers "man of Kerioth,"
raising the possibility that Judas was the one non-Galilean among the group; he immediately
concedes, however: "But that, too, is speculation." **The etymology is left unresolved.**

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **[[matthew|Matthew]] 4:18-22** — the calling of the first four (Simon, Andrew, James, John).
- **Matthew 9:9** — the call of Matthew the tax collector. France notes this is "the first and
  only such call story in this gospel after the initial calling of the four fishermen in
  4:18-22," readers being left to assume the other seven had similar experiences.
- **Matthew 9:35-38** — the harvest saying immediately preceding the list.
- **Matthew 10:1-4** — the summoning, empowering, and naming of the Twelve.
- **Matthew 10:5–11:1** — the mission discourse.
- **Matthew 17:16** — the only independent disciple operation Matthew narrates before the
  resurrection.
- **Matthew 28:19-20** — the commission in which, on France's reading, Matthew's narrative first
  envisages the disciples actually going out on their own.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### France's reading (Christian, historical-grammatical with redaction-critical attention)

France's headline for the second discourse is that the disciples move "From being the recipients
of his ministry ... to become its agents," sharing both the proclamation and the works of mercy
and power. He notes that "heal all sorts of illnesses and disabilities" is repeated verbatim from
the summary of Jesus' own ministry at 10:1, "showing that the mission of the disciples is to be a
continuation of that of Jesus." He reads the formal recognition of the Twelve as the renewal
movement's "first steps toward becoming a structured group" within which the values of chs. 5–7
can be lived out.

On the harvest saying France sharpens the point: "'the workers are few' is in fact an
understatement—there has been only one" (unless [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] is
included), and he notes the irony of sequence, since those told to pray for reapers "will turn out
in the next pericope to be the ones who go in answer to that prayer." Angelic eschatological
reaping, on his reading, must be preceded by human messengers.

France notes that Matthew omits Mark 3:14's primary purpose "to be with him," giving the
commission "in strictly functional terms," though his narrative continues to show that this was
their essential role.

### The number twelve

France holds that the number carried "obvious symbolic importance as the number of the sons of
Jacob and thus of the tribes of [[israelites|Israel]]," possibly echoing [[moses|Moses]]' choice
of twelve tribal leaders (Num 1:1-16) and even the formula "These are the names of" (Num 1:4). He
explicitly denies that the Twelve were drawn from all twelve tribes: "their significance was in
their number, not in their ancestry."

## Historical and Archaeological Context

France's historical observations on the group are largely social and legal rather than material.
He identifies the God–Jesus–disciples chain of authority in 10:40 as resting on the principle
later enshrined in the Jewish legal institution of the *šālîaḥ*, the "one sent" — an ambassador
carrying the full authority of the sender. He contrasts the Twelve with Paul on support: both
refuse fees, unlike itinerant philosophers, but unlike Paul (2 Thess 3:8) the Twelve are to accept
board and lodging, since they carry no money.

On the equipment instructions France makes a key lexical argument: in Matthew all the listed items
are objects of *ktaomai*, "do not get," which "does not naturally refer to what they are to carry
but rather to fund-raising and acquiring special equipment"; basic clothing and equipment are
assumed. He treats the staff discrepancy (Mark 6:8 permits one; Matthew and Luke forbid it) as a
genuine but low-stakes harmonization crux, notes that his own verbal solution fails because Luke
uses Mark's verb, judges that "Overall Mark's wording seems the more likely" — travelers would be
assumed to carry a staff (Exod 12:11), Essene travelers carried one against brigands (Josephus,
*War* 2.125), and even ascetic Cynics carried one — and concludes that "whether the tradition
actually forbade the disciples to carry a staff or not, the thrust of the passage is hardly
affected." **He does not resolve the harmonization.**

France also cites Luke 22:35-36 as evidence that these instructions applied specifically to the
Galilean mission of the Twelve rather than functioning as rules for all subsequent Christian
mission, since they are there rescinded for the new situation following Jesus' arrest.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

France divides the mission instructions into three parts: to whom they are to go (vv. 5-6); the
nature of their mission in both word and deed (vv. 7-8); and how they are to be fed and housed
(vv. 9-15). On the reception of the messengers he draws a theological point: "the villagers will
be welcoming or rejecting their Messiah," and the "peace" resting on the worthy "is not just a
social formality, but a real mark of God's blessing or judgment." He develops the greeting as "no
mere formality. It is envisaged as an effective blessing which goes out and takes effect provided
that it is suitably received; if not ... it will 'return' like an uncashed check" (cf. Isa 55:11).

Reading 10:40-42 together, France finds "a remarkably high estimate of the standing of Jesus'
disciples and the importance of their mission, 'little ones' as they are."

France flags a striking gap in the narrative: Matthew never narrates the mission — not what
happened, not when they returned, not even explicitly that they went — and omits the report-back
present in Mark 6:30; Luke 9:10; 10:17. His explanation is that "Clearly Matthew is more
interested in the principles underlying the disciples' mission (and therefore that of his
readers) than in any contribution it makes to his narrative of Jesus' Galilean period." He
concludes that the only independent disciple operation before the resurrection is 17:16, "where it
was not a success," and that "It will be only after Jesus' resurrection (28:19-20) that Matthew's
narrative will envisage the disciples actually going out on their own."

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The relation between the Twelve and the later office of "apostle" is, on France's reading, already
visible as a distinction in Matthew's own vocabulary — the single use of the term at 10:2 possibly
registering the gap between the Twelve as Jesus' companions and the apostles as church leaders in
Matthew's day.

The live interpretive question France raises without settling concerns the status of the mission
instructions themselves: "There are principles here as well as specific instructions; the problem
for the interpreter is to know which is which ... and on this problem Matthew gives us no explicit
help." The restriction of the mission to Israel at 10:5-6 and its relation to the universal
commission of 28:19 is treated on [[gentiles|Gentiles]].
