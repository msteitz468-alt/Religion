The Sabians (Maimonides' transliteration of the Arabic *al-Ṣābi'a*; Friedländer's text uses the forms "Sabeans," "Sabians," "Tzabians") are the ancient idolatrous nation whose religious beliefs and practices [[maimonides|Maimonides]] invokes throughout Part III of the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]] as the primary foil for the Mosaic commandments. The account is Maimonides' own reconstruction, drawn from written sources (chiefly the *Nabatean Agriculture* and a book he calls *Tomtom* or *Tintom*), not from direct observation of living Sabian practice.

## Identity and Nomenclature

Maimonides does not present the Sabians as a single geographically bounded people but as the dominant pre-Abrahamic and pre-Mosaic religious civilization, whose star-worship, blood-rites, and nature-cult pervaded the ancient world and shaped the idolatrous environment in which Israelite religion emerged. He acknowledges significant limits on his knowledge: "my knowledge of the Sabean doctrines, which I derived from books, is not as complete as the knowledge of those who have witnessed the public practice of those idolatrous customs, especially as they have been out of practice and entirely extinct since two thousand years" (Ch. XLIX). He does, however, note that some Sabian purity practices "are still widespread" and "even at present general in the East, among the few still left of the Magi" (Ch. XLVII) — suggesting an identification with, or overlap with, the Magi of his own day.

## Core Theology

Sabian belief as Maimonides presents it: "The Sabeans, in their ignorance of the existence of God, believed that the spheres with their stars were beings without beginning and without end, that the images and certain trees, the Asherot, derived certain powers from the spheres, that they inspired the prophets, spoke to them in visions, and told them what was good and what bad" (Ch. XLV). The Sabians did not recognize an incorporeal God above the spheres. Their "prophets" were the prophets of the Asherah — trees or cult-objects energized by stellar powers. Maimonides had explained their theory "when speaking of the prophets of the Ashera" in an earlier passage of the Guide (referenced at Ch. XLV).

The Sabians also worshipped demons (*se'irim*), whom they imagined assumed the form of **goats**: "Some sects among the Sabeans worshipped demons, and imagined that these assumed the form of goats, and called them therefore 'goats'" (Ch. XLVI; citing Lev. 17:7).

## Ritual Practices (as Maimonides Reconstructs Them)

**Blood-rites.** The Sabians regarded blood as the food of spirits: "by eating it man has something in common with the spirits, which join him and tell him future events." Some Sabians "killed a beast, received the blood in a vessel or in a pot, and ate of the flesh of that beast, whilst sitting round the blood... imagining that in this manner the spirits would come to partake of the blood which was their food, whilst the idolaters were eating the flesh: that love, brotherhood, and friendship with the spirits were established, because they dined with the latter at one place and at the same time" (Ch. XLVI). Although blood was considered unclean, they ate it for this ritualistic reason.

**Sacrificial offerings.** Idolaters offered leaven and sweetened sacrifices seasoned with honey; salt was absent from their sacrifices entirely (Ch. XLVI). They offered lions, bears, and wild beasts, not domestic livestock — in contrast to the Israelite command to offer only cattle, sheep, and goats (Ch. XLVI, citing the book *Tomtom*). Most idolaters refused to kill cattle, holding the species sacred: "the people of Hodu [Indians] up to this day do not slaughter cattle" (Ch. XLVI). Egyptians worshipped Aries and "abstained from killing sheep, and held shepherds in contempt" (Ch. XLVI; citing Exod. 8:26 and Gen. 46:34).

**Lunar and solar worship.** The Sabians sacrificed to the moon on New-moon days "in the same manner as they sacrificed to the sun when it rose and set in certain particular degrees" (Ch. XLVI). Universal sun-worship is stated to have been so general that "all people turned then to the East" in worship (Ch. XLV) — against which Abraham's choice of the western orientation of the Temple Mount was a deliberate counter-statement.

**Temple-building on high places.** "Idolaters selected the highest possible places on high mountains where to build their temples and to place their images" (Ch. XLV). The Israelite command to seek "the place which the Lord will choose" stands in contrast to this.

**Hewn stones and sacred trees.** Heathen altars were built with hewn stones; the Law requires unhewn stones (Ch. XLV). Planting trees near the altar is forbidden because the Asherot — sacred trees — were central to Sabian worship (Ch. XLV, citing Deut. 16:21). Similarly "worshipping over painted stones" (Lev. 26:1) is forbidden on the same anti-Sabian principle (Ch. XLV).

**Impurity practices.** Sabian purity regulations were extremely burdensome compared to the Torah's: "the practice of the Sabeans, even at present general in the East, among the few still left of the Magi, was to keep a menstruous woman in a house by herself, to burn that upon which she treads, and to consider as unclean every one that speaks with her: even if a wind passed over her and a clean person, the latter was unclean in the eyes of the Sabeans" (Ch. XLVII). Another custom: "whatever is separated from the body, as hair, nail, or blood, is unclean; every barber is therefore unclean in their estimation... whenever a person passes a razor over his skin he must take a bath in running water" (Ch. XLVII). The Torah's purity laws, by contrast, apply only to the Sanctuary and its consecrated objects — deliberately lighter than the Sabian regime.

**Child sacrifice.** Heathen nations in general offered their children by fire to their gods; against this background, the offering of a pigeon or a handful of flour in Israelite worship is a radical lightening: "Let him consider the difference between a man burning his own son in serving his god, and our burning a pigeon to the service of our God" (Ch. XLVII; citing Deut. 12:31).

## Source Texts Cited by Maimonides

Maimonides draws on at least two written sources for Sabian practice:
- ***Nabatean Agriculture*** (*Filāḥat al-Nabaṭiyya*): cited at Ch. XLI for the scattered feeding habits of oxen; referenced throughout.
- ***Tomtom*** (also *Tintom*): cited at Ch. XLVI for idolaters sacrificing wild beasts and for details of idolatrous rites.

He also references the discussion of the Asherah and the "prophets of the Asherah" elsewhere in the Guide (Ch. XLV), and the general body of Sabian literature he studied: "as far as I have seen the books on Sabean rites, nothing is mentioned of this custom" (Ch. XLVIII, regarding meat boiled in milk — which he suspects was a Sabian festival practice but cannot confirm).

## Role in Maimonides' System

The Sabians function as the historical-contextual key to the entire system of [[reasons-for-the-commandments|ta'amei ha-mitzvot]]. The governing hermeneutic is: the Torah was given in an environment saturated with Sabian belief; each commandment either prohibits a specific Sabian practice, mandates the opposite of a Sabian custom, or uses a familiar religious form (sacrifice) while redirecting it to monotheistic ends. "Most of the precepts serve... as a mere fence against those doctrines [of idolatry], and relieve man from the great and heavy burdens, from the pains and inflictions which formed part of the worship of idols" (Ch. XLIX).

The Sabians are contrasted with the Israelites not merely ritually but theologically: "our object is to lead mankind to the service of God, and to a good social order" (Ch. XLI), whereas the Sabian religious establishment "turned the mind of the people away from intellectual research and useful actions" (Ch. XLIX; citing 1 Sam. 12:21 and Jer. 16:19).

## Historical-Critical Note

Maimonides' "Sabians" are a composite drawn from his reading of Arabic encyclopedic and pseudo-historical literature (especially the *Nabatean Agriculture*, which is now believed to be a 10th-century Arabic forgery rather than an ancient Nabatean text). His account does not correspond to any single historically attested group called Sabians. Later scholarship has distinguished between the *Ṣābi'a* of the Quran (a protected monotheistic people), the Mandaeans, and the Harranians (astral polytheists of Harran, northern Syria) — the last of whom appear closest to Maimonides' description. Whether any of this source material is accurate regarding ancient Israelite religious context remains a major question in the study of Maimonides' historical method.
