# The Akali Movement (Shiromani Akali Dal and SGPC)

The **Akalis** ("the Immortals") are the political-religious movement that, since 1920, has controlled
the historic Sikh shrines through the **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC)** and acted as
the chief political vehicle of the [[sikhs|Sikh]] community through the **Shiromani Akali Dal** party.
Khushwant Singh (*A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2) treats the movement as the force that transformed the
Panth "from a community of petitioners into a disciplined political-religious force." The name revives
the martial [[nihang|Akali-Nihang]] tradition and the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] ideal. This page covers the
movement as a corporate historical actor; for the Khalsa as a religious order see [[khalsa|Khalsa]].

## Origins: from Singh Sabha Loyalism to Non-Cooperation

The [[singh-sabha|Singh Sabha]] reform had "made the Sikhs conscious of their rights," but its leaders
"were loyalists who believed in doing no more than making representations to officials or instituting
suits." Singh marks the qualitative break: "The impatience generated by the Ghadr and the nationalist
movement spurred the Sikh masses into jettisoning methods of petition… and to adopt instead the
non-cooperation (*nā milvartan*) and passive resistance of the newly formed party, the Akālīs." The
**Babbar Akalis**, a violent terrorist offshoot, "were largely recruited from the ranks of the Ghadr
party."

## The Gurdwara Reform Movement (1920–1925)

The grievance: gurdwara lands had been registered under the names of hereditary **Udasi mahants**, many
"depraved," who "converted these sacred places… to brothels and dens of gamblers." On **15 November
1920**, from the Akal Takht, a committee of 175 — the **SGPC** — was set up to manage all Sikh shrines;
the **Akali Dal**, a "semi-military corps of volunteers," was raised simultaneously to wrest gurdwaras
from recalcitrant mahants. The agitation's set-pieces, recorded by Singh:

- **Nankana Sahib massacre** (20 February 1921): at [[nankana-sahib|Nankana Sahib]], Mahant Narain Das's
  hired thugs killed an Akali *jatha* and burned the bodies on pre-collected logs — "130 men had been
  consumed by the flames." Gandhi called it "a second edition of Dyerism, more barbarous… than
  Jallianwala Bagh."
- **The Keys Affair** (1921): the government seized the Golden Temple treasury keys, then backed down —
  Gandhi's "first decisive battle won."
- **Guru ka Bagh** (1921–22): Akalis took a non-violence oath at the Akal Takht and submitted to police
  *lathi* beatings; C. F. Andrews called their conduct "Christ-like." "5,605 Akalis had been arrested,
  and 936 were hospitalized" — "the second decisive battle won."
- **Jaito Morcha** (1923–24): triggered by the deposition of the Maharaja of Nabha; 59 leaders (including
  **Master Tara Singh**) jailed for "conspiracy to wage war against the king," Jawaharlal Nehru arrested
  among them; police firing on a 500-strong *jatha* on 21 February 1924 killed (officially) 21, by Akali
  count "over 100."

The **Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925** crowned the agitation, transferring the shrines to the SGPC. Lt.-Gov.
Hailey passed it after splitting the leadership with a conditional-release offer — Mehtab Singh's faction
accepted, **Tara Singh** and Baba Kharak Singh refused as "derogatory to their self-respect," and "the
Akalis… now turned their venom against each other." The unchallenged cost given in the Punjab council:
"30,000 arrested, 400 killed and 2000 wounded, Rs 15 lacs of fine," plus a ban on Sikh military
recruitment. The SGPC became "a sort of parliament of the Sikhs… the Dal became its army," and Master
Tara Singh's faction dominated it "for the following four decades."

## The Sikh–Hindu Rift

Singh's pointed claim: because "the Hindus supported the Udasi mahants against the Akalis," the chief
outcome was "to widen further the gulf between the two communities." The Gurdwaras Act's definition of a
Sikh as one who is "not a *patit* [apostate]" — implicitly non-Hindu — was "particularly odious to the
Hindu members." See [[who-is-a-sikh|"Who is a Sikh?"]].

## Constitutional Marginalization and Partition

Through the 1920s–40s the Akalis fought a losing battle for Sikh representation (the 1932 Communal Award
gave Muslims 86 of 175 Punjab seats, Sikhs only 33) and then against the [[partition-of-punjab-1947|
Partition]]. Master Tara Singh declared the Sikhs "a separate nation" for the first time on 20 August
1944; the various Sikh-state proposals (Azad Punjab, Sikhistan, Khalistan) were, in Singh's reading,
"not… something inherently desirable, but simply… a point in an argument against Pakistan." See
[[partition-of-punjab-1947|Partition of Punjab]].

## The Punjabi Suba and After

Post-Partition the movement's central campaign was the **Punjabi Suba** — a Punjabi-speaking state which
Singh says was "in fact one for a Sikh majority state; language was only the sugar-coating." Master Tara
Singh's failed 1961 fast "killed his political career," and **Sant Fateh Singh** took over with a
linguistic framing. The state was reorganized in **1966**, gathering "nearly 80 per cent of the Sikh
population in one State," with a lasting sore over the shared capital [[punjab|Chandigarh]]. Later phases
— the [[anandpur-sahib-resolution|Anandpur Sahib Resolution]] (1973/1978), the Dharam Yudh Morcha (1982),
and the 1980s crisis around [[jarnail-singh-bhindranwale|Bhindranwale]] and
[[operation-blue-star|Operation Blue Star]] — are covered on their own pages. Singh's late verdict is
scathing: post-Partition "the quality of Sikh leadership was vulgarized," with SGPC patronage
"misused" and corruption "rampant in the gurdwaras."
