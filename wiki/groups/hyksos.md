# Hyksos

## Identity and Nomenclature

**Hyksos** is the Greek rendering of Egyptian *heqaw khasut*, "rulers of foreign
lands." They were a succession of foreign (Semitic, **Canaanite-origin**) kings based
in the eastern Nile Delta who formed Egypt's **Dynasty 15** (c. 1648–1540 BCE) during
the Second Intermediate Period (Pitard, Coogan Ch. 1; Redmount, Ch. 2). Their
Canaanite origin is established archaeologically at their capital **Avaris**
(Tell ed-Dabʿa) and the enclave at Tell el-Maskhuta.

## Primary Textual Appearances

The Hyksos appear in Egyptian records and, famously, in **Manetho** (preserved by
Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.16), who already equated their expulsion with the Exodus.
They do not appear in the Bible by name, but they are central to the modern Exodus
debate (see [[exodus]]).

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Egyptian**: foreign subjugation was a trauma "previously unknown," sending shock
  waves through the New Kingdom psyche; the native Theban Dynasties 17–18 (Ahmose)
  expelled them and founded an aggressively imperial, militarized Egypt.
- **Critical scholarship**: the Hyksos provide the **only historical parallel that
  incorporates all three elements of the Exodus narrative** — a Semitic descent into
  Egypt, sojourn/rule, and collective departure. This grounds the **16th-century
  hypothesis** for the Exodus (Josephus' equation), though most scholars favor a
  13th-century setting and treat the Hyksos as analogue rather than identity.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

Through the Middle Kingdom, Canaanite immigrants drifted into the eastern delta; as
Dynasty 13 weakened, their power grew until, in the mid-to-late 17th century, they
seized the eastern delta, then Memphis, then most of Lower and Middle Egypt, ruling
with Avaris as capital. They only partially assimilated Egyptian culture. Their
expulsion (mid-16th c.) marks the start of the New Kingdom and Egypt's imperial
expansion into Canaan. The Hyksos episode shows that **mass Asiatic movement into and
out of Egypt was historically real** — the backdrop against which the Exodus is judged
plausible even if undocumented (see [[sea-peoples]] for the later disruptions).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Hyksos do not carry biblical theology, but their history shapes the historical
frame of the Exodus traditions: the "new king who did not know Joseph" (Exod. 1.8) is
often read against the post-Hyksos native reaction, and the very absence of Egypt from
the later Exodus narrative fits a 13th-century date when Egyptian power was unraveling.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The Hyksos = Exodus equation is the oldest extant historical interpretation of the
Exodus (Manetho/Josephus) and still anchors the minority 16th-c. dating. The wider
question — whether and when an Exodus occurred, given the lack of any extrabiblical
witness — is a live controversy. See [[exodus]], [[israelites]], [[sea-peoples]],
[[philistines]]; cf. [[moses]].
