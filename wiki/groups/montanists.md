# Montanists

## Identity and Nomenclature

The Montanists — sometimes called the *New Prophecy* (*Hē Kainē Prophēteia*, their own self-designation), or *Cataphrygians*/*Phrygianism* (their origins in Phrygia, Asia Minor) — were a second-century charismatic revival movement within Christianity that claimed the Holy Spirit was currently speaking through its prophets in a new and definitive way.

Founded c.165-170 CE by Montanus in Phrygia (central Asia Minor, modern Turkey), the movement spread to North Africa, Rome, and across the Empire. Theologically they were broadly orthodox in their Christology and doctrine of God — the Muratorian Fragment condemns them but does not accuse them of Docetism or dualism. Their distinctive features were *how* Christianity was practiced, not the content of the rule of faith.

The hostile ancient designation *Cataphrygians* (people of Phrygia) and the modern name "Montanism" both come from opponents. The movement called itself simply the "New Prophecy."

## Primary Figures

### Montanus

Montanus (fl. c.165-170 CE) was a convert to Christianity who suddenly began speaking in ecstatic tongues, claiming to be the vehicle of the Holy Spirit — or, in some reports, to *be* the Paraclete promised by Jesus in John 14-16. Ancient accounts (Eusebius, *H.E.* 5.16-17) describe his utterances as delivered in an altered state of consciousness, unlike the reasoned speech of biblical prophets. He used the first-person voice: "I am the Lord God Almighty, dwelling in man" and "I am neither an angel nor a messenger, but I, God the Father, have come."

These self-descriptions were the primary theological flashpoint: was this Spirit-inspiration or demonic possession? The question of the "mode" of prophecy (ecstatic vs. rational) became a major criterion for distinguishing true from false prophecy in the church.

### Priscilla (or Prisca) and Maximilla

Two prophetesses who worked closely with Montanus. Maximilla claimed to be "word and spirit and power" and predicted continuing persecutions and the end of the world: "After me there will be no more prophecy, but the End." When the end did not come as predicted, opponents used this as a falsification argument. Both women may have left their husbands to pursue prophetic ministry — the ascetic and social disruption created by the movement was part of the orthodox concern.

### Tertullian

[[tertullian|Tertullian]] of North Africa adopted Montanist sympathies in his later career (c.207 CE onwards). His Montanist writings (*On Fasting*, *On Monogamy*, *On the Veiling of Virgins*) argue for stricter discipline: one marriage only; more severe fasting; women to be veiled. He did not abandon orthodox Christology or Trinitarian theology. His "Montanism" is better understood as enthusiastic rigorism than heterodox theology proper.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Claims

Montanists grounded their movement in several NT passages:
- John 14:16-17; 16:7-15 — the Paraclete ("Counselor," "Spirit of Truth") who would "guide you into all truth" — read as a promise of ongoing, specific Spirit-revelation
- Joel 2:28-29 / Acts 2:17 — "your sons and daughters will prophesy" — read as a present reality, not only a Pentecost fulfillment
- 1 Cor 12-14 — Paul's teaching on spiritual gifts, including prophecy, as ongoing gifts of the Spirit
- Rev 21:2 — the New Jerusalem descending at Pepuza, Phrygia — Montanists believed the New Jerusalem would descend at their base in Phrygia

The orthodox response: the Paraclete's guidance refers to the apostolic revelation (already given and preserved in the NT and rule of faith), not ongoing direct revelation beyond it. The principle of *apostolicity* — that authentic Christian teaching derives from the apostles — worked to close the canon of revelation as well as the canon of texts.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### Proto-Orthodox / Catholic Reception

Opposition developed quickly in Asia Minor — the region most directly affected and the stronghold of the Ignatian episcopal tradition. Anonymous anti-Montanist authors (preserved by Eusebius) identified several problems:
1. The mode of prophecy: ecstatic, uncontrolled speech was not the biblical prophetic pattern
2. The theological content of the oracles: Montanus' first-person divine speech claimed to speak *as* God
3. Social disruption: married women leaving husbands; prophets accepting payment; overturning household order
4. Eschatological failure: Maximilla's predictions of imminent end did not materialize

Provincial synods in Asia excommunicated the movement. Later Roman bishops (Zephyrinus, Callistus) initially sympathetic but ultimately distancing. The Muratorian Fragment (c.180) explicitly condemns "those who have recently composed a new book of psalms for Marcion, together with Basilides and the Asian founder of the Cataphrygians" — the earliest canonical list contains an explicit anti-Montanist line.

### Later Survival

The movement survived in Phrygia and North Africa for centuries:
- Tertullian's "Tertullianist" sect in Carthage persisted until Augustine's day (c.400)
- Phrygian Montanists survived into the 6th century; the Emperor Justinian reportedly ordered their suppression and they burned themselves in their churches rather than submit

### Modern Evaluation

Scholarly sympathy with Montanism increased in the 20th century, partly due to the charismatic renewal movement in mainline and Catholic Christianity. Scholars (de Labriolle, Trevett) have emphasized that the Montanists were orthodox in doctrine and that the conflict with mainstream Christianity was primarily about *authority* (who controls the prophetic voice?) and *asceticism* (how rigorous should Christian discipline be?) rather than Christology or the rule of faith.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The Prophecy Question

Montanism permanently shaped the church's theology of prophecy and the canon:
- The conflict sharpened the principle that the apostolic era was a *unique* and *closed* era of revelation — the Spirit's role after the apostles was to illuminate, not to supplement, what was given
- This contributed to the emerging sense that the canon of authoritative texts was not only closed as a list but closed as a *source of revelation* — the "sufficiency of Scripture" principle
- The explicit condemnation of Montanism in the Muratorian Fragment connects the canon debate with the prophecy debate: accepting only apostolic-era texts is part of the same move as rejecting post-apostolic claims to new revelation

### Authority and Charisma

The Montanist controversy represents the perennial conflict between *institutional* authority (bishops, who hold and protect the apostolic deposit) and *charismatic* authority (prophets, who claim direct Spirit access). Both have NT precedents. The resolution — subordinating charismatic claims to episcopal discernment and the rule of faith — shaped Catholic ecclesiology. The question whether this resolution was theologically correct or merely politically convenient continues to be debated in Pentecostal/charismatic scholarship.
