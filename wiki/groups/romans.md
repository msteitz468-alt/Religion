# Romans

The people of Rome — kingdom, republic, and empire — as treated above all in
[[augustine|Augustine]]'s [[city-of-god|*City of God*]], where they are the central historical
subject. Augustine writes in the aftermath of the **sack of Rome by Alaric's Goths (410 CE)**, against
Romans who blamed the catastrophe on the abandonment of the old gods.

## Identity and Nomenclature

Augustine treats the Romans both as the historical *populus Romanus* and as the paradigm of the
**earthly city** ([[two-cities]]) — "mistress of the nations, yet itself ruled by its lust of rule"
(*libido dominandi*). The empire is, on his account, not simply identical with the *civitas terrena*:
some Romans are or will become citizens of the city of God, the two being "entangled together in this
world… until the last judgment effects their separation" (Book I).

## Primary Textual Appearances (City of God, Books I–X)

- **Book I** — Romans who sheltered in the churches during the sack, then reproached Christ; the
  exempla of [[lucretia|Lucretia]], Cato, and Regulus.
- **Books II–III** — Rome's *moral* and *temporal* ruin under the gods before Christ.
- **Book IV** — the empire's greatness ascribed not to the swarm of petty Roman gods but to the one
  true God; "justice removed, what are kingdoms but great robberies?"
- **Book V** — Roman civic **virtue** — the "love of praise" (*gloria*) that suppressed worse
  vices — exemplified by Brutus, Torquatus, Camillus, Mucius Scaevola, Curtius, the Decii, Regulus,
  Cincinnatus, and Fabricius; God rewarded it with earthly empire.
- **Books VI–VII** — Roman **civil theology** ([[varro|Varro]]'s) and the select gods.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

**Roman / pagan self-understanding**: empire as the gift and reward of the ancestral gods, secured by
*pietas* ([[pietas]]) toward Jupiter and the civic cult.

**Augustine's Christian portrait**: the Romans achieved genuine but *merely civic* virtue, oriented to
human glory rather than God; "they have received their reward" (Matt. 6:2). Their gods gave them neither
temporal security (Books II–III) nor eternal life (Books VI–X). Yet the empire served
[[divine-providence|providence]] as an instrument, and Christian emperors (Constantine, Theodosius) show
that piety and earthly rule are compatible.

## Historical and Theological Significance

The Romans are Augustine's great test case for his thesis that earthly empire is distributed by the one
God "according to the order of things and times, which is hidden from us," not bought from gods by
worship — and for the contrast between the love of glory that builds the earthly city and the love of
God that builds the heavenly one.
