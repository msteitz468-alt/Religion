# Middle Platonists

The Middle Platonists (or "Middle Academy") represent a revival and transformation of Platonic philosophy beginning in the first century BCE, characterized by a dogmatic turn away from scepticism, a syncretistic tendency to find substantial agreement between Plato, Aristotle, and Stoicism (especially Zeno), and a markedly theistic orientation. This movement provided much of the philosophical vocabulary and conceptual framework that Hellenistic Judaism (above all Philo of Alexandria) and early Christian theology used to articulate the relation between the transcendent God and the created world, the status of the Logos, and the interpretation of Scripture.

## Identity and Nomenclature

The label "Middle Platonism" is modern. Ancient writers spoke of a revival of "genuine Platonism" (as opposed to the sceptical Academy of Arcesilaus and Carneades). The key initiator was **Antiochus of Ascalon** (c. 130–68 BCE), who around 80 BCE broke with the sceptical tradition and claimed that the true Platonic doctrine agreed in essentials with both Peripatetic (Aristotelian) and Stoic teaching. The movement continued through figures such as Plutarch of Chaeronea, Albinus (or Alcinous, author of the *Didaskalikos*), and Numenius of Apamea, and extended into the second and early third centuries CE.

Characteristic features include:
- Reassertion of the transcendent reality of Plato's Forms/Ideas (against Stoic reduction of them to mental conceptions).
- A theistic reading of the *Timaeus* Craftsman (Demiurge) as a supreme or near-supreme divine figure.
- Growing interest in intermediary principles (daimones, the World-Soul, or a Logos-like entity) that mediate between the highest God and the material cosmos.
- Use of allegory and symbolic interpretation, especially when applying philosophy to religious texts.

## Primary Textual Appearances

The Middle Platonists are known primarily through later reports, fragments, and surviving treatises rather than a single canonical corpus. Key loci include:
- Antiochus' teaching (reconstructed from Cicero and others).
- Plutarch's *Moralia* (especially theological and Platonic essays).
- The handbook tradition (Albinus/Alcinous).
- Numenius' fragments (preserved in Eusebius and others), which exerted strong influence on both pagan Neoplatonism and Christian thought.
- Most importantly for the religious traditions, the extensive corpus of **Philo of Alexandria**, who is treated by Stead as the outstanding Jewish representative of this philosophical milieu.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

**Hellenistic/Jewish (Philo)**: Philo stands as the premier example of a Jewish thinker who fully adopted the Middle Platonic framework to expound the Torah. He portrays the Logos as God's "image," "firstborn," or even "second God" — an intermediary through whom the transcendent, simple God creates and governs the world without direct contact with matter. Allegorical exegesis allows Philo to find Platonic metaphysics (Forms as thoughts in the mind of God or in the Logos, ascent of the soul, etc.) already present in Genesis and the rest of the Pentateuch. Philo's synthesis was enormously influential on Christian Alexandrian thought while remaining firmly anchored in Jewish Scripture and practice.

**Christian patristic**: Early Christian writers (Justin Martyr, Clement of Alexandria, Origen) inherited the Middle Platonic conceptual toolkit via Philo and the broader philosophical culture of Alexandria and the Eastern Mediterranean. They adapted the Logos doctrine to identify the pre-existent Christ with the mediating principle, while insisting on the full divinity and eventual incarnation of the Logos — a step Philo never took. The theistic and hierarchical tendencies of Middle Platonism helped shape subordinationist tendencies in some early Christian theology (later corrected at Nicaea and by the Cappadocians), as well as the strong emphasis on divine transcendence and simplicity. Stead notes that the Middle Platonic revival supplied much of the "philosophical background" that made the sophisticated Trinitarian and Christological formulations of the fourth and fifth centuries conceptually possible.

**Later and critical**: Neoplatonism (Plotinus onward) both continued and critiqued Middle Platonic syncretism, reasserting a stricter hierarchy (the One beyond Intellect). Christian theologians continued to draw selectively on the tradition while increasingly distinguishing their scriptural and ecclesial commitments from autonomous philosophical enquiry (see the "debate about Christian philosophy").

## Historical and Archaeological Context

The movement arose in the late Hellenistic period amid the fragmentation of the classical schools and the need for more dogmatic, religiously satisfying systems. It coincided with the rise of mystery cults, the continuing prestige of Plato's Academy (now in its "Middle" phase), and the increasing contact between Greek philosophy and Near Eastern religious traditions (including Judaism in Alexandria, the largest Jewish diaspora community of the period). Material evidence is limited; our knowledge rests on literary testimonia. Alexandria was a major center for this synthesis (see [[alexandria|Alexandria]]).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Middle Platonism supplied the dominant philosophical grammar for expressing:
- The transcendence and simplicity of God alongside genuine relation to the world.
- The role of a mediating Logos or divine reason in creation and revelation.
- The allegorical or spiritual reading of sacred texts as containing hidden philosophical truth.

For Judaism (Philo) it offered a way to present the Torah as the true philosophy. For Christianity it provided tools to defend the faith intellectually against pagan critics and to develop the doctrines of the Trinity and Incarnation in conversation with the best contemporary thought — while the biblical commitment to a personal, creating, incarnate God pushed beyond the limits of the philosophical systems themselves.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The Middle Platonic synthesis (especially through Philo) is a major source for the patristic Logos theology that runs from Justin and Clement through Origen to the Nicene settlement and beyond. It also feeds later Christian Platonism (Augustine, the Cappadocians' more apophatic and theistic developments, and the Dionysian tradition). The same conceptual world underlies many of the debates Stead analyzes in Part II (divine simplicity, immutability and the Incarnation, the precise meaning of ousia and hypostasis).

Modern scholarship (including Stead) continues to debate the precise degree of originality versus borrowing in early Christian theology and the extent to which Middle Platonic assumptions (e.g., a sharp matter/spirit dualism or hierarchical emanation) helped or hindered the articulation of distinctively Christian claims.

## Augustine and the Platonists in the City of God

[[augustine|Augustine]]'s [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Books VIII–X give the fullest Christian
engagement with the Platonist tradition (here shading into Neoplatonism). Augustine judges the
[[plato|Platonists]] "nearest of all to us" — they grasped God as immaterial, immutable cause of being,
truth, and goodness, and the soul's blessedness as participation in him. Yet they fatally conceded
worship to many gods and made **demons the mediators** between gods and men: [[apuleius|Apuleius]]'s
*De Deo Socratis* (Books VIII–IX) and [[porphyry|Porphyry]]'s theurgy (Book X) are his chief targets,
with [[plotinus|Plotinus]] quoted respectfully throughout. The Platonists' tragedy, for Augustine, is to
have glimpsed the Logos yet rejected the one incarnate Mediator out of pride.
