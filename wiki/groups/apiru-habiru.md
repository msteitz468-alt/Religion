# Apiru / Habiru

## Identity and Nomenclature

The **Apiru** (also Habiru/Hapiru; Sumerogram SA.GAZ) were **not an ethnic group but
a social class** — found across the entire Fertile Crescent through the second
millennium BCE, with no common ethnic or national affiliation and no common language
(Pitard, Coogan Ch. 1; Redmount, Ch. 2). They were "uprooted, propertyless persons"
(Moshe Greenberg) living on the margins of settled society: dependents, hired
laborers, mercenaries, slaves, and — in hard times — gangs of bandits and freebooters
under a warlord. In the Amarna letters the term is decidedly **pejorative**.

The long-debated equation of *Apiru* with Hebrew *ʿibrî* ("Hebrew") electrified early
scholarship, which thought it had found the invading Israelites of Joshua. Wider finds
showed the Apiru were everywhere and ethnically diverse; scholarly opinion remains
**divided** on the etymology, though many think the Apiru were *one component* of
proto-Israel.

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **The Amarna letters** (14th c. BCE): vassal kings accuse one another of "going over
  to the Apiru." [[jerusalem|Abdi-Hepa of Jerusalem]] warns the pharaoh that "the land
  of the king has gone over to the Apiru." Warlords like Abdi-ashirta of Amurru built
  power bases by organizing the Apiru of the Lebanese mountains.
- **Compared to the Bible**: the biblical "Hebrews" (ʿibrîm), and figures such as
  **Jephthah** (Judg. 11) and **David** before his kingship — outlaw warlords leading
  bands of the dispossessed — fit the Apiru profile exactly.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **"Peasants' revolt" model** (Mendenhall, Gottwald): early Israel = oppressed
  Canaanite peasants who withdrew from the city-states to the highlands, i.e. an Apiru
  phenomenon, with Yahwism as catalyst or product of the revolt. Coogan finds this
  model only *partially* explanatory (it cannot account for the equally egalitarian
  villages outside Israel, in Moab, Ammon, Edom). See [[israelites]].

## Historical and Social Context

Apiru appear in texts from Mari, Nuzi, Ugarit, Hatti, and Egypt, "frequently
encountered as outlaws, mercenaries, and slaves." Their proliferation in the Amarna
age tracks the breakdown of Late Bronze city-state authority — the same disintegration
that opened the highlands to new settlement and the emergence of Israel.

## Significance for Israelite Origins

The Apiru are a key term in every reconstruction of how Israel arose. Whether or not
"Hebrew" derives from "Apiru," the social *type* — propertyless people slipping out of
state control into the frontier — is woven into the biblical self-image (Israel as
former slaves, David as outlaw chieftain). Coogan's preferred **ruralization** model
folds Apiru-type withdrawal into a broader picture of decentralization.

## Controversies

The Apiru = ʿibrî debate is unresolved. The wider question — what social processes
produced "Israel" — is recorded under [[israelites]] and connects to
[[herem-canaanite-conquest]]. Related: [[canaanites]], [[shasu|Shasu]] (see
[[midianites]]), [[jephthah]], [[david]].
