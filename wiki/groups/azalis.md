# Azalis

Those Bábís who followed [[subh-i-azal|Subh-i-Azal]] (Mírzá Yahyá) and denied Bahá'u'lláh's claim to be He Whom God Shall Make Manifest (line 4474–4476).

## Identity and History

During the 1860s and 1870s the great majority of the Bábís became Bahá'ís, leaving a minority of "Azali" Bábís in several cities. Over the years their numbers diminished, with some becoming Bahá'ís and others Muslims (lines 4477–4481).

Azal had originally intended one of his sons to be his successor, but following a family dispute he appointed Hádí Dawlatábádí in his stead. By the 1970s there were perhaps only 500–5,000 Azalis left in Iran, seemingly with no formal leadership (lines 4482–4489).

One of Azal's grandsons, Jalál Azal (d. 1971), was involved in an attempt to bring together various groups of "Covenant-breakers" during the 1950s (lines 4490–4494).

## Notable Members

Despite their small numbers, the Azalis included several prominent Iranian political and literary figures, notably Shaykh Ahmad Rúhí and Mírzá Aqá Khán Kirmání, both sons-in-law of Azal, as well as supporters of Sayyid Jamálu'd-Dín "al-Afghání," both executed following the assassination of Násiru'd-Dín Sháh (1896) (lines 4495–4505).
