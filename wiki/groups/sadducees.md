# Sadducees

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Sadducee" (*Tzaduki*) — the etymology is disputed. The most common scholarly proposal derives
the name from *Tzadok* (Zadok), the Solomonic high priest whose line held the Jerusalem
priesthood; this would make the Sadducees essentially the "Zadokite party." Some propose the
name from *tzedek* (righteousness). The Sadducees were one of the three major Jewish "philosophies"
of the Second Temple period alongside the Pharisees and Essenes.

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **NT Gospels and Acts**: The Sadducees appear primarily in disputes over the resurrection
  (Matt 22:23-33; Mark 12:18-27; Luke 20:27-40) — they denied resurrection, and Jesus'
  answer implicitly sides with the Pharisaic position. In Acts 23:6-10, Paul exploits the
  Sadducee/Pharisee split over resurrection to divide the Sanhedrin.
- **Josephus** (*Jewish Antiquities*, *Jewish War*): Describes Sadducees as one of three
  Jewish philosophical schools; notes their rejection of fate/providence, their aristocratic
  character, and their acceptance of only the Written Torah.
- **Mishna**: References Sadducean legal positions contrasted with Pharisaic rulings, usually
  to establish the Pharisaic norm as authoritative.

## Tradition-Specific Portrait

### Unterman's Analysis (Internal Jewish Perspective)

[[unterman-talmud|Unterman]] (Ch. IX) characterizes the Sadducees primarily through their
socio-political and theological contrast with the Pharisees:

**Theological positions**:
- Accepted only the **Written Torah** (Pentateuch) — no Oral Torah, no tradition of the
  elders. This placed them at direct odds with Pharisaic Judaism's claim that an oral
  tradition coexisted with the written law from Sinai.
- Denied **divine providence** in day-to-day affairs; denied **resurrection of the dead**;
  denied **reward and punishment** in an afterlife.
- Methodologically literalist — interpreted the law by the plain text of the Pentateuch
  only, without the interpretive flexibility of Pharisaic Halacha. Unterman notes that
  opponents of capital punishment and its proponents both claimed to stand on the
  Pentateuch's literal text; the Pharisees used their hermeneutical methods to practically
  abolish it.

**Socio-political character**:
- The **aristocratic party** of the priestly class, the wealthy, and those allied with
  the Temple establishment and Hasmonean/Roman authority.
- Their power base was entirely Temple-dependent. When the Second Temple fell (70 CE),
  the Sadducees lost their institutional foundation and vanished from history.
- In contrast to the Pharisees (common people; synagogue-centered; mobile), the Sadducees
  could not survive the Temple's destruction.

**The "eye for an eye" controversy**: Unterman notes that the Sadducees and Karaites both
insisted on the literal reading of *lex talionis* ("an eye for an eye" = actual physical
retaliation), while the Pharisees definitively interpreted it as monetary compensation. The
Pharisaic position became normative Halacha (Baba Kamma 83b).

## Historical and Archaeological Context

The Sadducees' origins are placed in the Hasmonean period (2nd c. BCE). They were the ruling
priestly party of the Jerusalem Temple and controlled the high-priestly offices. Their
cooperation with Hellenistic culture (and later with Rome) brought them into conflict with
the more populist Pharisees.

Josephus notes that the Sadducees' rulings were accepted only by the wealthy and influential;
the common people preferred the Pharisees. The Sadducees had to defer to Pharisaic customs
in practice because of the people's preference.

## Theological Significance

The Sadducees serve in the NT narrative as the representatives of Temple-based, establishment
Judaism — the party most directly aligned with the political and priestly power that condemned
Jesus. In Acts, they are particularly hostile to the resurrection proclamation because it is
precisely the doctrine they deny.

Within Judaism, the Sadducean-Pharisaic dispute over Oral Torah is foundational: the entire
edifice of Rabbinic Judaism is built on the Pharisaic affirmation that the written text cannot
be read without its accompanying oral tradition.

## France on the Sadducees in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], [[france-mark-nigtc|*The Gospel
of Mark* (NIGTC)]]. All interpretive claims are France's unless another scholar is named.*

- **The resurrection challenge (Mark 12:18-27)**: France explains Sadducean rejection of
  resurrection by their conservative scriptural authority (Torah primacy, rejection of oral
  tradition), noting that "probably only two passages in the OT clearly express a belief in
  resurrection and life after death (Is. 26:19; Dn. 12:2)," the belief growing from the second
  century B.C. in apocalyptic and Maccabean-martyr traditions. For the Sadducees, "as for most
  of the OT writers, [[sheol|Sheol]] was a final resting place," continuity lying in reputation
  and posterity rather than personal survival.
- France argues Jesus' Exod 3:6 reply is not a simplistic tense argument (no verb stands in the
  Hebrew or in Mark's quotation) but "a reflection on the character of the covenant God" who
  identifies himself by the patriarchs' names; this is the only pericope where Jesus directly
  addresses the afterlife, and his support for the "[[pharisees|Pharisaic]]" view is
  "unequivocal," with 12:25 excluding marriage (not love) from a resurrection life like that of
  "angels in heaven."
- **In the passion narrative**: France identifies the elders–chief priests–scribes list (8:31;
  11:27; 14:43, 53; 15:1) as the three Sanhedrin power groups — the chief-priestly
  (Sadducean-aligned) establishment central among them — making the passion predictions "a
  comprehensive rejection of Jesus by all the leading representatives of God's people Israel."
  Following D. L. Bock, he treats the night hearing before the (Sadducee-dominated) Sanhedrin
  as "never seen or intended as a formal Jewish capital case, but a kind of preliminary
  hearing" to build a case for Rome — so the mishnaic-illegality debate is beside the point,
  and it is questionable whether the Mishnah's rules (codified c. A.D. 200) even applied
  c. A.D. 30.
- In an endnote (via Hoehner) France remarks that [[herod-antipas|Antipas]] "is unlikely to have
  shared Pharisaic views of an afterlife" — an incidental placement of the Herodian court
  nearer the Sadducean end of the afterlife spectrum.

## After 70 CE

The Sadducees ceased to exist as a distinct party after the Temple's destruction. The Karaite
movement (8th c. CE) is sometimes seen as a partial revival of the Sadducean principle
(Written Torah only), though this identification is disputed.
