# Marcionites

> **Group page** — the Marcionite community as a social/historical actor. For Marcionism as
> a *system* (its two-god doctrine, subtractive canon, hermeneutics, relationship to
> orthodoxy), see the sect page [[marcionism|Marcionism]].

## Identity and Nomenclature

The Marcionites were the followers of [[marcion|Marcion of Sinope]], who after his expulsion from the Roman church c.144 CE founded his own church organization. The movement was self-consciously a separate institution — not a school within the church (like the Valentinians) but a rival church with its own bishops, presbyters, deacons, baptism, and Eucharist.

[[tertullian|Tertullian]] is the primary source for the extent of Marcionism: "Marcion's heretical tradition has filled the whole world" (*Adv. Marc.* 5.19). Writing c.207-212 CE, Tertullian describes Marcionite communities from the Euphrates to Britain. This is polemical hyperbole, but it confirms substantial geographic spread.

## Primary Textual Appearances

Marcionites do not appear in the NT, though [[marcion|Marcion's]] hermeneutical approach — reading Paul's anti-law letters in isolation from the OT and from the other Gospels — is a creative misreading of Paul's actual argument. The movement shaped the reception history of several NT texts:
- **Galatians**: Marcion placed it first in his Apostolikon, highlighting its anti-Judaizing polemic
- **Luke**: The single Gospel in Marcion's Evangelikon; his edited version is the primary evidence for both his theology and, by comparison, the canonical text's integrity

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### Proto-Orthodox / Patristic Account

Major refutations:
- **Justin Martyr** (*First Apology* 26; 58): earliest mention; says Marcion teaches from a demon
- **Irenaeus** (*Against Heresies* 1.27; 3.12; 4.33-34): characterizes Marcion as cutting the Scriptures; the heresy of "two gods"
- **Tertullian** (*Against Marcion*, 5 volumes c.207-212): most extensive; Book 4 is a verse-by-verse comparison of Marcion's Gospel with canonical Luke; Book 5 covers Marcion's Pauline letters
- **Origen** (*Commentary on Luke*; *Against Marcion* — lost but known from quotation): philosophical refutation
- **Epiphanius** (*Panarion* 42): later; includes much of Tertullian

### The Marcionite Church's Structure

Marcionites organized their community as a parallel church structure — bishops, presbyters, deacons. This distinguishes them from Valentinians (who generally remained within and alongside orthodox communities). Notable features from patristic accounts:
- Strict asceticism: no marriage, no meat, fasting
- Baptism: offered a second baptism to the dying (possibly re-baptism; accounts vary)
- Eucharist: celebrated with water, not wine — an ascetic practice
- Open to women in leadership roles (Tertullian's charge: "women among the Marcionites are bold enough to teach, to dispute, to exercise exorcisms, to undertake cures")

## Historical Context

The Marcionite church persisted as a significant rival to proto-orthodox Christianity for several centuries:
- 2nd c.: Rapidly spread westward (Rome → Gaul → North Africa) and eastward (Syria → Mesopotamia)
- 3rd c.: Competitive with Manichaeism in the East; Mani's dualism shared structural features and may have absorbed Marcionite communities
- 4th-5th c.: Still present in Syria; inscriptions from Syrian villages record Marcionite dedications
- 6th c.: Still mentioned in Byzantine heresy lists; probably absorbed into Manichaeism or died out

Justin Martyr's account (c.150 CE) is the earliest, which means the Marcionite church was a major presence within two decades of Marcion's Rome excommunication (c.144).

## Theological Profile

### Two Gods

The defining Marcionite position: the God of the OT is a just but limited creator-god (Demiurge), not the Father of Jesus Christ. The Father revealed by Jesus is the "alien" or "strange" God who was previously unknown — supreme in goodness, without wrath or judgment, purely gracious. Marcion's *Antitheses* documented these contrasts from the texts themselves.

### Docetic Christology

The Marcionite Christ had no real material body — a phantasm, not a real human being. He "appeared" (*phantasia*) in the synagogue in Capernaum fully formed, not born. The physical suffering on the cross was apparently real in Marcion's account (unlike later Docetism which denied even the crucifixion), but the body was not material in an ordinary sense.

### Rejection of the OT

The OT reliably describes the Demiurge, not the Father. It is therefore *not* Christian Scripture. Paul's warning in Galatians about those who preach "another gospel" — and the Jerusalem apostles' compromised gospel — was read by Marcion as evidence that the original apostles had corrupted the pure gospel of Jesus. Only Paul received and transmitted the authentic gospel.

### Paul as the Only Apostle

Paul alone, for Marcion, preserved the unadulterated gospel. The other apostles (Peter, James, John) taught a Judaizing, law-keeping version of Christianity. This reading of intra-apostolic conflict as evidence for the corruption of the original gospel (rather than evidence of Paul's theological creativity in addressing specific situations) is the defining Marcionite hermeneutic.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The Canon Question

As noted under [[nt-canon-formation|NT Canon Formation]]: Marcion's canon (Evangelikon + Apostolikon) is the earliest known *closed* Christian canon list. It is critical evidence for the pre-existence of a larger Christian literary corpus, which Marcion reduced rather than created. The orthodox response — to articulate explicitly which texts were Scripture — was clarified (not created) by Marcion's challenge.

### The Paul Interpretation Debate

Marcion's radical Paulinism raised a question that has never entirely gone away: how should the anti-law passages in Paul (Gal 2-4; Romans 7; Phil 3:2-11) be read? Are they evidence of a deep rift between Paul and the Jerusalem church (Harnack's reading, echoed by parts of the "New Perspective")? Or are they situation-specific arguments about the role of Torah observance as the basis for *justification*, not rejections of the OT God or of Jewish identity (Sanders, Dunn, Wright)?

The anti-Marcionite response forced the church to develop a hermeneutic for reading Paul *within* the context of the broader canon and the rule of faith — a "canonical Paul" embedded in a larger narrative framework.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

- **Manichaeism**: Mani's teaching (3rd c.) shows possible Marcionite influence, especially the dualistic cosmology and the elevation of Paul
- **Modern resonance**: Marcion's "canon within the canon" move (elevating Paul; minimizing OT) is structurally similar to various Protestant tendencies identified by critics as "crypto-Marcionism" — the charge that certain Protestant approaches functionally subordinate the OT to Paul's letters
- The question "was Marcion right about Paul?" continues to generate scholarly discussion in NT studies
