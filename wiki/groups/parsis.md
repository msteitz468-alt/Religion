# Parsis (the Zoroastrians of India)

The **Parsis** ("Persians") are the [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]] community of India,
descended from [[persians|Iranians]] who emigrated after the **Arab Muslim conquest**
ended Zoroastrian rule (the death of Yazdegird III, 651 CE). Today, with the modern
Iranian Zoroastrians and the global diaspora, they are the principal living bearers of
the religion — and, since the nineteenth century, the chief sponsors of its scholarship.
Per [[rose-zoroastrianism-an-introduction|Rose]], "Parsi" became a **legal and ethnic**
category as much as a religious one, which is why the community wrestles continually with
the question **"Who is a Zoroastrian — and who is a Parsi?"**

## Identity and Nomenclature

*Parsi* derives from *Parsa* (Persia). The community distinguishes itself from the
**Iranis** — Iranian Zoroastrians who immigrated in the eighteenth–twentieth centuries
(many fleeing persecution, and after 1979 the revolution) and who keep a divergent
calendar and cuisine. Many modern adherents prefer the self-designation **Zarathushti**
(and the religion *Zarathushti Din*) precisely to transcend the Parsi/Irani ethnic split.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Foundation Story

The oldest account of the migration is the **[[rose-zoroastrianism-an-introduction|*Qesse-ye
Sanjan*]]** ("Story of Sanjan"), composed in Persian verse by the priest Bahman Kaikobad
in **1599**. It narrates refugees fleeing via Khorasan and Hormuz to **Diu** and then to
**Sanjan** in Gujarat, where the Hindu ruler **Jadi Rana** granted asylum on conditions
(adopt the local language and dress, wed in the evening, lay down arms). Their first act
was to consecrate an **Atash Bahram** ("Victorious Fire"), the **Iran Shah** ("King of
Iran"), which moved Sanjan → Navsari → and (1742) to **Udwada**, where it still burns —
the emblem of Parsi continuity with Iran. Dating is debated (mid-7th to early-10th c.);
Parsis traditionally favor **936 CE**, and excavation at Sanjan points to 8th-century
settlement on a Sasanian-era trading post.

The **Persian Rivayats** (15th–18th c.) — letters of inquiry from the Parsis to the
Iranian priests of Yazd and Kerman — re-established contact between the two communities
(the Iranians were astonished to learn co-religionists survived in India) and record
their shared concerns: calendar, ritual, purity, the *kusti*, disposal of the dead, and
the life of [[zarathustra|Zarathushtra]].

## Tradition-Specific Portraits and Adaptations

Settling among Hindus and later Muslims, the Parsis **assimilated selectively** while
retaining the Iranian core:

- They presented their faith to Jadi Rana by stressing **shared ground** with Hinduism
  (reverence for sun, moon, water, fire, and the cow; a sacred girdle; menstrual
  seclusion).
- They **gave up cow sacrifice and beef** (for Hindu neighbors) and **pork** (for
  Muslims), and by the early twentieth century had largely abandoned animal sacrifice
  altogether (the Parsi Vegetarian and Temperance Society).
- They adopted **endogamy** functioning like a caste, the **sari**, the **Gujarati**
  vernacular, and minor Hindu ritual elements (*kumkum*, coconuts), without altering
  underlying belief.
- India is the **only** country where Zoroastrians still practice **dakhmenashini** —
  exposure of the dead in the *dakhma* ("Tower of Silence," a British-coined term) —
  though the collapse of the vulture population now imperils it.

Three grades of consecrated fire (**Atash Bahram, Atash Adaran, Atash Dadgah**) and three
priestly grades (**dastur, mobed, herbad**) are maintained; Navsari has been the chief
center of priestly authority, organized into the five **panthak** families.

## Historical and Colonial Development

Under the **Mughals**, Emperor **Akbar** summoned the dastur Meherji Rana (1579) and
folded Zoroastrian elements (calendar names, sun/fire veneration) into his syncretic
**Din-i Ilahi**. Under the **British East India Company**, Parsi trading skill and the
absence of caste barriers to dealing with Europeans propelled a dramatic rise: the
**Wadias** became master shipbuilders at Bombay; families such as the **Jeejeebhoys**
pioneered modern philanthropy ("Charity, thy name is Parsi"). High literacy, schools,
hospitals, and a powerful **Bombay Parsi Punchayat (BPP)** followed.

### Internal Controversies

- **Calendar schism**: the *Qadimi* ("ancient," following Iranian usage), the *Shehenshai*
  (traditionalist majority), and the later *Fasli* ("seasonalist," Nav Ruz on 21 March).
- **The Wilson controversy (1830s–40s)**: the missionary **John Wilson** attacked
  Zoroastrian "dualism" and "polytheism" and the credibility of the prophet, exposing
  that lay and priestly Parsis lacked the philological tools to answer; this **galvanized
  a Parsi scholarly revival** (K.R. Cama; priestly colleges).
- **Reform vs. tradition**: the German philologist **Martin Haug** supplied a
  **Gatha-only "ethical monotheism,"** dismissing the *yazatas* and ritual as later
  corruption — adopted by reformers (Naoroji's Rahnumae Mazdayasne Sabha; the "Protestant
  dastur" **M.N. Dhalla**) and resisted by traditionalists, **fragmenting** the community
  over the authority and canon of scripture.
- **Ilm-i Khshnoom**: an esoteric, theosophy-tinged "Zoroastrian gnosis" (Behramshah
  Shroff, from 1907) reading fire and ritual as living cosmic energies.
- **The 1908 *obiter dicta***: Justice Davar's Bombay High Court ruling defined a **Parsi
  patrilineally** (excluding the spouses/children of intermarried Parsi *women*) while
  conceding that the religion "permits, and enjoins, conversion" — the legal root of the
  still-unresolved **conversion and intermarriage** disputes.

## Modern Community, Diaspora, and Decline

Trade across the British Empire scattered Parsis to **Britain** (the first three Indian
MPs were all Parsis — Naoroji, Bhownagree, Saklatvala; Freddie Mercury, b. Bulsara, is
buried at Brookwood), **China, Hong Kong, East Africa, Aden, Sind**, and later **North
America, Australia, and the Gulf**. Organizations include the **BPP**, the European
**ZTFE**, North American **FEZANA**, the **WZO** and the anti-assimilationist **WAPIZ**.
India still holds the largest population (~70,000) but it is **aging and shrinking**
(emigration, low birth rate, intermarriage), which lends urgency to the modern fault
lines — **traditionalist / neo-traditionalist / reformist**, birth vs. belief — that now
play out globally and online. See the [[zoroastrianism|tradition page]] for the wider
"Who is a Zoroastrian?" debate.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Parsis embody Zoroastrian resilience: a refugee minority that **kept the fire
literally burning** (the repeated trope of rescuing a sacred fire) through a millennium
of subaltern status, and that — through colonial-era wealth and education — became the
community most responsible for **preserving, translating, and contesting** the religion's
texts. Their negotiation of identity under successive majority cultures is itself a case
study in how a tradition survives diaspora.
