# Christians (as the Romans saw them)

This page records how Christians appeared to Roman and Greek observers, beginning with the first Roman mention of the movement (Pliny the Younger, c. 112 CE) through the fourth century. It is structured to receive additional tradition-specific portraits as other pagan observers (Galen, Celsus, Porphyry, Julian) are ingested. The Christian self-understanding is treated separately; this page tracks external perception.

**Source for this entry**: Robert Louis Wilken, *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them* (Introduction and Chapters I–II, range ll. 300–2182 of the OCR'd text).

## Identity and Nomenclature

The name *Christiani* (Latin) / *Christianoi* (Greek) was coined by outsiders, not by the movement itself. As Wilken notes: "This term, *Christianus*, which would become the characteristic name for the followers of Jesus, was first used by outsiders (Acts 11:26). Pliny, too, calls them *Christiani*, identifying them by reference to their founder, just as the followers of Pythagoras were called Pythagoreans, the followers of Epicurus, Epicureans, the worshipers of Dionysus, Dionysiacs" (Wilken, Ch. II, ll. 39–44 within range).

Christians themselves used the term *ekklesia* (assembly/church) for their communities. Romans would have found this term puzzling: in common Greek and Latin usage *ekklesia* referred to the political assembly of a city, not a religious sect. When Pliny wrote to Trajan shortly after his encounter with Christians, he referred to the "local *boule* and *ekklesia*"—the civic council and assembly—in an entirely political sense (Ep. 10.11; Wilken, ll. 49–50).

## Primary Textual Appearances (Roman Sources)

### Pliny the Younger, *Epistulae* 10.96–97 (c. 112 CE)

The first mention of Christianity in any Roman writer. Pliny, governing Bithynia-Pontus, received complaints (probably from merchants in the sacrificial-meat trade) about Christians living in the vicinity. He described them as a *superstitio* and a *hetaeria* (political club/association). His investigations found that they:

- Met "regularly before dawn on a fixed day to chant verses alternately among themselves in honor of Christ *as if to a god*"
- Bound themselves by oath to abstain from theft, robbery, adultery, breach of trust, and denial of deposits
- Shared a common meal of "ordinary harmless kind" (Ep. 10.96)

Pliny estimated that at this early date Christians were drawn chiefly from "humble backgrounds, freedmen and slaves, working people engaged in menial tasks, artisans" (Wilken, ll. 303–305). He treated the group as distinct from Jews: "There is no hint that the Christians had anything to do with Jews or that they came from Jewish background . . . Pliny treated the Christians as an independent sect" (ll. 300–302).

### Tacitus and Suetonius (contemporary with Pliny)

Both used the term *superstitio* for Christianity—confirming that Pliny's characterization was not idiosyncratic but reflected "conventional attitudes" in educated Roman society. Tacitus is mentioned briefly in this range; his fuller account (in *Annales* on Nero's persecution) is introduced at the opening of Chapter III but falls outside the present scope.

### Lucian, *Peregrinus* (second century)

The satirist Lucian used the term *thiasarches* (head of a *thiasos*, a religious association) to describe the leader of the Christian group who worship "the man who was crucified in Palestine" (*Peregr.* 11; Wilken, ll. 453–460). He portrayed Christians as gullible and easily exploited.

### Celsus (c. 180 CE)

Celsus, whose work survives through Origen's *Contra Celsum*, used the language of associations to describe Christianity: Christians formed "associations contrary to the laws" (*c. Cels.* 1.1). Instead of joining public religious rites, they carried on their affairs "in the fashion of an **obscure and secret association**" (*c. Cels.* 8.17; Wilken, ll. 468–473). Celsus is treated in detail in later chapters outside this scope.

## Tradition-Specific Portrait: The Roman/Pagan Portrait

### Christianity as a *Collegium* / *Hetaeria* / Burial Society

The primary Roman frame for categorizing Christianity in the second century was that of the **voluntary association** (*collegium*, *hetaeria*, *thiasos*). These associations were a familiar feature of Roman urban life from at least the third century BCE—professional guilds, funerary societies, religious cult clubs—and served similar social functions: communal meals, mutual aid, decent burial for members, religious rites honoring a patron deity, and a sense of belonging for those excluded from elite civic life.

Wilken identifies three main types of association (ll. 150–158):
1. **Professional corporations** (shipowners, fruit merchants, wool-workers)
2. **Funerary/burial societies** (*collegia funeraticia*): chief purpose was providing burial expenses and decent burial for members
3. **Religious societies** devoted to a particular deity (Bacchus, Isis, Heracles)

In practice most associations combined all three functions. The Christian communities, observed from outside, fit this pattern: they "met regularly for a common meal; [had] their own ritual of initiation, rules, and standards for members; when the group came together, the members heard speeches and celebrated a religious rite involving offerings of wine, prayers, and hymns; and certain members of the group were elected to serve as officers" (Wilken, ll. 437–442). "Like the followers of Heracles who were called Heraclists, the devotees of Asclepius called Asclepiasts, or the followers of Isis called Isiacs, the Christians were called *Christiani*" (ll. 445–447).

The Roman social historian Jean Gagé is quoted: the Christian communities "offered at first glance an astonishing resemblance to a type of fraternal association, namely the **funerary or burial society**" (ll. 448–450).

### The Iobacchoi (Bacchic Society) as Parallel

Wilken's closest structural parallel is the inscription of the Iobacchoi (a Dionysiac association) at Athens, dated to shortly before 178 CE. Like the Christian communities, this association had: formal admission procedures (a vote on candidates, entrance fees), regular meetings with set rituals, a communal drink-offering and "sermon," officers, penalties for disruptive members, and provisions for honoring members at death (Wilken, ll. 322–432). Wilken draws explicit comparison: both the Christian meeting and the Bacchic society had to regulate disputes (compare 1 Cor. 6 and 11:18–21 with the Iobacchoi statutes, ll. 380–397).

### The Sociological Reading: Belonging and Social Status

Wilken emphasizes that the associations drew their membership not from the elite—who had other outlets—but from "craftsmen and artisans, merchants and shopkeepers, some of whom were freedmen or slaves" (ll. 122–127). The wealthy scorned manual labor and trade (Cicero's ranking: even less respectable than custom-collecting and usury were "fishmongers, butchers, cooks, poulterers," ll. 140–141). The associations gave this class "a sense of belonging" and the means to "develop an organization, which at once cultivated social feeling, heightened their self-respect, and guarded their collective interest" (Samuel Dill, quoted ll. 147–149). A member of such a society, however humble, could hold an office, receive public recognition, and be confident of a decent burial.

This sociological reading frames why Christianity appeared to Roman observers as simply another such society: its membership profile, communal structures, and social functions matched the pattern of the *collegium* exactly.

### Christians as Politically Suspect: the *Hetaeria* Frame

Pliny categorized the Christians under the same term—*hetaeria*—he used for a proposed firemen's association that Trajan had refused to license. The problem with all *hetaeriae* was that they "had the potential of becoming political and thereby of disturbing the life of a city" (ll. 71–72). Trajan's standing policy was that any gathering, however innocent its stated purpose, tended to "turn into a political club" (Ep. 10.34). Christianity was not uniquely singled out; it fell under a general Roman anxiety about private associations.

### Christianity's Self-Presentation Within the Association Framework

Tertullian, writing in defense of Christianity to outsiders (*Apologeticum*), self-consciously adopted the *collegium* vocabulary: calling the Christian community *factio Christiana*, *corpus*, *secta Dei*, *coitio Christianorum*, *curia*—and describing its common chest, regular meetings, official leaders, and festive meals (Wilken, ll. 484–536). Wilken's point: Tertullian's willingness to use this frame confirms that "when the Christian movement began to appear to public view in the second century it was perceived by outsiders as a religious association or burial society" (ll. 540–543).

### Later Observers: From Association to Intellectual Rival

As Christianity drew educated converts and apologists, the pagan portrait shifted from "obscure association" to a serious religious and intellectual rival. Each later critic (treated on his own page) added a distinctive characterization:

- **[[galen|Galen]]** (c. 130–c. 216) reclassified Christianity (with Judaism) not as a *superstitio* but as a **philosophical school** (*haeresis*) — the first pagan to place it on the same footing as Greek philosophy. He admired Christian self-discipline, sexual restraint, and "contempt of death . . . patent to us every day," ranking some Christians "not inferior to . . . genuine philosophers," but faulted them for accepting "undemonstrated laws" on authority rather than proof (*De pulsuum differentiis* 2.4). Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, by contrast, dismissed Christian fearlessness as mere "obstinacy" or "habit," not reasoned virtue.

- **[[celsus|Celsus]]** (c. 180) portrayed Christians as appealing to the credulous and uneducated — "wool-workers, cobblers, laundry-workers, and the most illiterate and bucolic yokels" who target women and children (*c. Cels.* 3.55) — as fideists ("Do not ask questions; just believe," 1.9), refusers of military service and public office (8.73–75), and an "obscure and secret society" (8.17) draining civic loyalties. He charged them with worshipping "a corpse" (7.68).

- **[[porphyry|Porphyry]]** (c. 234–305) judged Christians "polluted and contaminated and entangled in error" (*Civ. Dei* 19.22–23) and guilty of *asebeia* for worshipping the soul of Jesus rather than the one supreme God. Crucially, he traced the error not to Jesus but to the **disciples**, who "taught something different from what he taught." His language closely matches the official rhetoric of Maximin Daia's anti-Christian rescript ("persist in that accursed folly").

- **[[julian-the-apostate|Julian]]** (r. 361–363) labeled them "**Galilaeans**" — a deliberately provincializing epithet — and "atheists" for despising "the sacred rites which the forefathers observed" (*Misopogon* 357d). In his account Christians abandoned Greek tradition for Jewish, then abandoned that too, inventing "a new sacrifice, which does not need Jerusalem" (the Eucharist, *Gal.* 306b); stripped of classical *paideia* by his school edict, they would be left only with a "barren and servile tradition."

The double charge running through Celsus, Porphyry, and Julian — that Christianity is an **apostasy from Judaism** (claiming Jewish antiquity while keeping none of the Jewish law) — is treated at [[christianity-apostasy-from-judaism|Was Christianity an Apostasy from Judaism?]].

## Historical and Archaeological Context

By the early second century, when Pliny encountered Christians, "Christian groups could be found in perhaps forty or fifty cities within the Roman Empire. Most of these groups were quite small, some numbering several dozen people, others as many as several hundred. The total number of Christians within the empire was probably less than fifty thousand, an infinitesimal number in a society comprising sixty million. The Jews, by contrast, were a significant minority numbering four to five million" (Wilken, ll. 571–578). Most inhabitants of the empire had never heard of Christianity; "even among educated people little was known about the Christian movement" (ll. 580–581).

For almost a century after its founding, Christianity went unnoticed by Roman writers. Pliny the Elder's *Natural History*, written a generation after Jesus's death, does not mention Jesus or the Christian movement at all (Wilken, Introduction, ll. 145–150 within range).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The pagan perception of Christians as an *obscure and secret association* carried both neutral and negative valences. Neutral: it placed Christians in a familiar frame of reference and explained their communal life to outsiders. Negative: the secrecy of their meetings aroused suspicion of criminal behavior (*flagitia*); their refusal to participate in civic cults marked them as antisocial; and their exclusive devotion to one deity appeared as *superstitio* (foreign, illicit cult). On the charge of immorality and ritual crime, see [[superstitio]].

Wilken's methodological point (Introduction): "How something is perceived is an aspect of what it is. This is especially true in the social world, where the perception of others is an essential part of the reality people inhabit" (ll. 155–158 within range).
