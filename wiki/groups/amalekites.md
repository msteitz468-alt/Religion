# Amalek / Amalekites

Amalek (and the Amalekites) is the paradigmatic enemy people in the Hebrew Bible — the first to attack Israel after the exodus, the object of an eternal war commandment, and the genealogical and typological ancestor of later foes (most famously Haman the Agagite in Esther). In the Zohar this figure receives its most developed mystical-typological portrait as the historical face of the **Sitra Aḥra** (the "Other Side").

## Identity and Nomenclature

- **Amalek** is the eponymous ancestor (Gen 36:12, 16; 1 Chr 1:36): grandson of Esau through Eliphaz and his concubine Timna. Thus linked to Edom/Esau, the biblical cipher for Rome and later Christian powers in Jewish typology.
- **Amalekites**: the people / clan descended from him; associated with the Negev, wilderness raiding, and (in some texts) with Midianite or Kenite confederations.
- In Esther, Haman is called "the Agagite" — descendant of Agag, the Amalekite king spared by Saul (1 Sam 15). The book of Esther is thus read as a continuation of the Amalek war.
- Rabbinic and later tradition treats "Amalek" as both a specific historical nation and an eternal spiritual category: any force that seeks to destroy Israel or "cool" its faith.

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Exodus 17:8–16** (Parashat Be-Shallaḥ): First attack, immediately after the sea crossing and before Sinai. Joshua fights; Moses’ hands raised (with Aaron and Hur) bring victory. "The Lord will have war with Amalek from generation to generation" (17:16). The attack targets "the faint and weary" at the rear — the vulnerable "heels."
- **Deuteronomy 25:17–19**: Command to remember and blot out the memory of Amalek once Israel is secure in the land. The attack is framed as striking "all the stragglers at your rear when you were faint and weary."
- **1 Samuel 15**: Saul commanded to apply the *ḥerem* (total ban) to Amalek and Agag; he spares the king and best livestock. Samuel executes Agag; this failure leads to Saul’s final rejection as king.
- **Esther**: Haman the Agagite’s plot against the Jews is the ultimate Amalekite attempt at genocide; the reversal and hanging of Haman and his sons is read as the execution of the Amalek commandment in diaspora.
- Other: Num 24:20 (Balaam’s oracle: "Amalek was the first of the nations, but his end is to perish forever"); 1 Chr 4:43 (Simeonites destroy remnant of Amalek); etc.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

**Jewish (especially Kabbalistic / Zoharic)**: The Zohar (Pritzker Vol. IV, Be-Shallaḥ) makes Amalek the archetypal embodiment of the **Sitra Aḥra** / Samael in history. Amalek "knows the measure of the feet" and attacks the heels (Yesod / Shekhinah / the vulnerable covenant sign); he "cools" the bath of Israel’s faith after the fiery redemption at the sea. The eternal war is the ongoing struggle between the holy side and the demonic shadow that parasitizes it. "Blotting out Amalek" is not merely ethnic but theurgical/cosmic: the eradication of the left-side excess that threatens the sefirotic union and the redemption of Shekhinah. In later Kabbalah and Ḥasidism this typology is applied to any internal or external force of doubt, coldness, or destruction (Amalek = *safek* / doubt by gematria in some traditions). See [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]], [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]], [[zohar|The Zohar]] (Vol. IV).

**Rabbinic**: Amalek is the first and prototypical enemy after the exodus; the *ḥerem* command remains in force until the messianic era. Haman is explicitly Amalekite; Purim is the annual commemoration of Amalek’s defeat. The "three commandments" given at the entry into the land include blotting out Amalek.

**Christian**: Typological reading often identifies Amalek with the forces of evil or with specific historical opponents of the Church; the "eternal war" is spiritualized (Eph 6). Less central than in Jewish tradition, but present in patristic and medieval exegesis as a type of the devil or of persistent sin.

**Modern / political**: "Amalek" rhetoric has been deployed in Israeli discourse (e.g., against certain enemies or in settlement contexts) and has generated controversy when used to justify contemporary violence. The Zoharic identification with the Sitra Aḥra makes the category spiritual and cosmic rather than purely ethnic, but the historical command remains a sensitive locus in Jewish ethics and inter-religious polemic. See [[controversies/herem-canaanite-conquest|Ḥerem and the Canaanite Conquest]] (extended to Amalek).

## Historical and Archaeological Context

- No unambiguous extra-biblical identification of "Amalek" as a distinct polity in the Late Bronze / Iron I period matching the biblical portrait. Some scholars link the name to regions in the Negev or northern Sinai; others see it as a generic term for desert raiders or a confederation including Midianite/Kenite elements.
- The biblical portrait is strongly etiological and theological: the first attacker after liberation, the one whose memory must be erased, the genealogical link to Esau (rival brother).
- In the Persian period (Esther), the Agagite label for Haman reactivates the ancient enmity in a diaspora setting.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Amalek functions as the dark mirror to Israel’s election: the first to test the newly redeemed people, the one whose existence is defined by opposition to the covenant people and their God. In the Zohar this opposition is internalized within the divine economy — Amalek / the Sitra Aḥra is the necessary shadow-side that must be overcome for the full revelation of the sefirot and the union of Tif’eret and Shekhinah. The commandment of remembrance and blotting out makes the struggle with Amalek a perpetual theurgical and ethical task: "from generation to generation" until the eschaton, when (per Obadiah and Isa 63, already linked to Edom/Esau in Zohar Vol. III) the defeat of this force inaugurates redemption.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

- **Ḥerem ethics**: Amalek (with the Canaanites) is the primary biblical locus for the problem of divinely commanded total destruction. See the dedicated controversy page.
- **Purim and diaspora resistance**: The book of Esther is read as the Amalek story continued; the festival enacts the commandment in a world without a Jewish army.
- **Zoharic and mystical**: The most sustained inner-Jewish development — Amalek as metaphysical evil within the sefirotic drama rather than (or in addition to) a historical nation.
- **Modern deployments**: Sensitive and contested use of "Amalek" language in contemporary Jewish/Israeli discourse; requires careful distinction between the Zoharic cosmic typology and any direct political application.
