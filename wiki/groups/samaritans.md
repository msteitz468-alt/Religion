# Samaritans (Samarians)

## Identity and Nomenclature

After Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom and made its capital the province of
**Samerina** (720 BCE), the population of **[[samaria|Samaria]]** — surviving Israelites
plus resettled foreigners — gradually coalesced into a community of **Yahweh
worshipers**. By the Persian period these **Samarians** were a Jewish (Yahwistic) group
in their own right; the later sectarian **Samaritans** (Shomronim) grow out of them
(Cogan, Coogan Ch. 7; Leith, Ch. 8).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **2 Kings 17.24–33**: the polemical "lion" account — foreign settlers, ignorant of
  the land's god, attacked by lions until taught Yahweh-worship at **Bethel**, yet
  continuing their own deities (the charge of **syncretism**).
- **Ezra-Nehemiah**: the Samarian governor **Sanballat** and his allies as **enemies**
  of the returnees' Temple- and wall-building — though framed by the exclusivist
  ideology of Ezra-Nehemiah.
- **2 Chronicles 30/34 & Jeremiah 41.4–5**: a **more inclusive** picture — northern
  Israelites invited to the Jerusalem Passover; eighty mourners from **[[shechem|Shechem]],
  Samaria, and Shiloh** bringing offerings to the ruined Temple after 586.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits — the Key Correction

Coogan's contributors directly challenge the 2 Kings 17 portrait. **The Assyrians did
not deport the entire Israelite population**, and the imported "syncretism" had
**disappeared from Samaria by the late 7th century**. The Persian-period evidence — the
**Wadi ed-Daliyeh (Samaria) papyri** and the **Elephantine papyri** — shows the ruling
elite bearing overwhelmingly **Yahwistic names**; the Elephantine Jews appealed to
*both* Jerusalem *and* Samaria for help rebuilding their temple; and Samarian and
Jerusalem priestly families **intermarried** (Neh. 13.28; Josephus). The conclusion:
the Samarians were **another group of Yahwists with a variant understanding**, not
pagans — and Samaritanism shows "**more derivation than deviation** from Judaism of the
Second Temple period" (Leith).

## Historical Context

The province of Samerina, administered by the **Sanballat family**, remained populous
and prosperous through the Persian period (continuity of settlement; flourishing
farmsteads), in contrast to impoverished [[jerusalem|Yehud]]. The rivalry between
Samaria and Judah over the governorship and the Temple — sharpened by Ezra-Nehemiah's
**policy of exclusion** ("the peoples of the land") — is the seedbed of the later
Jewish-Samaritan schism, with its competing temple on Mount Gerizim and its own
version of the Pentateuch.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Samarians are the great test case for the Persian-period question **"Who is
Israel?"** — defined now by ethnicity and ritual purity rather than territory. Ezra-
Nehemiah's exclusive answer (only the returned exiles) writes the Samarians out;
Chronicles and Zechariah preserve a more inclusive counter-voice. This contested
boundary anticipates the broader sectarianism (Pharisees, Qumran, early Christians)
that Coogan traces back to this era.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

The Jewish–Samaritan estrangement underlies the NT's charged use of "Samaritan"
(the Good Samaritan; the woman at the well, John 4 — "you worship what you do not
know"; "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans"). The community survives to the
