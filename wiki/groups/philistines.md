# Philistines

## Identity and Nomenclature

The Philistines (Egyptian **Peleset**) were one contingent of the [[sea-peoples|Sea
Peoples]], who, beginning c. 1185 BCE, left an Aegean homeland and resettled the
southeast Mediterranean coast that had long been [[canaanites|Canaanite]]. The
prophets **Amos (9.7) and Jeremiah (47.4)** recall that they came from **Caphtor**
(Hebrew for Crete) — a tradition the archaeology (Mycenaean material culture) confirms
(Stager, Coogan Ch. 3).

Their culture centered on the **pentapolis** — five cities each ruled by a "lord"
(Hebrew *seren*, perhaps cognate to Greek *tyrannos*): **[[ashdod|Ashdod]],
[[ashkelon|Ashkelon]], [[ekron|Ekron]] (Tel Miqne), [[gaza|Gaza]], and Gath.** This is
the biblical "land of the Philistines," **Philistia** (Josh. 13.2–3).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Judges**: the **Samson** saga (Judg. 13–16) — borderland conflict between Israelite
  and early-Greek cultures; the displacement of the **tribe of Dan** to the far north.
- **1–2 Samuel**: the Philistines as Israel's great Iron Age enemy — the capture of the
  ark (1 Sam. 4–6), **Goliath of Gath** and David, Saul's death at Gilboa; their
  highland advances are the immediate cause of the **request for a king** (see
  [[monarchy-request]]). **David** finally pushes them back into the coastal pentapolis
  (c. 975 BCE), "completing the Israelite 'conquest' of Canaan."
- **Prophets**: oracles against Philistia (Amos 1; Jer. 47; Ezek. 25; Zeph. 2).

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

- **Israelite/Jewish**: the uncircumcised "other"; Israel's distinctive markers
  (circumcision, the **pig taboo**) crystallized partly to differentiate Israel from
  the pork-eating Philistines (Stager). The paradigm of the menacing, technologically
  superior foe.
- **Modern idiom**: "Philistine" came to mean an uncultured materialist — a usage
  Coogan calls ironic, since the Philistines were a sophisticated Aegean people.

## Historical and Archaeological Context (three stages)

- **Stage 1 (c. 1180–1150)**: arrival *en masse* by sea; destruction of Late Bronze
  cities and rebuilding of much larger ones; **Mycenaean IIIC pottery**; hearth-centered
  architecture; weaving with unperforated spool weights; **swine herding/pork**;
  **wine** mixed with water (the krater); mother-goddess figurines.
- **Stage 2 (c. 1150–1050)**: after Egypt's withdrawal, expansion north, east, and
  south; distinctive **bichrome** pottery showing Canaanite acculturation.
- **Stage 3 (c. 1050–950)**: pottery debased, but the polity at its strongest —
  expansion into the highlands triggers the wars that produce Israelite kingship.

Economically Philistia became a maritime *and* agrarian power: [[ashkelon]]'s wine and
ports, **[[ekron]] as the great olive-oil center** (over a hundred installations by
the 7th c.). Under Assyria the pentapolis (minus Gath) were vassal city-states; they
fell to Babylon c. 604–586 (see [[assyrians]], [[babylonians]]).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Philistines are the corporate adversary against whom the Israelite monarchy is
forged. Their pressure reframes Israel's politics (judges → king), and their cultural
contrast sharpens Israel's identity markers. The ark narrative (Dagon falling before
Yahweh, 1 Sam. 5) stages a theological contest between Yahweh and the Philistine god.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

"Palestine" itself derives from "Philistia." The Goliath/David and Samson stories
became enduring cultural archetypes. Related: [[sea-peoples]], [[israelites]],
[[canaanites]], [[ashkelon]], [[ekron]], [[ashdod]], [[gaza]], [[david]], [[saul]],
[[samson]], [[monarchy-request]].
