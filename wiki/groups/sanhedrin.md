# Sanhedrin

## Identity and Nomenclature

The Sanhedrin (συνέδριον, *synedrion*) is the Jerusalem governing council, which [[darrell-bock|Bock]] describes as a "Jewish supreme court" of some 71 members. On Bock's account its composition mixes several parties: the chief priests (chiefly [[sadducees|Sadducees]]), scribes (γραμματεῖς, law-interpreters, "mostly [[pharisees|Pharisees]]"), and elders (πρεσβύτεροι, civic leaders). Bock notes that "most of the power" lay "with the Sadducees," who controlled the council and shared power with Rome. At Acts 5:21 Luke pairs "the Sanhedrin" with "the senate" (γερουσία, gerousia) — its only NT use — which Bock reads as a **hendiadys**, an emphatic way of saying "every available leader" (against Haenchen, who sees Lukan ignorance).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Acts 4:5–22** — the council (rulers, elders, scribes) gathers with Annas and Caiaphas to examine [[peter-apostle|Peter]] and John over the healing of the lame man; unable to deny the "notable sign," it warns them not to speak "in this name."
- **Acts 5:21–40** — reconvenes to try the apostles after their miraculous release from prison; hears Peter's defense ("We must obey God rather than men"); is counseled to restraint by [[gamaliel|Gamaliel]]; has the apostles beaten and released.
- **Acts 6:12–15** — [[stephen|Stephen]] is seized and brought "before the council," where false witnesses testify and his face appears "like the face of an angel," leading to his speech and stoning.
- **Acts 22:30–23:10** — (later in Acts) Paul is brought before the council, which divides between Pharisees and Sadducees over the resurrection.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits (Bock's reading)

**Composition and power politics.** [[darrell-bock|Bock]] stresses that the council's reaction to the apostles is driven by "theology and power politics": the [[sadducees|Sadducees]], who "have more to lose," control the council and share power with Rome, so the apostolic preaching of Jesus's resurrection is "politically, socially, and religiously destabilizing." At the first hearing (4:1–2) the Sadducees are "annoyed" both at the teaching about Jesus and at the proclamation of resurrection, which they denied. Bock reads the council's deliberations (4:16–17) as showing that "sociological factors" — the apostles' growing "political influence, power, and control" — sit alongside religious ones, undercutting "the picture of the leadership's integrity."

**Procedure.** Bock notes the council's officers (ὑπηρέται) are Levites under the temple captain, and that the leadership investigates "to make sure no sorcery or magic was used" (m. Sanh.). At the rearrest (5:26) the officers act "without violence, for they were afraid of being stoned" — the populace being sympathetic, a fear Bock parallels to the leadership's earlier fear over Jesus (Luke 22:2). Bock reads the council's practice as one of warning first, then punishment.

**The blind opposition.** For Bock the council functions in Luke's narrative as the body that repeatedly confronts the apostolic witness and repeatedly fails to recognize God's work: "God is at work, but the leadership is blind to it." The escalating trials — warning (4:21), beating (5:40), and the death of [[stephen|Stephen]] (7:60) — mark the council's hardening opposition.

## Historical Context

Bock places the high-priestly family at the center of the council's leadership: **Annas**, called high priest though he served AD 6–14, "patriarch of a family that held high-priestly power for several decades," and **Caiaphas**, the current high priest (AD 18–36), whose ossuary Bock notes "may have been discovered recently" (Greenhut 1992). Bock suggests Luke's information about the closed-door deliberations may have come from Paul, Nicodemus, or Joseph of Arimathea.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock reads the Sanhedrin scenes as staging the central conflict of early Acts: "God has exalted Jesus, but you killed him," "a power struggle for the hearts of the Jewish people" (Witherington). The apostles' refusal before the council — "we must obey God rather than the leadership" (4:19; 5:29) — carries, on Bock's reading, the implication that "the leadership no longer represents God's will," while also raising the question of the limits of human institutions (cf. Luke 20:25; Rom. 13).
