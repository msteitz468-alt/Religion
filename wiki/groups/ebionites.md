# Ebionites

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Ebionites" derives from the Hebrew *ebyōnîm* ("the poor") — a term reflecting either self-designation (identification with the poor of the Sermon on the Mount or the Jerusalem church's poverty; cf. Rom 15:26 "the poor among the saints in Jerusalem") or a pejorative label applied by opponents. The designation appears in patristic sources from Irenaeus onwards (*Haer.* 1.26.2; 5.1.3). No Ebionite text uses the name as a self-designation in surviving sources.

It is disputed whether "Ebionite" names a single movement or a family of related Jewish-Christian groups that patristic writers loosely aggregated. The theological profiles given by different heresiologists (Irenaeus, Origen, Hippolytus, Epiphanius) are not fully consistent, suggesting the label covered diverse Jewish-Christian expressions.

## Primary Textual Appearances

Ebionites do not appear by name in the NT, though their connection to the Jewish-Christian communities of Jerusalem and its surroundings is generally assumed. They may represent the continuation or development of the Jewish-Christian wing of the earliest church:
- Acts 1–15 — the Jerusalem church's Torah-observant character; the controversy over Gentile inclusion
- Galatians 2 — the Antioch incident; the "men from James" who pressured Jewish believers to eat separately from Gentiles
- Matthew's Gospel — especially its Jewish-Christian emphases (5:17-19; the Torah fulfilled not abolished) — the Gospel most favored by Ebionites

The Ebionites are described in patristic sources as concentrated in the Transjordanian regions (Pella, Cochaba, Beroea) — areas associated with the flight of Jerusalem Christians before the 70 CE destruction.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### Proto-Orthodox / Patristic Account

Irenaeus (*Haer.* 1.26.2; 3.21.1; 5.1.3) provides the earliest substantial account:
- Use of Matthew's Gospel only (later sources add a modified Matthew = "Gospel of the Ebionites," in Greek)
- Rejection of Paul as an apostate from the Law
- Jesus born naturally of Mary and Joseph; received the Spirit at baptism (*adoptionist* Christology — the divine Spirit "adopted" the human Jesus)
- Obligatory Torah observance (circumcision, Sabbath, food laws) for salvation
- Jerusalem-oriented piety; facing Jerusalem for prayer

Origen (*Contra Celsum* 2.1; *Commentary on Matthew*) adds: some Ebionites accepted a virgin birth (a "Ebionite" minority who accepted the incarnation but not pre-existence); others denied it. Origen distinguished two groups on this basis.

Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30) is the most detailed but probably the most unreliable — he conflates Ebionites with related groups (Nazarenes, Elchasaites) and adds legendary details.

### Modern Scholarship

The "Nazarenes" are distinguished from "Ebionites" in patristic sources (Jerome, Epiphanius): Nazarenes are Jewish-Christians who accepted Paul and orthodox Christology but maintained Torah observance; Ebionites are the specifically heretical wing (adoptionism, rejection of Paul). Whether the Nazarenes are the original Jewish-Christian tradition and the Ebionites a second-century development (the dominant modern view: Mimouni, Skarsaune) or vice versa is debated.

The "Gospel of the Ebionites" (preserved in Epiphanius) is a Greek harmony of the Synoptics modified for Ebionite purposes: vegetarian Jesus ("I have come to abolish the sacrifices" — not attested in canonical Gospels; the phrase "and" in the Passover account altered to remove the lamb). This text is the strongest primary evidence for a distinctive Ebionite literary tradition.

## Historical and Archaeological Context

The earliest identifiable Jewish-Christian communities are in Palestine/Syria. After 70 CE (the destruction of Jerusalem) and 135 CE (Bar Kokhba revolt; re-founding of Jerusalem as Aelia Capitolina, from which Jews were banned), Jewish-Christian groups found themselves marginalized by both sides: rejected by the Gentile-majority church for insisting on Torah observance; rejected by mainstream Judaism for confessing Jesus as Messiah.

No substantial archaeological remains can be definitively attributed to the Ebionites. The Pella tradition (Eusebius reports the Jerusalem church fled to Pella before 70 CE) provides the geographical connection to Transjordan.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The Adoptionist Christology Question

Ebionite Christology — Jesus as a natural human being on whom the Spirit descended at baptism — is the earliest named adoptionist position in Christian history. It recurs later as "Dynamic Monarchianism" (Paul of Samosata, c.260 CE). The question of whether adoptionism or pre-existence/incarnation Christology is older is central to contemporary Christology debates:
- Harnack, Bousset, Dunn argued for adoptionism as earlier; pre-existence Christology as a later Hellenistic development
- [[larry-hurtado|Hurtado]], Bauckham, Kruger argue for high Christology (pre-existence) as present in the earliest accessible sources — Paul's undisputed letters — while adoptionism appears as a *reduction* of existing high Christology

On the current wiki's reading (following Hurtado/Kruger): Ebionite adoptionism is not a survival of the *original* Jewish-Christian Christology but a *reduction* of the Christology already present in Paul's letters — which are earlier than any datable Ebionite source.

### Paul's Rejection

The Ebionite rejection of Paul is theologically significant: their reading of Paul as an apostate from the Law represents one trajectory of how Paul was received in Jewish-Christian circles. This is directly relevant to the debates about Paul's relationship to the Law in NT scholarship (E.P. Sanders, Dunn's "New Perspective on Paul"). See [[paul-apostle|Paul the Apostle]].

### The Continued Presence of Jewish Christianity

The Ebionites refute any account of early Christianity as uniformly Gentile or uniformly dismissive of the Law. They represent a genuine, sustained Jewish-Christian tradition that maintained the Law as binding even as it confessed Jesus as Messiah. Their existence complicates both the "parting of the ways" narrative (clearly there were communities that hadn't entirely parted) and the "Hellenization" thesis of early Christianity's development.

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

- **Islamic reception**: Medieval Islamic sources (Ibn Hazm, al-Shahrastani) describe the Ebionites favorably as the most authentic of the early Jewish-Christian groups — predecessors of the Islamic understanding of Jesus as a human prophet, not God incarnate. The possible historical connection between Ebionite communities in the Transjordan and the context of early Islam has been proposed by scholars (Schonfield, Ludemann) but remains speculative.
- **Modern use**: "Ebionite" is sometimes used loosely in popular discourse for any low-Christology Christianity; this is imprecise.
