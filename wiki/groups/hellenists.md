# Hellenists

## Identity and Nomenclature

"Hellenists" (Ἑλληνισταί) are, on [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s reading, Jews "whose primary language was Greek," as distinct from the Semitic-speaking "Hebrews." Bock estimates Greek-speakers at 10–20% of the Jerusalem population. He notes the debated question of whether the Hellenists also held a looser view of the temple and the law (Hengel and Conzelmann see some difference in practice; Jervell reviews the debate).

**Two distinct referents.** Bock keeps two groups sharply distinct, both called "Hellenists":
1. **Hellenist believers** within the Jerusalem church — the Greek-speaking Christians whose widows are neglected in the daily distribution (Acts 6:1), on whose behalf the Seven (all with Greek names, including [[stephen|Stephen]]) are appointed.
2. **Non-Christian Hellenistic Jews** — members of a diaspora synagogue who dispute with and then move against Stephen (Acts 6:9).

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Acts 6:1** — a "grumbling" (γογγυσμός) arises because the Hellenist widows "were being neglected in the daily distribution" (διακονία), prompting the appointment of the Seven. Bock notes the term echoes the wilderness murmuring of Exod. 16 / Numbers.
- **Acts 6:9** — "some of those who belonged to the synagogue of the Freedmen" — Cyrenians, Alexandrians, and men from Cilicia and Asia — dispute with [[stephen|Stephen]] and instigate the charges against him.

## Tradition-Specific Portraits (Bock's reading)

**The widow-distribution tension (6:1).** [[darrell-bock|Bock]] defends the historicity of the Hebrews/Hellenists tension (against Conzelmann's "artificial construct") by appeal to Luke's selectivity. He surveys three scenarios for its cause: (1) Jewish leaders targeting Hellenist Christians to split the community along old lines; (2) Hellenists being less attached to Jerusalem and more likely to leave; (3) that ethnic-targeting is a critical misreading, since persecution touched both groups (Witherington, Hurtado). Bock leans against reading it as a purely ethnic-targeting persecution. Widow-care, he notes, is an OT/NT measure of a community's compassion (Deut. 24:17; James 1:27), and the community's refusal to fragment along ethnic lines is "a powerful multiethnic testimony."

**The synagogue that opposed Stephen (6:9).** Bock identifies the opponents as coming from a synagogue of "freedmen, slaves who now are independent," possibly descended from Jews imprisoned by Pompey in 63 BC and later freed. He raises the possibility that [[paul-apostle|Paul]], as a man of Tarsus in Cilicia, "might have participated," and notes that "Asian Jews will also form strong opposition to Paul later." Crucially, Bock stresses that Stephen himself is a Hellenist believer, so "it is not Stephen's Judaism that leads to his controversial stance... It is his embrace of Christianity."

## Historical Context

Bock notes that the presence of a substantial Greek-speaking Jewish population in Jerusalem reflects the return and settlement of diaspora Jews, some drawn from freedmen communities. Among the Seven, only Nicolaus is identified as "a proselyte of [[antioch|Antioch]]," which Bock takes to imply the others were born Jews — one basis for his observation that [[cornelius|Cornelius]] (Acts 10) is "not strictly the first Gentile Christian" but the first to come directly without full conversion to Judaism.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

For Bock, the Hellenists are a hinge in Luke's narrative. The appointment of the Seven to serve them is a "bridge passage" introducing [[stephen|Stephen]] and Philip (Acts 6–8) and pointing toward Antioch (Acts 11). With Stephen's martyrdom, Bock observes, "Jewish Hellenists join the Jewish leadership in hostile opposition," and the ensuing persecution scatters the church beyond Jerusalem, advancing the mission.
