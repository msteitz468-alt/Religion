# Gentiles

"Gentiles" (*ta ethnē*, "the nations") designates non-Jews as a corporate category over against
[[israelites|Israel]]. In [[matthew|Matthew]] the category is the site of a sustained tension: the
mission of the Twelve is restricted to Israel at 10:5-6 and Jesus' own commission is stated in
Israel-only terms at 15:24, yet the gospel closes with a command to make disciples of all nations
(28:19). This page records the treatment of the theme in R. T. France, *The Gospel of Matthew*
(NICNT, 2007), the wiki's sole ingested source on it; interpretive claims are attributed to
[[rt-france|France]] throughout.

## Identity and Nomenclature

France records that in the setting of Matt 15:26 the word "dog" carried "the force of Jewish
invective which could use 'dog' (an unclean animal; cf. 7:6) as a deliberately offensive term for
Gentiles." He also notes that the purity of God's people separated them from all others, so that
the food laws "became a barrier to social intercourse between Jew and Gentile" — on his reading
the cause of the long and painful early-church dispute reflected in Acts 10:9-48.

Matthew's usage varies with context. On 8:11-12 France stresses that the incomers are not depicted
as proselytes: "It is not suggested apparently that they come in as proselytes, but that they are
accepted simply as Gentiles," on equal terms with the patriarchs — and the patriarchs are shown
not fearing ritual defilement. He also notes a shift of comparison type within the gospel: 10:15
and 11:22, 24 invoke judgment on *wicked* Gentiles, but 12:41-42 "introduces Gentiles who turned to
God, and who can therefore appear for the prosecution" against "this generation."

## Primary Textual Appearances

- **Matthew 2:1-12** — the [[magi|magi]].
- **Matthew 4:15-16** — the [[formula-quotations|formula quotation]] naming "Galilee of the
  nations."
- **Matthew 8:5-13** — the centurion's servant; "many will come from east and west."
- **Matthew 8:28-34** — the Gadarene deliverance in Gentile territory.
- **Matthew 10:5-6** — the restriction: "go nowhere among the Gentiles."
- **Matthew 12:18, 21** — the Isaiah 42 quotation with its clauses about the nations.
- **Matthew 15:21-28** — the [[canaanites|Canaanite]] woman.
- **Matthew 15:29-31** — healings among a crowd that glorifies "the God of Israel."
- **Matthew 15:32-39** — the feeding of the four thousand.
- **Matthew 28:19** — "make disciples of all nations."

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

### "Galilee of the nations"

France explains the phrase by the region's "greater openness to surrounding Gentile populations"
and by Isaiah's awareness of Assyrian deportations and foreign resettlement (2 Kgs 15:29;
17:24-34). He reads it as foreshadowing the "all nations" mission of 28:19, even though Jesus'
ministry meanwhile is largely — not entirely — restricted to Jews: a principle he says "will be
explicitly stated in 10:5-6; 15:24, but breached in practice" at 15:21-39. See
[[galilee|Galilee]].

### The centurion (8:5-13)

France ties 8:5-13 tightly to 15:21-28 as the only two Gentile appeals in Matthew and the only two
distance healings, both structured as "request met by a racial rebuff, which in turn provokes a
remarkable declaration of faith" and restoration. He argues that Matthew omits Luke's Jewish
elders and the centurion's good local relations not merely to abbreviate but because "the
inclusion of the Jewish elders would distract attention from the direct confrontation between the
Gentile officer and the Jewish healer." His verdict on the redactional difference is that Luke's
is a story of a good humble man granted a request, while Matthew's is "more a paradigm for the
extension of the gospel of Israel's Messiah to include also those who had no natural claim on
him." Reading 8:7 as a question (a minority position France defends at length — see
[[miracles|Miracles]]) "places the racial issue firmly in the forefront."

On "many will come from east and west," France records and rejects a competing reading.

- **Davies and Allison (2:27-28)** argue the "many from the east and west" are unprivileged Jews,
  parallel to the tax collectors and prostitutes of 21:31-32.
- **France** rejects this: "but here the context is decisively against it: the faith commended in
  v. 10 is that of a Gentile in specific contrast to Israel."

France reads 8:11-12 as outlining a theology of the people of God worked out in chs. 21–23,
symbolized in chs. 24–25, and "triumphantly summed up in the command to go and make disciples of
all nations in 28:19."

### The restriction of 10:5-6

France candidly registers the tension: the Israel-only restriction "strikes a surprising note in
the wider unfolding of Matthew's story," given the magi, Galilee of the nations, all Syria and the
Decapolis, the centurion, the Gadarene deliverance, and 8:10-12's rejection of an exclusively
Jewish kingdom. He offers two moves in reconciliation.

1. **Geographical rather than absolute.** The terms used — "way of the Gentiles," "town of the
   Samaritans" — "indicate a restriction on the area to be visited rather than a total ban on
   contact with Gentiles and Samaritans as such," restricting the mission in practice to Galilee.
2. **Temporally phased.** The limitation held "for the initial period of proclamation until the
   undeniably primary focus of Jesus' mission as Messiah of Israel had been established"; only
   after that, "and after Jesus' own ministry within Palestine has culminated in his death and
   resurrection, would it be appropriate to widen the scope."

France notes that 10:5 contains Matthew's only mention of Samaria and Samaritans, and that its
negative tone "suggests a more conventionally Jewish perspective" than Luke and John — while
seeing no reason Matthew would exclude Samaritans from "all nations."

### The Isaiah 42 quotation (12:18-21)

France argues that by following the LXX in finding "the nations" also at Isa 42:4, "Matthew
ensures that his readers do not miss the theme of a gospel for the Gentiles," a theme developing
throughout and climaxing at 28:19. He states that the extension of God's purpose beyond Israel
"was not a new decision by God at the time of Jesus, but part of his long-declared purpose of
salvation." He also registers a tension he does not resolve: the messenger of Isa 61 is sent to
Zion with a message of national restoration, "whereas in Isa 42:1 it is the nations who will be
the beneficiaries of the justice which the servant brings." See [[isaiah|Isaiah]].

### The Canaanite woman (15:21-28)

France reads 15:21-39 as a decisive break: Jesus withdraws not merely outside Galilee but outside
Jewish territory altogether, in "a deliberate extension of the mission of the Messiah of Israel to
the surrounding non-Jewish peoples." He identifies "bread" as the thread holding the section
together, "metaphorically denied and then granted to the Canaanite woman," then literally made
available to the Gentile "dogs" in fulfillment of the agreement she extracted.

He notes that Matthew is careful to say Jesus entered only the "region," not the cities of Tyre and
Sidon: "This was not a mission to the pagan cities (like Jonah's to Nineveh), but a retreat to a
place where Jesus and his disciples could be away from Jewish opposition." He lists five parallels
with 8:5-13: the request comes from a concerned superior or parent rather than the patient; both
suppliants are Gentile; Jesus is initially reluctant; the faith is commended above any Jew's; and
"in each case (and only in these two cases in Matthew) the cure takes place by a word from a
distance."

On the harshness France does not soften the text. He records the invective force of "dog"
directly. He argues that the 10:5-6 restriction was essentially geographical, whereas here Jesus
is already outside Galilee, so "the sequel in v. 26 makes it clear that it is ministry to Gentiles
as such, rather than just the interruption of a retreat, which is the issue": on that reading
"Gentiles have no right to the ministry of the Jewish Messiah." He then insists the restriction
"can only be seen as temporary" in the wider context of the gospel and, given 8:5-13 and 8:28-34,
"can not be taken to be a rule without exceptions"; like the silence of v. 23, the rebuff tests the
woman's grasp of what her appeal involves.

France answers the "U-turn" question carefully: "So is it true to say that her argument has
changed Jesus' mind? Yes, in that the policy which he has declared in vv. 24 and 26 is then
abandoned" — but he holds this is not a hitherto unforeseen redirection, because 8:11-12, 8:28-34,
and the birth-narrative hints show Jesus has already envisaged a multiracial people of God.

He observes that these two healings of Gentiles are the only ones Jesus performs at a distance in
the Synoptic Gospels, and that the same verb "cure" is used as at 8:13 even though one is a
physical healing and one an [[exorcism|exorcism]], the nature of the problem being only a minor
interest here.

### Healings among Gentiles (15:29-31)

France's key evidence for a Gentile crowd is the unusual comment that they "gave glory to the God
of Israel": "This is never said about the Galilean crowds, and the terminology suggests that the
crowd are Gentiles" recognizing the Jewish Messiah's special power. He concludes that "the whole of
15:21-38 is presented as Jesus' ministry outside Israel, with the many healings and the feeding
miracle deliberately balancing those already granted to the Jewish crowds," the v. 31 list
paralleling the messianic deeds of 11:5. He notes the complaints listed recall Isa 35:5-6, the
blessings of God's redemption of his people — "but now those messianic blessings are also being
experienced outside the covenant people." He notes the absence of reluctance this time: "the
principle accepted in 15:27-28 does not need to be argued again."

On location France argues for the hills on the east (Golan) side of the lake, Mark's parallel
naming the Decapolis, since a boat was needed to return to Jewish Magadan — but he concedes that
"Matthew's geographical indications are not very specific, and the Gentile setting of these verses
is indicated as much by the content of the narrative as by what Matthew says about their
location."

### The two feedings as a Jewish/Gentile parallel

France catalogues three differences between the second feeding and the first: a Gentile rather
than Jewish crowd; different numbers — "fewer people (four thousand against five thousand), more
loaves (seven against five), fewer leftovers (seven baskets against twelve)" — reducing the scale;
and a more emphatic disciplinary protest at v. 33. He raises the reduced scale first as a question
and then asserts it: "But the numbers are scaled down; the children's bread remains the prior
commitment."

His answer to the doublet question requires two factors: "First, the belief of both Mark and
Matthew that as a matter of fact two such miracles did take place; and, secondly, a deliberate
intention to draw a parallel between Jesus' Jewish ministry and his ministry to Gentiles." He
argues the comparison is explanatorily necessary: without it "it is not easy to explain what seems
otherwise to be a needless (and strangely less impressive) repetition."

France supports the Jewish/Gentile contrast from vocabulary. He notes the word for "baskets" at
14:20 is of a type "especially associated with Jewish use" (BDAG 563 describing it as "prob. a
large, heavy basket"), while at 15:37 a more general term appears — "another minor pointer to a
different cultural setting for the two incidents," a distinction maintained when the miracles are
recalled at 16:9-10, which he takes to suggest more than stylistic variation. He also notes that a
non-Jewish crowd "stayed with me" three days and can be "sent away" at will, indicating "a
remarkable degree of personal attachment to Jesus and recognition of his authority on the part of a
non-Jewish crowd."

He holds the eucharistic wording is as clear here as at 14:19, "thus reinforcing the message of
8:11-12 that Gentiles are to share with Jews in the messianic banquet"; the Lord's Supper which
anticipates that banquet "is thus for people of all races and backgrounds who share the woman's
faith that there is also bread for the dogs."

## Historical and Archaeological Context

France's historical framing of the category is social and legal rather than material: the
separating function of the food laws, the invective force of "dog," and the regional demography
behind "Galilee of the nations" (Assyrian deportation and foreign resettlement, 2 Kgs 15:29;
17:24-34). On the Decapolis he draws the geographical inference noted above while conceding the
imprecision of Matthew's indications.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

France's positive formulation of the 8:11-12 saying is that "it is not a simple matter of 'Jews
out; Gentiles in'" — the people of God is reconstituted on faith rather than racial ancestry. He
holds the paradoxical force of the saying depends on the "sons of the kingdom" being those whom
everyone would expect to be included. Across the gospel he treats the Gentile theme as
developmental rather than as a reversal: foreshadowed at 4:15-16, breaking through at 8:5-13 and
8:28-34, formally restricted at 10:5-6 and 15:24, decisively extended at 15:21-39, and consummated
at 28:19 — with the extension itself "not a new decision by God at the time of Jesus, but part of
his long-declared purpose of salvation."

He also flags the contrast between the sceptical demand for a sign at 16:1 and "the enthusiastic
response of the Gentile crowd to the 'signs' in 15:29-31."

## Influence on Later Traditions and Controversies

France connects the food-law barrier directly to the early-church dispute reflected in Acts
10:9-48, describing it as long and painful. The interpretive controversy internal to Matthew — how
10:5-6 and 15:24 relate to 28:19 — is recorded above with France's two-part reconciliation
(geographical scope; temporal phasing) and his insistence that the restriction "can not be taken
to be a rule without exceptions." He does not adjudicate the tension he identifies between Isa 61's
Zion-directed message and Isa 42:1's nations-directed justice.

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names.*

For Bock the inclusion of the Gentiles is the central theological theme of Acts — the question of
"how salvation relates to Israel's God yet includes Gentiles" — worked out in stages along the 1:8
program (Jerusalem → Samaria → the end of the earth). The **Cornelius** episode (10:1–11:18) is "the
book's turning point": God, not any human decision, brings the gospel to a Gentile household, the
sheet-vision teaching that "what God has cleansed" (unclean food, and therefore table fellowship with
Gentiles) is clean, so that "God shows no partiality" (10:34) and the Spirit falls on Gentiles before
baptism — the "Gentile Pentecost." Bock stresses the food/fellowship link as load-bearing: Acts 10 is
"the strongest passage in Acts indicating the removal of food laws that have social implications."
The **Jerusalem Consultation** (Acts 15) then settles that the question is "not whether Gentiles
should be included but on what basis"; circumcision is "emphatically refuted" as a requirement, and
James's four abstentions (the *Jakobsklauseln*) are about fellowship, "not about salvation." Bock
notes that Cornelius is not strictly the first Gentile Christian (Nicolaus of Antioch, a proselyte,
precedes him, 6:5), but the first to come directly without full conversion to Judaism — a distinct
category from the **God-fearers** (Cornelius, Lydia, Titius Justus), Gentile sympathizers whom Bock
thinks Luke does not treat as a strict technical term. Across the Pauline mission the repeated "turn
to the Gentiles" (13:46; 18:6; 28:28) is programmatic but "not absolute" — Paul still begins with the
Jew first (Rom. 1:16) in each city. The theme culminates in Paul's Rome declaration that "this
salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen" (28:28), which Bock (rejecting
J. Sanders's anti-Semitism thesis) reads not as a final rejection of Israel but as God's continuing
appeal to a still-hoped-for ethnic Israel (Rom. 11).
