# Fear of the LORD

"The fear of the LORD" (Hebrew *yir'at YHWH*) is the epistemological and religious foundation
of Israel's [[wisdom-literature|wisdom literature]]. It is announced as the programmatic motto
of [[proverbs|Proverbs]] — "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (1:7; restated
9:10) — and appears as the conclusion of the wisdom poem in [[book-of-job|Job]] 28:28 and of
[[ecclesiastes|Ecclesiastes]] (12:13). This page states the concept primarily in the voice of
[[bruce-waltke|Bruce K. Waltke]]'s NICOT commentary on Proverbs, the wiki's most detailed single
treatment; other sources' framings should be added in their own attributed voice.

## Waltke: a compound expression

Waltke argues that *yir'at YHWH* is a **compound expression** — one "cannot be understood by
studying 'fear' and 'the LORD' in isolation," any more than "butterfly" is "butter" plus "fly,"
or water is understood by studying hydrogen and oxygen separately. It "involves both rational
and non-rational aspects at the same time."

- **Rational aspect**: an "objective revelation that can be taught... and memorized." Waltke
  notes that Psalm 19:7–9 makes "the fear of the LORD" co-referential with "law," "statutes,"
  and "commands" — i.e., it names a body of revealed content, not merely a feeling.
- **Non-rational aspect**: "an emotional response of fear, love, and trust." Deuteronomy treats
  "love of the LORD" and "fear of the LORD" as synonyms (Deut 6:2/6:5; cf. Josh 24:14). Isaiah
  29:13 rejects a distorted fear "made up only of rules taught by men." Waltke quotes Charles
  Bridges: "that affectionate reverence, by which the child of God bends himself humbly and
  carefully to his Father's law," and cites C. S. Lewis's Aslan ("Is he safe?" "Of course
  not... but he's good").

## Fear of the LORD vs. fear of God

A distinction Waltke returns to throughout: **"the fear of the LORD"** refers to God's **special
revelation to Israel** (the LORD is God's personal covenant name), while **"the fear of God"**
(*yir'at 'elōhîm*) refers to God's **general revelation to all people, especially through
conscience**. Waltke cites Whybray: "fear of God" refers "to a standard of moral conduct known
and accepted by men in general." The difference between the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*
and Proverbs is, for Waltke, precisely this: "the God of Proverbs is named and so known; that
of Amenemope is not."

## "Beginning" (rē'šît)

On 1:7 Waltke resolves the ambiguity of *rē'šît* ("beginning") by appeal to the parallel term
*tehillat* ("beginning") in 9:10: the fear of the LORD is "first" in a temporal *and* controlling
sense — "not on a horizontal axis that can be left behind but on a vertical axis on which all
else rests... both the *initium* and the *principium*." His analogy: "What the alphabet is to
reading... the fear of the LORD is to attaining the revealed knowledge of this book."

## Function: the anti–natural-theology foundation

For Waltke the fear of the LORD is the load-bearing premise of his rejection of a **natural
theology** reading of wisdom (against von Rad, Koch, Gese, Crenshaw, and — he confesses — his own
earlier self). Because Proverbs founds knowledge on the fear of the covenant God's special
revelation, wisdom cannot be a self-revealing cosmic order discoverable by neutral observation of
creation. Waltke cites Nel: "the fear of the Lord does not allow one to interpret wisdom as
natural theology," and marshals [Agur]'s confession (Prov 30:1–6) that no one has
ascended to heaven to know comprehensively — wisdom is a divine gift (2:6), not a natural
attainment. This is the same premise that underwrites his reading of [[woman-wisdom|Woman
Wisdom]] as a personification of *revealed* wisdom rather than of order-in-creation.

## Across the wisdom corpus

- **[[proverbs|Proverbs]]**: the programmatic motto (1:7; 9:10); Waltke calls it "the key to
  Proverbs." Recurs as "better-than" refrains (15:16) and as the security of the righteous
  (14:26–27, where 14:27 repeats 13:14 with "fear of the LORD" substituted for "the teaching of
  the wise" — Waltke's evidence that the sage's inspired teaching and the fear of the LORD are
  co-referential).
- **[[book-of-job|Job]]**: the hymn to wisdom concludes, "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom;
  and to depart from evil is understanding" (28:28) — the same epistemological terminus.
- **[[ecclesiastes|Ecclesiastes]]**: "Fear God and keep his commandments" (12:13) as the book's
  summative counsel.

## The frame of Proverbs (Waltke, Vol. 2)

Waltke's second volume shows the fear of the LORD **bracketing the whole book**: at its beginning
(1:7; 9:10), middle (15:33), and end (31:30). The concluding acrostic makes the point climactically —
"Charm is deceitful and beauty is fleeting; as for a woman who fears the LORD, she should be
praised" (31:30). On Waltke's reading this "vertical virtue … provides the necessary theological
base for [the [[valiant-woman|valiant woman]]'s] horizontal social virtues," so that her itemized
works "exemplify the fear of the Lord."

Its **antithesis, the "fear of man,"** is named at 29:25: "panic induced by a mortal lays a snare,
but the one who trusts the LORD will be protected." Waltke reads a single opposition running
through chs 28–29: "because the wicked do not fear God, they live in fear of people, but because
the righteous fear God (1:7), they do not fear people" (28:1) — the God-fearer is therefore "bold
as a lion." Trembling before the LORD "continually" (28:14) is "the fear of reverence, not of
bondage … equivalent of 'the fear of the Lord.'"
