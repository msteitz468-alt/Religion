*Nifāq* (Arabic: نفاق) refers to religious hypocrisy in Islam — the condition of one who outwardly professes faith while inwardly withholding it, or who performs religious duties from social pressure or ostentation rather than sincere conviction.

## Treatment in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book 1

The Kitāb al-Īmān of [[sahih-muslim|Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim]] does not treat *nifāq* as a chapter heading in the range covered by Agent 2 (Chapters 56–92), but the concept appears structurally in the eschatological material on the Day of Resurrection.

### Hypocrites on the Day of Resurrection

In the account of the Ṣirāṭ bridge (Chapter 81, Nos. 0349, 0367), hypocrites (*munāfiqūn*) are initially part of the Muslim community:

> "God will gather people on the Day of Resurrection and say: Let every people follow what they worshipped... This Ummah (of Islam) alone would be left behind and there would be hypocrites too amongst it." (No. 0349)

The hypocrites are distinguishable from believers on the bridge by the behavior of their light:

> "Every person, whether a hypocrite or a believer, would be endowed with a light, and there would be spikes and hooks on the bridge of the Hell... Then the light of the hypocrites would be extinguished, and the believers would secure salvation." (No. 0367)

### Ostentation vs. Sincere Prostration

The most theologically precise statement about hypocrisy in this range concerns the act of prostration (*sujūd*):

> "Those who used to prostrate themselves before God of their own accord would be permitted by God to prostrate themselves. But there would remain none who used to prostrate out of fear (of people) and ostentation but Allah would make his back as one piece, and whenever he would attempt to prostrate he would fall on his back." (No. 0352)

This distinguishes sincere prostration (from inner conviction) from social/ostentational prostration (from fear of people or display). The physical incapacity imposed in the eschaton maps the inner reality onto the body.

### The Muslim / Believer Distinction (Chapter 69)

A related concept appears in Chapter 69. Sa'd b. Abī Waqqāṣ repeatedly calls a man "a believer" (*muʾmin*); Muhammad corrects him to "a Muslim" (*muslim*) each time and explains:

> "Verily (at times) I give (a share) to a certain man apprehending that he may not be thrown prostrate in the Fire, whereas the other man (who is not given) is dearer to me (as compared with him)." (No. 0276–0277)

The distinction between *muslim* (one who has submitted outwardly) and *muʾmin* (one who has genuine inner faith) is here applied practically: the Prophet gives a share of booty to the less-certain Muslim precisely because he fears for him, while the man of deeper faith needs no such incentive. This is not *nifāq* proper but articulates the spectrum between outer profession and inner conviction that the concept of hypocrisy occupies one end of.

### Trustworthiness as an Index of Sincerity

Chapter 65 on the removal of trustworthiness (*amāna*) from hearts treats a phenomenon adjacent to hypocrisy — the decay of interior religious integrity while outward forms remain:

> "The people would enter into transactions amongst one another and hardly a person would be left who would return (things) entrusted to him... in his heart there would not be faith even to the weight of a mustard seed." (No. 0265, Hudhaifa)

The two-hearts doctrine (No. 0267) describes hearts impregnated by temptations as "black and dust-coloured like a vessel which is upset, not recognizing what is good or rejecting what is abominable."
