# Revealer / Revelation / Revealedness

The triad **Revealer / Revelation / Revealedness** is [[karl-barth|Karl Barth]]'s structural analysis of the biblical concept of revelation in *Church Dogmatics* I.1, §8, which he presents as the *root* of the Christian doctrine of the Trinity. The thesis is stated programmatically at the opening of §8: "God's Word is God Himself in His revelation. For God reveals Himself as the Lord and according to Scripture this signifies for the concept of revelation that God Himself in unimpaired unity yet also in unimpaired distinction is Revealer, Revelation, and Revealedness" (lines 72–75).

## The Three Aspects

Barth's derivation is not simply grammatical (subject/predicate/object of the sentence "God speaks") but structural: he claims these three aspects of the *biblical* concept of revelation are simultaneously identical (the same God in each) and irreducibly distinct (not collapsible into one another or into a synthetic fourth).

**Revealer** corresponds to the *Father*: the hidden God who, even in taking form in the Son, remains the "free ground and free power of His being God in the Son" (§8.2, lines 1419–1421). The Revealer is characterized by *veiling* — the Deus revelatus is simultaneously the Deus absconditus (line 1257). "God's presence is always God's decision to be present" (line 1283). This permanent freedom over His own utterances is the distinctive mark of Lordship attributed to the Father.

**Revelation** corresponds to the *Son*: God's assumption of form — His self-unveiling in the act of revelation. In the Old Testament this appears as the "hypostases" of the divine name, Word, and angel of Yahweh; in the New Testament it reaches its concrete fulfilment in the *humanitas Christi* (line 1351). "That He reveals Himself as the Son is what is primarily meant when we say that He reveals Himself as the Lord" (lines 1234–1236). The Son is God "a second time in a very different way" — genuinely other from the Father's hiddenness, yet the same God (line 1054).

**Revealedness** corresponds to the *Spirit*: the impartation of revelation to specific human beings in specific historical events. The *pneuma* is "the miracle of the presence of real men at God's revelation" (line 1719). Revealedness is characterized by *historicity* — revelation "comes vertically from heaven" (line 1653) to concrete people in concrete situations, not as a timeless mythological truth. "God reveals Himself as the Spirit, not as any spirit ... but as the Spirit of the Father and the Son" (lines 1760–1762).

## Relation to the Doctrine of the Trinity

The Revealer/Revelation/Revealedness schema does not generate the doctrine of the Trinity directly; it is its *root* — the soil from which the dogma grows as the Church's analytical interpretation. Barth is explicit that the schema is not itself proof: "We are not saying that the doctrine of the Trinity is merely the interpretation of revelation and not also an interpretation of the God who reveals Himself in revelation" (§8.2, lines 826–829). The immanent Trinity is prior to the economic Trinity; revelation is the *basis of the doctrine*, not the basis of the Trinity itself.

The three aspects correspond to three summaries Barth offers at the close of §8.2 (lines 1780–1787): unveiling/veiling/impartation; form/freedom/historicity; Easter/Good Friday/Pentecost; Son/Father/Spirit.

## Historical Precedents and Criticisms

Barth defends the schema against the charge that it is merely a "grammatical and rationalistic proof" derived from subject/predicate/object logic (§8.1, lines 114–116). He replies that the formula was "the subsequent formulation of something presented by the Bible" (lines 265–267), not a priori logic. He acknowledges that the Calvinist-influenced *Heidelberg Catechism* (Q.25) and Calvin's *Geneva Catechism* (1545) both grounded the Trinity in revelation (*quoniam ita se praedicat*), and claims to be doing "materially" what the older Reformed theology intended, while doing it more explicitly (§8.2, lines 809–825).

At the close of §8.3, Barth acknowledges that his own unveiling/veiling/impartation schema "came remarkably close to the fatal Augustinian argument" of finding a vestigium in a logical structure, and that one could question whether it too relies on "a possibility of logical construction" rather than Scripture alone (lines 2416–2431). He does not resolve this worry but notes it as an irreducible risk of all theological language.
