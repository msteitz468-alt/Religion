# Mindfulness of Death and Impermanence

The first contemplation of the **small-capacity** path in the **[[lamrim-chenmo|Lamrim Chenmo]]**
(Vol. I, ch. 9). [[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] holds that the conception "I will not die" is "the source
of all deterioration," generating preoccupation with this life alone, while genuine mindfulness of
death is "the source of all that is excellent" — to be cultivated "in the beginning, middle, and end"
of practice. The death to be feared is not separation from relatives (mere attachment) but dying with
one's future welfare unsecured: "Why should anyone who abides in the teaching fear death?" (*Garland
of Birth Stories*).

## The Three Roots, Nine Reasons, Three Decisions

1. **Death is certain** — the Lord of Death cannot be evaded by wealth, mantra, or medicine; the life
   span only diminishes; little time within it is free for practice (Chay-ga-wa: barely five usable
   years out of sixty).
2. **The time of death is uncertain** — the human life span is indefinite; the causes of death are
   many and those of life few ("like a butter lamp standing in a strong breeze," Nāgārjuna); the body
   is fragile. *This root* "is the most important … the very thing that will redirect your mind."
3. **At death nothing helps but practice** — neither friends, nor resources ("not even the slightest
   particle"), nor one's own body can follow.

Each root yields a decision: to practise the Dharma; to practise *now*; to practise it *purely*. Per
Bo-do-wa, until non-attachment to this life arises "the paths of all the teachings remain blocked."
The *Great Final Nirvāṇa Sūtra*: "Among all ideas, the idea of impermanence and death is supreme."
