# Shakti

**Shakti** (from *śak*, "to be able, to have power"; related to "capable") is the concept of
divine creative power — feminine, dynamic energy — and the name for the classes of goddesses who
embody or *are* that power. It is one of the most semantically flexible terms in Hindu theology,
covering at minimum:

1. **The power belonging to a male god**, usually personified as his wife or female counterpart
   (Shiva's shakti = [[parvati|Parvati]]/Sati; Vishnu's shakti = Lakshmi; Brahma's shakti = Sarasvati)
2. **An independent goddess who is herself shakti** — not derived from, or subordinate to, any
   male god (Chandika/[[durga|Durga]]; Kali in her independent Tantric form)
3. **The cosmological creative principle** itself (in Shakta theology): *mahāśakti* as the ground
   of being, beyond all masculine/feminine duality, though manifesting as the Goddess

The distinction between (1) and (2) is structurally central: it corresponds to the difference
between the **breast goddess** and the **tooth goddess** (A. K. Ramanujan's typology, invoked by
Doniger).

## Tooth Goddess vs. Breast Goddess

This typological pair, developed by A. K. Ramanujan and extensively deployed by Doniger, organizes
the two poles of Hindu goddess theology:

**Breast goddesses** — married, consort, subservient, fertile but controlled:
- Examples: Parvati (wife of Shiva), Lakshmi (wife of Vishnu), Sita (wife of Rama), Savitri
- Characteristics: auspicious (*mangala*), white or golden, give prosperity and fertility,
  associated with the household and civilized space
- Their shakti "belongs to" their husband in the sense that they are his power; separated from
  him, they are dangerous or incomplete

**Tooth goddesses** — celibate or unmarried, fierce, killer, independent:
- Examples: Chandika/Durga, Kali (the Puranic split of Parvati), Chamunda, Vindhyavāsinī,
  Karaikkal Ammaiyar's theology of the cremation-ground goddess
- Characteristics: blood-drinking, skull-wearing, fierce (*ugra*), associated with periphery
  (forests, cremation grounds, battlefields), non-maternal or "mother of all creatures"
- Their shakti belongs to no one; they are their own shakti

**The same goddess can split into both poles.** [[parvati|Parvati]] (breast goddess) is the same
divine entity as Kali (tooth goddess) — the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa explicitly narrates how Parvati's
dark skin is stripped off to become Kali. The topology is not a stable classification but a dynamic
tension.

## The Shakti Paradox in the *Devīmāhātmya*

In the "Glorification of the Goddess" (*Devīmāhātmya*, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, c. 5th–7th c. CE), the
tension between types (1) and (2) is maximally on display:

- **Brahmin frame narrative**: Chandika is created from the combined *tejas* (energies) of all
  the gods — she is derived from them, like a consort from her husband's power
- **Actual mythology**: She absorbs *all* the gods' shaktis into herself and kills Shumbha alone;
  she refuses marriage to Shumbha; Shiva is her messenger, not her lord

"Chandika is a shakti who is her own shakti." The text simultaneously concedes to and undermines
the consort-subordination framework.

## Shakti in Tantra

Tantric traditions (developing from c. 6th–8th c. CE onward) radicalize the concept:

- **Shakti as cosmic creative power** (*mahāmāyā*, *mahāśakti*): the Goddess is not one power
  among many but the ground of all manifestation; Shiva without Shakti is a corpse (*Shava*)
- **The Shiva–Shakti polarity**: Shiva is pure undifferentiated consciousness (*cit*); Shakti is
  dynamic power, the principle of manifestation; the universe arises from their union
- **Śrī Vidyā and the Tantric Goddess**: in some lineages, the Goddess (as Tripurasundarī, Lalitā)
  *is* ultimate reality; male gods are her emanations

For the Tantric sects (Pashupatas, Kapalikas, Kaulas) that emerge in this period, see
[[hinduism|Hinduism]] tradition page and the relevant sect pages.

## Shakti, Possession, and Women

A recurring tension in Doniger's analysis: **goddess-worship does not straightforwardly empower
women**. The inverse correlation appears repeatedly:

- The more powerful the independent tooth goddess in texts, the more tightly women are controlled
  in practice (the power of the goddess may reflect anxiety about female power rather than
  celebration of it)
- **The exception**: women who are *possessed by* fierce goddesses become shaktis themselves and
  temporarily override caste and social hierarchy (this exception matters for bhakti saints like
  Karaikkal Ammaiyar; see [[bhakti|Bhakti]])

Doniger's summary: "The pious hope of goddess feminists, and others, that the worship of goddesses
is Good for Women is dashed by observations of India."
