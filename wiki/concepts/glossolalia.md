# Glossolalia (Speaking in Tongues)

**Glossolalia** (Greek *glōssai*, "tongues") is the phenomenon of Spirit-prompted speech in the
early church, most prominently at [[pentecost|Pentecost]] ([[acts|Acts]] 2:4–13) and in the Pauline
congregation at Corinth (1 Cor. 12–14). As read by [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]] (BECNT *Acts*,
2007), the term covers a broad phenomenon with distinct sub-types, and its function in Acts is
revelatory and declarative rather than evidentiary.

## Bock's Two-Type Distinction

Bock's central analytic claim is that "tongues" in the New Testament is a broad phenomenon
comprising **two distinct sub-types**:

- **One-step foreign-language speech (Acts 2).** At Pentecost the disciples speak in real
  languages understood *immediately* by the hearers ("we hear ... in our own tongues," 2:8–11).
  Bock stresses there is **no "miracle of hearing"** — the Spirit works on the *speakers*, not the
  audience (following Haenchen against a hearing-miracle reading). Comprehension is direct; no
  interpreter is required.
- **Two-step ecstatic speech plus interpretation (1 Cor. 14).** The Corinthian phenomenon is
  ecstatic utterance that *requires interpretation* to be intelligible to the assembly. Bock treats
  both as sub-types of one broad category (following Marshall, Harrison, Larkin), against Johnson's
  reading of tongues as purely Greco-Roman ecstatic speech.

The differentiating mark is thus the **one-step immediate comprehension of Acts 2** versus the
**two-step ecstatic-plus-interpretation** structure of 1 Corinthians 14.

## Not Required "Evidence" of Spirit-Baptism

Bock draws a further conclusion for the theology of Christian initiation. At the Gentile
"Pentecost" (Acts 10:44–48) the Spirit "falls" on Cornelius's household *before* baptism and
without the laying on of hands, and its presence is manifested by tongues and praise. Bock reads
this to mean that tongues are **not a required "evidence"** of Spirit-baptism, nor a "second"
gift subsequent to conversion: the gift is the Spirit itself (2:38–39, an epexegetical genitive —
"the gift *is* the Spirit"), and its bestowal follows "no set pattern" (see
[[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]]). At Ephesus (19:1–7), Bock notes, glossolalia recurs when Paul lays
hands on the disciples of John — "the final mention of tongues in Acts."

## Revelatory / Declarative Function

For Bock the function of tongues in Acts is **revelatory and declarative**: at Pentecost the crowd
hears the disciples "telling ... the mighty deeds of God" (*ta megaleia tou theou*, 2:11), so that
the phenomenon serves the proclamation of God's saving work through Jesus. This coheres with Bock's
broader reading of the Spirit in Acts as the "Spirit of witness and testimony" — glossolalia is
speech that magnifies God and declares his acts, not private ecstasy for its own sake.
