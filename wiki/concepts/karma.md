# Karma (kamma)

**Karma** (Sanskrit *karman*; Pali *kamma*, "action") is the principle that intentional
action conditions future experience — the moral causality that powers
[[samsara|saṃsāra]]. It is shared across the Indian traditions but is given a distinctive,
intention-centered definition in [[buddhism|Buddhism]].

## Vedic Origins: From Ṛta and Sacrifice

Before karma was an ethical or psychological law it was, on [[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|
Dasgupta's]] account (Vol. I, Ch. II), a by-product of **Vedic sacrifice**. The Brāhmaṇas conceived the
sacrifice as a kind of **machinery** whose correct performance produced its result *of itself* — "like the
latent power of electricity," independent of any god's grace, the gods themselves being mere instruments.
Joined to the cosmic order of **[[rita|ṛta]]** (the inviolable law by which acts produce fitting effects)
and to the belief that one's enjoyments and sufferings track one's deeds, this yielded the **Law of
Karma** and [[samsara|transmigration]]. Sacrifice itself was called *karma* (action). The Buddha then
**relocated** this older ritual sense — see below.

## Desire, Not Karma: the Upaniṣadic Stage (Dasgupta, Vol. I, Ch. III)

A point [[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] stresses about the *earlier* [[upanishads|
Upaniṣads]]: "None of the complexities of the karma doctrine" found in later Hinduism are present yet.
The cause of rebirth named there is **desire (*kāma*)**, not karma — "the whole scheme is worked out on
the principle of desire, and karma only serves as the link between it and the actual effects desired."
As [[yajnavalkya|Yājñavalkya]] puts it (Bṛh. IV.4): "as he desires, so he wills; as he wills, so he acts;
as the act is done, so it happens." The man without desires "becomes Brahman"; so emancipation comes not
by accumulating good karma but by the **extinction of desire** through self-knowledge. This is the Hindu
backdrop against which the Buddha's intention-centered redefinition (below) is a further shift.

## The Pan-Indian Karma Theory (Dasgupta, Vol. I, Ch. IV)

In his "General Observations," [[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] makes karma the **first
of the points on which all Indian systems agree** (Cārvāka excepted): every action leaves "some sort of
potency" that ordains future joy or sorrow, carrying the doer across rebirths until its fruit is reaped.
He records the technical apparatus the *āstika* systems built on this:

- **The unseen residue** — the potency of an act that matures only after a delay is the ***adṛṣṭa*** ("the
  unseen") or ***apūrva*** ("the new"); its *modus operandi* is unknown, a survival of the Vedic idea that
  a correctly performed sacrifice produces its result by an automatic, quasi-magical virtue.
- ***Vāsanā*** — the dormant **instincts/impressions** of every past life, which "come forth" in whatever
  birth (as this animal or that man) the ripening karma determines; the length and content of a life are
  preordained by the maturing actions of past lives.
- **Four kinds of action** (a classification Dasgupta ties to Yoga, probably from Jaina influence):
  *śukla* (white/virtuous), *kṛṣṇa* (black/wicked), *śukla-kṛṣṇa* (mixed, as most acts are), and
  ***aśukla-akṛṣṇa*** (neither — acts of desireless self-renunciation/meditation). Only the last
  accumulates **no fresh karma**; true knowledge then burns up the stored, unripened karma, while already
  ripened karma must still be lived out (the seed of the later *jīvanmukti* idea).
- **The Jaina variant** — uniquely physicalist: karma is a **subtle matter** that the passions, acting "like
  a viscous substance," attract into the soul, forming the *kārmaṇa-śarīra* that encircles it from birth to
  birth and produces a soul-coloration (*leśyā*). It is exhausted by austerity, after which the liberated
  soul rises to the top of the universe.

## Doniger's Six Meanings of Karma (Modern Critical — *The Hindus*, Ch. 7)

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 7) traces karma's semantic evolution
through **six layered meanings**, each adding to but not eliminating the earlier ones:

1. **Action** (*karman* from *kṛ*, "to make or do" — cognate with Latin *creo*): the most basic meaning,
   the noun for any act. One can think, speak, or *do* something, with escalating moral consequences.
2. **Ritual action**, particularly Vedic sacrifice: karma's primary sense in the Rig Veda. Sacrifice *is*
   karma.
3. **Morally charged action** (good or bad): the Upaniṣadic innovation — a meter always running, constantly
   charging something to one's account. Begins here.
4. **Retributive karma across rebirths**: action whose moral charge determines the nature of future lives.
   "You become a sheep that people eat if you have eaten a sheep." This is the full saṃsāric doctrine.
5. **Fate / innate activity** (*sva-karma*): not the cause of future lives but the result of past lives —
   the "inescapable role in life that one was born to play, one's work, or innate activity." Manu's use:
   Brahmins are born to the work of Brahmins, potters to pottery — the karma assigned at creation.
   Fitzgerald's *Gatsby* ending — "borne back ceaselessly into the past" — captures this aspect, except
   that in Hinduism "we are also borne back ceaselessly into the future."
6. **Transferable karma**: good and bad karma that can be transferred from one person to another, either
   intentionally or unintentionally. An unfed guest takes away the host's good karma and leaves bad karma
   behind. Nachiketas "eats" Death's good deeds on three nights of fasting, which is what forces Death to
   teach him his secrets. This transfer logic has roots in the Vedic *Varuna* poems and the Brāhmaṇas'
   distribution of Brahminicide (see [[indra|Indra]]).

Doniger's key point: it is not always clear *which* meaning is operative in any given passage. The
layering of meanings — ritual, ethical, psychological, cosmic, social — is itself one of the major sources
of multiplicity and misunderstanding within Hinduism.

## The Buddhist Redefinition

The Buddha's pivotal move was to relocate karma from outward ritual act (its older Vedic
sense) to **intention** (*cetanā*): "It is intention (*cetanā*), monks, that I call karma;
having intended, one acts by body, speech, and mind." Karma is therefore *ethical and
psychological*, not mechanical: wholesome (*kusala*) volitions rooted in non-greed,
non-hatred, and non-delusion lead toward happiness and a [[samsara|fortunate rebirth]];
unwholesome (*akusala*) ones lead to suffering. The ripening (*vipāka*) of action may come
in this life, the next, or a later one.

## Place in the Path

In [[bhikkhu-bodhi|Bodhi]]'s anthology, **right view that affirms karma and rebirth** is
the *mundane* right view (chapter V, "The Way to a Fortunate Rebirth") — the indispensable
ethical foundation, distinct from the *supramundane* right view of the
[[four-noble-truths|Four Noble Truths]] that aims beyond rebirth entirely. Crucially,
[[nirvana|nirvana]] is **not** the product of karma: it is the *unconditioned*, attained by
exhausting craving, not by accumulating merit. Good karma improves one's place *within*
saṃsāra; the [[noble-eightfold-path|path]] leads *out* of it.

## Tsong-kha-pa on Karma (Lamrim Chenmo, Vol. I, chs. 13–16)

In the **[[lamrim-chenmo|Lamrim Chenmo]]**, certainty about karma is "the correct viewpoint for all
Buddhists … the foundation of all virtue." [[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] lays out **four general
features**: karma is *certain* (happiness only from virtue, suffering only from nonvirtue); it
*magnifies* (a tiny seed yields a great fruit — "like a poison ingested"); one does *not* meet the
effect of an action *not* done; and what *is* done *does not perish* ("Even in one hundred eons / Karma
does not perish"). He details the **ten nonvirtuous paths of action** (analyzed by basis, attitude,
performance, culmination) and their fruitional, causally-concordant, and environmental effects;
distinguishes **projecting** from **completing** karma; and orders how karma ripens (weightiest first,
then what is manifest at death, then the most habituated, then the earliest).

**Purification** is possible through the **four powers** (eradication/contrition; applying remedies —
e.g. recitation, emptiness, images, buddha-names; turning away from faults; and the foundation of
[[refuge|refuge]] and [[bodhicitta|bodhicitta]]): "karma does not perish even in one hundred eons" is
said only of karma for which no remedy has been cultivated. He nonetheless warns that understanding
emptiness while being uncertain about karma is "a mistaken understanding of emptiness," since
emptiness *is* the meaning of [[dependent-origination|dependent-arising]] and confirms cause and
effect. The **afflictions** (kleśas), not karma alone, are the primary root of cyclic existence — "in
the absence of afflictions … there will be no sprout of suffering."

## Karma and Non-Self

As with [[samsara|rebirth]], karma operates without a permanent agent ([[anatta|non-self]]):
there is action and its fruit, but "no doer is found." The continuity of moral consequence
across lives is secured by the conditioned mental stream, not by a substantial soul — a
point [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] later analyzes under the [[two-truths|two truths]]
([[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] 17, on action and its fruit).

## Cross-Tradition Note

- **Hinduism** ties karma to *dharma* (duty) and, in the [[bhagavad-gita|Gita]], teaches
  *karma-yoga* — acting without attachment to the fruits.
- **Jainism** treats karma as a subtle *material* substance literally weighing down the
  soul, removed by austerity — the most physicalist version.
- **Buddhism** alone makes intention constitutive and denies the self that bears it.

## Jainism — Karma as Material Substance (Dundas)

[[dundas-the-jains|Dundas]] (*The Jains*, ch. 4) details the most physicalist karma theory in South
Asia. Karma is a **fine material substance** (*pudgala*, a form of [[ajiva|non-soul]]) that adheres to
the [[jiva|soul]]; the mechanism by which a formless soul attracts matter "has to be taken on trust as a
matter of inference and faith." The binding agents are the **passions** (*kasaya* — anger, pride,
deception, greed; the word means "resin/stickiness"): when calmed, karma cannot stick, "just as a clod of
earth thrown at a dried-up wall will not stick to it" (Akalanka).

- **The nine *tattvas* (reals)** map the soul's career: [[jiva]], [[ajiva]], *asrava* (inflow of karma),
  *punya* (merit), *papa* (demerit), *bandha* (binding), *samvara* (warding off inflow), *nirjara*
  (burning off bound karma), and *[[moksha]]* (release). Umasvati's [[tattvartha-sutra|Tattvartha
  Sutra]] gives a seven-real version (TS 1.4).
- **Eight types of karma**, in two groups: four **harming** (*ghatiya*) — delusory (*mohaniya*, the
  keystone), knowledge-covering, perception-obscuring, obstacle — which damage the soul's innate
  [[kevala-jnana|omniscience]], bliss and energy; and four **non-harming** (*aghatiya*) — feeling, name
  (whose subtype *tirthankara-naman* destines one to become a [[tirthankara|fordmaker]]), life-span, clan.
- Karma imparts a soul-**colouring** (*lesya*), perhaps borrowed from the [[ajivikas|Ajivikas]]. Jain
  karma is "much more elaborately thought out and systematised than its equivalent in Hinduism or
  Buddhism" yet does **not** entail determinism. From [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]]'s *nishchaya* (ultimate)
  standpoint, karma does not truly modify the soul at all.
