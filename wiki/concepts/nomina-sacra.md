# Nomina Sacra

(*Latin*: "sacred names") A distinctive early Christian scribal convention in which certain theologically significant words were written in contracted (abbreviated) form with a horizontal overline (*titulus*) placed over the letters. The practice is evidenced in the earliest surviving fragments of Christian manuscripts (paleographically dated to the mid-to-late 2nd century AD) and became a standard feature of Christian manuscripts through the Byzantine period.

## The Convention

The *nomina sacra* involves writing a word using only selected letters — typically the first and last letters of the word ("contracted" form, e.g., ΙΣ for *Iēsous*), or sometimes just the first letters ("suspended" form, e.g., ΙΗ also for *Iēsous*). Over this abbreviated form, a horizontal stroke is placed. Even a reader unfamiliar with Greek can immediately spot these abbreviated forms in a manuscript — the overline makes them visually distinctive.

**The core four words** written as *nomina sacra* most consistently from the earliest period:

| Greek word | Meaning | Example contracted form |
|---|---|---|
| Ἰησοῦς (*Iēsous*) | Jesus | ΙΣ, ΙΥ, or ΙΗ |
| Χριστός (*Christos*) | Christ | ΧΣ, ΧΥ |
| κύριος (*kyrios*) | Lord | ΚΣ, ΚΥ |
| θεός (*theos*) | God | ΘΣ, ΘΥ |

By the Byzantine period, the list expanded to approximately fifteen words, including Father (*patēr*), Spirit (*pneuma*), Son (*hyios*), Cross (*stauros*), Heaven (*ouranos*), Jerusalem (*Ierousalēm*), Israel (*Israēl*), David (*Dauid*), Mother (*mētēr*), Man/Human (*anthrōpos*).

## Origin and Significance

The origin of the practice is debated. [[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]] argues that *Iēsous* was probably the word that initiated the convention, on the basis that:

1. *Iēsous* has a distinctive variant treatment (both the "suspended" and "contracted" forms), suggesting a more complex history that distinguishes it from the other words.
2. The horizontal overline (*titulus*) does not appear in standard Greek or Latin abbreviation practices; it seems to be a distinctively Christian invention.
3. Beginning with Jesus' name fits the centrality of Jesus in early Christian devotion — see [[dyadic-devotion|Dyadic Devotion]].

Others have argued for *kyrios* or *theos* as the initiating word, pointing to Jewish scribal reverence for the divine name (YHWH) as the analogical precedent.

**Motivation**: The *nomina sacra* were almost certainly reverential in intent — a visual expression of the copyist's piety toward the persons named. This parallels Jewish scribal practices around the divine name YHWH (writing it in a different script, using dots, substituting a surrogate word). However, the Christian and Jewish practices are importantly different:

- Jewish scribes used their devices to signal *oral substitution*: when the reader encountered YHWH, they substituted a spoken substitute (*Adonay*, *Elohim*). The scribal marker was audiobly functional.
- Christian *nomina sacra* were **purely visual**: readers pronounced the words normally when reading aloud. No one listening to a reading would know the words were written in abbreviated form. The *nomina sacra* were a visual phenomenon addressed to the *reader* (or copyist), not to the listener.

This makes them **the earliest recoverable evidence of a distinctly Christian visual culture** — an act of scribal devotion embedded in the manuscript page itself, predating catacomb paintings and other early Christian iconography.

## The Nomina Sacra and the Codex

The *nomina sacra* practice is intimately linked with early Christian manuscript culture more broadly. Both appear so early and so universally in surviving Christian manuscripts that they must have been established conventions well before any of our extant papyri. Hurtado draws a direct line from these early scribal conventions to the later development of explicitly iconographic Christian visual culture (icons, chi-rho symbols, etc.).

The *nomina sacra* also track what early Christians regarded as scripture: they appear much more consistently in copies of texts treated as sacred (OT writings, Gospels, Pauline letters) than in copies of other Christian literary texts.

## Jewish Precedent and Christian Innovation

The analogy to Jewish treatment of YHWH (reverential scribal devices) is real but limited:

- **Similarity**: Both reflect scribal reverence for the divine presence in/through textual names.
- **Difference 1**: The Christian list extends the reverential treatment beyond a single divine name to include Jesus (*Iēsous*), which is theologically significant — it implies a functional equivalence between the name of Jesus and the name of God.
- **Difference 2**: No instance of the *nomina sacra* practice has been found in pre-Christian Jewish manuscripts.
- **Difference 3**: The specific mechanics (horizontal overline over a contracted form) appear to be a Christian invention.

Hurtado's conclusion: the *nomina sacra* represent a **Christian scribal innovation** shaped by Jewish precedent but not derived from it, expressing specifically Christian piety — particularly the dyadic veneration of God and Jesus.

## Significance for the Dating of High Christology

Because the *nomina sacra* are evidenced in the oldest surviving Christian manuscripts, and because they reflect the reverential treatment of Jesus alongside God, they constitute independent material evidence that high Christology (treating Jesus as worthy of devotion equivalent to God) was present very early — consistent with the literary evidence in Paul's letters and the devotional pattern Hurtado calls [[dyadic-devotion|dyadic devotion]].

## Nomina Sacra in the Context of Second-Century Textual Culture (Kruger)

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] (*Christianity at the Crossroads*, ch. 6) situates the *nomina sacra* within the broader picture of early Christian "textual culture" — the distinctive bookishness of Christianity in the Greco-Roman world.

**The codex connection**: Christians adopted the codex (bound book, not scroll) as their standard book format from the very beginning of manuscript production — a striking contrast to both Jewish practice (scrolls) and standard Greco-Roman literary convention (rolls for literature). The combination of codex + *nomina sacra* signals that Christian manuscripts were produced within a specific community with recognized scribal conventions — not simply ad hoc private copying.

**Copy centres and scribal conventions**: The consistency of *nomina sacra* across geographically distant manuscripts implies either: (a) a centralized scribal tradition with "master copies" that were used as models; or (b) the early dissemination of the convention as a training practice among Christian scribes. Kruger follows Hurtado (and Paap) that the uniformity argues for early and widespread dissemination of the practice, consistent with active Christian publication networks.

**Reader's aids**: Second-century Christian manuscripts also show *reader's aids* — punctuation marks, sense-unit spacing (verse-like divisions), ekthesis (paragraph indentation) — indicating manuscripts designed for public reading to diverse non-elite audiences. This confirms that the primary use of these texts was liturgical and communal, not private scholarly study. The scribal investment in reader-accessibility reflects the same community that produced the *nomina sacra*.

**Evidence for "Christian" manuscripts**: The *nomina sacra* serve as a diagnostic marker: a manuscript with consistent *nomina sacra* is a Christian production. This is practically significant for identifying Christian texts in the papyrus finds, especially where content alone might not be conclusive.

**P103 and scribal correction**: Papyrus 103 (P103), a 2nd-3rd c. fragment of Matthew, shows second-hand corrections — a scribe reviewed and emended a copyist's work. This is evidence for quality control in Christian manuscript production, countering the assumption that early Christian copying was casual and unchecked.
