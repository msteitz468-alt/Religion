# Bhakti (Devotion)

**Bhakti** (from *bhaj*, "to share in, participate in, belong to") is the **devotional path** in
Hinduism — the way of loving dedication to a personal God. At its earliest attestations it means
little more than "belonging to" or "being devoted to" a person, as a servant or loyal friend. By the
medieval period it becomes the dominant soteriological mode of Hinduism, characterizing movements
of intense emotional love between the devotee and god (Kṛṣṇa, Rāma, Viṣṇu, Śiva, or the Goddess).

## Early History

The earliest clear reference to *bhakti* as a religious category is in the **[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad
Gita]]** (c. 2nd c. BCE–2nd c. CE), where it appears as one of three **yogas** (paths to liberation)
alongside **karma-yoga** (action) and **jñāna-yoga** (knowledge).

Even before the Gita, protean forms of bhakti appear:
- **Yudhiṣṭhira's dog** (Mahābhārata 17): the dog who follows Yudhiṣṭhira to heaven is *bhakta*
  (devoted, loyal) to him. "Yudhiṣṭhira refuses to abandon a dog who is 'devoted' (*bhakta*) to him;
  heaven will not be heaven if he cannot bring his dog with him." At this period bhakti denotes
  "belonging to someone, being dedicated to someone as a servant or loyal friend."
- **Hate-love (*dveṣa-bhakti*)**: the ogre Māricha becomes so terrified of Rāma that he thinks of him
  everywhere — "this whole wilderness has become nothing but Rāma to me… I see him in my dreams."
  Later bhakti theologians describe this as a path to liberation via the deity one hates. Doniger: "the
  reference to the R [in Rāma's name] also foreshadows the importance of the name of Rāma in later bhakti."
- **Heliodorus pillar (115 BCE)**: a Greek ambassador to the Śuṅga court, Heliodorus of Taxila, erected
  a pillar at Besnagar (near the Buddhist stupas at Sāñcī) topped with Viṣṇu's eagle (Garuḍa) and called
  himself a **Bhāgavata** (devotee of Bhagavān = Viṣṇu). This is significant evidence of the conversion
  of a non-Indian — not to Buddhism but to devotional Hinduism — as early as the second century BCE.

## The Gita's Three Paths

In the **Bhagavad Gita**, Krishna resolves the tension between **dharma** (sva-dharma, works) and
**moksha** (renunciation, knowledge) by introducing bhakti as a **mediating third term**:

| Path | Sanskrit | Approach | Tension it resolves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Karma-yoga** | action, ritual | "karma without kāma" — act without attachment to results | Sva-dharma requires violence; renunciation forbids it |
| **Jñāna-yoga** | knowledge, gnosis | Upaniṣadic insight: the self is Brahman | Transcendent goal of moksha vs. householder life |
| **Bhakti-yoga** | devotion, love | Personal surrender to Kṛṣṇa | Bridges the abstract (nirguṇa) and personal (saguṇa) |

Krishna tells Arjuna that bhakti is the **supreme** path (Gita 9–12); by acting with devotion, Arjuna
is freed from the "hellish consequences of his actions." Bhakti modifies moksha by allowing the devotee
to love a **god with qualities** (*saguṇa*) while still approaching the god *without* qualities
(*nirguṇa*): "You are Brahman, and I love you."

Doniger notes the Gita's "brilliant solution" to the householder/renouncer tension: "Karma without kāma"
means not that one should not desire certain results, but that one should not expect them or regard
them as the point. "It's the journey that counts, not where you end up."

## Bhakti and the Three Alliances

Doniger identifies three structural alliances running through the history of Vedic religion, of which
**bhakti is the third** (see the [[rig-veda|Ṛg Veda]] page and the [[wendy-doniger|Doniger commentator
page]] for the full typology):

1. Alliance of gods and humans *against* Asuras (the "orthodox" Vedic structure).
2. Alliance of ascetics *against* gods (tapas overcomes divine privilege).
3. **Bhakti**: humans bypass both sacrifice and asceticism through direct loving devotion — "a democratic
   move that cuts across the class and gender barriers of the first two alliances."

## South Indian Bhakti: Nāyanmārs and Āḷvārs (c. 6th–9th c. CE)

The first great bhakti movements arise in **South India** (Tamil Nadu and adjacent regions) among
wandering poet-saints. The South Indian situation gave bhakti its classical form.

### Antecedents: Caṅkam Poetry

The earliest Tamil literature — **Caṅkam poetry** (c. 1st–3rd c. CE) — is secular: love (*akam*)
and war (*puram*) poems organized by the **five landscapes of emotion** (each landscape corresponds
to a stage of a love affair: mountains = union; forests = anxious waiting; shores = separation;
fields = patient waiting; desert = infidelity). This secular love poetry was later absorbed wholesale
into bhakti, which replaced the human lover/beloved with the devotee/god. "The ancient Tamil poem
retains the same landscape, the same emotion, but the 'he' who was the beloved becomes the god."

The bhakti concept of **viraha** (the longing-in-separation for the absent god) is mapped directly
onto the Caṅkam literature's landscape of separation.

### Nāyanmārs: Śaiva Poet-Saints (63 in number)

The **Nāyanmārs** ("leaders of the way") were 63 Śaiva bhakti saints, whose verses were compiled
into the *Tēvāram* and related anthologies and are still sung as temple liturgy. They composed in
Tamil (not Sanskrit), worshipped Śiva in his *sthala*-specific forms (each of the 276 holy sites
of Śiva has its own form, its own image, its own story), and expressed intense personal devotion.

Key Nāyanmārs (from Doniger):

- **Campantar** (Sambandar): the most celebrated; reportedly responsible for the mass impalement of
  8,000 Jainas at Madurai after a public disputation with the Jaina court-poet. This **bhakti and
  violence** tradition is not marginal; it is central. Bhakti does not replace religious hostility;
  it intensifies it.
- **Appar** (Tirunavukkarasar): converted from Jainism; a leather worker; restored to Śaiva faith
  after illness; one of the most productive poets
- **Maṇikkavācakar**: deeply erotic-mystical vocabulary; the model of the devotee who surrenders
  everything to the god's "grace" (*arul*)

### Āḷvārs: Vaiṣṇava Poet-Saints (12 in number)

The **Āḷvārs** ("those who are deeply immersed in God") were 12 Vaiṣṇava bhakti saints whose
verses form the *Divyaprabandham* — 4,000 Tamil verses. Like the Nāyanmārs, they worshipped specific
*sthala* forms of Viṣṇu at specific pilgrimage sites. The theology is highly emotional:

- **Nammāḷvār**: considered the greatest; his *Tiruvāymoḷi* encodes the devotee's soul (identified
  as feminine) yearning for Viṣṇu
- **Āṇṭāḷ (Antal)**: the only female Āḷvār; imagines herself as Kṛṣṇa's wife; reportedly "married"
  the god's image at Śrīraṅgam and vanished into the image. Her position in the Āḷvār tradition was
  contested — some later authorities doubted a woman could be a true devotee — but she was eventually
  included as the ninth Āḷvār and given the epithet *Cūṭikkoḍutta Cuṭarkoḍi* ("bright vine who
  offered [garlands] she herself had worn").

### Female Bhakti Saints

South Indian bhakti produced several women saints who **defied marriage conventions** — inverting
Manu's framework by choosing devotion to the deity over wifely duty:

- **Āṇṭāḷ (Antal)**: see above. Refused to marry a mortal man; "married" the god.
- **Karaikkal Ammaiyar**: a Nāyanmār saint; performed intense asceticism; asked Śiva to transform
  her into a female ghoul (*pēy*) so she could live forever in the cremation ground singing his
  praise. She is depicted as a living skeleton with wild eyes and broken teeth (a tooth goddess in
  human form), haunting the cremation ground while Śiva dances. "I want to be the most repulsive
  thing in the universe, so that I will be as far from the human world as possible and entirely
  focused on you." Her body-rejection is the extreme form of bhakti renunciation.
- **Māhadēvī (Akka Mahadevi)**: (Kannada saint; later) threw off her clothes, wore only her hair as a
  covering; her poems to Śiva are erotic-mystical.

**The common structure:** These women saints do not simply "transcend gender" via bhakti's
democratic impulse; they instrumentalize gender — using the erotic vocabulary of wifely surrender
to subvert wifely duty. They are not reformers; they are devotees whose devotion has no room for
human marriage.

### Darshan and the Temple System

South Indian bhakti is inseparable from the **temple** — specifically the South Indian stone temple,
which became architecturally elaborate under the Pallavas and Colas (c. 7th–9th c. CE):

- The god is a **king** in his palace; the devotee is a supplicant at court
- **[[darshan|Darshan]]** — the mutual gaze between devotee and deity — is the central ritual act
- Temple worship (*pūjā*) follows the protocol of royal service: waking the deity, bathing, feeding,
  entertaining, putting to sleep
- **Kingship and bhakti are co-constitutive**: the king proves his legitimacy by devotion to the
  deity; the deity's glory is proven by the king's patronage. "The god requires the king as his image
  requires the worshipper."

### Bhakti and Caste: The Kannappar Case

**Kannappar** (Tin̠n̠an̠ar) is the Pariah hunter-saint — a tribesman (Nāyanmār) who discovers the
Śiva liṅga in the forest and worships it by spitting water from his mouth (he has no ritual vessel),
offering hunted meat (not vegetarian prasāda), and flowers carried in his hair (not offered with
clean hands). When told by Brahmin priests that his worship is polluting, he responds by tearing
out one of his own eyes and pressing it to the liṅga when the divine image's painted eye is damaged.

Śiva appears and acknowledges: Kannappar's devotion is greater than any Brahmin priest's ritual
compliance. "You have worshipped me truly."

The tale is simultaneously:
- A **caste critique**: the highest devotion comes from a Pariah, not a Brahmin
- A **caste reinforcement**: the story is narrated by Brahmins, in a context where Kannappar is
  praised by the god but remains exceptional — the exception proves the rule

Doniger's summary of the double bind: "Bhakti was simultaneously a protest against the hierarchical
structures of Hindu society and (in the temples and the royal ideology that supported them) an
instrument for perpetuating those same structures."

### Bhakti and Interreligious Competition

South Indian bhakti did not develop in a vacuum:
- **Jainism and Buddhism** were both well-established in South India before the bhakti saints;
  the Nāyanmārs and Āḷvārs explicitly competed with them and the Campantar/Jaina-impaling story
  is the most dramatic example of sectarian bhakti violence
- **Islam** arrived on the Malabar Coast via Arab merchants by the 7th–8th c. CE; Doniger suggests
  the Vaiṣṇava concept of **prapatti** (total surrender/self-abandonment to Viṣṇu's grace) may
  have been shaped by contact with Islamic *tawakkul* (trust in Allah). This is speculative but
  widely debated.
- **Thomas Christianity** (Mar Thoma tradition) claims apostolic presence in Kerala by the 1st c. CE,
  though historical evidence is more certain by the 3rd–4th c. The multi-religious South Indian
  context was genuine.

## Medieval Bhakti Movements (North India, later)

North Indian bhakti (14th–17th c. CE) — the **Sants** (Kabīr, Raidās, Mīrābāī, Tukārām, Sūrdās)
and the Sikh Gurūs — intensify the anti-caste, anti-ritual, and anti-Brahmin dimensions of bhakti,
composing in vernacular (Hindi, Punjabi, Marathi), and generally moving away from
temple-and-image devotion toward the nirguṇa (formless) divine. These belong to Doniger Scopes 8–9
and will be added when those chapters are read.

## Tulsidas and North Indian Rāma Bhakti (c. 1532–1623 CE)

[[tulsidas|Tulsidas]]'s *Ramcaritmanas* (Hindi/Avadhi retelling of the Rāmāyaṇa, c. 1574) is the
single text that most defines popular Rāma bhakti in North India — still performed annually at the
*Rāmlīlā* throughout the region. Two key theological moves:

- **The shadow Sita**: Tulsidas incorporated the *māyā-sītā* tradition to resolve Sita's fire
  ordeal — only the shadow Sita was tested; the real Sita returns from the fire unharmed, and the
  text ends with Rāma and Sita together. This is a Vedantic (illusionism) solution to a bhakti
  problem: the god cannot have treated his devotee unjustly.
- **The crow/Pariah**: the manifestation of god's universal form (seen by Arjuna, Brahma, and
  Krishna's mother) is here given to a crow — "an unclean scavenger" who was a Pariah in a former
  life. This extends bhakti's democratic potential through the masking device of an animal, avoiding
  direct confrontation with Brahmin authority.

## North Indian Bhakti: The Sants (c. 13th–17th c. CE)

As Islam entered and consolidated in North India under the Delhi Sultanate (1210–1526), bhakti
evolved a new and radical strand — the **Sant** (saint) movement — influenced by both Hindu bhakti
traditions and Sufi mysticism.

The Sants typically emphasized **nirguṇa** devotion: God "without qualities," an abstract Absolute
beyond name and form, beyond both Hindu and Muslim sectarian identity. Key features:

- **Vernacular language**: the Sants preached and sang in Hindi, Punjabi, and regional dialects —
  not Sanskrit.
- **Low-caste origins**: many Sants were drawn from artisan and untouchable communities:
  Ravidas (leatherworker/Chamar), Dadu (cotton carder), Sena (barber). But not all: Guru Nanak
  (Kshatriya) and Mirabai (Kshatriya princess).
- **Anti-institutional**: rejected temples, images, ritual purity, and caste hierarchy as obstacles
  to the direct experience of God.

**Kabir** (c. 1398–1448) is the paradigmatic Sant. Born into a Muslim weaver family with Hindu
ancestry, he refused both traditions — "One slaughters goats, one slaughters cows; they squander
their birth in isms" — while insisting that the nirguṇa Ram is complete in himself, a mantra. His
challenge to circumcision, sacred thread, untouchability, and sectarian ritual cuts from both
directions. Both Hindus and Muslims claimed him after death. See [[kabir|Kabir]].

**Sufism and bhakti**: The Sufi orders active in India from the 11th century — particularly the
Chishtis (Khwaja Muin-ud-din Chishti, settled at Ajmer in the late 12th century) — proclaimed
that Muslims, Christians, Hindus, and Zoroastrians all strove toward the same goal; that outer
observances were false; and that the abstract love of God transcended all religions. This was
absorbed into the Sant movement. "For many Hindus (though not for the Sufis themselves), Sufism
was Islam lite, or a walking incarnation of interreligious dialogue" (Doniger). Sufi narrative
poetry — erotic romance as vehicle for divine love — fused with the Sanskrit theory of aesthetic
emotion (*rasa*).

**Sikhism** emerged from the Sant tradition: Guru Nanak (1469–1539) founded it in the Punjab,
drawing on both bhakti and Sufi *prapatti* (surrender).

## Bengal Vaishnavism: Chaitanya and the Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa Tradition (c. 1486–1700 CE)

**[[chaitanya|Chaitanya]]** (1486–1533) founded Gaudiya Vaishnavism by declaring himself an
incarnation of both Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā in one body — resolving their eternal *viraha* (separation)
by embodying both poles simultaneously. This gave Bengal Vaishnavism its characteristic emotional
structure: the devotee does not merely worship Kṛṣṇa but identifies with Rādhā's experience of
longing.

**The svakiyā/parakīyā debate**: Is Rādhā Kṛṣṇa's wife ("his own") or his mistress ("someone
else's")? In 1717, Bengal Vaishnavas officially decided that **adulterous love (*parakīyā*) is
orthodox** — transgression of social norms intensifies the love that models the soul's relationship
to God. The Sahajiyas ("Naturalists") went further, making this a literal practice: to *become*
Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā in one's own body through quasi-Tantric techniques.

**Radhavallabhas**: A further branch held that Kṛṣṇa was not the supreme deity but **the servant
of Rādhā**. "He may do the coolie-work of building the world, but Rādhā sits as Queen."

**Tukārām** (1608–1649), a Shudra Maharashtrian poet, worshipped Viṭṭhal (Viṭhobā) at Pandharī
with extreme emotional intensity — his "God's Dog" poem ("I've come to your door like a dog looking
for a home / My Master's trained me hard / I am allowed to eat / Only out of his own Hand") extends
the bhakti dog-metaphor (earlier seen in the Kṛṣṇa *bhakta* who saved the thief) to the devotee's
self-understanding.

## Kshetrayya and the Courtesan Devotion Tradition (17th c. CE)

**Kshetrayya** (c. mid-17th c., Andhra) composed Telugu poems — sung by male Brahmin dancers
playing female roles — in which a **courtesan addresses her customer, who is simultaneously her
lover, her god, and her king**. The poems operate on three levels simultaneously:
- Sex as metaphor for religion and politics
- Religion as metaphor for sex and politics  
- The devotee as customer of god, as god is the customer of the courtesan

At the time Kshetrayya wrote, "the king had become a god, and the god in his temple had become a
king" — the distinction had dissolved. His most startling poem, "A Married Woman to Her Lover,"
depicts a woman seeking an abortion after being raped by her divine customer, then discovering her
husband has returned and she will bear the god's son — "so, in your image, I'll bear you a son."
The poem collapses rape, divine paternity (the myths of heroes sired by gods), religion, and the
most intimate human vulnerability into a single lyric.

## Accidental Grace: Bhakti's Theological Extreme (c. 800–1500 CE)

Later Puranic bhakti pushed the accessibility of grace to a theological extreme — the doctrine of
**accidental grace** (*ahetuka kripa*): even an unintentional or unwitting contact with the divine
can save even an unreformed sinner.

The logic develops in stages:
1. The good bhakta (a righteous man devoted to Shiva) is saved — uncontroversial.
2. The evil bhakta (an evildoer who nevertheless worships Shiva) is saved — initially shocking.
3. The accidental worshiper who neither reforms nor intends devotion is saved:
   - **The thief who rang the bell**: a Brahmin-killer, adulterer, drunkard, and gambler climbed
     on a Shiva-linga at night to steal its bell — inadvertently ringing it. Shiva sent his
     servants to bring the thief to Kailasa.
   - **The adulterer who lit a lamp**: went to a temple to rob it; made a new wick to see what
     was worth stealing; was granted eternal life in heaven because he had lit a lamp for the god.
   - **The dog's paw**: a dog's nails accidentally scratched the triple lines of Shiva's trident
     on the forehead of a dead thief; the trident mark saved the thief.
4. **By the grace of dog**: dogs intercede for sinners — the dog that bathed Vena in the river,
   making him barely eligible for divine purification; Shiva himself takes dog form.

Doniger reads these stories as a "Tantric argument that low people have to have low (or at least
very simple and easy) sources of grace" — a theology for sinners in the Kali Age. They imply that
certain ritual acts are efficacious *regardless of intention* — ritualism over bhakti.

The cosmic framing: the earlier "zero-sum universe" (heaven fills up; if someone is saved, someone
must be damned; Brahmin purity requires Pariah impurity) gives way, in the later Puranas, to an
**infinitely expansible cosmos** — Shiva's heaven can accommodate everyone, even all the world's
sinners, without losing its good karma. This is what Mircea Eliade called "breaking open the roof"
(*briser le toit*): the egg of the closed universe cracked open.

## Shri Vaishnava Cats and Monkeys (c. 14th c. CE)

The **Shri Vaishnava** sect (South India, 900–1300 CE) developed a split in the fourteenth century
over the question of how grace operates in relation to the devotee's effort:

**Monkey school (*Vadakalai*, North)**: The devotee *actively clings* to God, who saves him — as
a baby monkey clings to its mother moving through the trees. Devotion is necessary; grace cooperates
with the devotee's effort.

**Cat school (*Tenkalai*, South)**: The devotee is *entirely passive*, saved through grace alone —
as kittens allow a mother cat to carry them by the scruff without any effort on their part. Any
effort by the devotee would get in the way. Radical receptivity.

Both schools value *prapatti* (surrender) over active bhakti. The Cat theology is the logical
extreme of accidental grace theology: not just that sinners can be saved by accident, but that the
ideal devotee *should not try*.

The Monkey school sometimes said the Cat school was designed for low castes excluded from temples
who could only be liberated through surrender — precisely the "training wheels" logic of Tantric
accommodation.

## Bhakti and the Problem of Caste

Bhakti has a structural democratic potential — love of god does not require caste standing — and the
bhakti movements often *use* this potential to challenge Brahmin authority. At the same time, the
mainstream Brahmin imaginary absorbed bhakti and theologized it in ways that reinstated hierarchies
(the Vedānta schools' commentaries on the Gita all subordinate bhakti to their own preferred path).
The tension between bhakti's egalitarian promise and its hierarchical containment is a central theme
of medieval Hindu history.
