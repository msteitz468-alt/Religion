The *Maaseh Merkabah* (מַעֲשֵׂה מֶרְכָּבָה, "Account of the Chariot") is the rabbinic designation for the esoteric content of Ezekiel 1 and 10 — the prophet's visionary description of the divine throne-chariot. Within the philosophical tradition represented by [[maimonides|Maimonides]], it is interpreted as a veiled account of metaphysics (the nature of being, intellect, and the divine), taught only orally, in hints, to qualified pupils one at a time.

---

## Definition and Scope

*Maaseh Merkabah* stands alongside *Maaseh Bereshit* (Account of Creation) as one of the two supreme secrets of the Jewish law. In the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the Perplexed*]] (Part III, Introduction), Maimonides states that these subjects "belong to the mysteries of the Law" and that the Sages "blame those who reveal these mysteries, and praise the merits of those who keep them secret, although they are perfectly clear to the philosopher."

The rabbinic law governing transmission is strict: "a person favoured by Providence with reason to understand these mysteries is forbidden by the Law to teach them except viva voce, and on condition that the pupil possess certain qualifications, and even then only the heads of the sections may be communicated" (Part III, Introduction). As a consequence, "the knowledge of this mystery has entirely disappeared from our nation, and nothing has remained of it."

---

## Maimonides' Hermeneutical Method — The Esoteric Register

Maimonides faces a dilemma squarely in the Introduction: writing about the Chariot violates the oral-only rule, yet silence risks the knowledge dying with him. His solution is deliberately double-layered:

> "The method, viz., to explain the words of the prophet Ezekiel in such a manner that those who will read my interpretation will believe that I have not added anything to the contents of the text, but only, as it were, translated from one language into another, or given a short exposition of plain things. Those, however, for whom this treatise has been composed, will, on reflecting on it and thoroughly examining each chapter, obtain a perfect and clear insight into all that has been clear and intelligible to me." (Part III, Introduction)

This is the defining instance of Maimonides' esoteric hermeneutic: a text that reads as plain commentary to the uninitiated while conveying fully philosophical content to the trained reader. He explicitly denies relying on divine inspiration, attributing his reading to Scripture, the rabbinic utterances, and "the philosophical principles which I have adopted" — with the caveat that he may be wrong.

After completing his seven chapters on the Chariot, Maimonides closes the subject entirely: "Do not expect or hope to hear from me after this chapter a word on this subject, either explicitly or implicitly, for all that could be said on it has been said, though with great difficulty and struggle." (Ch. VII)

## The Three Visions and Their Hierarchy

The *Maaseh Merkabah* proper encompasses three distinct prophetic visions in Ezekiel 1 and 10, each introduced by *va-ereh* ("and I beheld"):

1. **The Hayyot** (living creatures) — the first and highest in rank among what is perceived. They have four faces, four wings, and human hands; their motion is entirely directed by the Divine Will: "Whithersoever the spirit was to go they went" (Ezek. i. 20). Each moves in the direction of its own face; the four are joined yet distinct.

2. **The Ofannim** (wheels) — a second body beneath the Hayyot, spherical (Ch. III, confirmed by Jonathan ben Uzziel's translation *gilgal* = "sphere"), covered with eyes. The Ofannim have no self-originating motion whatsoever: "The four Ofannim do not move of their own accord, as the Hayyot, and have no motion whatever of their own; they are set in motion by other beings" (Ch. II). "The relation between the Ofan and the Hayyah may be compared to the relation between a lifeless body tied to the hand or the leg of a living animal" (Ch. II). Their cause of motion: "The spirit of the Hayyah was in the Ofannim" (Ezek. i. 21).

3. **The hashmal and the likeness of man above the throne** — the highest and most secret vision. The Mishnah restricted even hints of this level. The "divided likeness of man" over the throne "does not represent God, who is above the whole chariot, but represents a part of the creation" (Ch. VII). It is *the glory of the Lord*, not the Lord Himself: "the glory of the Lord is different from the Lord Himself" (Ch. VII).

Maimonides cites a rabbinic dispute about where *Maaseh Merkabah* ends: Rabbi Jehudah held all three visions are the Chariot, permitting only heads-of-sections for all; Rabbi Yitzhak held the first two may be taught more fully, while only heads-of-sections apply from the *hashmal* onward (Ch. V, citing *Hagigah* 13b).

## Philosophical Reading of the Chariot Figures

Maimonides reads the Chariot imagery as philosophical allegory pointing to the hierarchy of being and intellect. Key interpretive moves:

- The **Hayyot** are identified with the cherubim of Ezekiel 10 (Ch. III): "This is the Hayyah that I saw under the God of Israel by the river of Chebar: and I knew that they were cherubims" (Ezek. x. 20). The four faces — lion, ox, eagle, man — are all variants of the human face (Ch. I).
- The **likeness** (*demut*) language is applied to the Hayyot and to the throne and the figure above, but *not* to the Ofannim, who are described in "positive" terms as beings in actual existence (Ch. VII) — a distinction with philosophical significance regarding the ontological status of each element.
- The **Isaiah parallel**: "Isaiah saw all that has been seen by Ezekiel: Isaiah is like a townsman that sees the king, Ezekiel like a countryman that sees the king" (*Hagigah* 13b, cited Ch. VI). Isaiah needed no detailed description; Ezekiel's audience in Babylonian exile did.
- Jonathan ben Uzziel's targum of the Ofannim as *gilgelaya* ("spheres") = the heavenly spheres. Maimonides discusses this view at length (Ch. IV) without fully endorsing it, while noting that *gilgal* means "anything rolling" rather than specifically "heaven."

## Relation to Negative Theology and the Separate Intellects

The vision explicitly culminates in what cannot be said. The figure above the throne is *the likeness of the appearance of man* — not God. "God cannot be compared to anything" (Ch. VII). The rainbow comparison in Ezek. i. 28 ("as the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud… this was the appearance of the likeness of the glory") is described as "very extraordinary, and is undoubtedly part of the prophecy" — pointing toward [[negative-theology]] by way of meteorological simile.

The *hashmal* — compounded of *bash* (swiftness) and *mal* (pause), or alternatively of *hehesheti* (I have been silent) and speech — "includes two notions, and indicates 'speech without sound'" (Ch. VII, citing a rabbinic saying "at times they are silent, at times they speak"). This is said to refer to "a created object" — not God.

The motion structure (Hayyot moved by Divine Will → Ofannim moved by Hayyot) maps onto the [[separate-intellects]] hierarchy elaborated elsewhere in the *Guide*: each sphere moves in accordance with a specific intellect; the Ofannim as perhaps the spheres themselves have only derived, secondary motion.
