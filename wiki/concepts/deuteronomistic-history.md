# Deuteronomistic History

The **Deuteronomistic History** (DtrH) is the scholarly hypothesis — formulated by Martin
Noth (*Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*, 1943) — that the biblical books from
**Deuteronomy through 2 Kings** (Deuteronomy, [[book-of-joshua|Joshua]], Judges,
[[1-samuel|1]]–[[2-samuel|2 Samuel]], [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]]) form a single, continuous
historical work, composed or edited by a "Deuteronomist" who interpreted Israel's history
from the conquest to the Babylonian exile through the theological lens of the book of
[[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]].

## The Deuteronomistic Outlook

The editor's hand is identified by a distinctive theology and a recognizable rhetorical
style:

- **Covenant retribution.** National fortune is tied directly to covenant fidelity:
  obedience brings blessing, apostasy (especially idolatry) brings catastrophe. The exile
  is thereby explained as deserved judgment, not divine failure.
- **Cult centralization.** Insistence on a single legitimate sanctuary (Jerusalem) and
  condemnation of the "high places."
- **The prophetic word as engine of history.** Prophecy is spoken and then fulfilled,
  structuring the narrative.
- **Formulaic phraseology.** Heavy strings of near-synonyms — "to walk in His ways," "keep
  His statutes, His commandments... and His dictates," "with their whole heart and with
  their whole being," the "Teaching (*torah*) of Moses" — recur as editorial markers.

Most scholars posit at least two editorial layers: a pre-exilic edition associated with
**Josiah's reform** (c. 622 BCE) and an exilic revision (6th century BCE) accounting for
the fall of Jerusalem.

## Disputed Reach Within Samuel

How heavily the Deuteronomist shaped the [[1-samuel|Books of Samuel]] is contested:

- **Robert Polzin** (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*; *David and the Deuteronomist*) finds
  the Deuteronomist's perspective manifested "in all the minute details of the story."
- **[[robert-alter|Robert Alter]]** ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) strenuously
  disagrees: the bulk of Samuel shows "no traces" of Deuteronomistic pietism, and the
  editors "did no more... than to provide some minimal editorial framing... and to
  interpolate a few brief passages." His showcase is the seam in
  [[solomon|Solomon's]] succession (1 Kings 2), where unmistakable Deuteronomistic
  phraseology is bolted onto an otherwise cold political testament — "nobody in the David
  story talks like this."

## Relation to Other Concepts

The DtrH is one of the foundational hypotheses of the **historical-critical** approach,
alongside the [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]] (which concerns the
Pentateuch). It stands in methodological tension with the
[[literary-reading-of-the-bible|literary reading]] of these same books. See also
[[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]] and [[deuteronomy-authorship|Deuteronomy: Authorship and Date]].
