# Lying (Mendacium) in Aquinas

In the treatise on **truthfulness** (a virtue annexed to justice), [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats
**lying** as the chief vice opposed to truth ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] II-II Q110), and defends the
strict Augustinian position that **no lie is ever lawful**.

## What a lie is

A lie is essentially **speech contrary to one's mind** — the formal element is the **will to speak
falsely** (the *intention to deceive* is a further, consequent element). Hence a statement that
happens to be false but is sincerely believed is not a lie, while a true statement uttered with
intent to deceive (by the speaker's lights) has the character of a lie (Q110, A1).

## The threefold division (Q110, A2)

Aquinas distinguishes lies by their end:

1. The **officious** (helpful) lie — told to help someone or avert harm;
2. The **jocose** lie — told in jest;
3. The **mischievous** (malicious) lie — told to harm someone.

Only the mischievous lie is **mortal** in its genus; the officious and jocose lies are **venial**
(Q110, A4). This gradation matters, but it does not make any lie *good*.

## No lie is ever lawful (Q110, A3)

Because a lie is "intrinsically evil" — disordered of its very nature, since words are naturally
signs of thought — **it can never be justified by a good end**, "not even to save a man from any
danger whatever." This is the position Aquinas inherits from **Augustine's** *De Mendacio* against
the view that an officious lie to save a life is permissible.

He reconciles the scriptural hard cases (the Hebrew midwives, Abraham, Jacob, Judith) not by
allowing the lie but by **reinterpreting** them — as rewarded for their good will rather than the
falsehood, or as figurative/mystical rather than mendacious. The licit alternative to lying is
**prudent concealment**: one may **withhold** the truth or speak so as to leave the hearer to deceive
himself (the seed of later doctrines of **mental reservation**), but one may not assert what is
false.

## Significance and tension

Aquinas's absolute prohibition is among the most discussed and contested of his ethical positions —
set against consequentialist intuitions (the "murderer at the door" case later pressed against
**Kant**, who reached a similar absolutism on independent grounds) and against the more permissive
casuistry of later mental-reservation debates. It is a clear instance of his doctrine of
**intrinsically evil acts**, which no intention or circumstance can rectify.
