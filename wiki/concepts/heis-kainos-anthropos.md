# The One New Human Being (*Heis Kainos Anthrōpos*)

"That he might create in himself one new human being (*hena kainon anthrōpon*) in place of the two, so making peace." (Eph 2:15b)

One of Ephesians' most distinctive theological images. By demolishing the "middle wall" (the Mosaic law as social barrier; see [[ephesians-middle-wall|The Middle Wall in Eph 2:14]]) through his death, Christ did not simply bring two previously divided peoples (Jewish humanity and Gentile humanity) into cooperation. He created a *third entity* — a new corporate human being that has never before existed. This is not amalgamation but creation: a *kainos* (new in kind, not merely in sequence) humanity.

## The Text (Eph 2:14–16)

> "For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the middle wall of partition, having abolished the law of commandments expressed in ordinances, that he might create in himself *one new human being* in place of the two, so making peace, and might reconcile us both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the enmity."

The *heis kainos anthrōpos* is:
- **Singular** (*heis* = one): a corporate individual, not a collective of individuals
- **New in kind** (*kainos*): qualitatively new, not a renovation of either predecessor
- **Created** (*ktizō*): the creation vocabulary signals this is a new-creation act, not a social reform
- **In himself** (*en autō*): Christ is both the site and the means of this creation — it exists only "in Christ"
- **In place of the two** (*anti tōn dyo*): the new humanity does not subsume the old two; it supersedes them

## The Corporate New Adam

The phrase draws on the "new Adam" typology implicit in Paul's anthropology (cf. 1 Cor 15:45–49; Rom 5:12–21): if the first Adam was the corporate head through whom sin and death came to all humanity, the last Adam (Christ) is the corporate head through whom the new creation comes. The *heis kainos anthrōpos* is the social-ecclesial form of this typology: not just a new individual but a new corporate humanity constituted in Christ.

The singularity is theologically load-bearing: the new humanity is *one*, not two reconciled communities. This oneness is not uniformity — Jewish and Gentile identities do not dissolve — but it is a real corporate unity that precedes and grounds any secondary ethnic or cultural distinctions within the community.

## Relationship to New Creation

The *kainos anthrōpos* language connects directly to Paul's new-creation theology:
- **2:10**: Believers are God's "workmanship, created (*ktisthentes*) in Christ Jesus for good works"
- **4:24**: Believers are to "put on the new self (*kainon anthrōpon*), created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness"
- **Gal 6:15**: "Neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation"
- **2 Cor 5:17**: "If anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation"

The 2:15 usage is the corporate, ecclesiological form of what 2 Cor 5:17 states individually. The "new creation" in Christ is not merely personal renewal but the constitution of a new humanity. See [[new-creation|New Creation]].

The Colossians parallel (Col 3:10–11) is close: "the new self which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator. Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all." Colossians uses similar vocabulary (*neon anthrōpon*, 3:10) but without the explicit "one" and "in place of the two" that Ephesians uses.

## The *Imago Dei* Connection

The new human being of 2:15 and 4:24 is explicitly linked to the image of God (*kata theon*, 4:24; cf. Gen 1:26–27). The creation of the *heis kainos anthrōpos* is a re-creation of humanity in the *imago Dei* distorted through the fall. Where the first Adam's failure extended to all humanity (the old self), the last Adam's obedience constitutes a new humanity in God's image. See [[imago-dei|Imago Dei]].

## The New Humanity's Content

Who is the *heis kainos anthrōpos*? Contextually, the immediately following verses define it:

- **2:19–22**: The new humanity is the household (*oikos*) and temple (*naos*) of God — built on the apostles' and prophets' foundation, with Christ as cornerstone, growing into a holy temple indwelt by the Spirit
- **4:17–24**: The new self is contrasted with the "old self" (*palaion anthrōpon*, 4:22) — the pre-conversion humanity characterized by futility, darkened understanding, hardened heart, and sensuality. The new self is "created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (4:24)
- **4:25–5:20**: The ethical imperatives that follow are not arbitrary rules but the behavioral expression of what the new human being is: truthful (4:25), anger-controlled (4:26), generous (4:28), gracious in speech (4:29), Spirit-filled (5:18)

The *heis kainos anthrōpos* is simultaneously a *declared* reality (it was created in Christ's death, 2:15) and an *unfolding* reality (it is being renewed, 4:23–24; Col 3:10). The already/not-yet structure of eschatology applies to the new humanity.

[[frank-thielman|Thielman]] reads the "put off … put on" clothing metaphor of 4:22–24 as ethical formation, not liturgy: he **rejects** the proposal (Meeks, Dahl, Kim) that the language reflects an early-Christian baptismal ritual of disrobing before and re-dressing after baptism — Paul does not mention baptism here, and no clear evidence for such a rite exists before the third century (and even then it involves the *same* garments, not old-for-new). The new *anthrōpos* of 4:24, *ton kata theon ktisthenta* ("created after the pattern of God," echoing Gen 1:26 LXX and Col 3:10), is the individual outworking of the corporate new creation already announced at 2:10, 15.

## Significance

This concept is central to Ephesians' distinctive ecclesiological contribution. In Galatians, Paul declares "there is neither Jew nor Greek" (3:28) but does not elaborate what that new entity *is*. In Romans, Jew-Gentile unity is extensive but still largely framed as Gentiles being grafted into Israel (11:17–24). Ephesians is distinctive: the church is not a reformed or expanded Israel but a qualitatively new corporate humanity, created at the cross, that transcends the old categories without destroying the particulars (Paul remains a Jew; Gentiles remain Gentiles) while genuinely superseding the Jew/Gentile binary as a fundamental division. This is why [[frank-thielman|Thielman]] insists that the church's unity is a *cosmic proclamation* to the powers (3:10): it demonstrates the defeat of the division that the powers sustain.
