# The Angel of the Lord

The **Angel of the Lord** (*mal'akh YHWH*) is a figure in the Hebrew Bible who is at once
identified *as* God and yet appears *as a human* — "regarded as the 'chief' angel," in
[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]'s words, who "in some passages … is identified as God himself. And
yet sometimes he appears as a human." This ambiguity is a load-bearing piece of the
[[two-powers-in-heaven|gradation of divinity within Judaism]] and a major resource for
later Christology.

## Biblical Appearances

- **Genesis 16 (Hagar)** — the Angel of the Lord speaks, but the text says it was "the
  LORD"; Hagar realizes she has "seen God and remained alive."
- **Genesis 18–19 (Abraham)** — "the LORD appeared," but as "three men," one explicitly
  "the LORD," the other two "angels," all eating the food Abraham prepares.
- **Exodus 3 (burning bush)** — "the angel of the LORD appeared … in a flame of fire,"
  yet it is "the Lord"/"God" who calls and says "I am the God of your father." The
  HarperCollins Study Bible (cited by Ehrman): "there is no substantive difference between
  the deity and his agents."

Other Jewish texts (~1st c. CE) extend the pattern: the *Prayer of Joseph* (Jacob speaks
as "an angel of God … the archangel of the power of the Lord" who "descended to earth and
tabernacled among people") and the *Apocalypse of Abraham* (the angel **Jaoel** appears
"in the likeness of a man" and "temporarily becomes incarnate").

## Christological Reception

[[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] identified the Angel of the Lord with the **[[logos-christology|Logos]]**:
"When God was manifest to humans, it was his Logos that made the appearance." Early
Christians took the further step of identifying the pre-incarnate Christ as this figure:
[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] argues Paul calls Christ an **angel** (Galatians 4:14, equating
rather than contrasting) — "the chief angel of God, known in the Bible as the Angel of the
Lord … a preexistent being who is divine; he can be called God." [[justin-martyr|Justin
Martyr]] likewise read the Old Testament theophanies (Mamre, the burning bush) as
appearances of the pre-existent Christ.
