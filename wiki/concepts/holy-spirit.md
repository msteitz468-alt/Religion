# Holy Spirit (Pneumatology)

## Definition

The *Holy Spirit* (*pneuma hagion*, Greek; *Spiritus Sanctus*, Latin; *Ruach HaQodesh*, Hebrew) is the third Person (or, in Barth's terminology, the third mode of being) of the Christian Trinity. In the economy of salvation, the Holy Spirit is the *subjective side of revelation* — the divine act by which men come to receive and appropriate God's self-disclosure in Jesus Christ. In God's immanent being, the Holy Spirit is the eternal act of communion (*communio*) between Father and Son, the love that unites them as a distinct mode of divine being.

The study of the Holy Spirit within Christian theology is called **pneumatology** (from *pneuma*, spirit/wind/breath).

## Biblical Background

**Hebrew Bible**: *Ruach Elohim* ("Spirit of God") in Gen. 1:2 hovers over the waters of creation; the Spirit is the source of prophetic inspiration (Num. 11:25; Is. 61:1), of artisanal skill (Ex. 31:3), and of life itself (Ps. 104:29–30; Gen. 2:7). The eschatological promise of the Spirit's outpouring is prominent in Joel 2:28–29 (cited at Acts 2:17) and Ezek. 36:27.

**New Testament**: The Spirit is promised by Jesus as the Paraclete (*parakletos*, Jn. 14:16–17, 26; 16:7–15), who will teach, remind, and guide into all truth; the Spirit is received at Pentecost (Acts 2); Paul treats the Spirit as the source of Christian life, freedom, and divine sonship (Rom. 8; Gal. 4–5; 1 Cor. 12–14; 2 Cor. 3). The Spirit "intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Rom. 8:26) and enables the cry "Abba, Father!" (Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6).

## Historical Development of Pneumatology

Pneumatology developed more slowly than Christology. The Nicene Creed (325) merely states "and [we believe] in the Holy Spirit" without opposing Arianism at this point. The Macedonians (Pneumatomachians, "Spirit-fighters") denied the Spirit's full deity — accepting Nicene Christology while treating the Spirit as a creature. Athanasius's *Letters to Serapion* (c. 360) provided the decisive theological response. The Council of Constantinople (381) expanded the creed to affirm the Spirit as "the Lord and giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified, who spoke through the prophets." The *Quicunque* (Athanasian Creed) gave the Spirit's position precise formulation corresponding to the Son's.

The *Filioque* controversy (see [[filioque]]) determined the subsequent pneumatological divergence between Western and Eastern Christianity.

## Barth's Account (Church Dogmatics I.1, §12)

**§12.1 God as Redeemer: the Spirit as subjective side of revelation**

Barth's governing question: how do men come to say "Jesus is Lord"? This is the problem of *Revealedness* — and it is a divine problem, not a human one. The Son is the *objective* element of revelation (God's self-disclosure as Reconciler); the Spirit is the *subjective* element (the act by which this disclosure reaches and is received by its human hearers). "The revelation of God in its subjective reality, i.e., as a revelation that actually reaches man, is the work of the Holy Spirit" (*CD* I.1, lines 7475–7477).

Three functions of the Spirit in the New Testament (lines 7638–7799):
1. **Personal participation**: the Spirit "speaks" the "Yes" of God within the believer — *en pneumati* as the subjective counterpart to *en Christo*.
2. **Instruction and guidance**: the Johannine Paraclete, teacher, reminder, guide into all truth; direction in concrete situations.
3. **Authorization to speak**: the Spirit commissions and equips witness; Pentecost as paradigm — the outpouring consists in the disciples beginning to speak.

The Spirit's work is formally "freedom" and materially "divine sonship." The cry "Abba, Father!" (Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6; echoing Jesus's own prayer in Mk. 14:36) is the distilled content of life in the Spirit. Luther on Gal. 4:6: the Spirit's cry "pierces the clouds, fills heaven and earth" even though the believer perceives it as "only a trifling sigh."

**Limits**: The Spirit has no independent content beyond Christ: "He will not speak on his own authority ... He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you" (Jn. 16:13–14). 2 Cor. 3:17 ("the Lord is the Spirit") is not an identification of Christ and the Spirit but a statement about the Spirit's mode of lordship. The Spirit does not transform man into God; "even in receiving the Holy Ghost man remains man, the sinner sinner" (*CD* I.1, lines 8113–8115).

**Eschatological reserve**: All statements about the man who receives the Spirit are eschatological. "The Spirit remains the Holy Spirit, wholly and utterly the Spirit of promise" (*CD* I.1, lines 8251–8252). Reception of the Spirit is a "human sign" of the eternal gift; the Spirit as God exceeds every subjective experience of having. "To have the Holy Spirit is to let God rather than our having God be our confidence" (lines 8132–8134).

**§12.2 The Eternal Spirit**

Barth's principle: "What He is in revelation He is antecedently in Himself" (*CD* I.1, lines 8314–8318). The Spirit is God's third mode of being: the eternal act of communion between Father and Son.

The Spirit as "common factor" (Augustine): "*Spiritus sanctus commune aliquid est Patris et Filii*" — the Spirit is the mutual love of Father and Son, "the 'result' of their common 'breathing,' *spiratio*" (*CD* I.1, line 8489). Barth: "This togetherness or communion of the Father and the Son is the Holy Spirit. The specific element in the divine mode of being of the Holy Spirit thus consists, paradoxically enough, in the fact that He is the common factor in the mode of being of God the Father and that of God the Son" (*CD* I.1, lines 8450–8453).

The Spirit is also *Creator Spirit* (*Spiritus vivificans*): "giver of life" in the Creed reflects the Spirit's cosmological role as principle of life in all creation (Gen. 1:2; Ps. 33:6; Ps. 104:29–30), alongside but distinct from the redemptive work.

For the *Filioque* (the Spirit's procession from Father and Son), see [[filioque]].

## Tradition-Specific Perspectives

- **Patristic East (Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus)**: The Spirit is "poured out" from the Father through the Son; pneumatology developed alongside anti-Macedonian polemic; the Spirit's full deity established by Constantinople (381).
- **Patristic West (Augustine)**: The Spirit is the mutual love (*amor*) and gift (*donum*) of Father and Son; proceeds from Father and Son in a single *spiratio*. This is the standard Augustinian framework Barth inherits.
- **Medieval West (Thomas Aquinas)**: The Spirit as *amor subsistens* — subsistent love; proceeds "by way of will" (*per modum voluntatis*), the Son "by way of intellect" (*per modum intellectus*).
- **Reformed tradition**: Emphasis on the Spirit's work in inspiration, illumination, and regeneration. Calvin: the Spirit is the "bond by which Christ effectually unites us to himself." The Spirit's *internal testimony* (*testimonium internum Spiritus Sancti*) authenticates Scripture.
- **Pentecostal/Charismatic**: Emphasis on the Spirit's gifts (*charismata*): tongues, prophecy, healing. "Baptism in the Spirit" as a distinct experience subsequent to conversion. Not covered in the current source.

## Pro-Nicene Pneumatology (Ayres, Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) judges the chief
[[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene]] contribution to pneumatology to be "the insistence that the
work of the Spirit is inseparable from Father and Son" (see [[opera-trinitatis]]) — establishing
"that the work of sanctification is the unmediated work of God." The standard tactic was to
"apply to the Spirit arguments about the unity of activity and nature that had been developed in
polemic over the Son's status" ([[basil-of-caesarea|Basil's]] *On the Holy Spirit*;
[[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]]; [[athanasius|Athanasius's]] *Letters to Serapion*). Yet
Ayres notes a limit: "on the subject of the Spirit's place in the Godhead as such little
progress is made" — Basil famously withholds calling the Spirit God in the same terms as Father
and Son — and "the later question of the [[filioque|filioque]] is not an issue" in the
fourth-century debate.

## The Spirit in Mark's Prologue (France, NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the fidelity reingest of [[france-mark-nigtc|France's NIGTC Mark]]. All
interpretive claims are [[rt-france|R. T. France]]'s.*

France notes that πνεῦμα is a prologue-concentrated term in [[mark|Mark]]: three mentions in
1:2-13 (1:8, 10, 12) where the Spirit "appears as a central figure in the launching of Jesus'
ministry," but only three thereafter, just one relating to Jesus' ministry — a distribution he
compares to Λόγος in John's prologue. His claims:

- **Jesus as both bearer and dispenser of the Spirit.** The prologue's Spirit-emphasis marks
  Jesus as empowered by the Spirit (Isa 11:2; 42:1; 61:1) and, "remarkably," as the one who
  "'dispenses' the Spirit, a role which in the OT passages ... was exclusively that of Yahweh
  himself." Read with the ἰσχυρότερος prediction (1:7-8), Mark's application to the human Jesus
  of words that in themselves point to the coming of God shows that for Mark "the coming of
  Jesus is the eschatological coming of God."
- **Against a two-stage "Baptism in the Spirit."** France argues Mk 1:8 is not a suitable NT
  grounding for the Pentecostal doctrine of Spirit-baptism as a second-stage experience: all NT
  uses of the phrase contrast Spirit baptism with John's water baptism, so "the immediate
  reference is ... not to a second stage of Christian experience, but to the authentic Christian
  experience as contrasted with the preliminary (pre-Christian) experience represented by John's
  water baptism"; the fulfilment of 1:8b is the whole experience of the early Christian
  movement, not Pentecost alone.
- **The Spirit as driver into testing.** Mark's ἐκβάλλει (1:12) — more vivid than Matthew/Luke's
  "leading," normally implying possible resistance — has the Spirit drive Jesus into the
  wilderness, where France reads πειραζόμενος positively as "testing" *initiated by the Spirit*
  on the pattern of Israel's wilderness πειρασμός (Deut 8). In the tableau of 1:12-13 the Spirit
  and the angels stand on Jesus' side against [[satan|Satan]] and the wild animals.
- **Blasphemy against the Spirit (3:28-30).** In Mark's setting the unforgivable sin is the
  scribes' allegation that Jesus' Spirit-empowered ministry is Beelzebul's work — "the
  unforgivable sin of calling good evil and evil good"; Mark (unlike Matthew/Luke) makes the
  charge of possession itself the unforgivable blasphemy. France adds the pastoral caveat that
  the saying "is a warning to those who adopt a position of deliberate rejection and antagonism,
  not an attempt to frighten those of tender conscience."
- **No pneumatology at the cross.** France holds Mark's ἐκπνέω (15:37) is a simple solemn
  euphemism for death, offering "even less scope than the πνεῦμα phrases of Matthew and John for
  reading into the scene any reference to the Holy Spirit"; he rejects the suggestion of a
  Spirit-blast tearing the temple curtain.
