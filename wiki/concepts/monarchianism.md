# Monarchianism

"Monarchianism" is the modern umbrella term for two distinct late-second/third-century movements that, from anxiety to preserve the *monarchia* — "the axiom that there was one divine source and principle of all things" — collapsed the distinction of the divine Persons. Both reacted against the [[logos-christology|Logos theology]] of the Apologists, which they feared compromised monotheism by setting up a "second God." Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], chs. IV–V. (In contemporary usage only *modalism* was actually called "monarchianism"; the label for the other branch is a later convenience.)

## The Two Branches

### Dynamic Monarchianism ([[adoptionism|Adoptionism]])
The theory that Christ was a "mere man" (*psilos anthropos*; hence "psilanthropism") on whom God's power or Spirit descended — at the baptism — without his becoming divine. Kelly judges its earliest exponents were not pious monotheists but "intellectuals inspired by current philosophical rationalism." Representatives: **Theodotus the leather-merchant** (Byzantine, came to Rome c. 190, excommunicated by Pope Victor; his circle prized Aristotle, Euclid, and Galen), Theodotus the banker, Artemas/Artemon, and — in its starkest form — **[[paul-of-samosata|Paul of Samosata]]** (condemned at Antioch, 268). See [[adoptionism]].

### Modalistic Monarchianism ([[modalism|Modalism]])
"Monarchianism proper" — the view that Father, Son, and Spirit are merely successive modes or aspects of one identical divine Person, with [[patripassianism|the Father himself]] suffering as the Son. Representatives: **Noetus of Smyrna**, **Praxeas** (refuted by Tertullian's *Adversus Praxeam*), and **[[sabellius|Sabellius]]** (who called the Godhead a *monad*, "*huiopatōr*", expressed in three operations). Popes **Zephyrinus** and **Callistus** were drawn toward a guarded version. See [[modalism]].

## The Underlying Problem

Both movements were generated, Kelly stresses, by "the twofold conviction… of the oneness of God and the full deity of Christ." The orthodox answer would require the vocabulary of *[[oikonomia]]* (economy) developed by [[hippolytus|Hippolytus]] and [[tertullian|Tertullian]], and ultimately the Nicene distinction of one *ousia* in three *hypostases*.
