# Apophatic Theology

**Apophatic theology** (Greek *apophasis*, "denial"; also called the *via negativa* or
**negative theology**) holds that the divine reality exceeds all human concepts, so that
it can be spoken of only by **denying** that our predicates apply to it: God is not good
(as we know goodness), not a being among beings, not existent in the way creatures exist.
Its complement is **cataphatic** (positive) theology, which affirms predicates of God ("God
is good, wise, living"). Most traditions hold the two in tension; the mystical strands
press the apophatic side hardest.

[[karen-armstrong|Karen Armstrong]] makes the apophatic God the hero of *A History of
God*, contrasting it with the **personal God** of popular monotheism.

## The Two Gods of Armstrong's History

Armstrong's narrative is organized around a recurring contrast:

- **The personal God** — anthropomorphic, "a reality 'out there,'" a quasi-person with a
  will, emotions, and a chosen people. Accessible and consoling, but perpetually liable to
  become an **idol**: a projection of the worshipper's own ego, fears, and nationalism, a
  God conscripted to bless cruelty and tribalism. When taken literally and defended as fact,
  the personal God is also the most vulnerable to the scientific critique that "disposed of
  the Creator God."
- **The God of the mystics** — apophatic, ineffable, "beyond" personality and even beyond
  "existence." One medieval mystic, Armstrong notes, "went so far as to say that this
  ultimate Reality — mistakenly called 'God' — was not even mentioned in the Bible." This
  God is not encountered as an objective fact but **deliberately cultivated within** the
  self through long contemplative discipline, "with the same degree of care and attention
  that others devote to artistic creation."

Her thesis is that the apophatic God is both the older insight (Jews forbidden to pronounce
the Name; Muslims forbidden to depict the divine — disciplines that "remind … that the
reality we call 'God' exceeds all human expression") and the more viable future: "God does
not exist in any simplistic sense … the very word 'God' is only a symbol of a reality that
ineffably transcends it."

## The Apophatic Strand Across the Traditions

The wiki already tracks the family of apophatic and emanationist moves Armstrong surveys:

- **Greek Christian** — Gregory of Nyssa, Pseudo-Dionysius: God known only in *darkness*;
  the distinction between God's unknowable **essence** and his **energies/powers**
  (*dynameis*); theology must carry "an element of silence, paradox and restraint." See
  [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]].
- **Jewish** — the *[[ein-sof|Ein Sof]]* ("the Infinite") of [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]], the
  utterly hidden Godhead beyond the [[sefirot|sefirot]]; the unpronounceable
  [[divine-name|Name]].
- **Islamic** — the hidden divine essence (*al-Dhat*) behind the revealed Names; the
  *[[tafsir|batin]]* (inner) reading cultivated in [[sufism|Sufism]]; *fana* (annihilation
  of the self in God).
- **Hindu (cited for contrast)** — *nirguna* [[brahman|Brahman]], the Absolute "without
  qualities," beyond all the personal *saguna* descriptions — the classic Indian apophasis,
  invoked on [[the-real|the Real]] as a structural parallel.

## The "God of the Philosophers" — a Third, Contested Term

Armstrong distinguishes both of the above from the **God of the philosophers**: the
immutable, impassible (*apatheia*) First Cause of Greek metaphysics, taken into
Christianity, Jewish and Muslim *Falsafah*, and scholasticism (Aristotle's Unmoved Mover →
the God of [[maimonides|Maimonides]], Avicenna, and Aquinas). Though this God is *also*
spoken of apophatically, Armstrong treats it with suspicion as **arid and alien** — an
abstraction that the prophets and mystics would not recognize as the living reality they
experienced. The distinction matters: apophatic *mysticism* (reached by practice) is, for
her, a world apart from apophatic *metaphysics* (reached by logic).

## Connection to the Wiki's Wider Debates

- Apophatic theology is the theological face of the [[mythos-and-logos|mythos/logos]]
  distinction: if all God-talk is symbolic mythos, negation is the only honest grammar.
- It is the keystone of [[the-real|the Real]] and of [[john-hick|Hick]]'s pluralism — the
  ineffable Real *an sich* is an apophatic posit, and the "vacuous Real" objection is
  essentially the charge that *radical* apophasis empties theism of content (the same worry
  that pushes [[religious-realism|toward non-realism]]).
- It frames the recurring question this wiki meets: when literal claims fail, is the move to
  abandon reference, relocate it, or fall silent before it?

## Clement of Alexandria: An Early Greek Christian Apophasis

[[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]] gives one of the earliest Christian statements of the
*via negativa*, anticipating Gregory of Nyssa and Pseudo-Dionysius. In [[stromata|Stromata]] V, by
**abstraction** ("abstracting all that belongs to bodies and things called incorporeal") we "may reach
somehow to the conception of the Almighty, **knowing not what He is, but what He is not**" — a formula
later writers echo almost verbatim. The First Cause "is not then in space, but above both space, and
time, and name, and conception"; it is "neither genus, nor difference, nor species, nor individual, nor
number... without form and name." We use the divine names ("the One, or the Good, or Mind... or
Father") only "for want," as "points of support," since "each one by itself does not express God; but
all together are indicative of the power of the Omnipotent." God is "above all speech, all conception,
all thought," knowable "only by His own power," by grace and "the word alone that proceeds from Him."
Clement grounds this partly in Plato's *Timaeus* 28c (the Father "impossible to declare") and in
[[moses|Moses]] entering "the thick darkness where God's voice was" — the same Sinai type Dionysius
will make central. The apophatic moment coexists in Clement with a robust [[logos-christology|
cataphatic Logos-theology]]: the unknowable Father is revealed in the Son.

## Pseudo-Dionysius: The Locus Classicus

[[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]] provides the most systematic late-antique
Christian account of apophatic theology, across two works that function as a pair:

**[[on-divine-names|On the Divine Names]]** executes the *cataphatic* program: it demonstrates how
each scriptural name for God (Good, Light, Beautiful, Love, Being, Life, Wisdom, Power, Peace,
One…) describes not God's unknowable essence but his "beneficent progressions (*proodoi*) outward
to creation." God is both "Nameless" (the essence is beyond all names) and "Many-Named" (the
progressions are legitimately celebrated under all names). The Caput I rule: "not in the persuasive
words of man's wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit-moved power of the Theologians."

**[[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]]** executes the *apophatic* program: in five brief chapters
it strips away first all sensible predicates (Caput IV) and then all intelligible predicates including
the highest divine names affirmed in the *Divine Names* (Caput V). The final sentence: "the
all-perfect and uniform Cause of all is both above every definition and... is also above every
abstraction." The climax is not simply negative theology but the transcendence of the opposition
between affirmation and negation: God is "above the privations."

Three technical terms define the Dionysian contribution:

- ***Agnosia*** ("unknowing" or "supra-knowledge"): not mere ignorance but "the most Divine Knowledge
  of Almighty God, which is known, through not knowing (*ἀγνωσία*) during the union above mind; when
  the mind, having stood apart from all existing things, then having dismissed also itself, has been
  made one with the super-luminous rays" (*Divine Names* VII.III; Parker Preface).
- **"Divine Darkness" (*skotos*)**: the mode of encounter with God beyond both knowledge and
  ignorance; Moses entering the dark cloud on Sinai (Exod. 20:21) is the governing type (*Mystical
  Theology* II).
- **Negations above privations**: the negations of apophatic theology are not mere privations of
  divine attributes (God is not-finite, not-temporal) but signify God's elevation *above* the
  opposition between having and lacking each attribute (*Mystical Theology* I.II).

The Dionysian framework also introduces the principle that discourse *contracts* as it ascends:
the Symbolic Theology (sensible images) requires the most words, the *Divine Names* fewer, the
*Mystical Theology* fewest — enacting what it describes. At the apex: "a complete absence of speech,
and absence of conception" (*Mystical Theology* III.I).

## Perl: Symbolism as the Positive Face of Apophasis

In *Theophany* Chapter 7, Eric D. Perl argues that Dionysian symbolism is not an afterthought or a concession to human limitation but the *necessary positive counterpart* to the apophatic metaphysics. Because God transcends all conceptual capture, no name or concept captures him directly — cataphatic and apophatic theology both remain in the order of mediation. But precisely because every being is a divine self-manifestation (theophany), **material symbols are the most honest medium available**: they make no claim to conceptual adequacy and so do not pretend to comprehend what transcends comprehension.

Perl draws on Semmelroth's analysis: "the conceptual expression still always itself remains a kind of symbol" [*Theophany* Ch. 7 n. 4; range line 5699–5700]. That is, even the most refined negative predicate — "God is not being," "God is not one" — retains a symbolic character; it does not transcend the logic of sign and signified. The advantage of explicitly *sensible* symbols (fire, worm, stone, darkness) is that they are manifestly inadequate, and so carry the reader directly toward the apophatic excess rather than pretending to a transparency they lack. Semmelroth: "As a Neoplatonist the Pseudo-Areopagite certainly represents a strong realism of symbols … Between sign and signified in the symbol obtains not only a connection of knowledge, but also a connection of presence … the symbol is realistically bound with the symbolized" [lines 5701–5705].

The key verb in Dionysius is *proballein* ("to project" or "throw forward"), which O'Rourke finds used "in two closely related significations … namely both the veiling and the unveiling or unfolding of God through creation" [lines 5712–5714]. The symbol is both veil and disclosure — it simultaneously hides God (by interposing a finite form) and reveals him (by participating ontologically in his self-gift). The angel *exangelos* — the revealer — "reveals something which remains hidden, and indeed hides it by revealing it, in that narration takes the place of direct portrayal" [lines 5736–5739]. This is the apophatic moment within the symbolic: revelation is always simultaneously concealment at the symbolic level.

Perl's Chapter 7 is thus the completion of the apophatic movement traced through the whole book: apophasis strips away conceptual adequacy, theophany restores the world as the space of divine self-showing, and symbolism names the mode of that showing — a positive disclosure that never ceases to be, in its positivity, an indication of what transcends all positive form.

Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], Ch. 7 endnotes, range lines 5692–5768.

#### Chapter 7 Body Text: Why Material Symbols Are the Right Mode of Theological Expression

*Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], body text, lines 4193–4566.*

In the body of Chapter 7, Perl develops the philosophical argument that material symbols are not a condescension to human weakness but the *necessary* form of any genuine knowledge of God. The argument runs through three steps.

First, Perl dissolves the distinction between "intelligible names" and "sensible symbols" that might lead one to think intelligible predication is more adequate to God than sensible imagery. Dionysius himself collapses the distinction in DN I.6, where he runs without pause from Being, Life, and Good to "sun, star, fire, water, wind, dew, cloud, rock itself and stone" (596C [lines 4243–4245]). The Celestial Hierarchy explains why: even the ostensibly "similar" expressions — Word, Mind, Being — are infinitely inadequate, "deficient in relation to the truth of the thearchic likeness. For it is above all being and life, no light characterizing it, every word and intellect incomparably falling short of its likeness" (CH II.3, 140CD [lines 4513–4518]). Since God is not any being, it is "as false, and false in the same sense, to say that God is Word, Mind, or Being as to say that he is lion, stone, fire, or worm" [lines 4270–4272]. And since nothing is "altogether deprived of participation in the Beautiful" (CH II.3, 141C [lines 4284–4287]), every finite thing is a genuine manifestation of God. All expressions are therefore equally inadequate and equally appropriate as symbols.

Second, Perl explains why symbolism is *the* right mode precisely because God is transcendent. The symbol both reveals and conceals in a single act: "it conceals precisely in and as revealing, and reveals precisely in and as concealing" [lines 4315–4316]. A symbol that merely revealed would deliver a finite content — and that content, being finite, would not be God. A symbol that merely concealed would deliver nothing. Only the symbol that "qua symbol conceals what it reveals, can make God known without objectifying him as a being, enabling us to know God without violating his unknowability, and thus truly to know God" [lines 4361–4365]. The mechanism is the verb *proballein*: "it is not possible that the thearchic ray illumine us otherwise than as anagogically cloaked in the variety of the sacred veils" (CH I.2, 121B [lines 4596–4598]). Perl parses this: God cannot be revealed except by being simultaneously hidden, because if the veil were removed the revealed content would be a finite being, not God. The veiling *is* the revealing, because what is revealed is precisely the inexhaustible divine depth that no finite symbol exhausts.

Third, the body text makes explicit that this means the apophatic movement — the taking-away of all things — is not a departure from symbols but a penetration into them. "There is no question, then, of stripping away all symbols so as to arrive at a direct, non-symbolic knowledge of God unveiled. Whatever is unveiled is not God" [lines 4369–4371]. To remove all the veils is not to encounter God directly but to enter the "cessation of intellectual activities, the union above intellect, the darkness of unknowing" [lines 4383–4385]. Dionysius: "It is necessary, then, for us, contrary to the popular assumption about them, to cross over into (*eisō... diabainein*) the sacred symbols in a way befitting the sacred, and not despise them, because they are the offspring and impressions of the divine characters, and manifest images of the unspoken and supernatural visions" (Ep. IX.2, 1108C [lines 4512–4516]). The apophatic and the symbolic are not opposed; the mystical ascent through and beyond symbols is the form apophasis takes in a world where all being is theophany. Material symbols are therefore not the lowest, most dispensable rungs of a ladder to be kicked away; they are where the apophatic movement begins and, rightly understood, where it remains — because "all beings are nothing but symbols of God" [lines 4521–4522].

## Perl on Apophasis as Philosophical Necessity

*The following draws on the endnotes to Chapter 1 of Perl, *Theophany*, lines 4742–4931.*

Eric D. Perl's *Theophany* advances a distinctive and philosophically pointed reframing of apophatic theology. Where much of the tradition — and much of modern scholarship — treats apophasis as a rhetorical acknowledgment of human cognitive limitation before an infinite God, Perl argues it is a philosophical conclusion that follows necessarily from the identity of being and intelligibility.

The argument runs: to be is to be intelligible, and therefore to be is to be finite and determinate. This is not an accidental feature of how humans happen to know things but a structural fact about what it means to be a being: "existence or being necessarily implies both limitedness and complexity" [4824–4826]. Consequently, the infinite first principle — God, the One — cannot be a being, and all predicates (including negative predicates) that apply to beings cannot apply to God. Apophasis is not a move to say "God is greater than our concepts" (which would still imply God is something our concepts fall short of grasping); it is the conclusion that "God has no intelligible content" (A.H. Armstrong [4831–4836]). Perl corrects Rorem's formulation — that "Negation is a human concept, and thus cannot capture an infinite and transcendent God" [4870–4872] — precisely: "human concepts and minds are unable to capture God, not because they are human, but because they are concepts and minds" [4873–4875]. The limitation is not anthropological but ontological.

The same argument disposes of Derrida's reading that negative theology always aims at "a superior, inconceivable, ineffable mode of being" [4877–4879] — a hidden superessential being beyond the categories. Perl's response: "If to be is to be intelligible, then an 'inconceivable, ineffable mode of being' is a contradiction in terms" [4880–4882]. Negative theology in the Neoplatonic-Dionysian tradition does not land on a higher being; it departs from being altogether. This is why even the negations must be negated: Plotinus states that the One is "really outside all multiplicity and any simplicity whatsoever" (V.3.16.15–16) [4842–4844]. And this is why Lossky's attempt to distinguish Dionysius from Plotinus by noting that Dionysius negates the name "One" fails: "in negating the name 'One' Dionysius is simply following the precepts of Plotinus" [4851–4852].

The conclusion is not a higher mystical claim about what cannot be said but a logical silencing of the question: "Precisely because nothing can truly be described as ineffable, the only recourse is silence" [4923–4925]. Proclus's *Commentary on Plato's Parmenides* ends with this silence, and Perl defends it: it is not irrational silence but the rational outcome of the argument. On Perl's reading, apophatic theology is not the mystical wing of Christian prayer but the philosophical conclusion of the Neoplatonic argument about being, intelligibility, and the first principle — a conclusion Dionysius inherits from Plotinus and Proclus and transposes into his own theological idiom.

Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], Ch. 1 endnotes, range lines 4742–4931.


## Perl: Apophasis as Philosophical Necessity (*Theophany* Ch. 1)

Eric D. Perl's Chapter 1 of *Theophany* provides the most sustained philosophical argument for why apophatic theology is not a rhetorical device, a spiritual exercise, or an acknowledgment of human cognitive finitude, but rather the conclusion of a rigorous sequence of reasoning about the nature of being, intelligibility, and the first principle of reality.

The argument begins with a principle Perl treats as foundational to the entire Neoplatonic tradition: "The foundational principle of Neoplatonic thought is the doctrine that to be is to be intelligible" (line 265). Parmenides had made this explicit: "the same thing is for thinking and for being [*to gar auto noein estin te kai einai*]" (fragment, lines 281–283). Thought and being are coextensive — thought is always the apprehension of some being, and being cannot extend beyond what can be thought. Plato develops this by identifying being with form: the forms are "really real" precisely and only because they are "altogether intelligible" (lines 318–319). Plotinus carries it to its conclusion by identifying being not merely as the object but as the content of thought, making being and Intellect one: "All together are one, Intellect, intellection, the intelligible [*nous, noesis, to noeton*]" (V.3.5.43–44, line 448).

From this principle it follows that being is necessarily finite and determinate. A being is intelligible only by being distinct from other beings, marked off by the determination that makes it this being and not another: "A substance [*ousian*] must be some one particular thing [*tode ti*], something, that is, defined and limited" (Plotinus V.5.6.6–7, lines 460–461). The consequence for the first principle is inexorable: since being is necessarily finite, the infinite first principle cannot be a being. Plotinus states this with exceptional clarity: "Since the substance which is generated from the One is form… the One must be without form. But if it is without form it is not a substance… if all things are in that which is generated from the One, which of the things in it are you going to say that the One is? Since it is none of them, it can only be said to be beyond them. But these things are beings, and being: so it is 'beyond being'" (V.5.6.2–11, lines 533–542). This cannot be softened into "infinite being": "Not because of an accidental restriction on the usage of the term 'being,' but because of the philosophically grounded principle that to be is to be intelligible, being necessarily entails finitude, so that 'finite being' is a redundancy and 'infinite being' a contradiction in terms" (lines 550–553).

Dionysius inherits this argument directly and applies it to his doctrine of God. "For if all knowledges are of beings and have their limit in beings, that which is beyond all being also transcends all knowledge" (DN I.4, 593A, lines 606–608). God's transcendence is "not in a vague, unspecified sense, but in the very precise metaphysical sense that he is not at all included within the whole of reality, of things that are, as any member of it" (lines 609–611). Apophasis follows as the philosophically necessary consequence: all language and thought deal in beings, and God is not a being. Even negations must be transcended — saying "God is not such-and-such" still treats God as a conceptual object defined by the lack of some feature. Dionysius therefore insists we "not think that the negations are opposed to the affirmations, but much rather that that which is beyond the privations is beyond every affirmation and negation" (*MT* I.2, 1000B, lines 641–644). The conclusion is "the absolute silence of the mind" (lines 665–666), the "cessation of every intellectual activity [*pases noeras energeias apopasin*]" (DN I.5, 593C, line 671) — which Perl describes not as mystical hyperbole but as "the strictly philosophical consequence of the identification of being and intelligibility: as long as any speaking or thinking is taking place, we are necessarily in the realm of beings" (lines 683–685).

Perl's reading thus corrects two common misframings. Against the view that apophasis acknowledges a limitation of *human* cognition (so that some other, non-human or divine mode of thought might reach God): "human concepts and minds are unable to capture God, not because they are human, but because they are concepts and minds" (Perl's correction of Rorem, paraphrased from endnotes lines 4873–4875). Against the view that negative theology aims at "a superior, inconceivable, ineffable mode of being" beyond the categories (Derrida): "If to be is to be intelligible, then an 'inconceivable, ineffable mode of being' is a contradiction in terms" (endnotes line 4880–4882). The Neoplatonic-Dionysian apophasis does not land on a hidden superessential being; it departs from the domain of being altogether. This is why Proclus's *Commentary on Plato's Parmenides* ends in silence — "by means of a negation Parmenides has removed all negations. With silence he concludes the contemplation of the One" (endnotes lines 4918–4921) — and Perl defends this as rational, not irrational, silence: the logical outcome of the argument, not its abandonment.
