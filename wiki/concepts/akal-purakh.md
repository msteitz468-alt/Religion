# Akal Purakh

**Akal Purakh** ("the Person beyond Time" or "the Eternal One") is the central designation for God in Sikh theology as taught by [[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]] and developed by the ten personal Gurus. It stands at the heart of [[gurmat|Gurmat]] — the teaching of the Gurus — and is the object toward which all Sikh practice, including [[nam-simaran|nam simaran]], is directed.

## Designations and the Mul Mantra

McLeod notes that "many terms, drawn from a variety of traditions, are used to designate God, each of them offering a facet of Nanak's total understanding" (~L468). The Adi Granth opens with the **Mul Mantra** (Basic Doctrinal Statement), which begins with *ik-Oahkār* — the figure 1 and the letter O — representing "the unity of God" and translated as "the One Oankar" or "the One Being." *Oankar* is cognate with *Om* but Sikhs resist this identification: "for them 'Om' is Hindu, whereas 'Oankar' is Sikh" (~L481). The name Akal Purakh itself "has achieved a particular prominence in Nanak's usage" (~L486).

## Essential Attributes

### Formlessness (Nirguna)
Akal Purakh is understood as **Nirahkar** — "the One without Form" — and "repeated emphasis is laid on the ineffable quality of Akal Purakh's being" (~L492–495). This places Nanak squarely in the [[sant-tradition|Sant tradition]], which insists that God is *nirguna* ("without qualities") rather than *saguna* (possessing physical form or incarnations). External forms — images, temples, pilgrimages — are therefore irrelevant as means of approach.

### Immanence in Creation
Despite ineffability, "Akal Purakh is in fact manifested in the created world and they whose eyes are opened to spiritual understanding will perceive the Presence immanent in all creation" (~L503–506). "Akal Purakh, the Creator and Sustainer, dwells immanent in all creation, and because all that exists is an expression of the divine being this creation represents the supreme manifestation of the [[nam-simaran|nam]]" (~L558–564).

### Grace
Akal Purakh is "a God of grace, speaking the Word of divine understanding to all who are prepared to shed their [[haumai|haumai]] and listen in humility" (~L597–599). The divine Name (*nam*) is "the sure remedy offered by Akal Purakh" (~L553).

### The Eternal Guru
"Akal Purakh is the eternal Guru, speaking through the creation which constitutes a visible form" (~L604–605). The Word (*shabad*) is the message inscribed in creation; the mystical "voice" that speaks it is the eternal Guru. This doctrine grounds the whole Sikh understanding of [[the-guru|the Guru]]: ultimately the Guru is Akal Purakh communicating through creation, scripture, and the community.

## Gobind Singh's Extension of the Doctrine

Under Guru Gobind Singh, the doctrine of Akal Purakh received "the most important of extensions" (~L877). The *Jap* (from the Dasam Granth) reveals a developed theology: Akal Purakh "can be described in an infinity of ways yet possesses an infinity that still awaits description" (~L931–932). Crucially, Gobind Singh emphasized Akal Purakh as "supremely just, exalting those who faithfully follow and punishing the wicked" (~L937–938). The divine intervenes in history: "In the everlasting struggle between the powers of evil and the forces of good, Akal Purakh intervenes on occasion to restore the balance in favour of those waging war on behalf of the good" (~L939–941). This militant dimension of the theology was the doctrinal basis for the Khalsa: "particular individuals are chosen to act as agents of God, combating with physical strength the forces of evil" (~L959–963).

The *Sikh Rahit Maryada* requires initiated Khalsa Sikhs to "worship only Akal Purakh, spurning all other gods, goddesses, incarnations and prophets" (~L2389–2391).

## Hermeneutical Note

Within [[gurmat|Gurmat]], Akal Purakh is approached through *nam simaran* — the disciplined interior remembrance of the divine Name — not through external ritual. This is the Sikh hermeneutical axiom: the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]] is read as the living voice of Akal Purakh, and the act of reading/chanting *bani* (sacred compositions) is itself a form of encounter with the divine.
