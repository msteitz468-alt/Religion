# Pentecost (Feast of Weeks / Firstfruits)

**Pentecost** (Greek *pentēkostē*, "fiftieth") is the Jewish **Feast of Weeks** (Shavuot) / **Day
of Firstfruits**, celebrated fifty days after the Passover Sabbath. In [[acts|Acts]] 2:1–13 it is
the occasion of the outpouring of the [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]] on the gathered followers of
Jesus — the event that launches the church's mission. As a hermeneutical-liturgical hinge in
Luke's narrative, Pentecost belongs both to the Jewish festal calendar and to Christian
pneumatology.

## Definition and Jewish Background

As read by [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]] (BECNT *Acts*, 2007), the day is the Feast of Weeks / Day
of Firstfruits, reckoned fifty days after the Passover Sabbath, and is **one of the three
pilgrimage feasts**. Bock stresses that this pilgrimage character explains the diaspora audience
present in Jerusalem: the "God-fearing" and Jewish crowd "from every nation under heaven" (2:5)
reflects the festival gathering of Diaspora Jews (Bock argues, with Hengel and Marshall, that these
are likely Diaspora Jews who had *returned to live in* Jerusalem rather than mere pilgrims). The
term *oikos* ("house," 2:2) rules out a temple setting, since Luke elsewhere uses *to hieron* for
the temple.

## The Spirit-Outpouring (Acts 2:1–13), on Bock's reading

Bock calls the account **"a fulcrum account in Luke-Acts."** Its imagery is theophanic:

- **Wind** — the sound "like a rushing wind" (*ēchos ... pnoēs biaias*, 2:2) is theophany imagery
  (Bock cites 2 Sam. 22:16; Job 37:10; Ezek. 13:13; Philo, *Decalogue* 46), trading on the
  association of Spirit (*pneuma*) with wind.
- **Fire** — the tongues "as of fire" (2:3) signify divine presence and heavenly glory. Bock
  judges the fire here to be **not primarily judgment** and **not the full fulfillment** of the
  purging fire of Luke 3:16–17 (which he reads as still-future judgment).
- **Tongues** — the disciples speak in other languages (see [[glossolalia|glossolalia]]); Bock
  reads this as one-step foreign-language speech understood immediately by the hearers, with "no
  miracle of hearing" (the Spirit works on the speakers).

Bock takes the outpouring as **"a visible indication that the new era of the Messiah had
arrived"** — the sign that, in his phrase, "the new era of the Messiah" is now present. Peter's
Pentecost sermon (2:14–36) interprets the event through Joel 2:28–32 in a "this-is-that"
(*touto estin*) form (see [[speeches-in-acts|the speeches in Acts]]).

## Bock: Luke does NOT develop the Sinai law-giving background

A key interpretive judgment of Bock's is that, although some Second Temple sources (e.g. *Jubilees*)
tie the Feast of Weeks to the giving of the law at Sinai, **Luke does not employ the Sinai
law-giving background in Acts 2**. Following Wedderburn, Bock holds that the debate over a
Sinai-Pentecost typology "is thus not of such great significance for interpreting Acts." He notes
the same judgment recurs at 2:33: where the exalted Jesus pours out the Spirit, the dominant image
is **Davidic/messianic** (Jesus exercising Davidic authority now), with Sinai/Moses present at most
by contrast (Num. 11:29), not as the controlling frame. This distinguishes Luke's Pentecost from
later Christian and Jewish readings that make the Spirit's coming a new Sinai.

## Theological Significance

For Bock, Pentecost is where the "two key gifts of salvation for Luke" — forgiveness and the Spirit
— become manifest, and where the mission commanded in Acts 1:8 (rooted in Luke 24:47–49) begins.
Chrysostom's designation of Acts as "the gospel of the Holy Spirit" turns on this scene: the Spirit
promised by the Father (1:4) is poured out by the exalted Jesus, inaugurating the era of empowered
witness. The Gentile "Pentecost" at Cornelius's house (10:44–48) is later read by Peter as the
"identical gift" given "just as on us in the beginning" (11:15–17), extending the Pentecost pattern
beyond Israel (see [[cornelius|Cornelius]], [[gentiles|Gentiles]]).
