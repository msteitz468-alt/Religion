The *Shema* (Hebrew: שְׁמַע, "Hear") is the central confession of Jewish faith, taking its name from the opening word of [[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]] 6:4:

> **שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ יְהוָה אֶחָד**
> *Shema Yisra'el: YHWH Eloheinu, YHWH ʾeḥad*
> "Hear, O Israel: YHWH is our God, YHWH alone [or: YHWH is one]."

In liturgical practice the *Shema* comprises three biblical passages recited morning and evening: Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Deuteronomy 11:13–21; Numbers 15:37–41.

## The Meaning of *ʾeḥad*

The Hebrew *ʾeḥad* is the crux of the verse and is interpreted differently:

**"YHWH is one"** (numerical unity): YHWH is a single, unified deity — not a divine couple (YHWH and his Asherah, as in some pre-exilic popular religion) and not a deity with multiple manifestations. This reading emphasizes metaphysical monotheism.

**"YHWH alone"** (exclusive uniqueness): YHWH is Israel's sole legitimate deity — not first among many but the only one for Israel. This reading emphasizes covenantal monolatry: Israel's absolute allegiance belongs to YHWH and no other. [[peter-craigie|Craigie]] leans toward this reading, connecting it to Deuteronomy's suzerain-vassal [[covenant|covenant]] structure: exclusive loyalty to the suzerain is the primary demand.

**"YHWH is our God, YHWH alone"** (Moshe Weinfeld): A double clause — YHWH is our God; YHWH is our only God. This reading combines both senses.

In rabbinic tradition, the recitation of the *Shema* with full concentration (*kavvanah*) constitutes "the acceptance of the yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven" — the central act of Jewish religious devotion.

## Immediate Context: Deuteronomy 6:5

The verse following the *Shema* is:
> "You shall love YHWH your God with all your heart (*levavkha*), and with all your soul (*nafshekha*), and with all your might (*meodekha*)."

This command to love is the Deuteronomic covenantal response to the *Shema*: the suzerain's identity and uniqueness (v. 4) demands total personal loyalty (v. 5). [[peter-craigie|Craigie]] emphasizes that "love" here is not sentiment but the covenantal disposition of loyalty, a term drawn from ancient treaty language.

## NT Use

Jesus cites Deuteronomy 6:4–5 as the first and greatest commandment when asked by a scribe (Mark 12:29–30; cf. Matt 22:37; Luke 10:27). In Mark 12, the scribe affirms the Shema's importance and Jesus commends him. The *Shema* thus functions in the NT as common ground between Jesus and his Jewish interlocutors — it is not contested but assumed as foundational.

Paul does not cite the *Shema* directly but engages its implications in 1 Corinthians 8:4–6 ("there is no God but one"), where he incorporates Christ into the *Shema*'s framework by identifying Jesus as "Lord" (*kyrios*, the LXX rendering of YHWH).

The christological application of the *Shema* in Paul (and later in John 1) is among the most theologically significant moves in the NT: the God of Israel's singular identity is, for Paul, now expressed through Jesus as Lord and God as Father.

### Hurtado's Analysis: 1 Corinthians 8:4-6 as a Modified Shema

[[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]] (*Destroyer of the Gods*, ch. 3; expanded in *Lord Jesus Christ* [2003]) gives particular attention to 1 Corinthians 8:4-6 as the single most concentrated literary evidence for the early Christian [[dyadic-devotion|dyadic devotional]] pattern. The text reads:

> *"For us there is one God (*heis theos*), the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord (*heis kyrios*), Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist."*

Hurtado identifies the structure as a **deliberate reformulation of the Shema**:

| Shema (Deut 6:4) | 1 Corinthians 8:4-6 |
|---|---|
| YHWH is *one* | "one God" + "one Lord" |
| YHWH *our* God | "the Father, from whom are all things" |
| [implicit: creator and goal] | "for whom we exist" |
| — | "one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things" |

The structural parallels are close enough that Hurtado (and others, e.g. N.T. Wright) argue Paul is *consciously* drawing on the *Shema* and *expanding* its referent to include Jesus as the "one Lord" (*kyrios*). Several features of the move are theologically striking:

1. **The LXX prepared the semantic overlap**: In the Septuagint (Greek OT), *kyrios* ("Lord") is the standard substitution for the divine name YHWH. A Greek-speaking reader of the *Shema* in the LXX would read "the Lord our God, the Lord is one" — using the same word (*kyrios*) that Paul now applies to Jesus. Paul is thus slotting Jesus into the divine-name slot.

2. **The emphatic "one" is applied to both**: The *Shema*'s point is the numerical and covenantal uniqueness of YHWH. Paul carries that same uniqueness language over to Jesus as "the one Lord." This is not one of many lords but the uniquely sovereign Lord.

3. **The cosmological functions are divided but both are universal**: The Father is the source/goal of all things; Jesus is the mediating agent *through whom* all things exist and through whom we are redeemed. This is not bitheism but a dyadic structure within monotheism — the *Shema*'s claim to uniqueness is preserved but now jointly expressed through two figures.

4. **The context is anti-idolatry**: Paul deploys this in the context of meat offered to idols. His argument is that pagan gods have no real existence, *therefore* there is room to eat such meat — but the knowledge of this freedom must not destroy a weaker believer. The *Shema* framework serves his pastoral argument: since there is truly only one God-and-Lord, idols are nothing.

**Significance**: This text is early (1 Corinthians is dated c. AD 53-55, but Paul had been in the movement since c. 32-34 AD and is drawing on received tradition). It shows that the integration of Jesus into the divine identity was not a later development but was present in the community's core confessional vocabulary — the most fundamental Jewish affirmation of faith — within a generation of the crucifixion. See [[kyrios-christology-origins|Kyrios-Christology Origins]] for the Bousset-Hurtado controversy over the date and location of this development.

## Jewish Liturgical Context

The *Shema* is recited twice daily (morning and evening), immediately before sleep, and by a dying person as their final words. The first word, *shema* ("hear"), is written with an enlarged *ʿayin* (ע) and the final word *ʾeḥad* ("one") with an enlarged *dalet* (ד), forming the word *ʿēd* ("witness") — the recitation is understood as bearing witness to God's unity.

The mezuzah scroll (Deut 6:4–9 and 11:13–21), affixed to doorposts, and *tefillin* (phylacteries worn during morning prayer), containing the *Shema* passages, are the primary physical embodiments of Deuteronomy 6:8–9's commandment.
