# The Five Ways (Quinque Viae)

The **Five Ways** are [[thomas-aquinas|Thomas Aquinas]]'s five *a posteriori* demonstrations
of the existence of God, given at [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q2, A3. They are
the most famous arguments in the Western natural-theology tradition. Each begins from an
observed feature of the world and reasons to a terminus that "everyone understands to be God."

## Setting: self-evident vs. demonstrable (Q2, A1–A2)

Aquinas frames the Ways by first denying that God's existence is **self-evident to us**. The
proposition "God exists" is self-evident *in itself* (since in God essence and existence are
identical — see [[divine-simplicity]]), "but because we do not know the essence of God, the
proposition is not self-evident to us; but needs to be demonstrated by things that are more
known to us, though less known in their nature — namely, by effects" (Q2, A1).

This entails a pointed **rejection of the Anselmian ontological argument** (raised as Objection 2):
that one understands "God" to mean "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" does not
establish that such a being exists *in reality* rather than merely *in the mind* — "nor can it
be argued that it actually exists, unless it be admitted that there actually exists something
than which nothing greater can be thought; and this precisely is not admitted by those who hold
that God does not exist" (Q2, A1). (This anticipates Kant's later critique.)

Demonstration of God is therefore **a posteriori**, "from effect to cause": "from every effect
the existence of its proper cause can be demonstrated" (Q2, A2). God's existence is not itself an
article of faith but a *preamble* to the articles — "faith presupposes natural knowledge, even
as grace presupposes nature" (Q2, A2).

## The Five Ways (Q2, A3)

1. **From motion** (*ex parte motus*). Whatever is moved is moved by another, since to be moved
   is to be reduced from potentiality to actuality by something already in act. An infinite regress
   of moved movers is impossible ("subsequent movers move only inasmuch as they are put in motion
   by the first mover; as the staff moves only because it is put in motion by the hand"). Therefore
   there must be a first mover, "put in motion by no other; and this everyone understands to be God."

2. **From efficient causation.** In the world there is an order of efficient causes; nothing is
   the efficient cause of itself (it would be prior to itself). The series of ordered efficient
   causes cannot regress to infinity, "for to take away the cause is to take away the effect."
   Therefore "it is necessary to admit a first efficient cause, to which everyone gives the name of God."

3. **From possibility and necessity** (contingency). Some things are able to be and not to be
   (they are generated and corrupt). If everything were merely possible, then at some time nothing
   would have existed — and nothing could then begin, since "that which does not exist only begins
   to exist by something already existing." There must therefore be a being whose existence is
   necessary; and since necessity received from another cannot regress infinitely, there must be a
   being "having of itself its own necessity… This all men speak of as God."

4. **From the gradation of being** (the degrees of perfection). Things are found to be more and
   less good, true, noble; "more" and "less" are said by approximation to a maximum. There must be
   something which is "to all beings the cause of their being, goodness, and every other perfection;
   and this we call God." (This Way leans on a Platonic-Aristotelian participation metaphysics — cf.
   *Metaph.* ii, "those things that are greatest in truth are greatest in being.")

5. **From the governance of the world** (the teleological / final-cause argument). Things lacking
   intelligence — natural bodies — "act for an end… so as to obtain the best result," acting not
   fortuitously but designedly. What lacks knowledge cannot tend to an end "unless it be directed by
   some being endowed with knowledge and intelligence; as the arrow is shot to its mark by the
   archer." Therefore "some intelligent being exists by whom all natural things are directed to their
   end; and this being we call God."

To the objection that the existence of **evil** disproves an infinite good, Aquinas answers with
Augustine (*Enchiridion* xi): God "would not allow any evil to exist in His works, unless His
omnipotence and goodness were such as to bring good even out of evil." See [[evil-as-privation]].

## Relation to Maimonides' proofs

Aquinas's Ways run closely parallel to the four Aristotelian proofs of
[[maimonides|Maimonides]] ("Rabbi Moses," whom Aquinas cites elsewhere in the *Prima Pars*):
both argue from motion, from a moved-mover series, from contingency/necessary existence, and from
the reduction of potentiality to actuality — see [[existence-of-god]]. The chief difference is
method and setting: Maimonides mounts his proofs by granting Aristotle's eternity of the world
hypothetically (proving God on *both* the eternity and creation hypotheses), whereas Aquinas
holds that the world's beginning cannot be demonstrated by reason at all (see
[[eternity-of-the-world]]) and frames the Ways as effect-to-cause demonstrations that do not
depend on whether the world began.
