# Transubstantiation

**Transubstantiation** is the doctrine that in the Eucharist the **whole substance** of the bread
and wine is converted into the substance of the body and blood of [[jesus|Christ]], while the
**accidents** (the sensible appearances — colour, taste, quantity) remain. It is the heart of
[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s treatise on the Eucharist ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Tertia Pars
Q73–Q83), the sacrament he calls the greatest of the [[sacraments|seven]] because, uniquely, it
contains not merely Christ's power but **Christ himself** substantially.

## The Eucharist in general (Q73–Q74)

Instituted by Christ at the Last Supper, the Eucharist is the "sacrament of the Church's unity" and
the memorial of the Passion. Its **matter** is **wheaten bread** and **wine of the grape**, with a
little water mixed in (signifying the union of the people with Christ, and the water and blood from
His side). Aquinas notes (and adjudicates) the Latin use of **unleavened** vs. the Greek use of
**leavened** bread, allowing each church its rite. Heretics who altered the matter (Artotyrytae,
Cataphrygae, Aquarii) are named and rejected.

## The conversion: transubstantiation (Q75)

- **Real presence, not sign only.** Christ is "truly, really, and substantially" present, *not*
  merely "as in a sign." Aquinas names **Berengarius** of Tours as "the first deviser" of the
  sign-only heresy and rejects it.
- **The whole substance is converted** — bread into body, wine into blood: a unique change Aquinas
  calls **"transubstantiation"** (Q75 A4), distinct from any natural change (which is a change of
  *form* in an abiding subject). Here the *whole substance* passes over; nothing of the bread's
  substance remains. He rejects both **annihilation** of the bread and any view that the substance
  of bread remains alongside Christ's body (the position later called consubstantiation).
- **The accidents remain without a subject** (Q77): the sensible species (quantity, qualities)
  continue to exist and even to nourish or corrupt, sustained miraculously by God, with **dimensive
  quantity** serving as the quasi-subject in which the other accidents inhere. This is the
  metaphysically boldest claim of the treatise.

## The mode of Christ's presence (Q76)

- The **whole Christ** — body, blood, soul, and divinity — is present **under each species** and
  **under every part** of each species (so the fraction of the host does not divide Christ).
- This is by two routes: what the words of consecration directly effect (the body under the bread,
  the blood under the wine — "the power of the sacrament") and, joined to it, everything really
  united to Christ's body — **by concomitance** (*ex naturali concomitantia*): since the living
  Christ is now inseparable, His blood, soul, and Godhead accompany His body under the species of
  bread, and vice versa.
- Christ is **not present locally or dimensively** (He is not "in this place" by His own dimensions,
  is not moved when the host is moved, and is not seen by the bodily eye) but **after the manner of
  substance**.

## Form, effects, reception, minister (Q78–Q83)

- **Form** (Q78): the consecratory words "This is My body" and "This is the chalice of My blood…"
  ("for you and for many" — Aquinas reads "many" as Jews and Gentiles). They effect the conversion at
  the moment of utterance.
- **Effects** (Q79): the conferral of grace and the pledge of glory; the forgiveness of **venial**
  (not all mortal) sins; the remission of part of the temporal punishment; preservation from future
  sin; and benefit to others, since the Eucharist is also offered as a **sacrifice**.
- **Reception** (Q80): the distinction between **sacramental** eating (receiving the species) and
  **spiritual** eating (receiving the grace through faith and charity); the sinner who receives
  unworthily commits sacrilege and "eats judgment to himself"; the discipline of the eucharistic fast;
  and the licitness of communion under one species.
- **At the institution** (Q81): Christ Himself received His own body; and **Judas received** the
  consecrated body (Aquinas holds the consecration was complete and Judas communicated unworthily).
- **Minister** (Q82): **only a priest** can consecrate (by the character of [[holy-orders|Orders]]);
  even a wicked, heretical, or degraded priest consecrates **validly**, the character being indelible
  — the anti-Donatist principle applied to the Eucharist.
- **The Mass** (Q83): the rite as the Church's offering of the one sacrifice of Christ, with detailed
  provision for defects in the matter or words.

## Reception and divergence

Transubstantiation, articulated by Aquinas and affirmed at Lateran IV (1215) and the Council of Trent,
is the defining eucharistic doctrine of [[catholicism|Catholicism]]. [[eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern
Orthodoxy]] affirms the real presence and the change but is wary of the Aristotelian
substance/accidents apparatus (preferring *metabolē*/"change" and mystery). The **Protestant**
Reformers split sharply: Luther affirmed a real presence "in, with, and under" the bread (later termed
consubstantiation) while rejecting transubstantiation; Zwingli read the Supper as memorial/symbol;
Calvin held a spiritual real presence. The eucharistic sacrifice of the Mass became a central
Reformation flashpoint. These divergences belong on the respective sect pages.
