# Jain Asceticism

## Definition

Jain asceticism (*tapas*, "austerity"; also *samyama*, "restraint") is the organised practice of renunciant life for Jain monks (*muni*) and nuns (*sādhvī*; Digambara: *āryikā*) structured around the [[mahavrata|five Great Vows]], a daily regimen of meditation, obligatory ritual actions (*āvaśyaka*), alms-seeking, and the cultivation of qualities that restrain the influx of [[karma|karma]] and eventually destroy karma already accrued. The entire range of ascetic behaviour is aimed at two goals: the imposition of mental and physical constraints to ward off new karma (*samvara*, "protection"), and the practice of austerities which, exercised with sufficient intensity, will destroy existing karma (*nirjarā*, "elimination") — leading ultimately to [[moksha|liberation]].

## Monks, Nuns and Terminology

Male ascetics are called *muni*, derived from the root *man* ("think, be silent") — similar in origin to English "monk." Female ascetics are generally called *sādhvī*, feminine of *sādhu* ("good"), or, among the Digambaras, *āryikā* ("noble"). The ancient terms *nirgrantha* ("bondless") and *śramaṇa* ("striver") also occur; the latter has been revived by the Sthānakvāsī sect (Dundas, *The Jains*, ch. 6).

Jain monasticism differs from Christian monasticism in a key respect: monks and nuns lead lives of mendicant wandering rather than settled, enclosed contemplation — unless granted dispensation by a senior ascetic for illness, old age or scholarly work. Ascetics are homeless and without attachments. The possibility of permanent residence is associated only with the Śvetāmbara *yati* (a near-defunct cleric descended from the medieval temple-dwelling monk) and the Digambara *bhaṭṭāraka*, neither of whom is a wandering mendicant.

## Reasons for Renunciation

One Śvetāmbara scripture recognises a variety of motives for renunciation in a list of ten reasons (Sth 712): the individual's own desire to renounce, anger, poverty, the promptings of a dream, a promise to leave the world, the memory of previous births, sickness, lack of respect, being enlightened by a god, and following one's child into the order. A recent survey of Śvetāmbara nuns found a mixture of spiritual and social reasons: nineteen were spiritually drawn to a prominent female teacher, seventeen were orphans preferring ascetic life to living with relatives, and three sought escape from marriage (Dundas, ch. 6).

Theoretically, renunciation should be carried out in a manner least likely to cause distress to one's family. The Śvetāmbara Terapanthi sect insists on written permission from the immediate next of kin. The scriptures often refer to the impossibility of family relationships enabling serious religious goals.

## Initiation (*Dīkṣā*)

*Dīkṣā* — "initiation" — is the only purely Jain rite of passage by which a formal change of status is ritually signalled. Other life-cycle events (birth, marriage, death) are marked by essentially Hindu ceremonies. The dress and demeanour of a monk or nun prior to initiation are overtly those of a person about to be married; *dīkṣā* replaces to some extent the social bonding of marriage.

**Digambara initiation** originally was comparatively simple: the postulant approached the initiating teacher, received permission, abandoned his clothes, uprooted his hair, took the ascetic vows and a new name, and received a whisk and water pot. Today, male initiates are additionally painted with symbols by a caste priest (*upādhye*) within a temple in the manner of an image being consecrated, while a *bhaṭṭāraka* oversees and legitimises the event.

**Śvetāmbara initiation** has two stages: a preliminary "little" (*choṭī*) *dīkṣā* followed by the formal "big" (*baṛī*) *dīkṣā*. The initial period begins with a formal declaration of sincerity, adoption of a new name (for image-worshipping sects), and a probationary period of about one month (up to two years for the Terapanthi) involving fasting and study of basic scriptural texts. The "big" *dīkṣā* includes:
- A procession involving the lay community
- Exchanges in Prakrit between the initiate and the initiating teacher in the ascetic lodging-house (*upāśraya*) before a fordmaker image
- The hair-pulling ceremony (not witnessed by the lay community), signifying austerity and abandonment of sexuality — either actual removal of hair in handfuls or, in a practice with ancient warrant, shaving with small tufts left to be removed at initiation
- The gift of robe, whisk, and alms bowl (and if Terapanthi or Sthānakvāsī, the *mukhpattī* or mouth-shield), serving as emblems of sectarian allegiance

The Digambaras hold that only adults can become initiated ascetics. Śvetāmbara minimum age for initiation has historically been as low as eight years; only the Terapanthi sect today accepts the validity of child initiation.

Digambara lower-order male ascetics who may never proceed to full initiation as naked monks include the *kṣullaka* ("lesser"), who may wear a robe and keep an alms bowl; and the more advanced *ailaka* (possibly meaning "partly clothed"), who wears only a loincloth and eats sitting down without a receptacle — pulling out his hair and, according to ancient texts, not washing himself.

## Restraint and Austerity: Two Pillars

### Restraint (*Samyama*)

Protection from karma (*samvara*) comes from a controlled stance toward the surrounding world. Its structure is formalised through:

**The Three Protections (*gupti*)**: general curbing of the three modalities — mind, body and speech — so that they are not employed for frivolous or spiritually irrelevant purposes (TS 9.4).

**The Five Careful Actions (*samiti*)**: a form of etiquette for conducting oneself in the world:
1. *Iryāsamiti* — care in motion; avoiding injury to life-forms while walking (the most publicly scrutinised Jain virtue)
2. Care in speech; prevention of malevolent statements or excessive laughter
3. Care in seeking alms (*eṣaṇā*); always ensuring offered food is appropriate
4. Care in receiving or putting down objects; inspection to prevent violence to tiny life-forms
5. Care in voiding the bowels; checking the ground beforehand (TS 9.5; BhA 1181–94)

Additional means of warding off karma include: reflection on the nature of transmigration (*anupreksā*), conquest of the endurances (*parīṣaha*) — disagreeable factors inevitable in ascetic life — and maintenance of pure and correct behaviour (TS 9.18).

### Austerity (*Tapas*)

Austerity ("the 'heating up' of the body," tapas) is required to eliminate karma already accrued (*nirjarā*) and is viewed in very positive terms in Jainism: "a cool house for those burnt by the fire of transmigration" (BhA 1457) and "a city with walls in the form of restraint which the passions cannot storm" (MA 879–80). It must be accompanied by confidence in Jain principles, lack of attachment, and friendship toward all living creatures, and must avoid ostentation.

Haribhadra defends Jain austerities as characterised not by physical suffering but by knowledge and sincere religious prompting — "a course of action undertaken when bad karma can no longer have any serious effect and which cannot cause pain because the mind is under control and fixed upon a pure goal" (AJP p. 218). As the fordmakers prescribed austerity to cure the illness of transmigration, so the doctor prescribes fasting to cure a malady.

Jainism distinguishes six external and six internal types of austerity:

**External austerity** involves: complete fasting; reduction of food intake; deliberately making alms-seeking difficult; giving up substances that impart flavour; temporary solitude; bodily mortification. The quintessential form is fasting for a predetermined period — cutting out food completely, omitting alternate meals, or interspersing a longer period with limited consumption of the unattractive food known as *āyambila*.

**Internal austerity** involves: atonement for faults committed; respect for ascetic superiors and for Jain truths; service to fellow ascetics; study and reflection; giving up personal attachments; meditation (TS 9.19–20).

## Non-Violence in Ascetic Life

The ascetic life is structured around an intensive understanding of [[ahimsa|non-violence]] (*ahiṃsā*). In Jainism, "first knowledge of the world, then compassion for it" (*Daśavaikālika* 4.10): correct understanding of reality — that all souls are like oneself in that they suffer distress, cling to life and do not wish to be destroyed (AS 1.2.3; DVS 6.16 and 10.5) — is the precondition for genuine non-violence. This demands that the ascetic cannot light fires, dig or plough the ground, must drink filtered or boiled water, inspect surroundings carefully to avoid injury to insects, and avoid exaggerated movement of the body.

*Ahiṃsā*, though negative in form, is also interpreted within Jain tradition in strongly positive terms as involving friendship, goodwill, peace, gentleness and lack of passion. Violence (*hiṃsā*) is defined by Umasvāti as "the removal of life through a careless action of mind, body or speech" (TS 7.8) — intention rather than external act is decisive (Jinabhadra, sixth/seventh century: "it is the intention which is the deciding factor, not the external act which is inconclusive").

## Meditation (*Dhyāna*)

Jain literature asserts the necessity of meditation as a weapon against the passions, yet Jainism — unlike Theravāda Buddhism — never fully developed a culture of true meditative contemplation. Early Jain teachings were more concerned with the cessation of mental and physical activity than their transformation. Dundas concludes that "later Jain writers discussed the subject only because participation in the pan-Indian socio-religious world made it necessary to do so."

The basic schema is fourfold: the anguished (*ārta*), the angry (*raudra*), the religious (*dharma*) and the bright (*śukla*) meditations. The first two are bitter, negatively intense emotions detrimental to a positive rebirth — they "hardly qualify as meditative states." Umasvāti acknowledges that only the last two have anything to do with gaining deliverance (TS 9.30). Only the bright meditation is placed in a strictly soteriological context in the early texts (SKS 1.6.16–17), associated with Mahāvīra's burning away of karma — involving a gradual reduction of activity to the point of cessation of breathing.

The culminating Digambara work on the subject is Subhacandra's *Ocean of Knowledge* (*Jñānārṇava*; eleventh century?), which added a fourfold pattern of contemplation alongside the older schema: contemplation of images and objects (*piṇḍa*); contemplation of words (*pada*) — mystical manipulation of mantras; contemplation of form (*rūpa*) — visualisation of the fordmakers; and contemplation of that which is beyond form (*rūpātīta*) — concentration on the soul in its purest state (JA 34–7). Subhacandra's idiom shows restructuring of Jain contemplation to incorporate elements of Hindu — specifically Śaiva Kashmiri mystic — provenance.

Hemacandra (Śvetāmbara) admits that his contemporaries were "totally incapable of attaining" the later stages of meditation, which he describes only because descriptions have come down through tradition (YS 11.4). Meditation is not practised today by Jain ascetics in any meaningful structured way. The second half of the twentieth century has seen a revival of interest among the Terapanthi sect, using Theravāda Buddhist techniques (see Ch. 9). Contemplative activity would most naturally take place during the ritual performance of the Obligatory Actions.

## Ascetic Ritual: The Obligatory Actions (*Āvaśyaka*)

The six daily Obligatory Actions (*āvaśyakas*), and the Prakrit ritual formulae associated with them, form the essential prerequisite for knowledge and practice leading to salvation (Haribhadra, comm. on AvNiry 2). The *Āvaśyakasūtra* is not among the oldest Śvetāmbara canonical texts but became enormously important, accumulated layers of medieval commentary, and was pre-sectarian in origin, with close parallels between Śvetāmbara and Digambara texts (cf. MA 503).

The six Obligatory Actions incumbent on ascetics are:

1. **Equanimity (*sāmāyika*)**: originally meaning "correct behaviour"; a quasi-meditative state consciously achieved in a motionless standing position for forty-eight minutes, suppressing the passions and developing goodwill to all creatures. "Sāmāyika is that state in which the ascetic is the same towards himself and another, to his mother and to all other women, to pleasant and unpleasant things and to honour and disrespect" (MA 521). The entire ascetic life is regarded as an act of sāmāyika; its ritual performance is merely a temporary actualisation.

2. **Praise of the fordmakers**: a hymn demonstrating that devotional activity (*bhakti*) — non-existent in the oldest texts — was from an early period a necessary part of the Jain path (BhA 748) and credited with the ability to destroy karma.

3. **Homage to the teacher (*vandanā*)**: a formalised act including a request for forgiveness of offences and an inquiry concerning the teacher's welfare; soon ritualised so that no articulated response was necessary or expected.

4. **Repentance (*pratikramaṇa*)**: standing at the centre of the Āvaśyaka ritual; must be performed in the teacher's presence twice daily and at fortnightly, four-monthly and annual intervals. It means "going back" to correct behaviour which has been interrupted by transgressions. Preceded by *ālocanā* ("inspection/acknowledgement of faults"). Revolves around recitation of six passages enumerating faults, each ending with *micchā mi dukkaḍaṃ* — "may evil which has been done by me be in vain." Concludes with one of the most famous Jain statements: "I ask pardon from all living creatures. May all creatures pardon me. May I have friendship for all creatures and enmity towards none" (AvS 32).

5. **Laying down the body (*kāyotsarga*)**: assuming a motionless position with arms hanging down without touching the body's sides — frequently portrayed in Jain art. Includes requests for forgiveness and expressions of homage; the repetition of the second Obligatory Action's formula suggests imitation of the fordmakers.

6. **Abandonment (*pratyākhyāna*)**: undertaking to abstain from transgressions and refrain from the consumption of various foods and drinks. Where *pratikramaṇa* refers to the past, abandonment relates to the present and future; it can also involve giving up even harmless substances as a form of austerity.

(Digambaras reverse the order of the last two and appear to have abandoned daily performance of some rituals.)

## Interaction with the Laity

The four-month monsoon rain retreat was key to the emergence of a lay supporter community, since regular residence near a settlement created sustained contact. The monk is described in one text as a deer and the layman as a hunter (NBh 1649), signalling the potential dangers of this relationship. Lay and ascetic roles intersect most clearly through the institution of *dāna* (giving).

**Giving (*dāna*)**: at its most basic, *dāna* is the giving of food by a lay person to a monk or nun — the institution that sustains the ascetic community, since monks and nuns cannot prepare food or possess money. The real beneficiary of *dāna* is the lay donor, for giving is accepted as a means of gaining merit and improving one's destiny. The ascetic does not beg but is humbly requested by the lay person to accept food; the process of alms-seeking has since the medieval period been designated by Śvetāmbaras as "grazing" (*gocarī*), indicating its supposedly random nature.

The *Daśavaikālika* (one of the basic texts for all Śvetāmbara ascetics) gives a detailed account of alms conduct: the ascetic must gain food "as the bee gets honey from flowers without damaging the blossoms" (DVS 1.2–5). Śvetāmbara ascetics seek alms twice daily; Digambara naked monks eat only once in the morning, using cupped hands as a receptacle, in total silence, eyes carefully fixed on the ground.

The ascetic himself also gives: teaching doctrine is a form of giving, and above all the ascetic gives "the highest of all forms of *dāna*, safety and protection to all living creatures (*abhaya*)" (SKS 1.6.23; MA 941).

## Vegetarianism

Jain dietary prescriptions extend beyond simple vegetarianism: "meat" includes not just animal flesh, fish and eggs but types of vegetables felt to contain life-forms — onions, garlic, potatoes and fruits with large numbers of seeds. Alcohol is proscribed because fermentation destroys life-forms. Hemacandra claims those who eat honey are "worse than butchers" (YS 3.37). Strict and precisely defined vegetarianism "is the most tangible social expression of adherence to the doctrine of non-violence and the most significant marker of Jain identity" (Dundas, ch. 6).

There is evidence that in ancient times Jain ascetics consumed meat — the *Exposition of Explanations* portrays Mahāvīra eating a cock — but this became abhorrent by early medieval times and was either reinterpreted with a vegetarian gloss or emended. Ascetics today are not allowed to eat meat under any circumstances; for a lay person to breach the rule would in India almost inevitably lead to rejection as a Jain.

## Rules of Monastic Conduct (*Chedasūtras*)

The *Chedasūtras* — Śvetāmbara canonical texts describing monastic rules — produced particularly extensive commentaries delineating rules for ascetic conduct, inter-monastic relationships, connections with lay supporters, and penances for misdemeanours. Three of these texts developed especially extensive commentarial traditions based on both scripture and learned consensus (Dundas, ch. 6). The name of the most important, the *Niśītha*, means "secret"; only experienced monks were allowed to study it, and Tapa Gaccha nuns are expressly forbidden to read the Chedasūtras.

Jain monastic law does not connect its injunctions with episodes in Mahāvīra's life as the Buddhists did with the Buddha. It abounds in narrative illustrations conveying positive and negative results rather than technical accounts of ramifications. The commentaries developed a characteristic of providing exceptions to general rules based on local custom — which by the seventh century had diluted the required rigours of ascetic life. Rather than condemning this as laxity, it may better be interpreted as a practical attempt to broaden the Śvetāmbara community by acknowledging differences in ability among its members.

## The Role of the Ācārya

The [[tirthankara|ācārya]] (teacher) holds the central position in Jain monastic polity. Five senior ranks are named: *ācārya*, *upādhyāya* (preceptor, responsible for teaching scripture to junior monks), *pravartaka* (promoter, responsible for discipline — now rare), *sthavira* (elder — discontinued), and *gaṇadhara* / *gaṇin* (leader of the troop). Among image-worshipping Śvetāmbaras, the rank of *paṇyās* (scholar) is given to learned monks below the rank of ācārya.

"As a ship full of treasure but without a captain can easily sink, so a monk who has correct faith but is without a good teacher can drown in the ocean of rebirth" (Vattakera, MA 88).

The importance of the ācārya is marked symbolically by the *sthāpanācārya* ("teacher in the form of a representation") — a small structure of three wooden sticks forming a tripod holding a pouch of conch shells, placed before the monk when preaching or performing the Obligatory Actions, signifying the continuing presence of the spiritual guide in the absence of the real teacher.

**Qualifications**: the minimal requirement for a Śvetāmbara ācārya is eight years as a monk and full familiarity with the third and fourth *aṅgas*. The relatively short qualifying period meant that in the medieval period (when child initiation was prevalent) quite a few monks became ācāryas as teenagers.

**Installation**: installation of an ācārya is in certain respects equivalent to the succession of a king — the ācārya is called "bearer of the *paṭṭa*" (*paṭṭadhāra*), the turban used at regal consecration. The new ācārya receives the *sūrimantra* — a sacred and secret formula supposedly taught by Mahāvīra to Gautama and transmitted through the lineage — and becomes the equivalent of Gautama from the moment of appointment. The ācārya is qualified to incorporate the honorific title *siri* ("wise man") into his name, oversee a group of monks, appoint a successor, give his own interpretation of the scriptures, and impose penances.

**Recent history — Digambara revival**: Ācārya Śāntisāgara (1873–1955) is the pivotal figure in the revival of Digambara ascetic culture. The Digambara naked-monk lineage had been all but obliterated by the nineteenth century, with numbers in serious decline from the seventeenth century. Śāntisāgara took dīkṣā in 1920 at Yarnal and was the first to regenerate the Digambara community by initiating pupils, defending Jain interests (opposing the Temple Entry Bill of 1948), and promoting the publication and translation of manuscripts. He performed [[sallekhana|sallekhānā]] in 1955. Virtually all current Digambara monks trace their lineage to Śāntisāgara; around 40 ācāryas and around 610 monks of differing ordination levels now exist. He was concerned with the Kundakunda mystical, soul-orientated variety of Digambara spirituality.

**Recent history — Śvetāmbara revival**: the image-worshipping Śvetāmbara ascetic order also went into severe decline; by around 1850 there were only about twenty-five monks and no ācāryas. Key transitional figures were Maṇivijaya (1796–1879), from whom around three-quarters of Tapa Gaccha monks today claim lineal descent, and his disciple Buddhivijaya (Buṭeyārājī, 1807–88). The lineage was reactivated largely through the lay community temporarily assuming responsibility for appointing ācāryas. Prominent later figures include Sāgarānanda Siri (1875–1950), who published the Śvetāmbara scriptures with traditional commentaries, and Vijayavallabha Siri (1870–1954), whose participation in the independence movement inspired many to seek initiation.
