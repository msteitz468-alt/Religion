# Sanskriti

*Saṃskṛti* (Sanskrit: "refined/cultured/perfected") — the Pan-Asian civilization historically organized around the Sanskrit language and its associated religious, philosophical, artistic, and social frameworks. The concept as developed by [[rajiv-malhotra]] in [[being-different-malhotra]] emphasizes that this was a civilization spread by **cultural "pull"** (prestige, trade, pilgrimage, voluntary adoption) rather than by conquest or forced conversion.

## Geographical Extent

At its historical peak, Sanskriti extended across:
- **South Asia**: The Indian subcontinent, including present-day India, Pakistan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh
- **Southeast Asia**: Present-day Indonesia (Java, Bali — Sanskritic kingdoms through c. 16th century), Cambodia (Angkor Wat, built as a Hindu temple), Thailand (Ayutthaya kingdom), Myanmar, Vietnam (Cham kingdom)
- **Central Asia**: Gandhara (present-day Afghanistan/Pakistan), the Silk Road transmission routes
- **East Asia**: Sanskrit Buddhist canon transmitted to China, Korea, Japan, and Tibet

The spread involved Sanskrit as a *lingua franca* of religion, philosophy, and literature — analogous to Latin in medieval Europe — but without the institutional apparatus of a single church or conquering empire. Local rulers voluntarily adopted Sanskritic frameworks to legitimate their authority; local deities were incorporated into the Hindu-Buddhist cosmological framework rather than replaced.

## "Pull" vs. "Push" Civilizational Spread

Malhotra contrasts Sanskriti's mode of spread with Abrahamic civilizational expansion:
- **Push model** (Abrahamic): Missionary conversion with an explicit claim to exclusivity; those who do not convert are saved or damned; armies and colonial structures often supported conversion; destruction of local religious sites and practices
- **Pull model** (Sanskritic): Local populations and rulers adopted Sanskrit culture because it conferred prestige, offered sophisticated philosophical and artistic frameworks, and integrated rather than replaced local traditions; Puranic stories served as "generic templates" that could be localized and into which tribal deities were incorporated

Malhotra notes that "Indian influence spread over a great part of Asia without Indian armies ever having to follow Indian traders, scholars and priests" — citing V.A. Smith and other historians of Southeast Asian Hinduism and Buddhism.

## Relationship to Dharmic Traditions

Sanskriti is the civilizational matrix from which Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism developed and within which they interacted. The three traditions share:
- A common technical vocabulary (Sanskrit concepts used across traditions, even when contested)
- Shared cosmological frameworks (yugas, cosmological time scales, karma, rebirth)
- Shared aesthetic and architectural vocabularies (temple design, iconography, performance)
- A shared pedagogical culture (guru-disciple transmission, *gurukula*, oral memorization, debate)

They diverge on metaphysics (Atman/no-Atman; Brahman/Shunyata) and on social organization (caste; monastic rules; lay ethics).

## Sanskrit Non-Translatables

A key feature of Sanskriti is what Malhotra calls the **non-translatables**: Sanskrit concepts that cannot be accurately mapped onto Western-language equivalents without distortion. He lists 18 major categories:

| Sanskrit | Western "equivalent" | The distortion |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Brahman | God | Brahman is non-personal, panentheistic; "God" imports a personal creator |
| Atman | Soul | Atman is identical with Brahman; "soul" suggests a separate created entity |
| Vedas | Bible | Vedas are *shruti* (heard); not commandments from a personal God |
| Dharma | Religion | Dharma is contextual ethics + cosmic order; "religion" is a separate institutional domain |
| Aum | Amen | Aum is a metaphysical vibration-reality; "Amen" is assent to a proposition |
| Yajna | Sacrifice | Yajna is cosmic exchange/correspondence; "sacrifice" implies slaughter or loss |
| Karma | Suffering/Fate | Karma is a causal mechanism of accumulated action; "fate" implies external predestination |
| Moksha | Salvation | Moksha is liberation from conditioned existence; "salvation" implies rescue from sin by a savior |
| Devatas | Pagan Gods | Devatas are cosmic intelligences/aspects of Brahman; "pagan gods" implies polytheistic error |
| Murtis | Idols | Murtis are loci of divine presence; "idols" implies worship of false gods |
| Rishi | Prophet | Rishis are inner scientists who discover eternal truths; prophets are uniquely chosen messengers |
| Guru | Priest/Minister | Guru transmits embodied knowledge; priest/minister administers institutional sacraments |
| Pandit | Scholar | Pandits hold and transmit Sanskrit learning as embodied wisdom; "scholar" implies academic analysis |
| Svadhyaya | Bible Study | Svadhyaya is self-study leading to self-knowledge; "Bible study" is exegesis of an authoritative text |
| Deva | Angel | Devas are cosmic forces/aspects of reality; angels are created beings serving a personal God |
| Asura | Demon | Asuras are forces of ego/adharma, not purely evil; "demon" imports absolute ontological evil |
| Ahimsa | Nonviolence | Ahimsa is non-harming across all life forms; "nonviolence" is primarily a political strategy |
| Darshana | Philosophy | Darshana is a "vision" of reality grounded in practice; "philosophy" is primarily conceptual analysis |

The insistence on non-translatables is both a philosophical claim (the concepts genuinely do not map) and a political one (accepting Western translations is a form of [[cultural-digestion]]).
