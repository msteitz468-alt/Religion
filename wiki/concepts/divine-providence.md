*Hebrew*: **hashgachah** (הַשְׁגָּחָה) — literally "watching over." The question of whether and how God exercises supervision or care over particular human beings and events, especially in the face of undeserved suffering.

## Definition and Stakes

Divine providence is the problem of reconciling the power, goodness, and knowledge of God with the observable fact that righteous individuals suffer and the wicked flourish. Every tradition that affirms a God who acts in history must face this question. The five positions Maimonides identifies via the Book of Job represent a nearly exhaustive taxonomy of the answers available in medieval philosophy.

## The Five Theories: Maimonides' Schema from Job

[[maimonides]] in the *[[guide-for-the-perplexed]]* (Part III, Ch. XXIII) reads the Book of Job as a dramatic survey of "all possible different theories" on divine providence: "In this manner consider well how the Book of job discusses the problem, which has perplexed many people, and led them to adopt in reference to Divine Providence some one of the theories which I have explained above: all possible different theories are mentioned therein" (Ch. XXIII, lines 141–144). He assigns each speaker a specific philosophical school:

### 1. Job = The Theory of Aristotle (No Individual Providence)

Job's initial position is that God makes no distinction between the righteous and the wicked: "He destroyeth the perfect and the wicked. If the scourge slay suddenly, he will laugh at the trial of the innocent" (Job ix. 22–23, cited Ch. XXIII, lines 35–38). The wicked prosper, the righteous suffer; death and the worm level all. This matches the Aristotelian view that divine providential care attaches only to the species and to the heavenly spheres, not to individual human beings.

Maimonides explains that Job held this view because "he was without wisdom, and knew God only by tradition, in the same manner as religious people generally know Him" (Ch. XXIII, lines 81–83). The error is epistemological: Job mistook bodily goods (health, wealth, children) for the true goods, so their loss seemed a genuine divine injustice.

### 2. Eliphaz = Scripture's Teaching (Strict Justice)

Eliphaz holds that all suffering is the result of divine justice for actual sins: "Is not thy wickedness great, and thine iniquities infinite?" (Job xxii. 5, cited Ch. XXIII, line 103). God does not punish the innocent; therefore Job must be guilty of sins he does not acknowledge: "Behold, he putteth no trust in his servants: and his angels he chargeth with folly: how much less in them that dwell in houses of clay" (Job iv. 17–18, cited lines 106–108). Eliphaz "never abandoned his belief that the fate of man is the result of justice, that we do not know all our shortcomings for which we are punished" (Ch. XXIII, lines 109–112).

This is the position Maimonides identifies as the teaching of Scripture in its simple sense: retributive justice operates in all cases, even when unknown sins are the cause.

### 3. Bildad = The Mu'tazilah (Compensation and Future Reward)

Bildad defends "the theory of reward and compensation": suffering that is genuinely undeserved will be recompensed in the future world. "If thou be pure and upright, surely now he will awake for thee, and make the habitation of thy righteousness prosperous. Though thy beginning was small, yet thy latter end will greatly increase" (Job viii. 6–8, cited Ch. XXIII, lines 119–122). Present misfortune is not injustice but a means to greater future bliss.

Maimonides identifies this with the Mu'tazilite position in Islamic *kalam*: God's justice is vindicated by eschatological compensation rather than immediate retribution.

### 4. Zophar = The Ash'ariyya (Divine Will as Sovereign — No Reasons)

Zophar holds that nothing can be asked about God's actions in terms of justice or wisdom: "the Divine Will is the source of everything that happens: no further cause can be sought for His actions, and it cannot be asked why He has done this and why He has not done that. That which God does can therefore not be explained by the way of justice or the result of wisdom" (Ch. XXIII, lines 126–131). "Canst thou by searching find out God? canst thou find out the Almighty unto perfection?" (Job xi. 7, cited line 138–139).

This is the Ash'arite position: God acts by sheer will, and his actions are not subject to rational evaluation. For Zophar, the question "why does the righteous suffer?" is simply unanswerable because divine will has no accessible rationale.

### 5. Elihu = A New Theory (Angelic Intercession; Prophecy as Providence's Channel)

Elihu introduces "a new idea, which is peculiar to Elihu and has not been mentioned by the others" (Ch. XXIII, lines 174–175): providence operates through the mediation of an angel or intermediary being who intercedes when a person approaches death: "If there be an angel with him, an interpreter, one among a thousand, to show unto man his uprightness" (Job xxxiii. 23, cited lines 184–185). The intercession is not guaranteed and does not always succeed: "Lo, all these things worketh God twice, three times with man" (xxxiii. 29, cited line 188). Elihu also introduces a description of prophecy as the medium through which providence reaches individuals: "Surely God speaketh in one way, yea in two ways, yet man perceiveth it not. In a dream, in a vision of the night" (xxxiii. 14–15, cited lines 189–192).

Maimonides places Elihu above the others in the allegorical ranking because his view is presented as later and more philosophically refined — Elihu is "younger in years but greater in wisdom" (Ch. XXIII, line 153). The distinctively new element is the role of prophetic mediation in connecting individual humans to divine governance.

## Maimonides' Own Doctrine: Providence Proportional to Intellect (III:17–21)

The chapters preceding the Job reading state Maimonides' own position directly. Surveying the
prior theories (Aristotle — sublunary events are chance; the [[asharism|Ash'ariyya]] — universal
determinism and "impossible duty"; the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] — justice extended even to
animals; and the "prophetical"/Jewish view), he stakes out a **hybrid**: "In the lower or
sublunary portion of the Universe Divine Providence does not extend to the individual members of
species except in the case of mankind" (III:17). For animals, plants, and minerals he agrees
with [[aristotle|Aristotle]] that events are due to chance; only humans enjoy *individual*
providence — and even then unequally.

The measure is intellect: "the action of Divine Providence is proportional to the endowment of
intellect" (III:18). Providence "is connected with Divine intellectual influence"; the more a
person shares in that overflow, the more providence attends him — prophets most of all, while the
ignorant and disobedient are "like unto the beasts" (Ps. 49:21) and fall under no individual
care. He cites [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]]'s commentary on the *Nicomachean Ethics* in support
(III:18). This connects providence directly to [[prophecy]] and to [[intellectual-perfection|
human perfection]].

On **omnipotence**, III:15 holds that the impossible is permanently impossible and "do[es] not
depend on the action of an agent" — God's power does not extend to logical contradictions, the
dispute being only over what belongs to the class of the impossible.

## God's Omniscience and the Disanalogy with Human Knowledge

Maimonides treats God's knowledge of particulars at length in III:19–21. Against the ancient doubt
(provoked by the prosperity of the wicked) that God knows individual human affairs, he argues that
the **homonymity of "knowledge" misleads**: God's knowledge differs from ours in five ways — it is
(1) one yet embracing multiplicity, (2) of the non-existent (the future), (3) infinite, (4)
unchanging though its objects change, and (5) such that it does not determine the realization of
possible events (III:20). His **clock analogy** (III:21) makes the point: the maker of a clock
knows its every movement *before* it occurs because the movements follow from his design, whereas
the observer learns each only *after* — "the things are in accordance with His eternal knowledge,"
not His knowledge with the things. Foreknowledge of a possible event therefore "does not yet
effect that realization" — Maimonides' attempted reconciliation of omniscience with human free
will, which he rests candidly on "the spirit of the Divine Law" rather than demonstration.

The principal theological lesson Maimonides draws from the Book of Job as a whole is that God's knowledge, rule, and providence are *not* analogous to human knowledge in the way the term suggests: "we should not fall into the error of imagining His knowledge to be similar to ours, or His intention, providence, and rule similar to ours. When we know this we shall find everything that may befall us easy to bear; mishap will create no doubts in our hearts concerning God, whether He knows our affairs or not, whether He provides for us or abandons us" (Ch. XXIII, lines 238–246).

More precisely: "as there is a difference between works of nature and productions of human handicraft, so there is a difference between God's rule, providence, and intention in reference to all natural forces, and our rule, providence, and intention in reference to things which are the objects of our rule, providence, and intention. This lesson is the principal object of the whole Book of job" (Ch. XXIII, lines 233–238). The term "rule" as applied to God and as applied to us "signifies two different notions, which have nothing in common but the name" (Ch. XXIII, lines 231–232).

This is a direct application of [[negative-theology]]: the divine attributes are shared with human predicates by name only (*equivocation*), not by genus. The consequence for theodicy is significant: the suffering of the righteous does not constitute evidence against divine providence, because we are in error when we model God's knowing and caring on our own.

## Job's Transformation as Epistemological Resolution

When Job finally says "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear; but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent because of dust and ashes" (xlii. 5–6), Maimonides reads this as the resolution of the theodicy problem through intellectual advance. Job's initial position (Aristotelian: no individual providence) was plausible given his knowledge of God by *tradition* alone. Once Job "acquired a true knowledge of God, he confessed that there is undoubtedly true felicity in the knowledge of God; it is attained by all who acquire that knowledge, and no earthly trouble can disturb it" (Ch. XXIII, lines 83–87).

The theodicy problem, in Maimonides' reading, dissolves when the sufferer moves from knowledge by hearsay to knowledge by investigation — because such investigation reveals that the true goods (intellectual apprehension of God) cannot be taken away by bodily affliction. The *pious do everything out of love, and rejoice in their own afflictions* (B.T. Shabb. 88b, cited Ch. XXIII, lines 248–249).

## The Trials / Nisayon (Ch. XXIV)

Maimonides addresses the related problem of *nisayon* (divine testing) in Ch. XXIV. He rejects the widespread view that God sends afflictions as tests in order to *learn* something about a person's faith that was previously unknown: "This is the way how we have to understand the accounts of trials; we must not think that God desires to examine us and to try us in order to know what He did not know before. Far is this from Him" (Ch. XXIV, lines 441–444).

The correct interpretation of passages like "to know whether ye love" (Deut. xiii. 4) is that *la-da'at* here means "that all may know" — the trial is a public demonstration, not a divine inquiry: "the sole object of all the trials mentioned in Scripture is to teach man what he ought to do or believe; so that the event which forms the actual trial is not the end desired: it is but an example for our instruction and guidance" (Ch. XXIV, lines 292–295).

The binding of Isaac (*'akedah*) illustrates this doubly: (1) it shows the extent of the fear of God (disinterested love and obedience, not motivated by reward or punishment), and (2) it confirms the reliability of prophetic perception — Abraham's willingness to act on a dream-command proves that prophets do not doubt what they receive in prophetic vision: "the act by which he confirmed the principle of the truth of prophecy, and showed how far we must go in the fear and the love of God" (Ch. XXIV, lines 436–438).

## The Purpose of Creation — World Not Made for Man (Ch. XXV)

Maimonides connects the theodicy problem to a pervasive anthropocentric error. The difficulties that drive people toward erroneous theories of providence arise from two causes: "firstly, man has an erroneous idea of himself, and believes that the whole world exists only for his sake; secondly, he is ignorant both about the nature of the sublunary world, and about the Creator's intention to give existence to all beings whose existence is possible, because existence is undoubtedly good" (Ch. XXV, lines 571–577).

The universe is not made for man; God's intention is to give existence to everything whose existence is possible ("existence is undoubtedly good"), and the divine wisdom that governs this is not accessible to human reasoning except partially. Nor are God's works purposeless or arbitrary: "no intelligent person can assume that any of the actions of God can be in vain, purposeless, or unimportant. According to our view and the view of all that follow the Law of Moses, all actions of God are 'exceedingly good'" (Ch. XXV, lines 485–490, citing Gen. i. 31). But the purpose of many things is not human welfare specifically.

## Augustine: Providence over History and the Two Cities

In [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Books I–V, [[augustine|Augustine]] develops a providential **philosophy
of history**. Temporal goods and ills befall good and bad alike, by design: the same affliction "proves,
purges, clarifies the good, but damns, ruins, exterminates the wicked" (Book I). Earthly **empire** is
not the gift of the pagan gods but is distributed by the one God "to good and bad… according to the order
of things and times, which is hidden from us, but thoroughly known to Himself" (Book IV) — Assyria, Rome,
and even tyrants like Nero receive dominion when God judges fit (Book V, citing Prov. 8:15; Job 34:30).
Against the Roman blame of Christianity for the 410 sack, Augustine reframes catastrophe as providential
chastisement and mercy (Ps. 89:32). On the compatibility of God's foreknowledge with human freedom, see
[[divine-foreknowledge-and-free-will]]; the providential "order of causes" includes the freely willing
will itself, and is distinct from astrological [[fate-moirai|fate]].

## Aquinas: the government of the world (Summa Theologiae I, Q103–119)

Where [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats providence *in itself* under God's knowledge and will
(Q22–23; see [[predestination]]), the *execution* of providence is the **government of the world**
(Q103–119). God governs all things toward the good as their end (Q103); by **design** He orders all
things immediately, but in **execution** He works through a hierarchy of **secondary causes**, "to
communicate the dignity of causality to creatures" (the archer–arrow image). He **conserves** every
creature in being at every moment — "not for a moment could it subsist" without God's sustaining act
(Q104) — and works in every agent as end, mover, and giver of form (Q105, A5). He alone moves the
**will** efficaciously, since only the universal good fills its capacity (Q105, A4). He can also act
**outside** the order of secondary causes He established — **miracles**, defined as what God does
beyond the order of the whole created nature, in three grades (Q105, A6–8).

On **fate and the stars**: the heavenly bodies **cannot impose necessity on the human intellect or
will**, which are not corporeal powers; they incline only indirectly through the bodily passions, and
"the wise man is master of the stars" (Ptolemy). What truth there is in **"fate"** is nothing but the
ordering of secondary causes under divine providence — not an astral necessity — and things God does
immediately are "above fate" (Q115–116; see [[fate-moirai]]). This secures human freedom *within*
universal providence.

## Cross-Tradition Notes

- **Aristotle** (as characterized by Maimonides): No individual divine providence; divine attention is confined to the heavenly spheres and to species-forms.
- **Mu'tazilah** (*kalam*): Divine justice is vindicated by eschatological compensation — suffering that cannot be explained by retribution will be recompensed.
- **Ash'ariyya** (*kalam*): Divine will is sovereign and inscrutable; rational theodicy is impossible.
- **Elihu's theory / Maimonides' own**: Providence attaches to individuals in proportion to their intellectual and moral perfection — stated directly in III:17–18 (see above) and dramatized in the Job reading, where Job's suffering becomes bearable, and his theodicy resolved, precisely when he attains true intellectual knowledge of God.
