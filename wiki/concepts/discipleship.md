# Discipleship

Discipleship is the category under which the [[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]] describes the
relationship between [[jesus|Jesus]] and those who attach themselves to him — a relationship
that in Matthew's narrative begins with a summons, proceeds through instruction, and is tested
by persecution. This page records the treatment of the theme in R. T. France, *The Gospel of
Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), supplemented (2026-07-18) by his *Gospel of Mark* (NIGTC, 2002 — see the
"Discipleship in Mark" section below); every interpretive claim on this page is attributed to
[[rt-france|France]] and would be revised if his reading were displaced.

## Definition and Vocabulary

Matthew uses the noun *mathētēs* ("disciple") and, uniquely among the gospels, the verb
*mathēteuō* ("make a disciple," 13:52; 27:57; 28:19). France argues that whereas in Mark
*mathētēs* is probably restricted to the Twelve, Matthew — like Luke — uses it more widely, and
he takes the distinctively Matthean verb as confirmation of that broader usage. He connects the
vocabulary to the yoke saying of 11:29: Jesus' yoke, like the yoke of Torah and of Wisdom, is one
of *learning*, and on France's reading "Discipleship (from the same Greek root, 'learn') is a
lifelong process of learning how to live as God requires." He rejects the devotionally appealing
image of the double animal yoke — being "in double harness with Jesus" — holding that the yoke in
view is the single human load-distributing shoulder yoke.

France cautions that the verb "follow" alone is not adequate evidence of full discipleship: the
crowds "follow" Jesus while only a few are his constant companions, and he judges Mark 3:7-8's
language of "coming to" Jesus perhaps more exact. He identifies a "grey area which existed
between the uncommitted 'crowd' ... and the fully committed Twelve," mapped further in the
parable of the sower.

## The Call Narratives

Matthew 4:18-22 records the calling of two pairs of fishermen. France stresses that gathering
followers is Jesus' *first recorded action*, and reads the placement as programmatic: from this
point the gospel offers "not stories about Jesus alone, but stories about Jesus and his
disciples," and Matthew's story is "not only that of the Messiah, but also of the messianic
community which is being formed around him." On his reading the disciples' presence is assumed
even where unmentioned, and Jesus is first left alone again only at the desertion in Gethsemane
(26:56). France notes that Matthew "betrays no awareness" of the earlier meeting near the Jordan
reported in John 1:35-42, so that the suddenness of leaving Zebedee suggests unpremeditated
action.

France argues the summons is prophetic rather than rabbinic in form. A rabbi's disciples normally
took the initiative in attaching themselves; "What Jesus issues here is not even an invitation,
but rather a demand" — a pattern he compares to Elijah and Elisha (1 Kgs 19:19-21). He notes that
Matthew, unlike Mark, uses "immediately" sparingly, so that its appearance in vv. 20, 22
emphasizes the extraordinary readiness of working men to abandon security "for the sake of a
charismatic stranger." He treats the "fishing for people" metaphor as a genuine interpretive
crux, since the OT background can point to catching for judgment (Jer 16:16; Amos 4:2; Hab
1:14-17 — "it is no blessing for a fish to be caught!"); he opts for the positive sense in
context while observing that 13:47-50 holds both together. France rejects the proposal of a
social stratification between shore-fishing Simon and Andrew and the boat-owning Zebedees, since
*diktyon* is not a specific term and the plural is used of both, and since the theory conflicts
with Luke 5:1-11. He also reports but declines the suggestion, drawn from 20:20 and 27:56, that
Zebedee's family was related to Jesus' own: "the inference of kinship is very uncertain, and
Matthew does not indicate it."

The call of Matthew the tax collector at 9:9 is, on France's reading, "the first and only such
call story in this gospel after the initial calling of the four fishermen in 4:18-22," readers
being left to assume the other seven had comparable experiences. He reads the calling of a
*telōnēs* as "a daring breach of etiquette, a calculated snub to conventional ideas of
respectability" at which ordinary people no less than the [[pharisees|Pharisees]] would balk, and
draws the thematic conclusion: "Discipleship is not for the comfortable and respectable, but for
those whom conventional society would rather keep at arm's length." He explains the offence of
the following meal by noting that "In the ancient world generally a shared meal was a clear sign
of identification."

## Renunciation and Its Limits

From the parallel language at 4:20, 22; 9:9 and 19:21-22 France concludes that "Matthew's
understanding of discipleship was ideally of 'giving up everything' to follow Jesus" — while
noting that later details modify any impression of total renunciation. He presses this caveat
using Peter's house at Capernaum (8:14-15): "Simon and Andrew left their nets, but not their home
and (extended) family." He flags the tension between the priority of discipleship over family
(8:18-22; 10:34-37) and Jesus' defence of proper concern for parents at 15:3-6, resolving it as
underlining "the radical urgency of his call." On the true-family pericope (12:46-50) he holds
that the implied repudiation of natural kin "is a matter of priority rather than an absolute
dissociation."

Regarding possessions, France lists four recurrent gospel themes: the expectation that disciples
will not be among the affluent; the priority of spiritual allegiance over material security;
assurance of God's care for material needs; and the call to uncalculating generosity. He
explicitly limits the treasure saying: "It is going beyond the intention of the saying to use it
as a basis for ruling out all material possessions and all provisions for the (earthly) future,"
noting that the itinerant ministry depended on supporters who had not divested. He limits the
provision promise as sharply: "The disciple is promised survival, not affluence; this is no carte
blanche," and reads 6:34's more pessimistic note as a deliberate check on "an unthinkingly
euphoric attitude which vv. 25-33 might evoke" — God's provision is assured, "but that does not
mean that the disciple's life is to be one long picnic."

## The Two Would-Be Followers (8:18-22)

France places 8:18-22 and 9:9-17 as the two narrative interludes dividing the three triads of the
miracle anthology (see [[miracles|Miracles]]), and holds that both "focus on the call to
discipleship," fleshing out the theme of chs. 5–7 in "case studies" that let readers weigh their
own response to 7:13-27. He argues the interest of 8:18-22 lies not in the two men — nothing is
told of them, not even whether they followed — but in Jesus' responses, which "raise an abrupt
challenge to any easy understanding of discipleship." He reads the scribe's approach as shaped by
his training, which leads him to assume it is his to choose to follow, as rabbis' disciples did,
rather than for Jesus to call him.

On "let the dead bury their dead" France records two positions.

- **The standard reading.** The request is entirely reasonable: burial fell within twenty-four
  hours, the arrangements were the eldest son's duty, and Jewish piety gave the duty priority
  over even the most essential prayers. On this reading Jesus' refusal "would be profoundly
  shocking."
- **The idiomatic reading (reported from K. E. Bailey).** Drawing on Arabic commentators and
  Middle Eastern cultural experience, Bailey argues the standard reading is a culturally
  impossible "western" one: to "bury one's father" is standard idiom for discharging filial
  responsibility for the remainder of the father's lifetime, making the request one for
  indefinite, years-long postponement.

France accepts that Bailey's reading makes the reply "less immediately shocking" but insists the
demand still cuts across deep-rooted cultural expectation and that calling the substitutes "the
dead" remains harsh. He concedes the saying looks like a prima facie breach of the fifth
commandment, which Jesus elsewhere endorses (15:3-6; 19:19), and that "no rabbi would have been
so cavalier." He notes the metaphorical use of *nekros* for the spiritually lifeless "does not
occur elsewhere in the gospels," though it appears in Eph 2:1, 5; Col 2:13; Rev 3:1, and
summarizes: "A disciple's business is with life, not with death." He reports that M. Hengel takes
8:22 as the paradigm for his portrayal of Jesus as "charismatic leader," whose call has a "unique
offensiveness" and "sharply runs counter to law, piety and custom."

## The Disciple Community in the Sermon

France insists the second-person plurals of 5:13-16 are corporate: "it is the corporate impact of
the disciple community, as an alternative society, which is here in view," and the hilltop town is
a symbol of collective, not individual, visibility. He explicitly criticizes "modern Western
individualism" for reading the light as "a variety of little candles shining, 'you in your small
corner, and I in mine'"; the point is "the collective light of a whole community." He links 5:14
back to the Isaiah quotation at 4:16: the light Jesus brings "is also provided by his disciples,"
who share his ministry (cf. Isa 42:6; 49:6). His summary of 5:11-16 is that disciples must be both
different *and* engaged: "Neither the indistinguishably assimilated nor the inaccessible hermit
will fulfill the mandate of these challenging verses." The corollary he draws is that "the 'job
description' of a disciple is not fulfilled by private personal holiness, but includes the witness
of public exposure." He suggests that "the prophets who came before you" "perhaps suggests that
Jesus' disciples are now the prophetic voice on earth" (cf. 10:41; 23:34), making persecution "a
badge of honor."

Against this corporate emphasis France sets the private register of 6:1-18. He insists Jesus
assumes disciples will perform the three duties — the quarrel "is not with the doing of them ...
but with the manner and the motive"; of fasting he notes "it is assumed that disciples will fast;
the issue is not whether to do it but how," an assumption he says causes surprise in a culture
giving fasting little serious attention. He reads the plural-to-singular shift in vv. 3, 6, 17 as
underlining that these duties are between the disciple and God rather than undertaken
corporately, and holds almsgiving to lack even the residual corporateness of prayer and fasting:
"It is entirely a matter of private decision." Of the [[lords-prayer|Lord's Prayer]] he observes
that to pray such a prayer "is, of course, to be committed oneself to honor God's name, accept
his kingship, and do his will," and he reads the petition about testing as concerning "the
testing experiences which are the normal lot of disciples who try to live according to the
principles of the kingdom of God." He notes that "Unlike many modern Christians, Matthew is not
coy about the 'reward,'" while the parable of 20:1-15 warns against a crude quid-pro-quo
conception. On 7:24-27 his verdict is that those who have heard Jesus' teaching "receive no
benefit from it unless they also put it into practice," and on 7:21-23 he redefines the criterion:
"'Doing the will of my Father in heaven' is not a merely ethical category; that will also
includes to know and be known by Jesus the 'Lord.'" His conclusion there: "There is no substitute
for personal discipleship." On the difficult applications of the [[antitheses|antitheses]] he
declines to legislate: "It is for each disciple to work out for themselves how this principle can
most responsibly be applied ... in the different personal and social circumstances in which we
find ourselves."

## Faith and "Little Faith"

France treats *pistis* in Matthew as, apart from 23:23, always denoting practical faith expecting
a miracle rather than "the Pauline sense of a soteriological commitment." He accordingly defines
worry (*merimna*) as "the antithesis of the practical trust in God which is the essential meaning
of faith (*pistis*) in this gospel," and glosses *oligopistoi* via 17:20 as faith less than a
mustard seed — "in effect no faith at all," so that however much a person may believe
intellectually, "they are for practical purposes 'faithless.'" He draws a lexical distinction
across the gospel: *apistia* ("unbelief") is used only of those who reject Jesus, while disciples
receive the less absolute *oligopistia*.

On the storm (8:23-27) France reports that modern interpretation, inspired by G. Bornkamm's
redaction-critical study, generally agrees Matthew added a symbolic dimension — the boat
experience as "a kerygmatic paradigm of the danger and glory of discipleship." He supports the
symbolic reading from Matthew's "apparently quite unnecessary" description of Jesus embarking
first with the disciples following, which differs sharply from Mark 4:36, and from the reordering
by which the rebuke precedes the calming: "Jesus deals with the disciples before he deals with
the storm." He guards against reduction, however: recognizing the paradigmatic function is "not
... to devalue the sheer miraculousness of the event itself," and Matthew "is quite capable of
maintaining multiple levels of meaning at the same time." He reads the rebuke via 6:30 —
cowardice shows the disciples "do not take God's fatherly care seriously, which is the essence of
practical faith."

On Peter walking on the water France reads the episode as "an illustration of the vulnerability
of the disciple who allows doubt, the natural human perspective, to displace the faith which
relies on the supernatural power of God," and defines the verb "doubt" (recurring only at 28:17)
as practical hesitation or being in two minds rather than theological uncertainty. He connects
the later charge of little faith to 6:30, where the same epithet describes those who worry about
food and clothing, noting the disciples have by then had tangible proof twice over.

## Understanding: the Parables Discourse

France identifies "understanding" as the controlling term of the sower's interpretation (13:19,
23), taken up from vv. 13-15 and recurring at 13:51; 15:10; 16:12; 17:13, and insists that the
crop-bearing shows understanding is not purely intellectual grasp but "the lifestyle commitment
which the 'message of the kingdom of heaven' demands." He holds the "you"/"them" contrast refers
first to disciples and crowds but states a wider principle of two classes of people, as at
11:25-27 — while stressing as his most decisive qualifier that "the two categories are not
hard-and-fast; the boundary can be crossed," since the disciples themselves are former outsiders
who responded. He reads 13:16-17 as a beatitude with a double contrast: with the crowds described
by Isa 6:9-10, and with prophets and righteous people of the past who were in no way hardened
"but who lived too soon."

Within the sower's interpretation France reads the rootless soil as enthusiasm "based on external
stimulus, not on inner conviction," takes "stumble" here in its drastic sense of deflection from
the path of God's will, and glosses the scorching sun: "The scorching sun represents the fact
that following Jesus will not always be fun." He connects the thorns to the rich young man
(19:16-22) over against disciples who "left everything" (19:27) and to the priorities of 6:19-34,
noting that wealth "promises a security which it cannot deliver." He reads the three yields as
indicating that genuine disciples differ in productivity by gifts and circumstances, and warns
expressly that the variation is in productivity, "not in their heavenly reward," since 20:1-15
blocks that extrapolation. He judges the traditional devotional use of the parable —
self-examination of one's own receptiveness — "probably justified" by the careful spelling out of
the hazards, noting that unreceptiveness and preoccupation are "problems not exclusively
experienced by those outside the group." Of the treasure and pearl he says they are "about
enthusiastic and wholehearted commitment to the kingdom of heaven," with costly renunciation as a
secondary theme, and adds the sharp qualification that the finder acts from "pure self-interest,
as is that of the person who opts for the kingdom of heaven" — the sacrifice done out of delight
rather than obligation. He judges soberly that "human nature being what it is, those who rise to
the challenge are likely to be few."

## Persecution and the Cross (ch. 10)

France reads the mission discourse as one in which the basic mission-charge element is superseded
by more general reflections on the experience of Jesus' disciples in a hostile world, so that "a
prominent subtext is the hostile response which that mission is to meet, so that this also becomes
a discourse on persecution." He assigns the discourse a pastoral function for Matthew's readers:
"to be less naive in their expectations, to strengthen them to continue as heralds of God's
kingship." He highlights that persecution arises "not out of sociological factors but 'because of
me'" (vv. 18, 22), carrying christological overtones, and pointedly notes that the
sheep-among-wolves image "sits uncomfortably alongside much of the subsequent history of the
church, as a power structure and itself often the agent of persecution."

France's strong reading of 10:34 is that "These are not just some unfortunate side-effects of a
basically acceptable mission. The very purpose of Jesus' coming is 'not peace but a sword.'" He
observes that the first mention of the cross in Matthew "comes on the scene, startlingly, not only
as his eventual fate, but as theirs. To follow Jesus is to embrace martyrdom," and formulates the
demand: "To agree to follow Jesus is to sign away all rights to a quiet life of
self-determination." He protests the domestication of the image — "Popular usage has sanitized
the language of having 'a cross to bear' so that its challenge has evaporated" — while balancing
that not every disciple will be a martyr, though all must accept the possibility. He draws a
narrative irony: "when the opportunity eventually comes for one of the disciples to follow Jesus
carrying his cross for him, they will not be there to take it up, and a stranger will fill the
unwelcome role (27:32)." On losing and finding life he argues the saying is "to be taken literally
in the first instance," given the cross of v. 38 and the execution of v. 21, then broadened by a
play on two senses of *psychē*; he treats "because of me" as load-bearing, since "the promise of
true life is attached not to dying as such, but to dying as a loyal disciple." He closes the
section with a borrowed aphorism: "Discipleship (to borrow the famous dictum of Bill Shankly about
football) is not a matter of life and death—it is much more serious than that."

Two points in this stretch France leaves unresolved. On the sparrow saying he states the limit of
the promise: it "does not, of course, promise immunity from death or suffering for God's people,
only the knowledge that it does not happen 'without your Father.'" And on the mission charge
generally he names a problem he cannot resolve from the text: "There are principles here as well
as specific instructions; the problem for the interpreter is to know which is which ... and on
this problem Matthew gives us no explicit help."

## The Portrait of the Disciples

On France's reading Matthew's disciples are neither idealized nor reduced to foils. He reads the
disciples' "Yes" at 13:51 without irony — "Matthew's disciples are far from perfect, but they are
now firmly on the road to understanding, and Jesus' response apparently accepts their claim
without irony" — and reserves the harsher vocabulary of unbelief for those who reject Jesus. At
the same time he records their failures without softening: the self-centered annoyance of "Send
her away" in the Canaanite woman episode, which he suggests probably means "Do what she wants, so
that she will go away"; their failure at 15:33 to volunteer even the small supply they have, so
that Jesus must ask directly; and their slowness generally. On that slowness he offers two
options without deciding between them — a literary motif Matthew included without weighing how it
reflects on them, or the possibility that they assume feeding Gentiles is outside Jesus' domain:
"If so, they have not yet grasped the significance of vv. 27-28 but have remained stuck at v. 24."
He traces the understanding arc from 13:11-17 through 13:19, 23, 51-52 and 15:16 to 16:12, where
understanding dawns and prepares the ground for Caesarea Philippi, while stressing how partial
that understanding remains right up to Gethsemane: "Perception and dullness will continue to
jostle in Matthew's portrayal of the disciples." He quotes Davies and Allison's comment on the
sea-walking episode: "The disciples are beginning to catch up with the readers of the gospel."

France also notes a Matthean subtlety in the disciples' commission: they "proclaim," but Matthew
never uses "teach" of them until after Jesus, "the one teacher" (23:8, 10), is no longer present
(28:20). On 13:52 he holds this is the only parable in the chapter addressed specifically to the
disciples, arguing from the verb "understand" and from "made a disciple," and canvasses as one
option that Jesus designates his disciples "a new 'alternative' scribal school, trained not in the
rabbinic schools but by his own instruction," with 23:34 the clearest hint.

## The Disciple Community as Family

France develops a familial ecclesiology from 12:46-50: disciples who do the Father's will enter a
new relationship with God as their heavenly Father, becoming brothers and sisters to one another,
and "this extended family under the one heavenly Father (23:8-9) includes Jesus himself, who
acknowledges the same Father." He notes that "does the will of my Father who is in heaven"
exactly repeats 7:21 and recurs at 21:31, pointing to the "greater righteousness" of 5:20. He
argues the "disciples" of v. 49 are primarily the Twelve but that Matthew's usage allows a wider
group — "the inclusion of 'sister' in v. 50 demands it" — and draws the historical inference that
there were women among Jesus' disciples at this stage, even if not among the Twelve, though they
remain otherwise invisible until 27:55 (cf. 20:20). He calls "and sister" "a rare case of
gender-inclusive language in the gospel accounts of Jesus' sayings" (cf. 19:29), the more
significant since only brothers were present. Elsewhere he holds "brother" in 7:3-5 probably means
a fellow disciple, "and not necessarily a male one," and reads 7:1-5 as addressing unfairly
critical attitudes combined with naive lack of self-criticism, which "threaten to disrupt a
close-knit community such as that of Jesus' first disciples."

France also reads the parable of the returning spirit (12:43-45) as bearing on discipleship: a
person liberated from possession remains vulnerable if they remain "vacant," and "We can only
assume in the light of Jesus' teaching elsewhere that the void is to be filled by discipleship"
and specifically by the Spirit of God. His moral: "Half-hearted repentance without a new
commitment will not last."

## Discipleship in Mark (France, NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the fidelity reingest of [[france-mark-nigtc|France's NIGTC Mark]]. All
interpretive claims are [[rt-france|France]]'s. Note the contrast with his Matthew reading above:
in [[mark|Mark]], France holds, οἱ μαθηταί and οἱ δώδεκα are functionally equivalent (with 4:34
conceded as a looser use), whereas Matthew uses* mathētēs *more widely.*

**The story of the "Jesus circle."** France frames Mark's message under the two broad areas of
christology and discipleship, and argues Mark's story is "the story of the 'Jesus circle'": "it
is through this flawed and vulnerable group of people that God's kingship will be established."
Following M. Hengel, he reads the call of the fishermen (1:16-20) on the "charismatic leader"
model of [[elijah|Elijah]] calling Elisha rather than a rabbi–talmid relation — "Rabbis did not
call their followers; rather, the pupil adopted the teacher" — the peremptory summons marking
Jesus "as a prophet rather than a rabbi," and the "fishers of men" metaphor as rescuing people
*from* judgment despite its judgment-laden OT background. The number twelve "suggests an
ideology of the restoration of Israel"; ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ (3:14) makes personal involvement
with the master "the essential prerequisite for the active ministry which follows." The true
family of 3:31-35 — "those who do the will of God" — is a tableau of insiders and outsiders, its
"doing the will of God" meaning response to the kingdom proclamation of 1:14-15.

**Both exemplary and negative models.** France argues Mark sets the first disciples before his
readers as exemplary models (leaving all, privileged insight, shared mission) and — more often —
negative ones (fear, ambition, failure, desertion), Peter "a tantalising mixture of special
insight (8:29) and extraordinary obtuseness." He defends Mark's realism as written "so that
history might not be allowed to repeat itself" among the disciples' successors. The disciples'
obtuseness theme opens at 4:40 (πίστις in Mark being "practical confidence in supernatural
power, the correlative to miracles") and turns ominous at 6:52, where hardened-heart / Isaiah
6:10 language previously reserved for opponents (3:5) and outsiders (4:12) is applied to the
disciples themselves — "remarkably strong language" — recurring at 8:17-18, though the saving
οὔπω ("not yet," 8:17, 21) implies their incomprehension, unlike the outsiders', is temporary
and curable, symbolised by the two-stage Bethsaida healing. The Gentile Syrophoenician woman's
grasp of the bread metaphor (7:28) pointedly contrasts with the disciples' failure to understand
"about bread."

**Act Two as re-education.** France reads 8:22–10:52, framed by two blind-man healings widely
taken as symbolic of the disciples' "blindness," as a "re-education of the disciples to the
revolutionary viewpoint of the kingdom of God": each passion prediction (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34)
is immediately followed by a disciple failure and remedial teaching, and ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ carries
overtones of the early Christian "Way," making the journey section "a study of discipleship."
Taking up one's cross (8:34) "is not to be domesticated into an exhortation merely to endure
hardship patiently" — it evokes the literal death march — and self-denial (quoting Best) is "not
the denial of something to the self, but the denial of the self itself ... on a different level
altogether from giving up chocolates for Lent." The λύτρον of 10:45 is Jesus' unique mission,
but the spirit of service and self-sacrifice is for all disciples; disciples "are to be a
church, not a sect" (9:38-41); renunciation is "pragmatically sitting light to possessions and
family ἕνεκεν τοῦ εὐαγγελίου," not monastic poverty. Bartimaeus (10:46-52), following Jesus ἐν
τῇ ὁδῷ with sight restored, "represents all those who have found enlightenment and follow the
Master." Of the porter parable (13:34-37): "The Christian disciple, it seems, is never off
duty."

**Failure and restoration.** France maps the prediction–fulfilment structure of 14:26-31
(betrayal, desertion, denial each predicted and fulfilled) as emphasising both disciple failure
and Jesus' control of events; the flight of 14:50 is "a factual statement rather than one of
blame." The trial/denial intercalation is "a study in witnessing under pressure, in how to do it
and how not to do it." Peter's threefold denial — culminating, France argues, in voluntarily
*cursing Jesus* (14:71) — is "not merely weakness but apostasy," yet his weeping marks "failure
under pressure rather than a deliberate change of allegiance," and the "and Peter" of 16:7
implies "an assurance of forgiveness and restoration, the more impressive for being left
unsaid." France also notes that only at 15:40-41 does Mark reveal that Jesus' entourage had all
along included a substantial group of women followers from Galilee — ἀκολουθέω and διακονέω
being "the language of discipleship" — a "remarkable shift" from the apparently all-male
movement of the narrative so far.
