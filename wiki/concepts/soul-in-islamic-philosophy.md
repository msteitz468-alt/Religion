# Soul in Islamic Philosophy (*Nafs*)

*Nafs* (Arabic: نفس) is the Arabic term for soul, used across Islamic theological (*kalām*) and philosophical (*falsafa*) traditions to denote the principle of life, rationality, and personal identity. The Avicennan tradition treats the rational soul (*al-nafs al-nāṭiqa*) as an immaterial, self-subsisting substance distinct from the body — not imprinted in it as animal souls are — capable of independent intellectual activity and, in the philosophers' view, necessarily immortal. [[al-ghazali]]'s *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (Discussions 18–19) does not straightforwardly deny this picture but systematically dismantles the *proofs* the [[falsafa|philosophers]] claim to have for it, arguing they yield probable opinion at best, not demonstrative certainty.

---

## The Avicennan Doctrine of the Rational Soul

Discussion 18 of the *Tahafut* opens by expounding the philosophers' psychology before attacking it. The animal soul has motive and apprehending faculties; the apprehending faculties divide into external (the five senses) and internal (the imaginative/common sense, the estimative faculty, the cogitative/combinative faculty, and the retentive faculties of form and meaning). These are explicitly located in the concavities of the brain and are imprinted in bodily organs.

The rational human soul (*al-nafs al-nāṭiqa*, "the discursive") is categorically different. It has two faculties — the theoretical (cognitive) and the practical:

> "This is a faculty whose function is to apprehend the true natures of the intelligibles stripped from matter, place, and [spatial] direction. These are the universal propositions which the speculative theologians call 'states' at one time [and] 'aspects' at another, and which the philosophers call 'abstract universals'." (Discussion 18, §9)

The rational soul occupies a mediating position — its theoretical faculty relates it to the angels, from whom it receives the sciences; its practical faculty governs the body below. It is **neither body nor imprinted in the body**: "neither connected with nor disconnected from the body, just as God is neither outside nor inside the world" (Discussion 18, title rubric). This means the soul's mode of relation to the body is categorically unlike that of colour to a coloured thing, of an accident to a substance, or of animal faculties to brain tissue.

Ghazali notes that the philosophers concede that animal souls, the imaginative faculty, and the bodily faculties are imprinted in the body and perish with it: "the bestial souls and bodily faculties" (Discussion 19, §3). The claim to immateriality and immortality is reserved for the *rational* soul alone.

## Discussion 18: Cannot the Soul's Immateriality Be Demonstrated?

Ghazali presents and rebuts ten philosopher's proofs for the soul's incorporeality as a self-subsisting substance. His governing strategy in each case is not to assert that the soul *is* material, but to show that the proof is either inconclusive or generates internal contradictions for the philosophers themselves.

### The indivisibility of knowledge (First proof, §§13–23)
The philosophers argue: intellectual knowledge is indivisible (one cannot have half a concept); whatever inheres in a divisible body must itself be divisible; therefore the receptacle of knowledge is not a body. Ghazali's main counter (Second standpoint, §17): the philosophers' own account of the ewe's estimative faculty — a *bodily* faculty — apprehending the wolf's enmity (itself indivisible, having no measurable parts) generates the same paradox. Either enmity is divisible (and the proof fails) or something indivisible can inhere in a divisible body. The philosophers face a contradiction within their own system: "one of the two things has been contradicted — either what they have said about the rational soul or what they have said about the estimative faculty" (§22). Conclusion: the proof "may well bequest opinion, it does not yield reliable certitude" (§46).

### The second proof (§§24–28): replacing "imprinting" with "relation" does not help
The philosophers restate the argument substituting "relation" for "imprinting." Ghazali reapplies the same counter: the same relational structure obtains for the ewe's apprehension of enmity, which is bodily. The reformulation does not resolve the difficulty.

### The third and fourth proofs (§§31–37): self-attribution and opposition of contraries
The third proof: "the state of being a knower is an attribute belonging to [the human] as a whole, without relating to a specific place" — attributed to the whole body, therefore the soul cannot be in a part. Ghazali: this is "madness" (*junūn*); one says "he is in Baghdad" even though he occupies only part of it (§32). The fourth proof: if knowledge and ignorance had different parts of the body as their receptacles, a person could be both knowing and ignorant of the same thing simultaneously. Ghazali counters with desire and repulsion — these contraries, though both bodily, do not produce the absurdity because the multiple faculties share one connecting link (§37).

### Proofs five and six (§§38–48): self-apprehension and apprehension of the organ
The fifth proof: bodily senses cannot apprehend themselves (sight cannot see sight); if mind were bodily it could not apprehend itself; but mind does apprehend itself; therefore mind is not bodily. Ghazali: (a) it is not improbable that sight could be related to itself, though habit runs contrary; (b) more strongly, bodily senses differ from one another in many modes — why could not mind be a bodily sense that happens to differ from the others in being self-reflexive, "as the crocodile stands in relation to the rest of the animals" in moving its upper jaw (§48)? The sixth proof (apprehending the organ): a faculty imprinted in a body cannot apprehend the body that is its receptacle; but mind apprehends brain and heart; therefore mind is not imprinted there. Same answer: bodily senses need not all obey the same rule.

### Proofs seven and eight (§§54–64): fatigue and strengthening with age
The seventh proof: bodily senses fatigue with prolonged use or strong stimuli; intellectual apprehension does not — it strengthens with contemplation of clear truths. Ghazali: it is not improbable that different bodies have different responses to movement; "the judgment that holds for some things need not necessarily hold for all" (§58). If the intellect's fatigue arises when it draws on the imaginative faculty, this is consistent with its operating through a bodily organ of a different kind. The eighth proof: sensory faculties weaken after forty; the rational faculty often strengthens. Ghazali: there are many causes of increase and decrease across the lifespan, and the differential timing of faculties (intellect matures later, hence ages later) is consistent with bodily dependence (§§63–64). "Nothing of this yields certainty" (§64).

### Proofs nine and ten (§§65–78): personal identity through bodily change and the universal
The ninth proof: bodily parts dissolve and are replaced through nourishment; yet the person remains the same, retaining knowledge from boyhood; therefore the self has an existence other than the body. Ghazali: (a) this applies equally to beasts and trees without proving their incorporeality; (b) the retention of imaginal forms undermines the argument — imaginative memory is explicitly bodily and persists; (c) some original parts of the sperm likely remain throughout life (the "pound of water" thought experiment), grounding numerical identity through what remains (§§68–70).

The tenth proof (§§71–78): the mind apprehends universal, immaterial concepts (abstract man, abstract triangle) stripped of matter, position, and measure; what apprehends the immaterial must itself lack position. Ghazali's denial: the universal is not a truly immaterial entity subsisting in the mind — it is the form of the first individual perceived, which has the same relation to all similar individuals encountered thereafter. "This does not allow for the affirmation of a universal that has no position at all" (§77). The mind's judgment that immaterial things exist (e.g., God, the Creator) does not prove that the mind's own form is immaterial (§78).

**Ghazali's overall conclusion for Discussion 18**: "what is denied is its being known with certainty, by a knowledge in which error is not allowed and which doubt does not frequent" (§23). The proofs are "among those things that strengthen belief, making it more likely" but none achieves demonstration. He notably does not say the soul is material — the Ash'arite theological tradition he inhabits (*kalām*) is in fact open to the soul's immateriality — but insists this is a matter for the religious law to convey, not unaided reason to demonstrate.

## Discussion 19: Cannot the Soul's Immortality Be Demonstrated?

Discussion 19 takes up the philosophers' second group of proofs — that once it exists, the rational soul *cannot be annihilated*.

### First proof for immortality (§§2–16)
The philosophers argue that annihilation could come about through only three causes: (a) death of the body; (b) occurrence of a contrary; (c) God's power. They deny each: (a) the body is a tool, not a receptacle; corruption of the tool need not corrupt the user; (b) substances have no contraries — only accidents can be annihilated by contraries, since contraries succeed one another in one receptacle; the soul, not being in a receptacle, has no contrary; (c) nonexistence is not a thing, so its being produced by power is unintelligible (an argument already deployed against the possibility of the world's future end).

Ghazali's four objections:
1. The proof presupposes that the soul does not inhere in the body — but Discussion 18 shows this has not been demonstrated (§7).
2. Even granting the soul is not imprinted in a body, [[avicenna]] himself argues that the soul *comes into existence with* the body (§8), based on his refutation of the pre-existence of souls (if pre-existent souls were one, Zayd's cognitions would be known to ʿAmr; if multiple, by what are they individuated before bodies exist? — §9). This creation-with-the-body implies a special relation of this soul to this body. If that relation is what specifies the connection, it may equally be what is required for the soul's continued existence — "when the relation ceases, the soul ceases [to exist]" (§15), "never returning thereafter except through its being returned by God ... by way of resurrection" (§10).
3. Annihilation by God's power is not impossible — Ghazali has already argued this in the discussion of the world's post-eternity (§16).
4. The philosophers' claim that annihilation can come about through only three modes is not demonstrated. "Restricting the ways in this to three alternatives is not known through demonstration" (§17).

### Second proof for immortality (§§18–23)
The philosophers' main philosophical proof: any substance not in a receptacle cannot be annihilated, because annihilation requires that the potentiality for non-existence precede non-existence, which in turn requires a *bearer* of that potentiality; but a simple, uncomposed substance has no such bearer (no matter in which the potentiality could inhere); hence, for a simple substance without receptacle, annihilation is strictly impossible. The soul is such a substance.

Ghazali's reply: this is the same argument the philosophers make about the impossibility of annihilating matter and the elements. The root confusion is positing possibility (*imkān*) as a characteristic that requires a receptacle in which to subsist. Ghazali has already refuted this in the context of the world's pre-eternity and post-eternity: "There is, thus, no difference between the thing discussed being a material substance or a psychological substance" (§23). The second proof for immortality fails for the same reason their pre-eternity of matter argument fails.

## Hermeneutical Significance

Ghazali's strategy in Discussions 18–19 is consistently **epistemological**, not ontological. He does not claim to *know* that the soul is mortal or material. His target is the philosophers' claim to demonstrative certainty (*burhān*) on these matters. The correct position, he implies, is that the soul's immateriality and immortality are *conveyed* by the religious law (as he indicates in Discussion 18, §12: "we may well show in detailing the explanation of the resurrection and the afterlife that the law gives credence to it") and may be believed on that basis, but cannot be established by unaided philosophical reason.

This has direct consequences for the zahir/batin question raised in Discussion 20: the philosophers, unable to demonstrate their positions, nevertheless use them as the foundation for allegorizing (*bāṭin* readings of) Quranic eschatological texts. Ghazali's dismantling of their proofs in 18–19 undermines the epistemic authority that would be needed to override the *zahir* of scripture in Discussion 20.

## Averroes's Reply (Tahāfut al-Tahāfut, Natural Sciences Disc. 2–3)

Against Ghazālī's epistemological challenge, **[[averroes|Averroes]]** largely **defends the
demonstrability** of the soul's immateriality. He endorses the proof that "every substratum which is
impressed by the inherence of the form in it… is corporeal," whereas "the receptacle of the
intelligibles is not impressed by the intelligibles" — so it is no body; and he resolves Ghazālī's
sheep-and-wolf counterexample by distinguishing two kinds of bodily divisibility (Ghazālī, he says,
"committed a sophism" by sliding between them). On **immortality** he meets the Avicennan
individuation problem with a tentative solution — surviving souls subsist in a "subtle heavenly
warmth" as their spiritual matter — and treats the **material intellect** as "absolutely immaterial"
(praising Anaxagoras, via Aristotle, for making intellect the prime mover). For the masses he offers
the **sleep/death analogy** as "a proof which all can understand." On whether immortality entails
*bodily* resurrection, see [[bodily-resurrection]].
