# History-Centrism

A structural feature of the Abrahamic religions identified and analyzed by [[rajiv-malhotra]] in [[being-different-malhotra]]: the dependence on **specific, unrepeatable historical events as the sole authorized channel through which God has revealed ultimate truth to humanity**.

## The Structural Claim

In Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, the core claims to religious authority rest on historical events that cannot be repeated or verified through present experience alone:
- **Judaism**: The Exodus; the giving of the Torah at Sinai; the covenant at Horeb; the specific historical line of the patriarchs and prophets
- **Christianity**: The Incarnation, death, and Resurrection of Jesus Christ — understood as a once-for-all event ("ephapax" — *hapax* in the New Testament; see [[covenant]] and [[lamb-of-god]])
- **Islam**: The specific revelation to Muhammad through Gabriel; the Qur'an as the final, authorically fixed word of God; the historical authenticity of the chain of transmission (*isnad*)

Malhotra argues that this structure makes the *historicity* of the founding events a non-negotiable doctrinal prerequisite: if the Exodus did not happen, the authority of the Torah is undermined; if the Resurrection did not happen, Christian soteriology collapses (Paul: "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile" — 1 Cor 15:17); if the Qur'an is not the verbatim word of God transmitted through Muhammad, Islamic law loses its foundation.

## The Dharmic Contrast

In dharmic traditions, religious authority is grounded in **repeatable present-tense experience** — the methodology of *adhyatma-vidya* (inner science; see [[adhyatma-vidya]]). The rishis discovered the Vedas through direct perception (*sruti* — "that which is heard"), and this perception is in principle accessible to any qualified practitioner in any age and place. Enlightenment (*moksha*, *nirvana*, *liberation*) is a present-tense attainment; it does not depend on what happened to a specific historical person at a specific historical moment.

Consequences:
- If the historical Rama or Krishna were shown to be legendary, the soteriological and ritual structures grounded in them would not necessarily collapse — because authority rests on the *teaching's* truth, not the *event's* occurrence
- Dharmic traditions are therefore less threatened by historical-critical scholarship than Abrahamic traditions
- The "ahistorical" quality that Western scholars have often labeled as Hindu deficiency (failure to produce "proper" historiography) is, on Malhotra's reading, a structural feature, not a failure

## Big Bang Counterargument (Addressed in the Text)

Malhotra addresses the objection that modern physics is also history-centric (the Big Bang as a unique unrepeatable event). His response: the Big Bang is a *conclusion* derived from physical laws and verifiable evidence; it is an effect, not a cause or premise. Abrahamic history-centrism is the *premise* — the founding assumption that must be believed before the religious system works. Physics does not require the Big Bang to be believed on faith before experiments can be conducted.

## Secular Heirs of History-Centrism

Malhotra extends the analysis to secular modernity: Hegel's philosophy of history (the West as the bearer of the World Spirit toward its culmination), Marxism (the proletariat as the messianic agent; the Revolution as the secular End Times), Darwinian progress narratives, and liberal "end of history" arguments (Fukuyama) all inherit the Abrahamic pattern of linear, goal-directed history with a privileged agent moving toward a final state. This makes Western secular universalism a disguised continuation of Abrahamic history-centrism.

## Limitations and Counterexamples

- Bhakti hagiography (Ramanandi and Dasnami lineages) does develop a strong sense of historical lineage — acknowledged by Malhotra via William Pinch's counter-example. His response: lineage-history is not the same as history-centrism; it does not claim a unique divine intervention for all humanity through that lineage.
- Sikhism has a defined historical lineage of ten gurus but is explicitly non-history-centric: it does not claim the gurus as sole intermediaries for all humanity's liberation, and it respects the legitimacy of other traditions' exemplars.
