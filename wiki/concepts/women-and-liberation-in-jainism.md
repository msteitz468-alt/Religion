## Definition

The question of whether women can attain [[moksha|liberation]] (*moksa*) in their female bodies is the **most extensively debated soteriological dispute** between the [[digambara|Digambara]] and [[svetambara|Svetambara]] sects of Jainism. The dispute is inextricable from the question of ascetic nudity: since the Digambaras hold that nudity is a necessary condition for liberation, and since female nudity is universally agreed to be socially impossible, the Digambaras conclude that women cannot attain liberation in female form. The Svetambaras, who hold that robes are a legitimate and sufficient form of ascetic practice, deny this inference.

Dundas notes that this question "will strike a contemporary audience as being of particular interest" and that female religiosity in Jain tradition had been "inadequately treated" until recently (lines 426–435).

## The Scale of Female Participation

The significance of women in Jainism's origins is attested in the [[kalpasutra|Kalpasuta]]: at [[mahavira|Mahavira]]'s death, his *tirtha* contained **36,000 nuns** and only 14,000 monks, and **318,000 laywomen** compared to 159,000 laymen. The text further states that during Mahavira's lifetime **1,400 women** (contrasted with 700 men) achieved salvation (lines 446–453). These numbers have "suspiciously symmetrical" ratios, and Dundas notes no conclusions can be drawn from them about their historical accuracy, but they demonstrate that "spiritually heroic Jain women are a frequent theme in the Universal History and in the literature of pious exemplification" (lines 455–457).

## Svetambara Position: Women Can Attain Liberation

**Scriptural basis:** The [[jain-agamas|Svetambara scriptures]] say nothing against female liberation and in fact contain "frequent references both in the scriptures and in the Universal History to women renouncing and advancing to the end of the spiritual path" (lines 542–546).

**The first liberator of this world age is a woman:** According to the Svetambaras, **Marudevi**, the mother of the first fordmaker Rsabha, "has the distinction of being the first person of this world age to achieve liberation" (lines 460–462).

**The nineteenth fordmaker Malli was female:** The Svetambaras claim that the nineteenth [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] Malli was a woman — born female as a karmic consequence of having secretly exceeded the limit on austerities agreed upon with fellow kings in a previous male birth, thus generating negative karma through deception. "Only one possible example of an image of Malli with breasts and a long braid of hair has so far been found, at Lucknow and dating from about the ninth century CE, and at no Svetambara sacred site is this fordmaker depicted as a woman" (lines 470–474). The Digambaras reject this claim outright and worship the nineteenth fordmaker as the male **Mallinatha** (line 475).

**Sakatayana's argument (ninth century, representing the Yapaniya-Svetambara position):** Gender is irrelevant to liberation. Key points (*SNP*):
- The scriptures provide no reason to deny female liberation and many examples of women achieving it.
- There is no logical reason to deny it.
- Women cannot fall to the lowest hell — but this does not preclude their rising to the "pure, exalted state at the top of the universe where Jainism locates the world of liberated souls" (lines 547–550).
- "Gender has no relevance for the religious life, just as it has none for liberated souls" (lines 550–552).
- Only pregnant women are specifically forbidden from becoming nuns (*SNP* 9).
- Nudity "cannot play any serious role in the development of the religious life, for otherwise deliverance could be attained simply by removing one's clothes" (lines 553–556); wearing robes is an adjunct to spirituality, like other ascetic practices.
- "There are many ways to spiritual deliverance, just as there are many cures for a disease" (lines 559–560).
- Jain legend demonstrates females are no more prone to "delusion and immorality than men" (lines 562–563); women are "equal members of humanity (*manusyagati*) with men and thus capable of enlightenment" (lines 563–565).

## Digambara Position: Women Cannot Attain Liberation in Female Bodies

**Core logic:** Since (a) ascetic nudity is required for liberation, and (b) female nudity is socially impossible — "if a woman wandering around naked would inevitably be raped, then that must surely prove the point about the unsuitability of women for the ascetic life" (Prabhacandra, lines 498–502) — women cannot achieve enlightenment in female form. "There cannot possibly be two different ascetic paths, nakedness and the wearing of clothes, which would bring about one result, enlightenment" (lines 504–507). Women must be reborn as men.

**Emergence of the position:** The early Svetambara literature shows "no sign that the possibility of female liberation was in any way controversial" (lines 476–478). Importantly, "important early Digambara texts, which can be located in about the third or fourth centuries CE, state that both monks and nuns can achieve deliverance" (lines 483–485). The fully worked out Digambara denial emerged only from about the fifth century CE, "as a consequence of the Digambara insistence, confirmed from about the fifth century CE or so as a result of the final hardening of sectarian boundaries, that ascetic nudity was an essential component of the path to liberation" (lines 479–482). A full Digambara defense appears in the eighth century.

**Prabhacandra's argument (eleventh century; *NKC* pp. 865–70 — the "fullest Digambara discussion available"):**

1. **Structural impossibility of female nudity:** A naked woman would inevitably be raped; this proves women cannot follow the ascetic path (lines 498–504).
2. **Inability to achieve genuine moral intensity:** Women cannot perform evil or good to the same extent as men. Corollary (partially positive): women can never be reborn in the seventh and bottommost hell. Corollary (negative): women can never follow Jain prescriptions with "the requisite degree of seriousness to bring about enlightenment" (lines 510–519). Women must be reborn as males to reach the goal.
3. **Rebirth as female implies past evil:** "For a soul to be reborn in female form in the first place suggests some sort of evil performed in the past" (lines 518–520).
4. **Social inferiority transfers to the ascetic realm:** "The values of the transient, social world, in which women are deemed to be inferior to men through their innate propensity to delusion and immorality, persist in the world of ascetic renunciation." Women's inability to exercise authority in society "must therefore preclude their ability to do so in [the ascetic realm]" (lines 521–527).
5. **Physical impurity (later argument):** A later Digambara claimed women "by their very physiology generate and destroy life-forms within their sexual organs and other orifices of their body, thus repeatedly infringing non-violence." Dundas notes this argument "was not a significant component of the debate" (lines 528–532).
6. **Todarmal (eighteenth century)** stated that "neither people of low social status nor women can attain enlightenment," linking the denial of female liberation to caste-based notions of physical purity (lines 532–536).

## The Charge of Misogyny

Dundas observes that "there is unquestionably a strong vein of misogyny which runs through much Jain literature, both Svetambara and Digambara" (lines 566–568). The literature contains a "welter of denunciation of women and their evil desire to lure men away from the path." The only significant scriptural warning to women about male sexuality is the story of the nun Rajimati, previously the intended wife of the fordmaker Nemi, who successfully resisted the seduction attempt of Nemi's monk-brother (lines 568–577).

The standard regulation in Svetambara monastic law that "a nun, no matter how long she has been in the order, must pay homage to even a newly initiated monk" is cited as "indicative of what has remained a patriarchal attitude within the religion" (lines 575–577).

## Contemporary Practice and Attitudes

Despite the theoretical Digambara denial of female liberation, "Jains of both sects today in fact subscribe to a view of women as more capable of matching the requirements of ascetic behaviour because of their innate capacity to be chaste" (lines 579–582). Contemporary Jains locate "a greater propensity to fall prey to sexual incontinence amongst males than women" (lines 584–585). The chaste Jain woman (*sati*), lay or ascetic, "and her ability to control the passions and thus exemplify the moral values of the path provide an inspirational model of religiosity for both sexes" (lines 586–590).

**Digambara nuns today:** Some 350 Digambara nuns currently exist, sometimes of great learning and occasionally charged with teaching monks. They accept the Digambara teaching that "because of their female bodies and wearing of clothes their ability to advance seriously towards enlightenment is contingent on being reborn as a man" (lines 592–596).

**Svetambara nuns today:** Svetambara nuns of "some charisma and frequently high education" preach to large audiences. However, "there is nonetheless a general feeling among the laity that interaction with a monk is more meritorious than with a nun" (lines 597–600). A survey of one hundred Terapanthi sect nuns found over seventy wanting some sort of change in their circumstances (lines 604–606).
