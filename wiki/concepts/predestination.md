# Predestination, Providence, and Foreknowledge (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats predestination as a sub-topic of divine providence and divine
knowledge, at [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q22 (Providence), Q23 (Predestination),
and Q14, A13 (knowledge of future contingents). His account became a principal reference point for
the later Catholic–Protestant and intra-Catholic (Báñez–Molina) disputes over grace and election.

## Providence (Q22)

Providence is "the type of the order of things towards their end," existing in the divine intellect
(Q22, A1). It extends to **all things individually** — including contingent and free acts, chance
events, and even defects — because God is the universal cause of being (Q22, A2). God executes
providence **mediately**, through secondary causes, "to communicate the dignity of causality even
to creatures" (Q22, A3); providence does not impose necessity on all its effects but ordains some
to happen contingently, through contingent causes. Defects are permitted "for the good of the
whole."

## Foreknowledge of future contingents (Q14, A13)

The certainty of God's knowledge of free future acts is secured by the **eternal present**: "His
glance is carried from eternity over all things as they are in their presentiality" (Q14, A13). A
contingent thing, considered as actually present to God's eternal vision, is infallibly known, "yet
they are future contingent things in relation to their own causes." Aquinas illustrates with the
traveler analogy: "he who goes along the road does not see those who come after him; whereas he who
sees the whole road from a height, sees at once all travelling by the way." The necessity is *de
dicto* (the proposition "what is known by God, is" is necessary), not *de re* (the event is not made
necessary in itself) — so divine foreknowledge and creaturely contingency are compatible.

## Predestination and reprobation (Q23)

Predestination is "a part of providence" directed to the supernatural end (eternal life) that
exceeds created nature (Q23, A1). It is "in God," not in the predestined — it is the divine plan of
ordering certain rational creatures to glory. The logical order in God is **love → election →
predestination** (Q23, A4).

- **Reprobation** is also part of providence: "the will to permit some to fall away from that end,
  and to ordain the punishment of damnation" for sin (Q23, A3). God's reprobation does *not* cause
  sin; it permits the defect that the reprobate themselves originate.
- **The cause of predestination.** Aquinas denies that foreseen **merits** are the cause of
  predestination as a whole; the cause is the **divine goodness/will** alone (Q23, A5). Created
  effects of predestination (grace, then glory) are ordered, but the predestining act itself rests on
  God's goodness, "that His goodness may be represented in those who are predestined, by way of mercy."
- **Certainty with contingency.** Predestination "most certainly and infallibly takes effect; yet it
  does not impose any necessity" on the free will or the contingent secondary causes through which it
  works (Q23, A6).
- **The number of the predestined** is certain to God — known both "formally" and "materially," by
  deliberate pre-ordination (Q23, A7).
- **Prayer and predestination** (Q23, A8): the prayers of the saints and other secondary causes
  *further the effect* of predestination, since God ordains the end together with the means; prayer
  does not change God's eternal plan but is one of the ordained causes within it.

## Calvin and double predestination (Reformed development)

[[john-calvin|Calvin]]'s account (as reported in [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Gordon's
biography of the *Institutes*]]) emerges with clarity in the 1539 edition of the
[[institutes-of-the-christian-religion|*Institutes*]] and is treated in Book 3. Calvin held that he
was saying "nothing new" — that predestination "simply flowed from Romans, Augustine, Thomas Aquinas,
and Luther." His controversial step was **double predestination**: from before creation God ordained
both the **elect to life** *and* the **reprobate to death**, "entirely apart from any reckoning of
human merit" — "by his just and irreprehensible but incomprehensible judgment he has barred the door
to those whom he has given over to damnation."

Key features distinguishing it from the Thomist account above:

- **Symmetry of the decree.** Where Aquinas frames reprobation as God's *permission* of a
  self-originated defect, Calvin states the reprobating decree more directly and symmetrically, which
  is precisely what separated him from his friends **Melanchthon and Bullinger**, who accepted election
  but rejected the reprobation half.
- **Pastoral function.** Gordon insists predestination is **not** the "cornerstone" of the *Institutes*
  (a later caricature) but a doctrine of **assurance**: by removing all human merit it places salvation
  wholly "in the hands of God," offering comfort and explaining the problem of evil by placing God's
  wisdom beyond human grasp.
- **God not the author of sin.** Against the charge that the decree makes God the author of evil,
  Calvin appealed to the **divine will as "the highest rule of righteousness"**: "whatever he wills, by
  the fact that he wills it, must be considered righteous… When one asks why God has so done, we must
  reply: because he has willed it."

The doctrine was "received with widespread repugnance, even among his friends," hated by Catholics and
most Lutherans, and was later systematised (well beyond Calvin's own emphasis) at the Synod of Dort.
See [[calvinism|Calvinism]].

## Reception and controversy

Aquinas's claim that predestination rests on divine goodness rather than foreseen merit, while
preserving free will and real contingency, situates him between later positions: the Reformed
(unconditional election and, in some forms, double predestination — see [[john-calvin|Calvin]]) and
the Molinist defense of conditional election via *scientia media*. The Catholic Báñez–Molina
*de auxiliis* controversy was in large part an argument over how to read these articles. The
material here is a candidate for a future controversies page on grace and election.
