# Figurative Interpretation

A foundational hermeneutical category developed within Christian scriptural interpretation for distinguishing passages that mean what they say (literal/proper sense) from those that mean something beyond their surface expression (figurative/metaphorical sense). The fullest early systematic treatment appears in [[augustine]]'s [[on-christian-doctrine]], Book III (lines 2376–3576 of the NPNF edition).

## The Two Dangers

Augustine identifies two symmetrical errors the interpreter must avoid (lines 2389–2391):

1. **Taking literal expressions figuratively** — treating as symbolic what was meant plainly.
2. **Taking figurative expressions literally** — what Augustine calls "a wretched slavery of the soul to take signs for things" (line 2584).

He quotes Paul on the second error: *"The letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life"* (2 Cor 3:6, cited at line 2575–2576). Literal adherence when figurative reading is required is described as subjecting "the intelligence…to the flesh by a blind adherence to the letter" (lines 2577–2579).

## The Master Rule: Charity as the Test

The governing rule for deciding between literal and figurative is stated at lines 2685–2688 (Ch. 10, §14):

> "Whatever there is in the word of God that cannot, when taken literally, be referred either to purity of life or soundness of doctrine, you may set down as figurative. Purity of life has reference to the love of God and one's neighbor; soundness of doctrine to the knowledge of God and one's neighbor."

This is the **rule of charity**: any literal reading that promotes neither love of God nor love of neighbor must be figurative. The converse holds too — if the literal sense already yields charity, it need not be re-read figuratively (lines 2832–2833).

Augustine defines the positive and negative poles:

- **Charity** (*caritas*): "that affection of the mind which aims at the enjoyment of God for His own sake, and the enjoyment of one's self and one's neighbor in subordination to God" (line 2707–2708).
- **Lust** (*cupiditas*): "that affection of the mind which aims at enjoying one's self and one's neighbor, and other corporeal things, without reference to God" (lines 2708–2710).

Scripture "enjoins nothing except charity, and condemns nothing except lust" (line 2698–2699). See also [[charity-and-cupidity]].

## Secondary Rules for Figurative Passages

Once a passage is determined to be figurative, further rules govern its interpretation:

### Commands and Prohibitions (Ch. 16, §24, lines 2835–2863)
- A command to do something wicked (e.g. John 6:53, "eat my flesh") is figurative.
- A command enjoining prudence or benevolence is literal.
- Where a benevolent command seems to entail malevolence in its outcome (e.g. "heap coals of fire on his head," Rom 12:20), charity must guide the reader back to a benevolent interpretation — the coals are "the burning groans of penitence" (line 2854–2856).

### Custom, Time, and Person (Chs. 12–14, 17–22)
Acts that appear sinful must be evaluated in their cultural and temporal context. Augustine treats the polygamy of Old Testament patriarchs: "the custom of one man having several wives was at that time blameless" for the purpose of "a numerous offspring" (lines 2788–2789). Such acts are "not only in its historical and literal, but also in its figurative and prophetical sense" and "interpret as bearing ultimately upon the end of love towards God or our neighbor, or both" (lines 2795–2796).

### Variable Symbol Values (Ch. 25, §35, lines 3023–3056)
The same image does not always bear the same figurative meaning. Augustine illustrates: "leaven" signifies corruption (Matt 16:6) and also the kingdom of heaven (Luke 13:21); a "lion" is Christ (Rev 5:5) and the devil (1 Pet 5:8). The context of each use must determine the valence.

### Interpreting by Clearer Passages (Ch. 26–28, §§37–39)
Obscure figurative passages are to be resolved first by parallel scriptural passages, and only secondarily by reason. "It is far safer to walk by the light of Holy Scripture" (line 3125–3126). Multiple valid interpretations of the same passage are permissible if each is "in harmony with the truth" confirmed elsewhere in Scripture (lines 3097–3120).

## Role of Grammatical and Rhetorical Knowledge

Augustine affirms that knowledge of tropes (allegory, enigma, parable, irony, antiphrasis, metaphor, synecdoche, catachresis) assists the reader of Scripture, "because when the words taken literally give an absurd meaning, we ought forthwith to inquire whether they may not be used in this or that figurative sense" (lines 3166–3168). The figures are used in Scripture "more freely and in greater variety" than classical writers (lines 3131–3133).

## The Rule of Faith as a Check

Before figurative reading is applied, ambiguous passages are first tested against the **rule of faith** — what has been gathered from "the plainer passages of Scripture, and from the authority of the Church" (lines 2417–2418). This is the anchor that prevents arbitrary allegorizing. See [[rule-of-faith]].

## Bondage to Signs

A sustained analysis in Chs. 5–9 (lines 2571–2680) distinguishes Jewish "bondage" to literal signs (which pointed to one God) from Gentile idolatry (signs pointing to creature-worship). The positive reading: spiritual persons (patriarchs and prophets) used signs knowingly without bondage to them (lines 2666–2668). The Christian liberty brought by Christ frees believers from sign-slavery by "interpreting the signs to which they were in bondage" and raising them to the realities signified (lines 2648–2649). The few Christian sacramental rites (baptism, Eucharist) are signs so transparent in meaning that observing them does not constitute bondage (lines 2671–2675).

## Connection to the Tichonius Rules

The seven rules of [[tichonius-seven-rules]] are presented by Augustine as practical aids to figurative reading — showing how prophetic texts move between the literal referent (a city, a nation, the devil, Christ) and a wider figurative scope. Augustine summarizes: "all these rules, except the one about the promises and the law, make one meaning to be understood where another is expressed, which is the peculiarity of figurative diction" (lines 3546–3548).
