# Usury and the Just Price (Aquinas)

Within his treatise on **commutative justice**, [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] develops the medieval
economic ethic of the **just price** ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] II-II Q77) and the prohibition of
**usury** (Q78) — the most influential scholastic statement of both.

## The just price (Q77)

Commutative justice requires **equality** between what is given and what is received; therefore "to
sell a thing for more than it is worth, or to buy it for less… is in itself unjust and unlawful"
(Q77, A1). The **just price** is not a single mathematically fixed figure but an estimate, admitting
of a reasonable latitude. Key rulings:

- A seller must **disclose hidden defects** in the goods; manifest defects need not be declared
  (Q77, A3).
- A seller is **not** bound to disclose circumstances (such as more sellers arriving soon) that will
  lower the future price, since he sells according to present conditions (Q77, A3, ad 4).
- **Trade for profit** (*negotiatio*) "has a certain debasement attaching thereto," because it
  serves gain without limit; yet it is lawful when the profit is moderate and ordered to a necessary
  or virtuous end — the support of one's household, the relief of the needy, or the public good
  (Q77, A4).

## Usury (Q78)

Aquinas holds that **to take interest (*usura*) on a loan of money is intrinsically unjust**, "a
sin in itself" (Q78, A1). The core argument:

> Money is a **consumptible** — its proper use is its very spending in exchange — so its **use cannot
> be separated from the thing itself**. To charge for the loan *and* require the principal back is
> therefore to sell the same thing twice, or "to sell what does not exist," and so violates the
> equality of justice.

He reinforces this with **Aristotle**'s claim that money is "barren" and breeding money from money is
"exceedingly unnatural," and with the canon-law and scriptural prohibitions. **Civil law tolerates**
usury (it cannot punish every vice), but **divine law forbids** it (Q78, A1, ad 3).

### What compensation *is* licit

Aquinas is not condemning all return on capital. He permits:

- **Damnum emergens** — compensation for actual **loss** the lender incurs (but not for merely
  hoped-for gain, *lucrum cessans*, at this stage);
- A **voluntary gift** the borrower makes out of gratitude, not stipulated as a condition;
- **Societas** — a genuine **partnership**, where the investor shares the **risk** of the venture
  (since he retains ownership), as distinct from a loan, where ownership and risk pass to the
  borrower.

Charging for **delay** in payment, or discounting for early payment, are both forms of usury (Q78,
A2). These distinctions — especially *societas* and *damnum emergens* — became the channels through
which later theologians accommodated commercial finance.

## Cross-tradition note

The prohibition closely parallels the Islamic ban on **riba** and the biblical/Jewish laws against
lending at interest to a fellow Israelite — a candidate for a comparison page on religious usury
doctrines. The scholastic just-price/usury framework was central to economic thought until the early
modern period.
