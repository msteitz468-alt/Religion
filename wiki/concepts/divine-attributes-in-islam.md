## Overview

The question of God's attributes (*ṣifāt*) — whether divine names such as "Knowing," "Powerful," and "Willing" refer to real, distinct attributes subsisting in the divine essence, or are merely relational and negative descriptions of a single, absolutely simple essence — is one of the central disputes of Islamic *[[kalam|kalām]]* theology. The debate divides the [[mutazilism|Muʿtazila]] and the Islamic Neoplatonist philosophers (*[[falsafa|falāsifa]]*) on one side from the mainstream Sunni tradition, especially the [[asharism|Ashʿarīs]], on the other.

This page covers the dispute primarily as analyzed by [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] in Discussions 5–8 of the [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]].

---

## The Two Main Positions

### Position 1: Denial of Real Attributes (Philosophers and Muʿtazila)

The Neoplatonic philosophers ([[avicenna|Avicenna]], [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]]) and the Muʿtazila converge on the conclusion that no positive attributes can be affirmed of God over and above His essence without violating divine unity (*tawḥīd*):

> "The philosophers have agreed, just as the Muʿtazila have agreed, on the impossibility of affirming knowledge, power, and will for the First Principle. They claimed that all these names have come about through the religious law and that it is permissible to use them verbally, but that … they reduce [referentially] to one essence." (Discussion 6, para. 1)

Their argument: if attributes such as knowledge and power are genuinely distinct from the divine essence, then the essence and its attributes are two different things; each would either need the other (neither would be self-sufficient) or neither would need the other (producing two necessary existents — but this is what the argument for divine unity was meant to prevent). Any "real" attribute is therefore an effect dependent on the essence, making it caused; but nothing in the Necessary Existent can be caused. Hence attributes must be denied.

### Position 2: Real Co-eternal Attributes (Ashʿarī/Sunni Mainstream)

The Ashʿarī position — which Ghazālī defends — holds that God's attributes (knowledge, power, life, will) are genuinely real, distinct from yet inseparable from the divine essence, co-eternal, and uncaused. This does not entail the problematic composition the philosophers fear, because:

> "He is an eternal [being] having attributes. There is neither a cause for His essence, [nor] for His attributes, nor for the subsistence of His attributes in His essence. Rather, all are eternal without a cause." (Discussion 6, para. 21)

Ghazālī insists that the regress-of-causes argument only proves that causal chains must terminate; it does not specify that the terminal being must be attribute-less. An eternal essence with co-eternal attributes, none of which have an efficient cause, satisfies the demonstrative requirement equally well.

## The Philosophers' Reduction of Divine Names to Negation and Relation

The philosophers elaborate how every divine name collapses into either negation or relation (Discussion 5, paras. 23–35):

| Divine name | Reduction |
|---|---|
| "First" | Relation — to existents that come after Him |
| "Principle" | Relation — the existence of other things is from Him |
| "Substance" | Negation — inherence in a subject is negated |
| "Pre-eternal" | Negation — nonexistence at a first beginning is negated |
| "Everlasting" | Negation — nonexistence at a final ending is negated |
| "Intellect" | Negation — existence free from matter |
| "Creator/Agent/Maker" | Relation — existents emanate necessarily from His existence |
| "Powerful" | Same as "Agent" — things under His power emanate from His existence |
| "Wilier" | Relation + negation — He is not oblivious of what emanates; "will" = non-aversion |
| "Living" | Relation — He is a doer and a knower; life = essence + relation to actions |
| "Generous" | Relation + negation — existence together with relation to act and negation of self-interested purpose |
| "Pure Good" | Negation — freedom from deficiency and the possibility of nonexistence |

The crucial claim: "Knowledge, Power, Will, and Essence are, with respect to Him, one [and the same]." (Discussion 5, para. 27) And: "Will would be nothing other than Power itself, Power nothing other than Knowledge itself, Knowledge nothing other than the Essence itself." (Discussion 5, para. 27)

## The Problem of Divine Knowledge: A Special Case

Within the attribute debate, the status of divine knowledge generates particular difficulties that Ghazālī traces through Discussion 6.

### Avicenna's Middle Position

Avicenna, embarrassed by the view (held by "the rest of the exacting philosophers") that the First knows only Himself, claims that God knows all things universally, "not particular knowledge whose renewal would necessitate change in the essence of the knower" (Discussion 6, para. 23). Ghazālī argues this position immediately generates plurality:

> "Is the First's knowledge of the existence of all the species and genera that are infinite identical with His knowledge of Himself, or is it [some] other [thing]? If you say that it is other, you would have affirmed plurality and contradicted the principle [of divine unity]. And if you say it is identical, then you are no different from one who claims that man's knowledge of another is identical with his knowledge of himself." (Discussion 6, para. 24)

The test is the *estimative faculty* (the mind's power to suppose things separately): one can suppose God's knowledge of Himself without knowledge of another, so they must be two distinct things — which is plurality.

### The Conservative Philosophers' Position

Those who hold God knows only Himself avoid this multiplicity problem but fall into an opposite scandal: their God is cognitively inferior to His own creatures:

> "Angel, man, and every one among the rational beings knows himself and his principle, and knows others, whereas the First knows only Himself. He is thus deficient in relation to individual people, to say nothing of the angels. Indeed, the beasts, in addition to their awareness of themselves, know things other than themselves." (Discussion 6, para. 48)

Furthermore, this position does not escape the plurality problem either: is God's self-knowledge identical with Himself or other than Himself? If other, plurality ensues; if identical, one could no more suppose His existence without His self-awareness than suppose "Zayd" to exist and not exist simultaneously — a manifest incoherence.

## The First Has No Quiddity (Discussions 7–8): Extension of the Attributes Debate

The philosophers extend the denial of attributes to a denial of any quiddity or reality distinct from the First's existence. Their claim (Discussion 7): since the First shares no genus with anything else and is differentiated by no specific difference, He has no definition and no quiddity.

Their position (Discussion 8): the First is "pure existence" — "necessary existence is for Him akin to a quiddity for another." There is no reality or quiddity to which existence is related in God.

Ghazālī's refutation:

> "Existence without quiddity and a real [nature] is unintelligible … We do not comprehend an unattached existence, but only in relation to a determinate real [nature]." (Discussion 8, para. 9)

The end point of the philosophers' argument, Ghazālī charges, is "pure negation":

> "They thought that they are elevating [God above all similitudes to His creation] in what they say, but the end result of their discourse is pure negation. For the denial of the quiddity is the denial of reality. Nothing remains with the denial of reality save the verbal utterance 'existence,' having basically no referent when not related to a quiddity." (Discussion 8, para. 11)

Even more sharply: the philosophers "have reached the point of denying Him self-subsistence, reducing Him to the realities of accidents and attributes that have no self-subsistence." (Discussion 6, para. 54)

## Ghazālī's Structural Argument: Demonstration Proves Less Than the Philosophers Claim

Running through all of Discussions 5–8 is a single meta-argument: the regress-of-causes proof (*burhān*) demonstrates only that the causal chain must terminate — "the termination of regress" — and nothing more. It does not prove that the terminal existent must be:

- devoid of attributes (Discussion 6)
- devoid of genus and differentia (Discussion 7)
- devoid of quiddity (Discussion 8)
- incapable of being two (Discussion 5)

> "Demonstration only shows the termination of regress, proving nothing else at all. To claim for it other than this is [sheer] arbitrariness." (Discussion 6, para. 10)

The philosophers' elaborate characterization of the "Necessary Existent" is therefore an imposition on the demonstrative proof that the proof cannot bear.

## Hermeneutical Note

Ghazālī explicitly notes the alignment between the philosophers and the Muʿtazila on attribute-denial: "The philosophers have agreed, just as the Muʿtazila have agreed" (Discussion 6, para. 1). He treats this alignment as significant — the philosophers' metaphysical theology produces, by a different route, the same radical divine simplicity the Muʿtazila defended on theological grounds. In both cases, Ghazālī argues, the result is a God stripped of the attributes affirmed by "all the Muslims, with the exception of the Muʿtazila" (Discussion 6, para. 3) — notably knowledge, power, will, and life as genuinely real descriptions.

His method throughout is internal critique on demonstrative (*burhān*) grounds: he does not simply invoke the Qurʾān against the philosophers, but shows that their own logical tools fail to establish what they claim. Positive appeals to revelation appear only briefly, as when he notes that "the giver of the law has said: 'Think on the creation of God and do not think on the essence of God.'" (Discussion 3, para. 80)
