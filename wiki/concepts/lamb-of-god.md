# The Lamb of God (*Arnion* / *Amnos*)

"Lamb" is the dominant title for Christ in the Book of Revelation and the central image binding its
Christology together. The Greek *arnion* ("lamb") appears **29 of its 30 NT occurrences in
Revelation** (all of Christ except 13:11), where the **slain Lamb** who was sacrificed is also the
**conquering Ram** who reigns, judges, and is worshiped alongside God. The image fuses the
self-sacrifice of the cross with cosmic victory, and it is the linchpin of [[grant-osborne|Osborne]]'s
reading of the Apocalypse: the great victory occurs "not at Armageddon but at the cross" — "it was
the slain Lamb who achieved the great victory" (5:5–6).

## The OT and Jewish Background

The lamb image draws on a cluster of OT sacrificial and prophetic streams:
- **The Passover lamb** (Exod. 12) — the lamb whose blood marks God's people for deliverance and whose
  death inaugurates the exodus. Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor. 5:7).
- **The *tāmîd*** — the lamb of the continual morning-and-evening temple sacrifice (Exod. 29:38–46).
- **The Isaianic Suffering Servant** — "like a lamb led to the slaughter" (Isa. 53:7), the silent,
  sin-bearing servant. See [[servant-songs|Servant Songs]].
- **The apocalyptic warrior-ram** — in Daniel 8 and 1 Enoch 89–90 a "ram" is a militant, ruling
  figure; Revelation's Lamb with "seven horns" (5:6) evokes this conquering ram. Osborne (with Aune)
  judges Revelation's *arnion* to rest chiefly on the **NT image of Christ as paschal lamb** (Isa. 53;
  John 1:29; 1 Cor. 5:7) rather than directly on the *tāmîd* or Passover rite.

## "Lamb of God" in the Fourth Gospel

The phrase **"the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world"** (John 1:29, 36; Greek *amnos*)
is John the Baptist's testimony to Jesus. It carries a *paschal* sense (the Passover lamb) and likely
an *apocalyptic* one as well — anticipating the conquering Lamb of Revelation. With *logos*, the
"Lamb" is one of the bridges between the Gospel and the Apocalypse (see [[john-apostle|John the
Apostle]]).

## The Slain Lamb and the Conquering Ram in Revelation

Revelation's central Christological move (5:5–6) is a deliberate **paradox**: John *hears* that "the
Lion of the tribe of Judah… has conquered" (Gen. 49:9; Isa. 11), but when he *looks* he sees "a Lamb,
looking as if it had been slain." The Lion conquers *as* the slaughtered Lamb — victory through
self-sacrifice. The two aspects then run through the book:
- **The slain Lamb**: by his blood he "freed us from our sins" (1:5) and "purchased for God persons
  from every tribe" (5:9); his blood is the ground of the saints' victory (7:14; 12:11). This is the
  decisive defeat of Satan — already accomplished at the cross.
- **The conquering Ram**: the Lamb has "seven horns" (fullness of power, 5:6); he opens the scroll and
  its seals (6:1); he is the object of worship equal to God (5:8–14); he executes "the wrath of the
  Lamb" (6:16); he is "Lord of lords and King of kings" who defeats the kings (17:14); he shepherds
  his people to springs of living water (7:17, a Davidic-shepherd switch); and the redeemed are his
  **bride** (19:7; 21:9).

## The Lamb and the "Oneness Motif" (Christ's Deity)

The Lamb consistently stands **alongside God**, sharing what belongs to God alone: the throne (5:6;
22:1, 3), worship (5:13, "to him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb"), the title Alpha and Omega/
First and Last (1:17; 22:13), the wrath of God (6:16), and the "book of life" (13:8; 21:27). For
Osborne, this "oneness motif" — shared with the Fourth Gospel — is how Revelation argues the **deity
of Christ**: the slain Lamb is fully God. The New Jerusalem has "no temple," for "its temple is the
Lord God Almighty *and the Lamb*," and its lamp *is* the Lamb (21:22–23).

## 1 Peter and the Petrine Lamb

[[karen-jobes|Jobes]]'s 1 Peter contributes the same paschal-Servant nexus: Christ as "a lamb without
blemish or defect" whose precious blood redeems (1 Pet. 1:18–19), and the Suffering-Servant Lamb of
1 Pet. 2:21–25 (Isa. 53) — the early identification of Jesus with the Isaiah 53 lamb. See
[[servant-songs|Servant Songs]] and [[1-peter|1 Peter]].

## Theological Significance

The Lamb image holds together what could otherwise fragment: **atonement** (the slain Lamb redeems by
blood), **victory** (the conquering Ram defeats evil and reigns), and **worship** (the Lamb receives
divine adoration). It reframes power itself — in Revelation, God's victory comes *through* sacrificial
suffering, and the martyrs "conquer" the beast precisely by sharing the Lamb's death (12:11). The
Lamb is thus both the ground of salvation and the pattern of discipleship.
