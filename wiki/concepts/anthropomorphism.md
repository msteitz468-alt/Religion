**Anthropomorphism** is the attribution of human or bodily form, organs, postures, emotions, or spatial location to God. In the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the Perplexed*]], [[maimonides|Maimonides]] treats anthropomorphism as the central intellectual-religious danger the work is designed to overcome. His anti-anthropomorphist argument operates on two levels simultaneously: **lexical** (showing that scriptural terms ordinarily read as physical descriptions actually carry non-physical meanings) and **metaphysical** (proving that divine incorporeality follows necessarily from divine unity and absolute existence).

## The Problem as Maimonides Frames It (Part I, Introduction)

The intended audience of the *Guide* is the religiously observant person who has also studied philosophy and is "lost in perplexity and anxiety" because the literal interpretation of scriptural language implies corporeality of God, yet abandoning that literal sense seems to threaten the authority of Torah. Maimonides' method is to show that the apparent conflict rests on a misunderstanding of scriptural language — the same language that the prophets and rabbis deliberately left in figurative form precisely because esoteric metaphysical truth cannot be stated plainly for the multitude.

## The Lexical Program: A Homonym Lexicon (Part I, Chs. I–XXVI)

The bulk of Part I, Chs. I–XXVI constitutes a systematic lexicon of biblical terms that naïve readers take as corporeal descriptions of God. For each term Maimonides: (a) establishes the term's primary (physical) sense; (b) shows that the term is a **homonym** with a secondary, non-physical sense; and (c) asserts that wherever the term is applied to God, *only* the non-physical sense is admissible.

Key terms and their non-corporeal readings:

| Term | Primary sense | Maimonidean figurative meaning when applied to God |
|------|---------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| *tselem* (image) | physical figure/shape | specific intellectual form (Part I, Ch. I) |
| *demut* (likeness) | physical resemblance | abstract relational similarity (Ch. I) |
| *temunah* (similitude) | visible outline; imaginative impression | "true form … perceived only by the intellect" — "the similitude of the Lord shall he behold" (Num 12:8) means "he shall comprehend the true essence of the Lord" (Ch. III) |
| *raah / hibbit / hazah* (to see / behold / perceive) | ocular perception | intellectual perception: "All these instances refer to intellectual perception, and by no means to perception with the eye" (Ch. IV) |
| *makom* (place) | spatial location | "the distinguished position of His existence, to which nothing is equal or comparable" (Ch. VIII) |
| *kisse* (throne) | physical seat of authority | "God's Greatness and Essence, which are inseparable from His Being" — "God is incorporeal … how, then, can He be said to occupy any space?" (Ch. IX) |
| *alah / yarad* (ascend / descend) | bodily movement upward/downward | divine communication granted to or withdrawn from a prophet; divine judgment approaching a people: "we must not imagine that the Supreme Being occupies a place to which we can ascend, or from which we can descend. He is far from what the ignorant imagine." (Ch. X) |
| *yashab* (to sit / dwell) | sitting in a chair | permanence, immutability: "He is mutable in no way whatever … 'I, the Lord, do not change' (Mal 3:6)" (Ch. XI) |
| *kam / amad* (to rise / to stand) | bodily posture | confirmation/execution of divine decree; endurance and permanence: "In the world above there is neither sitting nor standing" (Ch. XII–XIII) |
| *karab / naga' / nagash* (to approach / touch / draw near) | spatial proximity or physical contact | "approach by means of knowledge" — "those only approach Him who obtain a knowledge of Him" (Ch. XVIII) |
| *male* (to fill) | one substance filling another | "attainment of the highest degree of excellency": "The whole earth is full of his glory" (Isa 6:4) = "All the earth gives evidence of his perfection" (Ch. XIX) |
| *ram / nasa* (high / exalted) | spatial elevation | elevation in rank, power, absolute existence (Ch. XX) |
| *abar* (to pass) | bodily motion | manifestation or withdrawal of divine action; not movement of God himself: "God, being incorporeal, cannot be said to move" (Ch. XXI) |
| *ba* (to come) | arrival at a place | coming of the Shechinah or fulfilment of the divine word (Ch. XXII) |
| *yaza* (to come out) | motion from one place to another | manifestation of a divine work previously hidden (Ch. XXIII) |
| *halak* (to go / walk) | bodily locomotion | manifestation of something incorporeal, or withdrawal of divine protection (Ch. XXIV) |
| *shakan* (to dwell) | inhabiting a place | continuance of the Shechinah or Providence in a place — not God's own body dwelling (Ch. XXV) |

## The Talmudic Principle (Part I, Ch. XXVI)

Maimonides grounds the entire lexical exercise in the Talmudic dictum: **"The Torah speaks according to the language of man"** — expressions "which can easily be comprehended and understood by all, are applied to the Creator … because the multitude of people do not easily conceive existence unless in connection with a body, and that which is not a body nor connected with a body has for them no existence."

This principle explains both *why* Scripture uses corporeal language and *why* that language must not be taken literally: "All expressions … which imply the various modes of movement in living beings, are employed with regard to God … although there is no doubt that, without corporeality, expressions like the following could not be imagined: 'to descend, to ascend, to walk, to place, to stand, to surround, to sit, to dwell, to depart, to enter, to pass.'"

Maimonides adds a significant qualification: God is never described as performing acts universally regarded as degrading (eating, drinking, sleeping, being ill, using violence), because "the multitude consider [these] a defect or want." Conversely, locomotion is attributed to God even though "the necessity of locomotion is the result of some want" — purely because human convention does not perceive locomotion as a defect. The inconsistency is popular, not philosophical.

## The Metaphysical Basis: Divine Incorporeality

Throughout these chapters Maimonides anticipates the philosophical proof of incorporeality he promises to give later (Part II, Ch. I): "The incorporeality of the Divine Being, and His unity, in the true sense of the word — for there is no real unity without incorporeality — will be fully proved in the course of the present treatise" (Ch. I). The lexical program of Part I is the *hermeneutical* preparation; the philosophical proof is the *demonstrative* foundation.

Key corollaries stated in Chs. I–XXVI:
- "For God is not a being to be perceived by the eye" (Ch. V).
- "The Supreme is incorporeal, and consequently He does not approach or draw near a thing, nor can aught approach or touch Him; for when a being is without corporeality, it cannot occupy space, and all idea of approach, contact, distance, conjunction, separation, touch, or proximity is inapplicable to such a being." (Ch. XVIII)
- "Everything which moves is corporeal and divisible; … God is incorporeal and … He can have no locomotion; nor can rest be ascribed to Him; for rest can only be applied to that which also moves." (Ch. XXVI)

## Role of Onkelos

Maimonides commends Targum Onkelos (the Aramaic translation of the Torah) as an authoritative anti-anthropomorphist reader: wherever Scripture implies corporeality, Onkelos supplies "an ellipsis of a *nomen regens*" before "God" and substitutes "the Glory of the Lord," "the Word of the Lord," or "the Divine Presence (*Shechinah*)." Maimonides cites Ch. XXI: "And behold the Lord stood upon it" becomes "The glory of the Lord stood arrayed above it"; "The Lord watch between me and thee" becomes "The word of the Lord shall watch."

## Hermeneutical Method

Maimonides operates squarely in the *sod* (esoteric/philosophical) register. His argument is that the prophets and rabbis deliberately concealed metaphysical truth within figurative language, both to protect the multitude from truths they cannot bear and because the subjects resist direct exposition. The *Guide* itself follows this method: "My object in adopting this arrangement is that the truths should be at one time apparent, and at another time concealed" (Introduction). The reader must identify which terms are homonyms and which meaning applies to God — the skill the lexicon of Part I, Chs. I–XXVI teaches.
