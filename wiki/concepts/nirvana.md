# Nirvana (Nibbāna)

**Nirvana** (Sanskrit *nirvāṇa*; Pali *nibbāna*, literally "blowing out" / "extinguishing")
is the soteriological goal of [[buddhism|Buddhism]] — the cessation of [[dukkha|suffering]]
and of the round of [[samsara|saṃsāra]]. It is the third of the
[[four-noble-truths|Four Noble Truths]] (*nirodha*, cessation) and what the
[[noble-eightfold-path|Eightfold Path]] leads to.

## What Is "Blown Out"

The metaphor is the extinguishing of a flame: what goes out is the **"threefold fire" of
greed, hatred, and delusion** (*rāga, dosa, moha*) and the [[karma|kamma]]-fueled craving
(*taṇhā*) that drives rebirth. Nirvana is therefore not annihilation of a self (there is no
permanent self to annihilate — see [[anatta|non-self]]) but the ending of the *process* of
grasping. The Pali suttas describe it positively as the unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*), the
deathless (*amata*), peace, and the highest happiness, and refuse to characterize the
state of a liberated one after death as either existing, not existing, both, or neither
(one of the Buddha's "undeclared" questions).

## Two Nirvana "Elements"

The early canon, as anthologized by [[bhikkhu-bodhi|Bodhi]], distinguishes:

- **Nibbāna with residue** (*sa-upādisesa*) — attained at awakening, in life: the defilements
  are extinguished but the body (the "residue" of past clinging) remains. This is the
  living **arahant**.
- **Nibbāna without residue** (*anupādisesa*) — at the death of the arahant: the final
  cessation of the conditioned aggregates, with no further rebirth (*parinibbāna*).

## Not Negative, Not a Result (Rahula)

[[walpola-rahula|Rahula]] devotes the longest chapter of *What the Buddha Taught* to
defending nirvana against two misreadings. It is **not negative / not annihilation**:
described in the suttas with positive epithets (truth, the deathless, the further shore,
peace), it is the *extinction of craving*, not of a self that never existed. And it is
**not a result** *produced by* the [[noble-eightfold-path|path]]: the path *reveals* the
unconditioned but does not *cause* it (a caused nirvana would itself be conditioned and
impermanent). For Rahula nirvana is "Absolute Truth" — beyond the categories of existence
and non-existence that language imposes.

## Nibbāna in the Visuddhimagga

[[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa]] in the [[visuddhimagga|*Visuddhimagga*]] (Chs. XXI–XXIII) provides
the most systematic Theravada account. Key characterizations:

- **The unconditioned** (*asaṅkhata*): "the signless, non-occurrence, non-formation, cessation,
  Nibbāna" — the object of change-of-lineage knowledge and of path knowledge
- **Void, signless, and desireless**: the three aspects of Nibbāna corresponding to the three
  liberations — void liberation (object seen as not-self), signless (object seen as impermanent),
  desireless (object seen as painful)
- **The "bank opposite"** to the ocean of conditioned becoming — stream-crossing simile: change-of-
  lineage knowledge "falls on to the unformed Nibbāna, the bank opposite"
- **Safety and non-arising**: "Non-arising is safety. Non-arising is Nibbāna." The insight
  knowledges move from seeing arising as terror to seeing non-arising as the goal
- **The deathless** (*amata*): Nibbāna = "the deathless medicine that destroys the poison of
  defilement"
- **Nibbāna as object of fruition attainment**: Noble Ones enter the attainment of cessation
  (*nirodha-samāpatti*) "for the purpose of abiding in bliss here and now," with Nibbāna as
  the object; "his consciousness inclines to seclusion, leans to seclusion, tends to seclusion"
  (M I 302) on emerging
- **Not produced, not classifiable**: the attainment of cessation is "not classifiable as formed
  or unformed, mundane or supramundane" — it has no individual essence. Nibbāna itself is the
  un-positively-produced and un-produced (*anipphanna*); among individual essences (*sabhāva-dhamma*)
  it is the only thing that is not positively produced
- **The root metaphor resolved**: the Arahant is "like a fire with no more fuel" — it is not
  that a flame continues elsewhere, but that the process of fueling ceases. Final Nibbāna at
  death = "the complete extinction," with no remainder of clinging aggregates

## Tradition-Specific Developments

- **[[theravada|Theravada]]** treats nibbāna as the final, unconditioned goal attained by
  the **arahant** who completes the path — the culmination of [[bhikkhu-bodhi|Bodhi]]'s
  "planes of realization" (stream-enterer → once-returner → non-returner → arahant).
- **[[mahayana|Mahayana]]** reframes the goal: the [[bodhisattva|bodhisattva]] postpones
  final nirvana out of compassion, and under [[sunyata|emptiness]] the
  [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] asserts the **non-duality of saṃsāra and nirvana** — they are
  "not different," since both are empty of intrinsic nature ([[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]]
  25). The reified goal of an escape *from* the world is itself relinquished.
- **[[zen|Zen]]** and the [[thich-nhat-hanh|engaged]] traditions stress nirvana as
  available *now*, in the present moment, rather than as a posthumous state.

## Cross-Tradition Note

The word predates Buddhism (a cognate goal appears in the [[bhagavad-gita|Gita]] as
*brahma-nirvāṇa*) and in Jainism, but the Buddhist sense is distinctive precisely because
it is **not** union with a self or absolute ([[anatta|non-self]]) — the sharpest contrast
with the Hindu *moksha*-as-realization-of-*ātman*.
