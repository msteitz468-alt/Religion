# Freedom (Heschel)

In [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]] (Part III, ch. 41),
[[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]] confronts the "formidable array of cumulative evidence for
determinism" and redefines **freedom** so that "without such a belief there is no meaning
left to the moral life."

## Freedom as Event, Grounded in Creation

Freedom is not a standing faculty or mere independence from motive but an **event**: "Freedom
is an act, an event … the state of going out of the self, an act of spiritual ecstasy." It is
rare and intermittent — "we are free at rare moments; most of the time we are driven by a
process" (compare the [[revelation|event-vs-process]] distinction). Its metaphysical ground
is the biblical doctrine of **creation** rather than the Greek **cosmos**: "the ultimate
concept in Greek philosophy is the idea of cosmos, of order; the first teaching in the Bible
is the idea of creation," which means "the universe did not come about by necessity but as a
result of freedom."

## Compelled to Choose; God's Waiting

"We are free to choose between good and evil; we are not free in having to choose … all
freedom is a situation of God's waiting for man to choose." Freedom is thus rooted in
[[divine-pathos|divine concern]]: "at the beginning is God's concern," and the supreme
categories of Jewish ontology are "not being and becoming but law and love (justice and
compassion, order and pathos)." The chapter culminates in Heschel's most contested claim —
"God is in need of man" — the partnership in which human [[mitzvah|deeds]] matter ultimately.

## Self-Transcendence

Freedom is one expression of the human capacity for **self-transcendence**: "the essence of
man … is in his power to surpass the self." Selfish interest is centripetal; spiritual life
is centrifugal — "the self is spiritually immature; it grows in the concern for the
non-self." Heschel rejects self-effacement ("elimination of the self is in itself no
virtue"); the Hebrew for sacrifice means "to come near."
