The divine name **YHWH** (Hebrew: יהוה, the *tetragrammaton*, "four letters") is the personal name of Israel's God, revealed progressively in the book of Exodus and central to the theology of the Hebrew Bible. Its pronunciation is uncertain; "Yahweh" is the scholarly convention. Jewish tradition proscribes pronouncing the name (following Exod 20:7), substituting *Adonai* ("Lord") in speech and writing; English Bibles follow this convention by rendering YHWH as "LORD" (small capitals).

## The Three Sinai Revelations

Exodus presents three moments where God's name is revealed with increasing fullness:

**1. Exodus 3:14-15 — The burning bush**: Moses asks God's name. God replies: *ʾehyeh ʾasher ʾehyeh* — "I AM WHO I AM" (or "I will be who I will be"). The name YHWH (*yhwh*) is then given as the form by which Israel is to know God. The name is a form of the verb "to be" (*hayah*), but as [[james-bruckner|Bruckner]] notes, "God's name is first revealed as an active verb, not a noun. Yahweh is not an abstraction, but a living, acting being."

**2. Exodus 20:1-7 — The Decalogue**: God's self-introduction to all Israel: "I am the LORD (*YHWH*) your God, who brought you out of Egypt." The name is historicized — YHWH is identified by the exodus event, not by metaphysical abstractions. The third commandment prohibits taking "the name" in vain — treating the covenant relationship frivolously.

**3. Exodus 34:5-7 — The golden calf resolution**: The fullest proclamation, given after Israel's most severe breach of covenant:

> "The LORD, the LORD, compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, abounding in *khesed* and faithfulness, maintaining *khesed* to thousands of generations, forgiving guilt, rebellion and sin. Yet he certainly will not clear away [the effects], visiting the impact of the fathers' guilt on the children and grandchildren to the third and fourth generation."

This declaration became the most-quoted confession in the Hebrew Bible, cited in Num 14:18; Ps 86:5, 15; 103:8; 111:4; 145:8; Joel 2:13; Neh 9:17, 31; Jonah 4:2; Lam 3:22; Mic 7:18; Nah 1:3.

## The Thirteen Attributes (Exod 34:6-7)

Rabbinic tradition identifies thirteen divine attributes in Exod 34:6-7, derived from the names and descriptions. Bruckner analyzes each Hebrew term:

| Hebrew | Transliteration | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *rakhum* | compassionate | From *rekhem* (womb); softness of a mother for her child |
| *khannun* | gracious | Freely giving without reciprocal demand |
| *ʾerek ʾapayim* | slow to anger | Lit. "lengthened nostrils" — anger cooled before acting |
| *rab khesed* | abounding in unrelenting love | Tenacious fidelity; "pursuing love" |
| *rab ʾemet* | abounding in faithfulness | From *ʾemet*, "truth" — completely reliable |
| *notser khesed laʾalapim* | maintaining love to thousands | Guards *khesed* to a thousand generations |
| *noséʾ ʿawon* | forgiving guilt-wickedness | *ʾawon* = sin with ongoing consequences |
| *noséʾ pesha* | forgiving rebellion/betrayal | Violation of an agreement |
| *noséʾ khattaʾah* | forgiving sin (generic) | Every kind of offense against God and others |
| *naqqeh loʾ yenaqqeh* | yet will not clear [effects] | Emphatic negative — consequences persist even through forgiveness |
| *paqed ʿawon ʾabot* | visiting the fathers' guilt | On children and grandchildren |
| *ʿal-banim* | on the children | |
| *ʿal-shilleshim weriba'im* | to the third and fourth generation | |

The central term is *khesed* — it appears twice, opening and closing the list of attributes. Bruckner: *khesed* means "tenacious fidelity in a relationship, readiness and resolve to continue to be loyal to those to whom one is bound" — not generic affection. It is inadequately rendered by "mercy," "love," or "kindness" alone.

## The Exod 34:7 Crux: Forgiveness and Consequences

The juxtaposition of "forgiving guilt" (v.7a) and "visiting the guilt on children to the fourth generation" (v.7b) has generated persistent debate:

**Retributive reading** (traditional): God forgives the repentant and punishes (through their descendants) the unrepentant. The "third and fourth generation" marks those who "hate God" (Exod 20:5 parallel).

**Consequence reading** ([[james-bruckner|Bruckner]], following contextual grammar): God forgives the guilt itself; but the *ʿawon* (guilt with ongoing impact) continues to reverberate in families and communities. "Forgiven people live with the consequences of the sins of others or their own forgiven sins every day." The *same word* (ʿawon) appears in both halves — both the forgiven guilt and the "visiting" are the same category, which undermines reading the second half as a separate punitive statement.

**Numbers 14:18-23** corroborates Bruckner's reading: Moses quotes Exod 34:6-7 to persuade God to forgive the wilderness rebellion. God forgives — but that generation nonetheless dies in the wilderness. Forgiveness restores relationship; it does not reverse the trajectory set in motion by the sin.

## Exod 6:2-3 and the Documentary Hypothesis

"I am YHWH. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob as God Almighty, but by my name YHWH I did not make myself known to them." This verse claims that YHWH was *not* the name used with the patriarchs — yet Genesis repeatedly uses YHWH in narratives about Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Source critics read this as definitive evidence for separate J (which uses YHWH throughout Genesis) and P (which uses only *Elohim* in the patriarchal narratives) sources. For P, the revelation of the name YHWH is Mosaic, not patriarchal.

Evangelical commentators read it as indicating a *qualitative* deepening of the name's meaning, not a historical claim that the name was unknown to the patriarchs. See [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]].

## NT Connections

**The "I AM" sayings (John)**: Jesus' seven "I am" statements in John (*egō eimi*), and especially the absolute "I am" in John 8:58 ("Before Abraham was, I am"), deploy the Greek equivalent of *ʾehyeh* (Exod 3:14). Bruckner: "Jesus claimed the name revealed to Moses in the three Exodus texts (3:14-15; 20:1-7; 34:6-7)."

**John 1:14**: "Full of grace (*kharis*) and truth (*alētheia*)" — the Greek equivalent of *khesed* and *ʾemet* (Exod 34:6). The verse deliberately echoes the fullest declaration of the divine name.

**2 Corinthians 3–4**: Paul reads Moses' veiled face (Exod 34:29-35) as now unveiled in the face of Christ (2 Cor 3:16, 18; 4:6).

## Jewish Liturgical Use

The *Kedushah* prayer's refrain ("Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts") invokes the name. The *Shem HaMeforash* ("the explicit name") was pronounced once annually by the High Priest in the Temple on Yom Kippur and is no longer spoken. The substitution *Adonai* (Lord) in speech and *HaShem* ("the Name") in casual reference preserve the name's sanctity. See [[shema|Shema]] for the covenant declaration.
