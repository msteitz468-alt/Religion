# Occasionalism

The doctrine that there is no necessary causal connection between any two observable events in the natural world; God (or, mediately, His angels) is the sole efficient cause, creating each event on each occasion as an act of direct divine will. The connection we observe between what we call "causes" and "effects" is real but contingent — a matter of **habitual conjunction** rather than logical or ontological necessity.

Occasionalism is the metaphysical foundation of classical [[asharism|Ash'arite]] kalām and receives its most celebrated philosophical defense in the Seventeenth Discussion of [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]].

---

## The Denial of Necessary Causal Connection

Al-Ghazālī opens the Seventeenth Discussion with the governing thesis:

> "The connection between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary, according to us. But [with] any two things, where 'this' is not 'that' and 'that' is not 'this' … it is not a necessity of the existence of the one that the other should exist." (§1)

The argument applies universally across the sciences:

> "the quenching of thirst and drinking, satiety and eating, burning and contact with fire, light and the appearance of the sun, death and decapitation, healing and the drinking of medicine … Their connection is due to the prior decree of God, who creates them side by side, not to its being necessary in itself, incapable of separation." (§1)

The core epistemological move is that observation establishes *correlation*, not *causation*: "Observation … [only] shows the occurrence [of burning] at [the time of the contact with the fire] but does not show the occurrence [of burning] by [the fire]" (§5). Co-occurrence in experience cannot demonstrate that one thing produces the other.

---

## The Cotton-and-Fire Example

Ghazālī's signature illustration (§§2–5) is fire and cotton:

> "For we allow the possibility of the occurrence of the contact without the burning, and we allow as possible the occurrence of the cotton's transformation into burnt ashes without contact with the fire." (§2)

On the occasionalist account, the *real* agent is God:

> "The one who enacts the burning by creating blackness in the cotton, [causing] separation in its parts, and making it cinder or ashes is God, either through the mediation of His angels or without mediation. As for fire, which is inanimate, it has no action." (§5)

The analogy of the congenitally blind man who, seeing colors for the first time at midday, mistakenly attributes vision to the opening of his eyelids rather than to sunlight (§6), illustrates how habit misleads observers into misattributing efficient causation.

---

## "Habit" as the Ground of Experienced Regularity

Rather than necessary connection, what grounds the reliability of nature is *'āda* — **God's customary or habitual practice** of creating the same sequences:

> "they are possibilities that may or may not occur. But the continuous habit of their occurrence repeatedly, one time after another, fixes unshakably in our minds the belief in their occurrence according to past habit." (§15)

God creates in us knowledge of these habitual sequences; when He wills to depart from the habit (as in a miracle), He also ceases to create that expectation in believers' hearts: "these cognitions … slip away from [people's] hearts, and [God] does not create them" (§17).

---

## The Philosophers' Intermediate Position and Ghazālī's Critique

Ghazālī (§§8–9) notes that the more careful philosophers (*al-muḥaqqiqūn* among them) attribute observable accidents not to fire or bread directly but to a cosmological intermediary, the **bestower of forms** (*wāhib al-ṣuwar*), one of the angels, for which "the sun's appearance, the healthy pupil and the colored body [are] only 'readiers' and preparers for the receptacle's acceptance of these forms" (§8). This already concedes that inanimate fire is not the true agent. A second position (§9) admits that events emanate from cosmic principles but holds that these principles act *necessarily*, not by choice — which is the view Ghazālī targets when addressing miracles.

---

## The Objection: Does Occasionalism Collapse Into Chaos?

The philosophers press a reductio (§13): if nothing follows necessarily from anything, one ought to fear that a book left at home might return as a horse, that fruit might spontaneously become a human being, and so on — eliminating all practical cognition. Ghazālī's reply (§§14–15) distinguishes *possibility* from *actuality*: God creates in us reliable knowledge of what He habitually does and will do, so the chaos scenario requires supposing that God both acts unusually *and* creates in us knowledge of the unusual — which He does not do (except in miracles, where He simultaneously removes the habitual expectation from human hearts).

---

## The Impossible vs. the Merely Unusual

A critical philosophical clarification (§§26–36): occasionalism does *not* mean that God can do the logically impossible. The impossible is defined as "affirming a thing conjointly with denying it, affirming the more specific while denying the more general, or affirming two things while negating one [of them]" (§29). Blackness and whiteness cannot coexist in the same receptacle because the affirmation of blackness *just is* (among other things) the negation of whiteness (§30). What occasionalism asserts is that the causal nexus between distinct, non-definitionally-linked things (fire and burning) involves no such logical entailment and is therefore within divine power to suspend.

---

## Two Approaches to Securing Miracles

Given occasionalism, Ghazālī offers two approaches (§10) to the Abraham-in-the-fire problem:

**First approach** (§11): Deny that the cosmic principles act by necessity rather than by will. If God voluntarily creates the burning when fire contacts cotton, He can equally choose not to create it — no further explanation is needed.

**Second approach** (§18): Concede that fire habitually burns, but allow that God or His angels can introduce a quality into the fire or into the prophet's body that prevents the effect:

> "there would come about either from God or from the angels a quality in the fire which restricts its heat to its own body so as not to transcend it … or else there will occur in the body of the prophet a quality which will not change him from being flesh and bone [but] which will resist the influence of the fire. For we see [that] a person who covers himself with talc and sits in a fiery furnace is not affected by it." (§18)

The analogy of talc demonstrates that unknown qualities capable of blocking normal causal sequences are not inherently impossible — only unfamiliar. The raising of the dead and the staff-into-serpent miracle are handled similarly: "matter is receptive of all things," and God can accelerate its normal developmental cycles into a shorter time (§19).

## Hermeneutical and Theological Significance

- **Ash'arite pedigree**: Occasionalism as articulated here is the paradigmatic Ash'arite doctrine on causation, contra the Mu'tazilite tendency toward natural causation and against the philosophers' necessary emanation.
- **Not a charge of apostasy**: Ghazālī does *not* declare the philosophers' commitment to necessary causation *kufr* (unbelief) in the Seventeenth Discussion. He reserves that charge for three other issues (eternity of the world, God's ignorance of particulars, denial of bodily resurrection — not treated here). The denial of causal necessity is treated as a serious error (*bid'a*) shared with Mu'tazilite tendencies, not as apostasy; this is an important nuance distinguishing it from the three capital charges.
- **Divine omnipotence**: The doctrine directly defends God's absolute power over nature and His freedom from constraint by any natural order.
- **Prophetic miracles**: Occasionalism makes [[prophetic-miracle|prophetic miracles]] (*muʿjizāt*) metaphysically coherent without requiring violation of logical possibility. See that page for the full account.
