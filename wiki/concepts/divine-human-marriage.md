Throughout the Hebrew Bible and New Testament, God's relationship with his people is described by the metaphor of **marriage**: God is the husband; his people are his wife. This is not a peripheral image — it runs through the Prophets, appears in the Pentateuch (YHWH's jealousy), is developed theologically by Paul (Ephesians 5), and reaches its eschatological climax in Revelation's wedding banquet.

Understanding this metaphor illuminates both the Song of Songs (which explores what marriage feels like from the inside) and the canonical significance of marital faithfulness and adultery throughout the OT.

## Why Marriage as Metaphor?

The metaphor works because of the structural parallel between two exclusive, covenantal relationships:

| Marriage | Divine-Human Covenant |
|---|---|
| One spouse | One God |
| Exclusivity is constitutive | Exclusivity is constitutive (first commandment) |
| Jealousy can be a positive, protective emotion | YHWH is a "jealous God" (Exod 20:5; 34:14) — a positive attribute in this context |
| Betrayal = adultery (the most intimate form of betrayal) | Idolatry = spiritual adultery |
| Covenant (*bĕrît*, Mal 2:14) | Covenant (Sinai, Deuteronomy) |

Both God and human are cast within their respective roles *for the sake of the metaphor*, not to assert that God is literally male. Female images of God also appear in Scripture (Ps 131; Prov 8-9; Isa 66:13); the marriage metaphor uses the husband role for God because in ancient Israelite society the husband was the head of the household, whose faithfulness and provision the wife depended upon.

## The Canonical Trajectory

### Pentateuch (Origins of the Metaphor)

The Mosaic covenant already contains the structural elements:
- **YHWH's jealousy** (Exod 20:5; Deut 5:9; Exod 34:14): "I am a jealous God" — an emotion only appropriate in an exclusive relationship
- **Spiritual prostitution** (Exod 34:15-16; Lev 17:7): following other gods = prostituting oneself; idolatry is described with the language of marital unfaithfulness
- **Covenant as bond**: Deut 7:6-11 frames Israel's election in covenantal love (*ʾahăbâ*) that parallels marital commitment

### Hosea (The Originator — First Extended Development)

Hosea (ca. 760-720 BCE) is the **earliest writing prophet to develop the marriage metaphor** for the divine-human covenant. Everything from Jeremiah 3 through Ezekiel 16/23 through Isaiah 54 builds on what Hosea introduced. His root metaphor is broader than marriage alone — YHWH as *head of his household* (*bêt 'āb*), which encompasses husband, father, shepherd, and king as aspects of the single patrimonial role. The marriage metaphor is the household's most intimate dimension.

Hosea is the first prophet to develop the marriage metaphor at length. God commands Hosea to marry "an adulterous wife" (Hos 1:2) as a living embodiment of the relationship between YHWH and Israel. Key moves:
- Israel's idolatry = adultery against YHWH (Hos 2:2-13)
- God will "allure her" again and "speak tenderly to her" — the language of courtship (Hos 2:14-15)
- The restored relationship: "I will betroth you to me forever; I will betroth you in righteousness and justice, in love and compassion. I will betroth you in faithfulness" (Hos 2:19-20)
- The use of "Baali" (my husband/master) → "Ishi" (my man) — a shift from legal ownership to intimate partnership (Hos 2:16)

### Ezekiel (Most Graphic Extension)

Ezekiel develops the metaphor to its most graphic extent in two long chapters:

**Ezekiel 16**: The most extensive and graphically sexual expression of the metaphor in the entire OT. Jerusalem as an abandoned infant, rescued and raised by YHWH, married when mature, elevated to queen — then becoming a harlot who surpassed even Sodom and Samaria in infidelity. Block defends the passage against modern critics who call it misogynistic: the "woman" is metonymically Jerusalem/Israel; the sexual imagery conveys theological betrayal at its most visceral. Crucially, the chapter ends with an unconditional promise: "I will establish an everlasting covenant with you" (16:60) — YHWH's word cannot be permanently broken by Israel's infidelity. The divine *qinʾâ* (passion/jealousy) drives the judgment and is itself a measure of the depth of YHWH's prior love.

Detail from the Vol. 1 re-ingest (Block, NICOT 1997): the chapter is a *rîb* — for Block a strictly two-party covenant dispute, "particularly appropriate where the marriage metaphor is employed" — likely inspired by Hos 2. The opening acts are legal, not merely romantic: "In your blood, live!" is "a formal declaration of adoption" (ANE emergency-adoption parallels), spreading the garment is the marriage gesture (Ruth 3:9), and the covenant formula has "its source in ancient Near Eastern marriage declarations." The bride's tabernacle-vocabulary wardrobe makes "Queen Jerusalem… a surrogate for the temple." Block chose the word: Ezekiel prefers *znh* (prostitution) over the "more fitting" *nʾp* (adultery) as "a more forceful rhetorical tool." He treats the graphic style as deliberate — "the semipornographic style is a deliberate rhetorical device" — and devotes a formal excursus to feminist objections (Halperin's pathology reading, the abusive-husband charge), responding in seven points while endorsing Darr's four theological convictions behind the troublesome texts; his closing warning: rejecting the portrayal means "we sit in judgment over God — a dangerous inversion indeed." The *qinʾâ* is for him covenant passion: "the zeal of his anger is a reflex of the intensity of his love."

**Ezekiel 23**: Two sisters Oholah (Samaria) and Oholibah (Jerusalem) as two faithless wives, both prostituting themselves to Assyria and Babylon. The most graphic use of the metaphor for political alliances as spiritual adultery. The chapter explains the successive Assyrian and Babylonian destructions through the lens of the violated covenant marriage.

Block (NICOT 1997) reads ch. 23 as an intentional exposition of Jer 3:6–11 and distinguishes it from ch. 16 on five counts: the defilement is **political** (foreign alliances), not cultic; the past reaches back to Egypt, not Canaan; Samaria gets a full biography; Jerusalem is the lusting subject rather than the gazed-at object; and there is no restoration ending. The sisters' matched names (both built on *ʾōhel*, "tent") are for him "artificial archaizing constructs" whose chief point is siblinghood — "the nation is one, and her sin is one." The shift from *znh* to *nʾp* in vv. 36–49 deliberately raises the charge from illicit sex to covenant violation.

### Jeremiah (Fullest Legal Development)

Jeremiah develops the legal dimensions of the metaphor most precisely. The key text is Jeremiah 3:1, which invokes Deuteronomy 24 (a divorced wife cannot return to her first husband): Israel left God voluntarily (not sent away), has had "many lovers," and yet God offers to take her back — which is legally impossible and therefore an act of pure grace. **Lalleman** frames the essential issue of the book: "will God still take his people back?" (TOTC raw 1022); *šûb* and marriage/adultery language interlock in 2–3, with Hosea as primary prophetic predecessor (raw 1019–1022, 1447–1451). The answer arc, in her reading, is the [[new-covenant|New Covenant]] (31:31–34): only through God's unilateral initiative after the 604 "point of no return."

Key passages:
- "I remember the devotion of your youth, how as a bride you loved me and followed me through the wilderness" (2:2) — YHWH's wistful memory of the honeymoon period
- "On every high hill and under every spreading tree you played the whore" (2:20) — Israel's serial infidelity
- Two sisters (Israel and Judah) as two faithless wives (3:6-10; parallels Ezek 23)
- God has "sent away" Israel (given the northern kingdom a bill of divorce, 3:8) — exile as enacted divorce
- "Return, faithless Israel... I will not be angry forever" (3:12) — the call back, grounded in grace not legal right

### Isaiah (Most Extensive Prophetic Development)

Isaiah provides the most sustained prophetic development of the marriage metaphor. Notably, Isaiah 53 (the Suffering Servant's atoning death) immediately precedes ch. 54's renewal of the marriage covenant — the restored relationship is only possible *after* the atonement:
- The exile was not a divorce — "Where is your mother's certificate of divorce with which I sent her away?" (Isa 50:1) — but a temporary separation due to sin
- The fullest statement: "For your Maker is your husband — the LORD Almighty is his name — the Holy One of Israel is your Redeemer; he is called the God of all the earth" (Isa 54:5)
- The everlasting covenant: "though the mountains be shaken and the hills be removed, yet my unfailing love (*ḥesed*) for you will not be shaken" (Isa 54:10)
- The restoration as remarriage: "your God will rejoice over you as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride" (Isa 62:5)
- The covenant of peace (54:10) follows and depends on the Servant's atoning sacrifice (ch. 53) — the marriage can only be renewed through atonement

### Malachi (Marriage as *Bĕrît*)

Malachi 2:14 identifies human marriage explicitly as a **covenant** (*bĕrît*): "the LORD is the witness between you and the wife of your youth. You have been unfaithful to her, though she is your partner, the wife of your marriage covenant." This is the verse that most directly grounds human marriage in the same covenantal language as Israel's relationship with God — making the marriage metaphor bidirectional: YHWH models his covenant on marriage *and* human marriage participates in the structure of the covenant.

### New Testament

**Ephesians 5:21-33**: Paul gives the fullest NT theological development. Husbands should love their wives as Christ loved the Church — sacrificially, to make her holy. Wives should submit to their husbands as the Church submits to Christ. Then the key statement: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the Church" (5:32). The marriage relationship *signifies* the Christ-Church relationship. This means: the Song of Songs, which illuminates what the marriage relationship feels like at its best, also illuminates the Christ-Church relationship.

**John 3:29**: John the Baptist describes himself as "the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, and rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice." Jesus is the bridegroom; the Church/Israel is the bride.

**Matthew 22:1-14; 25:1-13**: Kingdom parables framed as wedding banquets and the waiting for the bridegroom.

**Revelation 19:7-9**: The eschatological climax is a wedding:
> "Let us rejoice and be glad and give him glory! For the wedding of the Lamb has come, and his bride has made herself ready. Fine linen, bright and clean, was given her to wear."

And Revelation 21:2, 9: "I saw the Holy City, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride beautifully dressed for her husband."

The entire history of salvation culminates in the *consummation of the marriage* between God and his people — which means the Song of Songs' longing for perfect union ("his desire is for me") finds its ultimate fulfillment beyond history.

## The Song of Songs and This Metaphor

Longman's canonical synthesis: The Song is *primarily* human love poetry (not allegory), but because Scripture uses marriage as the governing metaphor for the divine-human relationship, the Song *also illuminates* that metaphor — from the inside.

The allegorical tradition was right to insist on the theological dimension; wrong to suppress the human dimension and wrong to press the details arbitrarily. The proper approach is to hold the human and divine dimensions simultaneously:
- Read the Song as human love poetry (primary)
- Allow the canonical marriage metaphor to *add* a second dimension without *replacing* the first
- The intensity, exclusivity, mutual pursuit, and jealous love of the Song's lovers all illuminate what it means for God to call his people his bride

Specific connections:
- Song 8:6-7 ("love is as strong as death, its jealousy unyielding as the grave") — divine jealousy in Hosea and Ezekiel; the covenant's unconditional character
- The woman's pursuit of the man — Israel "following me through the wilderness" (Jer 2:2)
- The *tĕšûqâ* (desire) of Song 7:11 — reversing the curse, modeling what restored relationship feels like

## Jewish Interpretation

The rabbinic interpretation of the Song as divine-human love was not wrong — it was allegorically *over-pressed*, but the theological instinct was correct. Rabbi Aqiba's declaration — "the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies" — reflects an understanding that the most intimate human relationship is the best available analogy for the most fundamental of all relationships.

In medieval Jewish mysticism (Kabbalah), the marriage metaphor becomes central: the *Šěkinâ* (divine presence) as feminine, and her reunion with the transcendent aspect of God (*Ein Sof*) as a kind of cosmic marriage — the divine counterpart to human marriage.
