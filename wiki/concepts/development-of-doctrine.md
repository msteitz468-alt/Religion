# Development of Doctrine

> **Concept page.** The question of *how* Christian teaching changes and grows over time while
> claiming continuity with apostolic faith — and the historiographical frameworks for narrating
> that change. Documented here principally through [[jaroslav-pelikan|Jaroslav Pelikan]]'s
> *[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|The Christian Tradition]]*.

## Pelikan's Definition and Triad

Pelikan defines **Christian doctrine** as "**what the church of Jesus Christ believes, teaches, and
confesses on the basis of the word of God.**" The three verbs name three registers:

- **Believes** — doctrine "present in the modalities of devotion, spirituality, and worship."
- **Teaches** — the content of the word of God "extracted by exegesis... and communicated... through
  proclamation, instruction, and churchly theology."
- **Confesses** — "the testimony of the church, both against false teaching from within and against
  attacks from without, articulated in polemics and in apologetics, in creed and in dogma."

He distinguishes three histories: the history of **doctrine** (his subject), of **theology** (the
professional theologians' work), and of **dogma** (normative conciliar definition). Creed and dogma
function as an **"index"** to what the church believes, teaches, and confesses. The method is
**"regressive"** — reading back from confession to teaching to belief.

## Doctrine vs. Dogma vs. Theology vs. Tradition

- **Doctrine** is broader than **dogma** (the church's normative, conciliar statements) and not
  identical with **theology** (which can be individual and speculative). Across the centuries the
  history of doctrine "will move into, but will never quite become, the history of theology."
- The **form** doctrine takes in history is **[[sant-tradition|tradition]]** — both the *process* of
  handing down and the *content* handed down. Pelikan's celebrated epigram: tradition is "the living
  faith of the dead," traditionalism "the dead faith of the living."
- Doctrine, once formulated, does **not** stop developing — "not even the dogma of the
  [[trinity|Trinity]] has stood perfectly still since its adoption."

## Why Begin at the Second Century

Pelikan deliberately starts his history **after** the New Testament: "the theology of the New
Testament is not what Jesus and the apostles may have taught but **what the church has understood
them to have taught**." This sidesteps a New Testament "epitome" and locates the "kerygma and
dogma" problem inside the development of tradition itself, where biblical **exegesis** is the engine
of doctrinal change.

## Hellenization vs. Dehellenization

Against **Harnack's** thesis that dogma is "in its conception and development a work of the Greek
spirit on the soil of the gospel," Pelikan argues that "hellenization is too simplistic and
unqualified a term," and that "in some ways it is more accurate to speak of dogma as the
**'dehellenization'** of the theology that had preceded it" — "by its dogma the church threw up a
wall against an alien metaphysic." His key move: **the chief place to look for hellenization is in
the speculations and heresies *against which* the creeds and councils were directed**, not in
orthodoxy itself. He likewise dissolves the **apologetic/kerygmatic** distinction as a division of
persons or works — the same theologians (Origen, Augustine) wrote both — treating it instead as a
distinction of tasks. (Developed in [[praeparatio-evangelica|Praeparatio Evangelica]], with the
"Pyrrhic victory" corollary that orthodoxy was nonetheless permanently shaped by classical
metaphysics.)

## Rival Frameworks

- **John Henry Newman** — the 19th-century *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine*, the
  classic Catholic theory (organic growth via "notes" of authentic development); Pelikan's title
  page bears Newman's motto *Cor ad cor loquitur*.
- **Adolf von Harnack** — *Dogmengeschichte*, the influential thesis of doctrine as the
  **Hellenization** of the gospel (Pelikan's title page also bears Harnack's epitaph *Veni Creator
  Spiritus*, signaling a dialogue with both traditions).
- The **orthodox** self-understanding of unchanging consensus — *ubique, semper, ab omnibus* —
  formulated by [[vincent-of-lerins|Vincent of Lérins]] (treated in Pelikan's final chapter).
