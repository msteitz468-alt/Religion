# The Old Law (and the Decalogue) — Aquinas

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats the **Old Law** (the Mosaic Law) at [[summa-theologica|*Summa
Theologiae*]] I-II, Q98–105, between the general treatment of [[natural-law|law]] and the [[new-law|New
Law]]. It is the longest stretch of the Treatise on Law and a major site of Christian engagement with
the Mosaic legislation.

## Goodness and purpose (Q98)

The Old Law is **good but imperfect**: good because it accords with reason and restrains evil, yet
imperfect because "the law brought nothing to perfection" (Heb. 7:19) — it could not confer the grace
needed to fulfil it (see [[grace]]). It was **from God**, given to dispose humanity toward Christ
(by prophetic witness and by withdrawing Israel from idolatry into monotheism), promulgated **through
angels** as befits a preparatory law (the New Law was given directly by the incarnate God). It was
given **to the Jews alone** by gratuitous election (the Abrahamic promise), binding all on its
natural-law content but binding only Israel in its distinctive precepts, and given **in Moses's time**
to expose in sequence the failures of human reason (idolatry) and pride.

## The threefold division (Q99)

Every precept of the Old Law is **moral**, **ceremonial**, or **judicial** (Q99, A4): moral precepts
are dictated by [[natural-law|natural law]]; ceremonial precepts are determinations of *divine
worship*; judicial precepts are determinations of *justice among men*. Paul's "holy, just, and good"
(Rom. 7:12) maps onto the three.

## The moral precepts and the Decalogue (Q100)

All the moral precepts reduce to the **natural law** at three levels of self-evidence; the **Ten
Commandments** are the middle tier — needing promulgation yet graspable "with but slight reflection."
The two **tables** order man to God (the first three: one God, no vain oath, the Sabbath) and to
neighbour (the remaining seven, by gravity: parents, then no killing, adultery, theft, false witness,
coveting). The Decalogue is **indispensable** — it contains "the very intention of the lawgiver" and
"the order of justice itself" — so it admits no dispensation; apparent exceptions (Abraham and Isaac,
Hosea, holy war) are not dispensations but re-descriptions of what counts as murder, adultery, or
theft under the essential justice the precepts protect. **Charity** is the end of all the commandments
(1 Tim. 1:5), falling under precept as a direct act though not as a required *mode* of every precept.

## The ceremonial precepts: literal and figurative causes (Q101–103)

The ceremonial precepts (sacrifices, sacred things, sacraments, observances) ordered Israel's worship
of God and, Aquinas argues, had a **double set of causes** (Q102, A2): a **literal** cause (serving
divine worship at the time) and a **figurative/mystical** cause (prefiguring Christ and the New Law).
He applies the full **Quadriga** to the Mosaic ceremonies — they are *allegorically* figures of Christ
and the Church, *morally* of the Christian life, *anagogically* of heavenly glory (see
[[fourfold-sense]]): Passover and circumcision *literally* commemorate the Exodus and covenant, and
*allegorically* prefigure Christ's sacrifice and baptism; the Old-Testament sacraments map onto the
New (circumcision→Baptism, Passover→Eucharist, purifications→Penance, priestly ordination→Orders).

Throughout Q102 Aquinas leans heavily on **[[maimonides|Maimonides]] ("Rabbi Moses")** for the
*literal* reasons of the precepts — the lessening of bodily/animal sacrifice, the gradation of
sin-offerings, laws of cleanness against Gentile superstition — while correcting Maimonides' view that
the ceremonies are simply precepts "for which there is no evident reason": their obscurity, Aquinas
says, is a *consequence* of their being figurative, not their definition. On **cessation** (Q103,
A3–4): the ceremonies died at Christ's coming, and to observe them now *as binding/salvific* is a
**mortal sin** — "just as it would be a mortal sin now... to say that Christ is yet to be born... so
too it would be a mortal sin now to observe those ceremonies" (Gal. 5:2; Augustine, *Contra Faustum*).

## The judicial precepts and the mixed constitution (Q104–105)

The judicial precepts determine justice among men; unlike the ceremonial, they are figurative only
*consequently* (because the whole people was prophetic), and on cessation they are "dead but not
deadly" — a ruler may re-enact their content, just not as deriving force from the Old Law. Notably
(Q105, A1), Aquinas reads the Mosaic polity as the **best, mixed constitution** — "partly kingdom"
(Moses), "partly aristocracy" (the seventy elders), and "partly democracy, ... in so far as the
rulers can be chosen from the people, and the people have the right to choose their rulers" — a
much-cited text in the history of political thought (drawing on Aristotle's *Politics*).
