# Gunas (the Three Qualities of Matter)

The **gunas** ("strands" or "qualities") are the **three constituent qualities of all material existence**
in classical Hindu thought, attested in brief references as early as the Atharva Veda and the Chandogya
Upaniṣad and most fully elaborated in **Sāṃkhya** philosophy:

| Guna | Meaning | Quality | Color | Social correlate | Animal symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sattva** | lucidity, goodness | intelligibility, purity | white | Brahmins | cow |
| **Rajas** | energy, activity | passion, motion | red | Kshatriyas | horse |
| **Tamas** | darkness, inertia | entropy, torpor | black | lower classes (Vaishya/Shudra) | dog |

The three gunas are "woven together like the three strands of a braid" — all three are present in everything,
but in varying proportions that determine a being's nature, and hence its dharma and karma.

## Social Mapping

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 8) notes that classical Sāṃkhya philosophy
overlays the guna triad onto the **three social classes**: sattva predominates in **cows and Brahmins**,
rajas in **horses and Kshatriyas**, and tamas in **dogs and the lower classes**. This is the same
horse/cow/dog social cipher that runs through the full history of Hindu literature (see the
[[wendy-doniger|Doniger commentator page]]) — now given a metaphysical foundation.

The mapping provides a cosmological warrant for the varna hierarchy: the class system reflects the
distribution of intrinsic qualities in matter, not merely human convention.

## The Transcendent Fourth

The triad of gunas belongs to **prakriti** ("matter, nature") — the feminine, material pole of Sāṃkhya
dualism. The transcendent counterpart is **purusha** ("spirit, self, or person") — the masculine, immaterial
witness. When *prakriti* is squared with *purusha*, the three-guna material world is contrasted with a
fourth, transcendent element that lies beyond all three qualities.

This is one instance of Doniger's documented **triad-to-quartet** pattern in Hindu thought (three qualities
of matter + transcendent spirit; three aims of life + moksha; three times + the fourth that is "just called
the fourth" / *turīya*).

## In the Bhagavad Gita and Later Hinduism

In the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]], Krishna explains that the three gunas bind the self to the body
and to rebirth; the goal is to transcend all three — to become *triguṇātīta* ("beyond the three gunas"),
which is the state of liberation.
