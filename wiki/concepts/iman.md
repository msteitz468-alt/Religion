# Īmān (Faith)

**Īmān** (Arabic: إيمان, "faith" or "belief") is the term in Islamic theology denoting interior belief and conviction, as distinct from outward religious practice ([[five-pillars|Islām]]) and excellence of worship ([[ihsan|Iḥsān]]). [[sahih-muslim|Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim]] opens its entire collection with Book 1 (*Kitāb al-Īmān*), signaling the centrality of this concept to the hadith tradition.

---

## The Tripartite Structure: Islām, Īmān, Iḥsān

The foundational framework appears in the **Jibrīl hadith** (Ch. 1, No. 0001), in which an unknown man — later identified as the angel Jibrīl — questions the Prophet on three ascending levels of the religion. This same exchange is transmitted in multiple variants throughout Book 1 (Nos. 0001, 0004, 0006).

**Al-Islām** (external practice) is defined as:
> "Al-Islam implies that you testify that there is no god but Allah and that Muhammad is the messenger of Allah, and you establish prayer, pay Zakat, observe the fast of Ramadan, and perform pilgrimage to the (House) if you are solvent enough (to bear the expense of) the journey." (Ch. 1, No. 0001)

**Īmān** (interior faith) is defined as:
> "That you affirm your faith in Allah, in His angels, in His Books, in His Apostles, in the Day of Judgment, and you affirm your faith in the Divine Decree about good and evil." (Ch. 1, No. 0001)

**Iḥsān** (excellence / moral beauty) is defined as:
> "That you worship Allah as if you are seeing Him, for though you don't see Him, He, verily, sees you." (Ch. 1, No. 0001)

A variant formulation of Iḥsān appears in No. 0006: "that you fear Allah as if you are seeing Him" — substituting *fear* for *worship*, without otherwise altering the sense.

---

## The Six Articles of Faith

The Jibrīl hadith specifies six objects of Īmān:

1. **Allah** — belief in the one God
2. **His angels** (malāʾika)
3. **His Books** (kutub) — the revealed scriptures
4. **His Apostles/Messengers** (rusul)
5. **The Day of Judgment** (Yawm al-Qiyāma)
6. **Divine Decree** (al-Qadar) **— good and evil** (Ch. 1, No. 0001; No. 0006)

A variant in No. 0004 (Abu Huraira) lists "His meeting" rather than "the Day of Judgment" as the fifth article, and omits the explicit mention of Divine Decree — illustrating the transmission variation within the collection.

The inclusion of **Qadar** is theologically emphasized from the opening of Book 1: Abdullah b. Umar swears that if a person "who does not believe in the Divine Decree had with him gold equal to the bulk of (the mountain) Uhud... Allah would not accept it unless he affirmed his faith in Divine Decree." (Ch. 1, No. 0001)

---

## Īmān Increases and Decreases

Book 1 explicitly asserts the non-static nature of Īmān. Chapter 21's heading states: "faith increases and diminishes; Enjoining that which is good and forbidding that which is abominable are obligatory (acts)." Several hadiths illustrate this:

- **Commanding right and forbidding wrong** is the criterion: "He who amongst you sees something abominable should modify it with the help of his hand; and if he has not strength enough to do it, then he should do it with his tongue, and if he has not strength enough to do it, (even) then he should (abhor it) from his heart, and **that is the least of faith**." (Ch. 21, No. 0079)
- Major sins (fornication, theft, drinking wine) suspend the quality of belief during their commission: "The fornicator who fornicates is not a believer so long as he commits it." (Ch. 25, No. 0104) However, "repentance may be accepted after that." (No. 0109)
- Ingratitude and cursing are described as diminishing Īmān (Ch. 35, No. 0142).
- Neglect of prayer (*salāt*) is equated with polytheism and unbelief: "between man and between polytheism and unbelief is the negligence of prayer." (Ch. 36, No. 0146)

## The Branches of Īmān (Shuʿab al-Īmān)

> "Iman has over seventy branches, and modesty is a branch of Iman." (Ch. 13, No. 0055)
>
> "Faith has over sixty branches, the most excellent of which is the declaration that there is no god but Allah, and the humblest of which is the removal of what is injurious from the path: and modesty is the branch of faith." (Ch. 13, No. 0056)

The two variant counts (sixty vs. seventy) appear within the same chain of narrators, suggesting transmission uncertainty about the precise number. The conceptual range spans from the Shahāda at the apex to a minor act of clearing harm from a path at the base.

---

## The Sweetness (Ṭaʿm) of Faith

Three qualities are said to allow a person to "relish the sweetness of faith":

> "he to whom Allah and His Messenger are dearer than all else; he who loves a man for Allah's sake alone; and he who has as great an abhorrence of returning to unbelief after Allah has rescued him from it as he has of being cast into Hell." (Ch. 16, No. 0067)

A related formulation (Ch. 17, No. 0071) requires that the Prophet be dearer to the believer than "his child, his father and the whole of mankind."

---

## Signs of the Hypocrite (Munāfiq)

The opposite of sincere Īmān is *nifāq* (hypocrisy). The signs:

> "Three are the signs of a hypocrite: when he spoke he told a lie, when he made a promise he acted treacherously against it, when he was trusted he betrayed." (Ch. 26, No. 0112)

A variant adds a fourth (Ch. 26, No. 0111): "when he quarreled he deviated from the truth." Critically, these signs apply **even if** the person "observed fast and prayed and asserted that he was a Muslim" (No. 0114) — external religious practice cannot compensate for the three signs of hypocrisy.

---

## Minimum Threshold: The Shahāda

The repeated insistence of Book 1 is that sincere profession of the Shahāda — "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger" — constitutes the threshold of Īmān sufficient for entry into Paradise:

> "He who died knowing (fully well) that there is no god but Allah entered Paradise." (Ch. 11, No. 0039)

This is extended remarkably in Ch. 41: Abu Dharr reports the Prophet stating that "there is none among the bondsmen who affirmed his faith in *Lā ilāha illā Allāh* (there is no God but Allah) and died in this state and did not enter Paradise... (Yes) even though he committed adultery and theft." (No. 0172) The Prophet withheld this teaching from the general public lest people "trust in it alone" and abandon good deeds (Nos. 0047, 0051).

---

## Evil Promptings and the Sincerity of Faith

Book 1 includes a remarkable treatment of involuntary evil thoughts (waswās):

> Companions troubled by thoughts "too grave to express" brought this to the Prophet, who replied: "That is the faith manifest." (Ch. 61, No. 0239)

The horror at evil thoughts itself demonstrates genuine Īmān. Similarly, the Prophet taught that Allah forgives "the evil promptings which arise within their hearts as long as they did not speak about them or did not act upon them." (Ch. 59, No. 0230)

---

## Quantitative Faith and Eschatological Thresholds (Chs. 82–92)

A major theological development in the later chapters of Book 1 is the treatment of Īmān as quantifiable. The intercession (*shafāʿa*) sequence extracts people from Hell who have faith at ever-decreasing quantities — the weight of a dinar, a barley grain, a wheat grain, a mustard seed, an atom — culminating in a divine declaration that reserves the final extraction for Allah himself:

> "By My Honour, Glory, Greatness and Might, I would certainly take him out who professed it: There is no god but Allah." (No. 0377)

See [[shafaa|Shafāʿa]] for the full intercession doctrine.

## Muslim (muslim) vs. Believer (muʾmin) Distinction (Ch. 69)

The Prophet corrects Saʿd b. Abī Waqqāṣ who calls a companion a *muʾmin* (believer), substituting *muslim* instead, to avoid overstating the man's inward state. The deeper term *muʾmin* is reserved for one whose faith is inwardly secure. This maps precisely onto the Islām/Īmān axis of the Jibrīl hadith.

## Faith is Taken Away Gradually (Ch. 65)

Trustworthiness/faith (*amāna*) can be progressively extracted from the heart during sleep, leaving traces like blisters from an ember: "He would again sleep and trustworthiness would be taken away from his heart leaving an impression of a blister." (No. 0265) This is the eschatological diminishment of faith as an experiential reality.

## Two-Hearts Typology (Ch. 65)

> "One white like a white stone which will not be harmed by any turmoil or temptation... and the other black and dust-coloured like a vessel which is upset, not recognizing what is good or rejecting what is abominable, but being impregnated with passion." (No. 0267)

## Temporal Limit: Faith after the Signs (Ch. 73)

Faith becomes eschatologically irrelevant after three signs appear: the sun rising from the west, the Dajjāl, and the beast of the earth. "When three things appear faith will not benefit one who has not previously believed or has derived no good from his faith." (No. 0296)

## Iman in Sahih al-Bukhari — Book 2 (Belief / Kitab al-Iman)

*Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, Vol. 1, Book 2 (Nos. 7–55); Book 8 (No. 345). M. Muhsin Khan translation.*

### The Three-Tier Structure: Islam / Iman / Ihsan

The **Jibril Hadith** (Vol. 1, Book 2, No. 47, narrated by Abu Huraira) defines the three-tier structure with the angel Jibril (Gabriel) appearing as a man to question the Prophet:

- **Islam** (*submission*): "To worship Allah Alone and none else, to offer prayers perfectly, to pay the compulsory charity (Zakat) and to observe fasts during the month of Ramadan."
- **Iman** (*faith*): "Faith is to believe in Allah, His angels, (the) meeting with Him, His Apostles, and to believe in Resurrection."
- **Ihsan** (*perfection/excellence*): "To worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you cannot achieve this state of devotion then you must consider that He is looking at you."

Abu Abdullah adds: "he (the Prophet) considered all that as a part of faith" — the three tiers are not separate religions but nested dimensions of one.

### The Five Pillars as the Content of Islam

The Five Pillars are stated directly in No. 7 (narrated by Ibn Umar): *Islam is based on five principles* — the Shahada, prayer, Zakat, Hajj, and fasting Ramadan. No. 44 (narrated by Talha bin Ubaidullah) narrates a Bedouin man's encounter that confirms only these as *obligatory*, with additional acts (*nawafil*) voluntary.

### Faith as More than Sixty Branches

Vol. 1, Book 2, No. 8 (Abu Huraira): "Faith (Belief) consists of more than sixty branches (*shu'bah*). And *Haya* (self-respect, modesty, bashfulness, scruple) is a part of faith." This quantification suggests faith is a compound quality with multiple ethical, behavioral, and creedal components — not merely intellectual assent.

### The Sweetness of Faith

Nos. 15 and 20 (both narrated by Anas) present three qualities that produce the *halawa* (sweetness/delight) of faith:
1. Allah and His Apostle are dearer to one than anything else.
2. One loves a person only for Allah's sake.
3. One hates to revert to disbelief (*kufr*) after Allah has brought one out of it, as one hates to be thrown into fire.

### The Muslim/Mu'min Distinction

No. 26 (narrated from Sa'd) records a crucial distinction: the Prophet calls a man Sa'd considers a "faithful believer" (*mu'min*) merely a "Muslim" (*muslim*). Outer conformity (*islam*) and inner faith (*iman*) may diverge. This parallels the Sahih Muslim treatment (see above) and Qur'an 49:14.

### Faith and the Afterlife: Consequences

Several hadiths in Book 2 ground iman in eschatological consequence:
- No. 21: Those with faith equal to a mustard seed are taken from Hell and revived "like a grain that grows near the bank of a flood channel."
- No. 42: Even atomic-weight faith in the Shahada will eventually bring one out of Hell.
- Nos. 34–37: Standing in prayer during Ramadan, fasting Ramadan, and performing Laylat al-Qadr prayer — "out of sincere faith (*imanan*) and hoping to attain Allah's rewards (*ihtisaban*)" — each cause past sins to be forgiven. The formulaic qualifier *imanan wa ihtisaban* defines the iman-criterion for acts of worship to count as valid.

### Hypocrisy (Nifaq) as the Negation of Iman

Nos. 32–33 define hypocrisy (*nifaq*) as the structural opposite of iman:
- Three signs (No. 32): lying, breaking promises, betrayal of trust.
- Four characteristics of the "pure hypocrite" (No. 33): betrayal when entrusted, lying when speaking, treachery when covenanting, imprudent conduct when quarreling.

### The Mi'raj and the Origin of Prayer

The Mi'raj account (Book 8, No. 345, narrated by Abu Dhar) provides the narrative origin of the five daily prayers: Allah originally enjoined fifty; Moses advised the Prophet to seek reductions. After multiple reductions, five prayers were established with the promise that they are "equal to fifty in reward, for My Word does not change." This narrative frames the five daily prayers not merely as obligation but as the primary embodied expression of iman — the point where interior conviction is enacted in daily bodily practice.
