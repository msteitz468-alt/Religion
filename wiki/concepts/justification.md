# Justification (*Dikaiosynē* / *Dikaiōsis*)

The doctrine of justification concerns how a person comes to be right (righteous, justified) before God. In Christian theology it is primarily a Pauline concept, developed most fully in Romans and Galatians, and became the central controversy of the Protestant Reformation. The doctrine involves three interrelated questions: (1) What does it mean to be "justified"? (2) What is the basis or ground of justification? (3) What is the instrument or means by which justification is received?

## The Greek Word Group

The *dik-* word group (*dikaiosynē* = righteousness; *dikaiōsis* = justification; *dikaioun* = to justify/declare righteous; *dikaios* = righteous/just) covers both forensic and relational meanings in Greek and in the LXX. In legal/forensic contexts, *dikaioun* means "to declare righteous" or "to vindicate" — as in Deuteronomy 25:1, where judges are to "justify the righteous and condemn the wicked," meaning to *declare* them such, not to *make* them so.

## The Forensic Core

The Reformation tradition (Luther, Calvin, and most Protestant theology since) has insisted that justification is a forensic act — a declaration by God the judge that the sinner stands in a righteous status before him, on the basis of Christ's atoning work. Key Pauline texts:
- Romans 3:21–26 — God's righteousness is demonstrated in the death of Jesus; the justified are declared "not guilty"
- Romans 4:3–5 — Abraham's faith was "reckoned" (*elogisthē*) as righteousness; the ungodly are justified, not on the basis of works
- Romans 8:33 — "Who will bring a charge against God's elect? God is the one who justifies" — law court language unmistakable
- Philippians 3:9 — Paul contrasts his "own righteousness" (from the law) with "the righteousness from God on the basis of faith"
- Galatians 2:16 — "a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ"

[[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (BECNT Romans, 1998) accepts the forensic core as irreducible: those whom God declares righteous are genuinely in a righteous status before the divine law court, a status not their own but Christ's, imputed to them through faith.

## The Transformative Dimension

Schreiner also insists that justification cannot be isolated from transformation. The "righteousness of God" (*dikaiosynē theou*) in Romans 1:17 is both a gift (the believer's forensic status) and a power (God's saving activity that transforms). Those whom God declares righteous are *also* made righteous:

- Romans 5:19 — "Just as through the one man's disobedience the many were made [constituted] sinners, so through the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous"
- Romans 6 — freedom from sin as a power, not merely as a guilt
- Romans 6:7 — "*dikaiōtheis* from sin" — "justified/freed from sin"
- 2 Corinthians 3:8–9 — "ministry of righteousness" as producing transformation

Schreiner's formulation: "God's forensic declaration is effective because the Lord who was crucified on behalf of sinners was also raised from the dead, and thus sinners now live in a new way." The forensic is the basis for the transformative; the one cannot be sundered from the other. This distinguishes Schreiner's position from a purely extrinsic (forensic-only) doctrine without falling into the Catholic position that justification is primarily infusion.

## The Basis: Christ's Atoning Death

Justification is grounded in the substitutionary death of Christ (Romans 3:24–26). The *hilastērion* (3:25 — propitiation/mercy seat) demonstrates God's righteousness in passing over former sins and justifying those who have faith in Jesus. Schreiner: *hilastērion* = propitiation (turning away divine wrath), not merely expiation (cleansing). See [[righteousness-of-god|The Righteousness of God]] for the debate about what the "righteousness of God" contributes to the doctrine of justification.

## The Instrument: Faith Alone

"By faith" (*ek pisteōs*, *dia pisteōs*) is the consistent Pauline description of how justification is received. Romans 3:22, 28, 30; 4:3, 5, 9, 11, 13; 5:1; 9:30; 10:4, 6, 10 all emphasize that righteousness is received through faith and not works. Faith for Schreiner involves:
- Mental assent (believing that God raised Jesus from the dead, 4:24; 10:9)
- Commitment and reliance (Abraham staking his future on God's promise, 4:18–22)
- Obedience (faith always expressed in obedience; the "obedience of faith," 1:5, is both faith and its fruit)

Faith is distinguished from works because it is reliance upon another (Christ), not self-achievement. But genuine faith always produces obedience; the two cannot be divorced.

## Justification and the Law

Romans 3:20 ("no one will be justified by works of the law") is the summary claim. The meaning of "works of the law" (*erga nomou*) is a major controversy:

**Traditional Protestant reading** (Luther, Reformation, Schreiner): "works of the law" = human moral achievement generally; any attempt to earn righteousness before God by obeying the Mosaic law.

**New Perspective on Paul** (Dunn, Wright): "works of the law" = primarily Jewish ethnic identity markers (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath observance that bounded Jews from Gentiles). The problem Paul addresses is not Pelagianism but ethnocentrism.

Schreiner explicitly rejects the NPP narrowing. "Works of the law" is broad: any human achievement or merit, not merely boundary markers. See [[new-perspective-on-paul|The New Perspective on Paul]].

## Justification and the New Perspective

The NPP raises whether Paul was responding to a legalistic Judaism (earning salvation by law-keeping) or a "covenantal nomism" (saved by grace, maintained by obedience). If the latter, the Reformers misunderstood the Jewish background and may have misread Paul. Schreiner's response: even within covenantal nomism, Paul critiques the assumption that covenant membership through the law (however graciously entered) can produce righteousness before God. The problem is not merely ethnocentrism but the incapacity of the law to deliver what it demands.

## Catholic-Protestant Distinction

Catholic teaching (Trent) understands justification as both declarative and transformative from the outset — righteousness is *infused*, not merely *imputed*. The justified person is genuinely made righteous by God's grace, though they may fall from this state through mortal sin. Protestant tradition (Lutherans, Reformed) insists on *imputation* — Christ's righteousness is credited to the believer; the believer is not inherently righteous but is reckoned as righteous because of union with Christ. Schreiner's "forensic + transformative" position is not a collapse into Catholicism: the transformative is the *result* of the forensic declaration, not part of the act of justification itself.

## Galatians' Distinctive Contribution (Moo)

[[douglas-moo|Moo]] (BECNT Galatians, 2013) adds several important dimensions to the doctrine not emphasized in Schreiner's Romans treatment:

**The anthropological argument**: Galatians 3:10 presupposes human inability — not just that the Torah era is over, but that sinful humanity cannot render the obedience Torah demands. The syllogism in 3:10 (only perfect Torah-keepers escape the curse; no one can perfectly keep Torah; therefore, all who rely on Torah are cursed) grounds the polemic against Torah observance in a pessimistic anthropology. Westerholm: "The fundamental question addressed by Galatians is not 'What is wrong with Judaism?' but 'What is wrong with humanity that Judaism cannot remedy?'"

**Future-oriented justification**: In Galatians the language of justification has a primarily *future* orientation. The letter is less about "how you get saved initially" than about "ultimate justification" — the final eschatological verdict. The agitators argued that only Torah observers could expect God's vindication at the judgment; Paul argues for faith alone. The "already/not yet" character of justification: there is a definitive initial justification (presumed throughout), but the letter's focus is on the eschatological vindication believers await by faith through the Spirit (5:5).

**Union with Christ as more fundamental**: Moo (following Calvin) places union with Christ as the theological foundation: both forensic justification and moral transformation are inseparable products of being "in Christ." This is the "double gift" — not justification and sanctification as two stages, but as two aspects of one union. This prevents the forensic from being divorced from the transformative without collapsing them into one act (as in Catholicism).

**"The law of Christ" (6:2)**: Galatians introduces the concept of "the law of Christ" — the new covenant's distinctive ethical authority (Christ's teaching and the apostles), which replaces the Mosaic Torah's direct authority over believers. The OT law "fulfills" (5:14) when the love command, as taught by Christ, achieves all the OT law intended.

## James's "Justify" — Vindication at the Judgment (Moo)

The [[james|Letter of James]] is the great NT counter-pole to Paul on justification: "a person is
justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24). [[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC James, 2000) argues
the contradiction is apparent, not real, because *dikaioō* carries a **different sense** in James:
- In **Paul**, justification is God's *initial, forensic declaration* of the sinner's innocence at
  the point of faith — how one *gets into* relationship with God (the sense developed above).
- In **James**, *dikaioō* means God's *ultimate vindication in the final judgment*, which takes a
  person's works into account — grounded in OT/LXX and Jewish judicial usage (1 Kgs. 8:32; Mic.
  6:11) and in Jesus' "by your words you will be justified" (Matt. 12:37).

Two further moves complete Moo's resolution: (1) James's "faith alone" is the *bogus*, word-only,
demonic faith (2:19) he attacks throughout 2:14–26, not Paul's dynamic, obedient faith — so on the
*meaning of faith* James and Paul agree; (2) read against **covenantal nomism**, James 2 looks
synergistic ("get in" by the new birth and faith, 1:18; 2:1; "stay in" by the royal law, 2:8), but
Moo absorbs this into Pauline monergism by treating James's "works" as the Spirit-produced *fruit*
of grace (cf. Phil. 2:13). Moo rejects the popular evangelical reading that James's "justify" merely
means "demonstrate to be righteous" (the soteriological force is inescapable, tied to "save" in
2:14), and reserves the term "justification" for Paul's initial act while classing James's concern
under "judgment." See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on Justification]] and
[[covenantal-nomism|Covenantal Nomism]].

The result is a canonical complementarity: faith alone justifies (Paul, initial); true faith
inevitably produces the works God vindicates at the last judgment (James, final). "Paul strikes at
legalism; James at quietism."
