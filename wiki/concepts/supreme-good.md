# The Supreme Good (*Summum Bonum*)

The final or highest good — the end "for the sake of which other things are to be desired, while it is
to be desired for its own sake." The category structures [[augustine|Augustine]]'s critique of the
philosophers in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XIX.

## Augustine against the philosophers

Augustine reports [[varro|Varro]]'s combinatorial enumeration of **288 possible philosophical sects**
on the question of the supreme good, reduced to twelve and finally to the Old Academy's position. Against
all of them he argues that no philosophy can locate the supreme good **in this life**, because this life
is shot through with misery: bodily affliction, derangement, the very cardinal virtues being signs of an
ongoing war with vice (temperance bridles lust *because* the flesh lusts; fortitude bears ills *because*
ills exist). He presses the Stoics in particular — they call the wise man's life happy yet permit
**suicide** to escape it: "O happy life, which seeks the aid of death to end it?"

## The Christian answer

**"Life eternal is the supreme good, death eternal the supreme evil; and… to obtain the one and escape
the other we must live rightly."** Happiness is real now only **by hope** (Rom. 8:24, "we are saved by
hope"); the just "lives by faith" (Hab. 2:4). The supreme good is finally the eternal [[peace|peace]] and
felicity of the city of God — "the peace of this blessedness and the blessedness of this peace." Its
opposite, the supreme evil, is the **second death**, eternal separation of the soul from God, "the more
severe, because no death shall terminate it."
