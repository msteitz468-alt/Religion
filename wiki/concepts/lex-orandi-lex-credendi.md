# Lex Orandi, Lex Credendi (Worship and Doctrine)

The principle that "the rule of prayer" (*lex orandi*) grounds and norms "the rule of belief" (*lex credendi*) — that what the church *prays and worships* is evidence for, and a constraint upon, what it *teaches and confesses*. [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Jaroslav Pelikan]] makes this one of his master themes for the development of doctrine: dogma frequently *followed* liturgy rather than the reverse.

The maxim itself derives from **Prosper of Aquitaine** (5th c.), who, defending Augustinian grace against the [[semi-pelagianism|Semi-Pelagians]], wrote *"ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi"* — "let the rule of prayer establish the rule of belief": because the whole church prays for the conversion and perseverance of all (including those who resist), grace must be wholly God's gift. [[gregory-the-great|Gregory the Great]] extended the principle hermeneutically — the *actio* (practice/lives of the fathers) helps make sense of the *praedicatio* (the preaching of Scripture).

## The Trinitarian Argument from Worship

Pelikan shows that the deity of Christ and of the Spirit was established less by metaphysical demonstration than by appeal to **worship and baptism**:

- **Against the Arians:** the decisive charge was an "inconsistency between their dogmatic principle and their liturgical practice." [[arianism|Arians]] still baptized "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit," and could not avoid praying to the Logos — yet by calling him a creature they told Christians to "worship a creature and a work." [[athanasius|Athanasius]] argued that the very worship of Christ "proved his essential difference from all creatures."
- **The soteriological corollary:** "God the Logos became man in order that men might become God" — but only one who is himself uncreated can [[apotheosis-divinization|deify]]. Athanasius on the Spirit: "how can he deify me by baptism?" if he is a creature. The [[homoousion]] was thus, for Pelikan, "a soteriological confession" as much as a cosmological one.
- **Basil and the doxology:** [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]] defended glorifying the Spirit "*with* (μετά) the Son" — not merely "*through* the Son" — precisely because that form "had been preserved in the liturgical language of the common people." If liturgical usage is authoritative for confession, it could not be set aside for the Spirit.

## Significance

The principle explains why doctrinal definition so often **trailed and ratified** established prayer: the church confessed the Spirit's deity because it already worshipped and was baptized into him. It connects to Pelikan's broader account of [[development-of-doctrine|the development of doctrine]] and to the patristic appeal to [[consensus-patrum|the tradition]].
