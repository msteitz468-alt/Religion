# Bahá'í Marriage

*All content from Peter Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (Oneworld, 2000), lines 21432–21636.*

## Overview

[[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]] bade his followers to marry, both to bring forth children and as an 'assistance' to themselves. Marriage is not obligatory, however. True marriage is 'a fortress for well-being and salvation'; a spiritual as well as a physical relationship that will continue through 'all the worlds of God'. [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] described marriage as the bedrock of the whole structure of human society — a divine institution (lines 21432–21443).

## Legal Requirements

The following stipulations are at present applicable only for Middle Eastern Bahá'ís: betrothal, the bridal gift, virginity, and travel (lines 21444–21449).

### Consent
Marriage requires first the consent of the couple, and then of their parents. Parental permission is required regardless of the age of the parties and whether or not they have been married before. In cases of adoption, the permission of the natural parents is required unless they cannot be located. Both partners must have reached the age of maturity (fifteen), and neither can become engaged before this age (lines 21450–21471).

### Betrothal
Betrothal must not exceed ninety-five days (line 21473).

### Marriage Ceremony
The ceremony consists of both partners saying the verse 'We will all, verily, abide by the Will of God' in the presence of two witnesses. Additional prayers and scriptural readings may be added, but the ceremony should remain simple. Any civil marriage in places where the Bahá'í marriage has no legal standing should take place on the same day. Modern Bahá'í marriages take place under the jurisdiction of a spiritual assembly, which must ensure a proper ceremony and parental consent (lines 21474–21491).

### The Bridal Gift (Mahr)
The marriage is conditional on payment of a relatively small amount of money by the husband to the wife (not from the bride's family, and not to her parents). The payment is fixed at a minimum of 19 mithqáls (69 grams) of precious metal — gold if the husband is a city-dweller, silver if a villager — and a maximum of 95 mithqáls (346 grams), with the lower rate preferable. A promissory note may substitute for immediate payment (lines 21492–21511).

### Virginity
If a man discovers his bride was not a virgin when he supposed she was, the bride-payment and wedding expenses may be demanded back. If the marriage was made conditional on virginity, it is thereby invalidated. To conceal the matter is described as highly meritorious (lines 21512–21522).

### Travel
If a man embarks on a journey, he must fix a return date with his wife. If unable to return by the appointed date, he must inform her. If she receives no word for nine months beyond the appointed date, or receives reliable report of his death from two witnesses, she can remarry. If his fate is unknown, she is encouraged to be patient (lines 21523–21533).

## Sexual Relationships
Sexual relationships outside marriage are not permitted. 'Trial' and 'companionate' marriages are excluded. Modern Bahá'í practice permits only strict monogamy, though polygamous marriages contracted prior to an individual becoming a Bahá'í are accepted (lines 21534–21552).

## Prohibited Relationships
Bahá'u'lláh forbade men from marrying their father's wives. The Universal House of Justice extends this prohibition, *mutatis mutandis*, to include marriage between a woman and her stepfather (i.e., a man and his stepdaughter). 'Abdu'l-Bahá stated that the more distant the relationship between marriage partners the better — for physical well-being and wider fellowship. He also encouraged interracial marriages (lines 21555–21576).

## Interreligious Marriages
Bahá'ís are free to marry those of other religions, but participation in another religion's ceremony should not involve dissimulation of their own faith (lines 21578–21585).

## Domestic Violence
Bahá'í marriage should be based on mutual respect and equality. All forms of spousal abuse are condemned. The use of force by one partner to compel the other to obey contradicts the principle of consultation on which marriage should be based (lines 21586–21594).
