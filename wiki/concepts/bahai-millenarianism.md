# Millenarianism (Bábí and Bahá'í)

*All content from Peter Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (Oneworld, 2000), lines 22492–22579.*

## Definition
Millenarianism is the belief in a divinely destined future paradise on earth, commonly linked to the acceptance of a particular leader as a messiah. It is a major motif in Bábí-Bahá'í history (line 22496).

## Bábí Origins
Bábism emerged in the context of Shi'i messianic expectation. [[the-bab|The Báb]] proclaimed that he was the Shi'i messianic figure, the [[qaim|Qá'im]], who had come to usher in the events of the resurrection. Initially, many of his followers apparently expected him literally to fulfil Shi'i prophecies leading to the establishment of a theocratic kingdom. Following the crushing of Bábism (1849–52), further messianic expectations gave hope to the remaining Bábís, and framed [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]]'s claim to be [[he-whom-god-shall-make-manifest|He Whom God Shall Make Manifest]] (lines 22497–22514).

## Bahá'u'lláh's Millenarian Vision
Bahá'u'lláh laid claim to being the promised one of other religions, and prophesied the establishment of the millennial future Most Great [[bahai-peace|Peace]]. There were apocalyptic elements in his vision. The present situation of the world was 'lamentably defective', with injustice, discord and corruption prevalent. The 'signs of impending convulsions and chaos' were evident, and the 'winds of despair' were blowing in every direction (TB 171) (lines 22515–22531).

## Western Expansion and Christian Adventism
The [[expansion-bahai|expansion]] of the Bahá'í Faith to the West (from 1894) brought the religion into more immediate contact with the Christian tradition. Early American Bahá'ís linked their faith to Christian adventist expectation. The pioneer (and heterodox) Bahá'í teacher I.G. [[kheiralla|Kheiralla]] taught his converts to see 'Abdu'l-Bahá in Christ-like terms and to expect the Great Peace to be established in 1917. In the aftermath of World War I a number of Bahá'ís continued to believe the Most Great Peace would soon be established (lines 22532–22546).

## Shoghi Effendi's Metahistorical Schema
[[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] developed a metahistorical schema combining:
- A millennial vision of the destined but seemingly far-distant future Peace
- Apocalyptic warnings of immediate sufferings (governmental collapse, anarchy, political oppression, racial strife, the burning of cities) to be experienced by an as yet unregenerate humanity resisting the impulse towards world unity

Greater Bahá'í teaching activity would hasten the advent of peace, and the pressure of events would cause the world's governments to establish the Lesser Peace of their own accord. Strongly apocalyptic themes became an important element in the thinking of the dissident C.M. [[remey|Remey]] and his followers (lines 22547–22571).
