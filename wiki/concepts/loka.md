# Loka (Jain Cosmology)

The *loka* ("world"; sometimes *triloka*, "triple world") is the Jain conception of the universe — a vast but finite structure that is the arena of action, rebirth, and the attainment of liberation. "The loka is both a shorthand designation for the five basic ontological categories of souls, motion, rest, atomic matter and space which permeate the universe (*Bh* 13.4) and, in its more usual, concrete sense, the massive structure which contains the heavens and the hells, along with the system of island continents divided by mountain ranges and surrounded by oceans which lies at its centre" (lines 79–85).

## Etymology and Derivation

Although *loka* originally meant "open space," Jain teachers preferred to derive it from the verbal root *lok*, "to see," explaining it as "that which is seen by the omniscient ones" (Abhayadeva on *Sth* 5–6) or "that which is seen by the soul" (*TSRV* p. 455) (lines 76–79). This etymology grounds cosmology in the authority of [[kevala-jnana|omniscient knowledge]].

## Structure

The classical description, found already in the "Exposition of Explanations," describes the *loka* as "expanded at the bottom, narrow in the middle and broad in its upper dimensions" (*Bh* 5.9) (lines 113–115). Later cosmographical texts (early centuries CE onwards) describe it in "fantastic mensural complexity," and it is also depicted as the *loka-purusa*, "the universe in the shape of a giant man (*purusa*), or sometimes woman, measuring fourteen ropes from head to foot," frequently depicted in Jain art from about the sixteenth century (lines 108–110). The unit of measurement is the *rajju*: "the distance travelled by a god flying for six months at a speed of ten million miles a second" (lines 105–107).

### Middle World

The central strip is the **Middle World** — "only one rope wide and one hundred thousand leagues high" but of the greatest significance, "since it is inhabited by human beings, the only creatures who can attain enlightenment in the course of their lives" (lines 123–127). It consists of alternating oceans and continents; the central continent, **Jambudvipa** ("The Island of the Roseapple Tree"), together with Dhatakikhanda and half of Puskaradvipa, forms the abode of mankind (lines 129–131). Within Jambudvipa, the regions of **Bharata** (India), **Airavata**, and **Mahavideha** are *karmabhumi* — "lands where fordmakers appear to preach and where motivated religious action can come to fruition" (lines 133–135). Mahavideha is especially notable: "the influence of the current corrupt world age does not hold sway there … it is a perpetual karmabhumi where fordmakers are preaching at this very moment" (lines 136–139).

### Hells Below

Below the Middle World lies "a series of hells, each successively darker, more dismal and unpleasant, where hellbeings suffer grievously at each other's hands and through the tortures inflicted by their demonic gaolers. Underneath the bottommost hell, there are no habitations of any sort, only clouds (*Bh* 6.8)" (lines 140–144). The torment is finite: "after lengthy expiation in hell rebirth must at some point ensue" (lines 151–152).

### Heavens Above

Above the Middle World are heavens of increasing brightness whose divine inhabitants "lead lives of pleasure reminiscent of those of earthly monarchs." However, gods are not objects of adoration: "their situation is ultimately profoundly unsatisfactory since, after the disappearance of their stock of merit which enabled them to be reborn as gods, they will fall from heaven" (lines 147–151).

### The Slightly Curving Place (*Isatpragbhara*)

At "the very crown of the *loka*" is the *Isatpragbhara* ("The Slightly Curving Place"), "shaped like a parasol and where the liberated and disembodied souls live, experiencing pure knowledge, energy and bliss, without any further rebirth" (lines 153–157). This is the realm of the *siddhas*, the fully liberated [[jiva|jivas]].

### The *Aloka*

Outside the *loka* is the *aloka* (non-*loka*), "where there is nothing except strong winds" (line 86). Souls and atoms cannot exist in the *aloka* because of the absence there of the entity of motion (dharma) (lines 309–310).

## Eternity and Atheism

"The *loka* is without beginning or end in time and was not brought into existence through the agency of any divine being. To this extent, Jainism is an atheist religion, inasmuch as it regards it as an illegitimate conclusion that there is a conscious creator who can intervene in or control the affairs of living creatures" (lines 87–91). Jain arguments against a creator god include: a bodiless creator would lack a locus for intention and effort; an embodied god would not be all-pervading; a non-pervading god would have component parts and thus be non-eternal. "Deities such as Brahma and Visnu … are themselves subject to the process of rebirth in the same manner as all other embodied souls in the *loka*" (*VTP* pp. 42–67) (lines 98–101).

## Doctrinal Function

The *loka* serves multiple purposes: "It provides a framework within which the manifold nature of human destiny can be structured, a backdrop for a vast narrative literature of pious exemplification and a focus for contemplation" (lines 170–173). The "massive dimensions of the *loka* and the insignificant space occupied by the karmabhumis … serve as a reminder of the rarity and value of human birth and the limited confines in which serious religious activity can be conducted" (lines 173–176). Dundas compares the *loka* functionally to Manichaean cosmography, arguing that "the classical description of the *loka* … while admittedly drawing on material also found in Hinduism, is uniquely Jain … The *loka* is thus an emblem of religious identity and separateness through which the Jains could differentiate themselves from other religions" (lines 195–204).

## Modern Significance

The *loka* was incorporated into the common symbol of the Jain religion adopted in 1975. Both the Digambara and Svetambara communities have recently "expend[ed] large sums of money … to construct scale replicas of the *loka* at Hastinapur and Palitana respectively" (lines 164–168). Some Jains accept it as an exact portrayal of physical reality; others reconcile it with terrestrial geography; most "would on reflection be happy to subscribe to two cosmographical systems, one relating to the everyday, transactional world, the other to the more profound symbolic realm of religion" (lines 211–213).
