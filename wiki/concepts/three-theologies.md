# The Three Theologies (*theologia tripertita*)

The classification of theology into three kinds — **mythical/fabulous**, **natural/physical**, and
**civil** — formulated by [[varro|Varro]] and transmitted to the Christian West through
[[augustine|Augustine]]'s [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Books VI–VIII, where Augustine adopts the scheme
as a framework and then turns it against pagan religion.

## The Scheme (Varro's)

- **Mythical / fabulous** (*genus mythicon*) — the gods of the **poets**, "adapted to the theatre,"
  containing "many fictions contrary to the dignity and nature of the immortals" (gods who steal, commit
  adultery, are wounded).
- **Natural / physical** (*genus physicon*) — the theology of the **philosophers**, "fitted for the
  world," disputed inside the schools: whether the gods are fire, number, atoms, or the world-soul.
- **Civil** (*genus civile*) — the theology of the **state**, "fitted for the city," the public cult that
  priests and magistrates must know and administer — which gods to worship, with which rites and
  sacrifices.

## Augustine's Use

Augustine accepts the tripartition heuristically but collapses Varro's defenses:

- **The civil is no better than the fabulous** (Book VI): the theatre stands *in the city* and was
  instituted *by the state*; the same obscene gods appear in both, and the gods themselves demanded the
  obscene plays. Civil theology therefore merely incorporates the fabulous it pretends to disown.
- **The natural/physical allegories fail** (Book VII): reading each god as a force of nature (Janus = the
  world, Saturn = seeds) is internally contradictory and, even if coherent, terminates in "things
  corporeal, temporal, mutable, and mortal" — never reaching the true, immutable God or *eternal life*.
- **Natural theology, rightly pursued, points beyond paganism** (Book VIII): the [[plato|Platonists]]
  carried natural theology furthest, grasping God as immaterial and immutable — yet still conceded
  worship to many gods and demons. Only the worship of the one true God ([[sacrifice|latreia]], Book X) answers what
  the three theologies sought.

The scheme thus becomes, in Augustine's hands, an instrument of *praeparatio evangelica*: pagan religion
convicted out of its own most learned theologian's mouth.
