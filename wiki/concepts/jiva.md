# Jiva (Jain Soul)

The *jiva* — usually translated "soul," though Dundas suggests "life-monad" as more accurate (line 221) — is the most fundamental of Jainism's five ontological categories (*astikaya*). All other categories collectively constitute the [[ajiva]] (non-soul). The *jiva* is "that which experiences and is responsible for all intellectual and spiritual operations" (*KA* 188), as opposed to the body, "which is merely a conglomerate of atoms" (lines 245–247).

## Intrinsic Nature

The *jiva* is defined by four key characteristics:
1. **Eternal** — though impermanent in respect of its modifications (line 240).
2. **Pure consciousness with innate will** — enabling it to act (line 242).
3. **Totally without form** — it is not a physical substance (line 243).
4. **Self-luminous** — "identical with knowledge and can therefore be internally grasped as an existent entity through that same knowledge" (Abhayadeva on *Sthr* 2, lines 248–250).

In their "purest state [all jivas] are identical and fully and equally endowed with the qualities of bliss, energy and omniscience" (lines 285–287). These qualities are not acquired but are the *jiva*'s natural constitution, obscured by karma.

## Expansion and Contraction

A distinctive Jain teaching is that the *jiva* "always expands and contracts according to the size of the body in which it is reborn, just as the light of a lamp always expands or contracts according to the size of the room or receptacle in which it is put" (lines 274–277). The *jiva* of a tiny insect and an elephant are thus the same *size* in principle, adapting to their host. This teaching is attributed in the Svetambara text "Questions of King Prasenajit" (*RP*) to the monk Kesin in dialogue with the empiricist king Prasenajit.

## The *Loka* Filled with *Jivas*

"The *loka* is eternally filled with *jivas* and, despite the immensity of its dimensions, there is not a single 'space point' (*pradesa*: the unit occupied by an atom) in which a *jiva* has not entered or left an existence" (*Bh* 12.7, lines 278–283).

## Classification of Embodied *Jivas*

Embodied *jivas* fall into two broad types:
- **Stationary (*sthavara*)**: plants and other one-sensed beings
- **Moving (*trasa*)**: insects, gods, hellbeings, animals, and human beings (lines 289–292)

They are further arranged in a hierarchy by number of senses:
| Senses | Examples |
|--------|---------|
| One | *nigoda* (microscopic life-clusters), earth-, air-, water-, fire-bodies, plants |
| Two | Worms |
| Three | Ants |
| Four | Flies |
| Five | Hellbeings, gods, animals, humans |

The *Dasavaikalika* (*DVS* 10.5) asserts that "the mark of a great monk is to realise that all of them are essentially like oneself" (lines 299–300) — the basis of Jainism's claim to universal compassion.

## *Nigoda*

The lowest forms of life are the *nigoda* — "infinitesimally small" beings that "do not themselves have bodies but live for extremely short periods of time in conglomerates in the bodies of other *jivas*" (lines 761–763). Some nigodas are condemned to remain in that existence eternally; others can "mount the Jain evolutionary scale and eventually gain human birth" (*KA* 284–90, lines 764–766).

## The *Jiva* and Karma

Although the *jiva* is non-material and formless, karma — conceived in Jainism as a fine physical substance — adheres to it through the passions (*kasaya*). "The binding of atoms on to the soul brought about by being wetted by false belief develops to karma in the same way as seeds, fruit and flowers thrown into a pot ferment into wine" (*TSRV* p. 566, lines 398–401). When karma is eliminated, the *jiva* regains its innate qualities of bliss, energy, and [[kevala-jnana|omniscience]].

## The *Jiva* at Death and Rebirth

"At death, the *jiva* leaves its body and progresses to its next place of birth virtually instantaneously" (line 616). The transition is described as "leaping like a monkey" (*Bh* 25.8) (line 636). During transit, it "is sheathed by subtle and invisible karmic and luminous bodies … which facilitate the speedy process of rebirth and provide a degree of continuity in the course of transmigration" (*TS* 2.41–3, lines 640–643).

## The Liberated *Jiva* (*Siddha*)

On gaining full liberation (*moksha*), the *jiva* "rises through innate capability in one instant … to the realm of the *siddhas*, the liberated *jivas* at the top of the universe where it will exist perpetually without any further rebirth in a disembodied and genderless state of perfect joy, energy, consciousness and knowledge" (*Aup* pp. 286–314 and *TS* 10, lines 713–720). Despite all liberated *jivas* being identical in quality and interpenetrating each other, "Jainism fiercely resists the possibility of their constituting a unified world-soul of the non-dualistic variety found in Hinduism" (lines 729–730).

## Spiritual Categories: *Bhavya* and *Abhavya*

"Jainism dichotomises all *jivas* into two spiritual categories: the *bhavya* ('capable') who has the potential to attain liberation and the *abhavya* ('incapable') who at no point in the limitless future will be capable of achieving this goal" (lines 745–748). The sectarian dispute over Makkhali Gosala's fate — bhavya (Svetambara) or abhavya (Digambara) — illustrates the significance of this distinction (lines 753–757).

## Kundakunda's Mystical Reading

For [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]], the *jiva* is "the only true and ultimate category in existence" (line 873), and all other entities and practices are to be evaluated from the standpoint of the *jiva*'s pure inner nature. He argues that the soul "cannot be in any meaningful way modified by contact with karmic matter" (*NiyS* 159–66), and therefore the goal of religion is the *jiva*'s recognition of its own already-liberated nature. This re-centres the entire religious life on inward experience rather than external practice.
