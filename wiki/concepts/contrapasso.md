# Contrapasso

*Contrapasso* ("counter-suffering") is the principle, governing the punishments of
Dante's [[inferno|Inferno]], by which each penalty mirrors or inverts the sin —
the soul's eternal condition made into a fitting image of what it chose in life.
The lustful, who let passion sweep them, are blown forever on a wind; the
soothsayers, who tried to see too far ahead, walk with heads twisted backward; the
schismatics, who tore communities apart, are themselves hacked apart.

## The Term

Strikingly, the word appears only **once** in the whole *Commedia*, spoken by the
troubadour Bertran de Born — who carries his own severed head — in Inferno 28:142:
"Since persons so close-linked I put apart, / so I, alas, apart now bear my brain…
In me, then, counter-suffering can be seen." Kirkpatrick notes that this single use
"is sometimes taken to characterize the whole vengeance system of the Inferno."

## Relation to the Plan of Hell

Contrapasso operates within Dante's larger graded scheme (see [[inferno|Inferno]]
and [[hell|Hell]]), in which sins are ordered by how deliberately they corrupt
reason: intemperance, then violence, then fraud, then treachery. The gate of Hell
declares the whole apparatus a work of divine "Justice… Wisdom… and Primal Love"
(Inf. 3:4–6) — punishment as the intelligible outworking of the sinner's own
choice, not arbitrary cruelty. The damned even "desire" their punishment (Inf.
3:124–25), fear converted by divine justice into longing.
