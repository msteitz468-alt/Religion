## Definition

*Ma'aseh Bereshit* (Hebrew: מַעֲשֵׂה בְּרֵאשִׁית, "Account/Work of Creation") is the rabbinic term for the cosmological and natural-philosophical teachings that Jewish tradition treats as esoteric — unsuitable for public exposition or instruction of the unprepared. In [[maimonides|Maimonides]]' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]], it belongs to the cluster of "Secrets of the Law" (*sitre torah*) and "Mysteries" (*sodot*) that demand careful, graduated pedagogy.

## Maimonides' Treatment in Part I, Chs. XXXI–XXXV

The chapters immediately preceding the homonymous-term series (Chs. XXXI–XXXV) function as the epistemological and pedagogical preamble to the entire work. Maimonides here establishes *why* the deep doctrines of the Law — including those touching on creation and natural science — must be taught esoterically.

### The Esoteric Framework

In Ch. XXXIII, Maimonides identifies the "true principles of science" as having been:

> "presented in enigmas, clad in riddles, and taught by all wise men in the most mysterious way that could be devised, not because they contain some secret evil … but because of the incapacity of man to comprehend them at the beginning of his studies: only slight allusions have been made to them to serve for the guidance of those who are capable of understanding them. These sciences were, therefore, called Mysteries (*sodoth*), and Secrets of the Law (*sitre torah*)." (Ch. XXXIII)

The esoteric character of these doctrines is not a temporary pedagogical convenience but reflects the limits of human intellect and the danger of premature metaphysical speculation.

### Ma'aseh Mercabah as Metaphysics

Ch. XXXIV explicitly identifies *Ma'aseh Mercabah* (the Account of the Chariot) with Metaphysics, the highest intellectual discipline:

> "they must have become moderate and settled, humble in their hearts, and subdued in their temperament; only then will they be able to arrive at the highest degree of the perception of God, i.e., the study of Metaphysics, which is called *Ma'aseh Mercabah*." (Ch. XXXIV)

**Note (faithfulness):** This range (Chs. XXVII–LVI) does not contain an explicit equivalent statement equating *Ma'aseh Bereshit* with Physics or natural science. That identification — Maimonides' famous parallel between Ma'aseh Bereshit = Physics and Ma'aseh Mercabah = Metaphysics — is made in other chapters of Part I (outside this range). What this range establishes is the general esoteric framework and the identification of Ma'aseh Mercabah with Metaphysics.

### The Preparatory Curriculum

Ch. XXXIV prescribes an ordered curriculum for approaching the esoteric doctrines:

> "he who wishes to attain to human perfection, must therefore first study Logic, next the various branches of Mathematics in their proper order, then Physics, and lastly Metaphysics." (Ch. XXXIV)

This progression — Logic → Mathematics → Physics → Metaphysics — underlies Maimonides' assessment of Ma'aseh Bereshit and Ma'aseh Mercabah as requiring extensive preparation. Without mastery of natural science, the student cannot understand:

> "the defect implied in emotions, the difference between potentiality and reality, the non-existence implied in all potentiality." (Ch. LV)

### Five Reasons for Esoteric Teaching

Ch. XXXIV offers five reasons why Metaphysics (and by extension the Secrets of the Law) must not be taught publicly:

1. The subject is intrinsically difficult and subtle.
2. Human intelligence begins in potentiality, not in actuality.
3. Preparatory studies are lengthy and many abandon them prematurely.
4. Moral constitution must precede intellectual perfection: "moral conduct is a preparation for intellectual progress" (Ch. XXXIV).
5. Material needs and distractions impede sustained study.

### The Rabbinic Restriction

The rabbinic rule governing transmission is quoted in Ch. XXXIII:

> "the principle of our Sages 'not to discuss the *Ma'aseh Mercabah* even in the presence of one pupil, except he be wise and intelligent; and then only the headings of the chapters are to be given to him.'" (Ch. XXXIII)

The Talmud further restricts this: "The headings of the sections must only be handed down to an *Ab-bet-din* (President of the Court), whose heart is full of care, i.e., in whom wisdom is united with humility, meekness, and a great dread of sin" (Ch. XXXIV). And the Talmudic anecdote in Ch. XXXIV: R. Jochanan offered to teach R. Elasar *Ma'aseh Mercabah*, who replied, "I am not yet old" — i.e., not yet past the hot blood and rashness of youth.

### Object of the Torah's Figurative Language

Ch. XXXIII connects the Torah's figurative style directly to the need to protect these esoteric doctrines:

> "This also is the reason why 'the Torah speaks the language of man' … for it is the object of the Torah to serve as a guide for the instruction of the young, of women, and of the common people; and as all of them are incapable to comprehend the true sense of the words, tradition was considered sufficient to convey all truths which were to be established." (Ch. XXXIII)

The "Secrets of the Law" are thus what the Guide is designed to unlock for the qualified reader — reading beneath the surface of scriptural figurative language to the philosophical doctrines concealed within.

## Relationship to the Doctrine of Divine Attributes

The esoteric-teaching framework of Chs. XXXI–XXXV is the backdrop for the entire attribute-negation argument (Chs. L–LVI). The inadmissibility of positive essential attributes, the purely homonymous use of terms like "existence," "wisdom," and "power" when applied to God (Ch. LVI), and Moses' unique knowledge of God through His actions (Ch. LIV) all constitute part of the inner content that the Torah's figurative language conceals and the Guide partially reveals.
