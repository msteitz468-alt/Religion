# Golden Age of Tech

The **Golden Age of Tech** (GAT) is a major revision to **Scientology training and auditing procedures** announced by [[david-miscavige|David Miscavige]] in **1996** (piloted at the [[church-of-scientology|Flag Land Base]] in Clearwater in **1995**). It represents the most significant institutional overhaul of Scientology's technical delivery since [[l-ron-hubbard|Hubbard]]'s death.

## Overview

Miscavige presented the Golden Age of Tech as a correction of accumulated errors in how Scientology training had been delivered since Hubbard's death — a restoration of "standard tech" to a purer form. Critics within the ex-member community characterize it differently: as Miscavige's personal imposition on Hubbard's methodology, amounting to an alteration of what Hubbard intended.

The practical effect of GAT was to **eliminate individual judgment from the auditing process**: auditors were required to follow exact, scripted procedures with no deviation — reducing auditing to a technical drill rather than a responsive interpersonal process. Hubbard had always emphasized that auditing required the auditor's judgment and communication skill; GAT critics argue this was stripped out.

## Origin — "A Talk on a Basic Qual"

**Marc Headley** (*Blown for Good*, 2009) provides the most granular account in this wiki of GAT's origins:

- GAT was born from Miscavige's work with a **Hubbard tape** titled **"A Talk on a Basic Qual"** — an address on the purpose and structure of the Qualifications Division
- Miscavige used this tape as the basis for a systematic revision of all training checksheets and drill sequences
- The process was piloted at Flag (Clearwater) in **1995**, with Miscavige personally overseeing implementation
- **Weekly income at Flag** jumped from approximately **$1 million+** per week to significantly higher figures in the immediate aftermath of the GAT rollout — a metric Miscavige cited as proof of its success
- **RTC** (Miscavige's organizational arm) enforced strict compliance with the new standards across all Scientology organizations worldwide

## Institutional Context

The Golden Age of Tech rollout in 1995–96 coincided with two other significant events at Flag in 1995:

1. **Lisa McPherson**'s death (December 1995) — she was at Flag under Miscavige's personal supervision during the GAT pilot period
2. The early development of the **[[super-power|Super Power]]** rundown project, which Miscavige was also directing from Flag

Headley places all three in the same institutional moment — a period of intense personal involvement by Miscavige at the Flag Land Base.

## Controversy

Many former senior Scientology staff — including **Marty Rathbun** (Miscavige's former second-in-command, post-departure writings not yet ingested) — have argued that the Golden Age of Tech was not a restoration of Hubbard's intention but a departure from it. This view holds that GAT fundamentally changed what auditors do, reducing skilled practice to rote procedure.

**The Church's position** is that GAT corrected decades of sloppy training and restored Hubbard's actual intentions. The two sides are irreconcilable on first principles: one holds that Miscavige has the authority to refine Hubbard's tech; the other holds that any change to the tech by anyone other than Hubbard is a violation of Scientology's foundational "Source" doctrine.

A **Golden Age of Tech Phase II** was announced approximately 2013, representing a further revision. Not yet ingested.

**Contradiction Protocol**: the Church's and ex-members' accounts are recorded without adjudication.

## Hermeneutical Significance

The Golden Age of Tech controversy is one of the most illuminating cases for understanding Scientology's hermeneutics:

- Hubbard established a radically **anti-interpretive hermeneutic** — "standard tech" must be applied verbatim; commentary and interpretation are forbidden
- Yet GAT demonstrates that some post-Hubbard revision is inevitable, and whoever controls that revision controls the tradition
- Miscavige's authority thus rests on a paradox: he claims to be protecting the no-change doctrine while making substantial changes

This connects to the broader controversy over [[scientology-religion-or-commercial-enterprise|Scientology as religion vs. commercial enterprise]] and the question of who holds legitimate authority in a tradition whose founder is dead and whose doctrine forbids revision.
