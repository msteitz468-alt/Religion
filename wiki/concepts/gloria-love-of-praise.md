# Glory and the Love of Praise (*gloria*, *amor laudis*)

[[augustine|Augustine]]'s account, in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Book V**, of the **love of glory**
as the human motive God used and rewarded in building the Roman empire — a vice that nonetheless
suppressed worse vices, and the defining drive of the [[two-cities|earthly city]].

## The Argument

Drawing on Sallust, Augustine grants that the early Romans were "greedy of praise, prodigal of wealth,
desirous of great glory, and content with a moderate fortune." The **love of praise is a vice** — but a
*lesser* one, "nearer to virtue than avarice," which restrained graver vices (greed, cruelty, lust) and
produced genuine public service and self-sacrifice: Brutus, Torquatus, Camillus, Mucius Scaevola,
Curtius, the Decii, Regulus, Cincinnatus, Fabricius.

God "purposely granted [empire] to such men as, for the sake of honor, and praise, and glory, consulted
well for their country." But because they sought *human* glory rather than God, their reward is purely
terrestrial and temporal: "they have received their reward" (Matt. 6:2). True virtue is referred to God
alone and seeks "glory from God" rather than from men (John 12:43).

## Significance

The concept marks the precise boundary between the two cities: the same self-sacrificing deeds, done for
the love of human praise, build the earthly city; done for the love of God, they build the heavenly one.
It also frames Augustine's treatment of [[lucretia|Lucretia]] (suicide from "proud dread" of opinion) and
his portrait of the truly *felicitous* Christian emperor, who rules justly and "is not lifted up amid the
praises of those who pay him sublime honors."
