# The Beatific Vision

The eternal, face-to-face sight and enjoyment of God by the saints — the final felicity of the
[[two-cities|city of God]], treated in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XXII (ch. 29, "Of the Beatific
Vision") and its closing chapter.

## Augustine's account

Augustine confesses he is "at a loss to understand the nature of that employment, or… repose and ease,"
which surpasses understanding (Phil. 4:7). Now "we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face"
(1 Cor. 13:12). He leaves open the difficult question whether God will be seen **by the bodily eyes** of
the resurrection body, as we now see the sun, or by the spirit alone. His fuller view: "God will be so
known by us, and shall be so much before us, that we shall see Him by the spirit in ourselves, in one
another, in Himself, in the new heavens and the new earth, in every created thing" — and also, somehow,
through the transformed body's "keen vision."

## The eternal Sabbath

The vision is the rest of the **eternal Sabbath**. Augustine lays out the seven ages of history (Adam to
the flood; to Abraham; to Christ; the present sixth, unnumbered); after them "God shall rest as on the
seventh day," giving the saints — who "shall be the seventh day" — rest in Himself, crowned by an "eighth
and eternal day… consecrated by the resurrection of Christ." The saints retain *intellectual* memory of
past ills (so as to "for ever sing the mercies of God") without experiential suffering; degrees of glory
exist, yet "no inferior shall envy any superior," as the finger does not envy the eye.

The work ends on its most famous formula: **"There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise.
This is what shall be in the end without end."** The blessed will see God "without end, loved without
cloy, praised without weariness." This presupposes the [[resurrection|resurrection body]]'s confirmed
freedom — the *non posse peccare*, the inability to sin received from God.

## Aquinas's account

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] gives the beatific vision a precise scholastic framework at
[[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q12 ("How God is known by us") and I-II, Q3, A8 (that
man's final happiness consists in it). Against those who denied that any creature could see God's
essence, he holds it "absolutely granted that the blessed see the essence of God" (I, Q12, A1) —
otherwise the rational creature's natural desire to know the first cause of what it sees "would
remain void." Key determinations:

- **By the light of glory, not by any created image** (Q12, A2, A5). No created likeness can
  represent the divine essence; instead God's essence is united directly to the intellect, which is
  raised to this by a supernatural disposition, the *lumen gloriae* — "In Thy light we shall see
  light" (Ps. 35:10). By it the blessed are made **"deiform."**
- **By the intellect, not the bodily eye** (Q12, A3); and **not by nature but by grace** (Q12, A4):
  "to see the essence of God is possible to the created intellect by grace, and not by nature," for
  to know self-subsistent being naturally belongs to the divine intellect alone.
- **God is seen but not comprehended** (Q12, A7). The infinite God is "infinitely knowable," but no
  finite light of glory lets a created intellect know Him infinitely. Aquinas distinguishes
  *comprehension* as enclosure (denied of all creatures) from *comprehension* as attainment (the
  blessed do attain God) — pairing vision with faith, comprehension with hope, and fruition with charity.
- **Degrees of glory** (Q12, A6): "one sees Him more perfectly than another," according to a fuller
  share of the light of glory, which follows greater **charity** — "Star differs from star in glory"
  (1 Cor. 15:41). This grounds the same gradation Augustine affirms above.
- **Not in this life, save by rapture** (Q12, A11): the wayfarer cannot see God's essence while the
  soul is bound to mortal corporeal life — "Man shall not see Me, and live" (Exod. 33:20) — except by
  a miraculous elevation such as Aquinas (with Augustine) ascribes to Moses and Paul.
- **Natural reason still reaches God's existence** (Q12, A12): from sensible effects we can know
  "whether God exists" and what must belong to the first cause (Rom. 1:19–20; see [[five-ways]]),
  though not His essence — and grace gives a yet higher knowledge (Q12, A13).

In the *Prima Secundae* Aquinas makes this the *telos* of human life: "final and perfect happiness
can consist in nothing else than the vision of the Divine Essence" (I-II, Q3, A8), an act of the
**speculative intellect** that no created good and no natural power can supply (I-II, Q2, A8; Q5,
A5). See [[beatitude]].

## Dante's poetic vision (Paradiso 33)

[[dante-alighieri|Dante]]'s [[paradiso|Paradiso]] gives the doctrine its supreme
**imaginative** rendering. The whole cantica is built on Aquinas's principle that
**beatitude rests first on sight, then love** (Dante siding explicitly with the
Dominicans over the Franciscans, Par. 28:109–11), and on the **ineffability** of a
vision that exceeds memory and language — a topos escalating from Canto 23 to its
climax. In the final canto, God appears as creation gathered "in a single book," then
as the **three circles of the Trinity**, and within the second circle the **human
image** (the Incarnation), which defeats the intellect "as some geometer" fails to
square the circle — until "my mind was stricken through / by sudden lightning"
(33:140–41). Consistent with Aquinas's *degrees of glory* and contented wills (cf.
[[piccarda-donati|Piccarda]], "In His volition is the peace we have"), the poem ends
not with sight but with the **will harmonized** "by love that moves the sun and other
stars" (33:145).
