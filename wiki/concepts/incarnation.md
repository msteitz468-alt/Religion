# The Incarnation

The **Incarnation** (Latin *incarnatio*, "enfleshing") is the central Christian doctrine that
the eternal Son of God, the Word, assumed a complete human nature in the one Person of
[[jesus|Jesus Christ]] — true God and true man. The fullest scholastic treatment is
[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s, which opens the [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] Tertia
Pars (TP QQ1–26). Aquinas inherits the conciliar settlement (Nicaea, Ephesus, Chalcedon,
Constantinople II–III) and renders it in Aristotelian metaphysical vocabulary — *natura*,
*persona*, *suppositum*, *hypostasis* — drawing above all on **John Damascene**, Augustine,
Pseudo-Dionysius, Boethius, and Cyril.

## The patristic motive: Athanasius's "Divine Dilemma" (*On the Incarnation*)

Centuries before the scholastic synthesis, [[athanasius|Athanasius]]'s
[[on-the-incarnation|*On the Incarnation*]] (c. 318) gives the classic patristic account of *why*
the Word became flesh, structured around the **"Divine Dilemma"** (§§6–10). Humanity, made in the
[[imago-dei|Image]] and meant for incorruption, fell into **corruption and non-being** (*phthora*)
through sin (§4, "evil is non-being"). This sets God's **goodness** (which cannot let his creatures
perish) against his **truthfulness** (which cannot revoke the sentence of death). Repentance is
insufficient — it cannot undo *corruption* and would leave God "untrue" (§7). Only the Word, who made
all from nothing, can both "recreate all" and "maintain for the Father His consistency."

The solution has a **double purpose**: (1) the Word takes a real body "as an instrument" and offers
it to death "instead of all," so that "in His death all might die, and the law of death thereby be
abolished" — the death of the one body indwelt by "the Word who was above all" being a "sufficient
exchange for all" (§§8–9); and (2) the **Image himself re-draws the defaced portrait** on the same
panel (the simile of §14), restoring the knowledge of God that creation, law, and prophets had failed
to keep alive. Athanasius caps it with the deification formula: "He, indeed, assumed humanity that we
might become God" (§54; see [[theosis-divine-nature]]). His "instrument/temple" Christology (the body
as the Word's *organon*) is characteristically Alexandrian and says little of a human rational soul —
the gap later Christology closed with the axiom "what is not assumed is not healed" (Damascene),
treated below. Note this is an **early, pre-Arian** work: its opponents are Jews and Gentiles, not
[[arianism|Arians]]. Compare Aquinas's later, juridically framed treatment of the same "fittingness"

## Fitness and motive of the Incarnation (TP Q1)

Aquinas argues the Incarnation was **fitting** (*conveniens*), not strictly necessary: "it
belongs to the essence of goodness to communicate itself to others" (Dionysius), and there was
"not a more fitting way of healing our misery" (Augustine) — God became man "that man might be
made God" (Q1 A1–A2). It was *necessary* only in the secondary sense of the most convenient
means. On the question **whether God would have become incarnate had man not sinned**, Aquinas
takes the more probable view that the Incarnation was ordained as the **remedy for sin** — "had
sin not existed, the Incarnation would not have been; and yet the power of God is not limited to
this" (Q1 A3) — registering, without fully adjudicating, the contrary opinion (later the Scotist
position) that the Word would have become incarnate regardless. He cites the *felix culpa*: "O
happy fault, that merited such and so great a Redeemer" (Q1 A3).

The infinity of the offense against God grounds his account of **[[atonement|condign
satisfaction]]**: "a sin committed against God has a kind of infinity from the infinity of the
Divine majesty," so condign satisfaction required "an act of infinite efficiency, as being of God
and man" (Q1 A2) — only the God-man can satisfy.

## The mode of union (TP Q2–Q6): the [[hypostatic-union]]

The union is **not in the nature** (against monophysitism / Eutyches and Dioscorus, who held
"one incarnate nature" resulting from two) but **in the Person** — the one *hypostasis* or
*suppositum* of the Word (Q2 A1–A3). Aquinas deploys the distinction between **nature** (the
*quid est*) and **suppositum/person** (the subsisting *who*): in composite beings the two really
differ; the human nature of Christ is real and complete but subsists in the divine Person rather
than in a created human person. "The hand of Socrates is not a person, because it does not exist
by itself"; just so the assumed humanity, existing in something nobler, is no second person
(Q2 A2). Against **Nestorius** and Theodore of Mopsuestia (who made the union one
of indwelling, dignity, or "equivocation" — an *accidental* union of two persons), Aquinas insists
on a single subsisting *who*, citing Ephesus's anathemas. The Person of Christ is therefore
"composite" only "by reason of number" — one being subsisting in two natures (Q2 A4). Full
treatment: [[hypostatic-union]].

He affirms the **complete humanity**: a true earthly body (against Docetism — Manes, Valentine),
a rational **soul** and a human **mind/intellect** (against Apollinaris and
Arius, who denied Christ a human soul or mind), on the axiom "what was not assumed is not curable"
(Damascene) (Q5). The order of assumption is logical, not temporal: the Word assumes flesh through
the medium of the soul, and the parts through the whole, all simultaneously at conception (Q6).

## The grace and knowledge of Christ (TP Q7–Q12)

- **Grace of union vs. habitual grace.** The **grace of union** is "the personal being given
  *gratis* from above to the human nature in the Person of the Word" — the term of the assumption
  itself; **habitual grace** is its effect, flowing into Christ's soul "as light from the sun"
  (Q2 A10; Q6 A6; Q7 A13). Christ's habitual grace is at the highest possible degree, cannot
  increase, and overflows to all (Q7 A9–A12); Lk 2:52 ("advanced in wisdom and grace") refers to
  its *effects*, not the habit.
- **Christ as [[the-guru|Head]] of the Church** (Q8): Christ alone is Head by *intrinsic influx of
  grace* (others — the Pope, bishops — are heads only by exterior governance). The mystical body
  includes the angels; membership has five degrees (in glory, charity, faith, predestination, mere
  potentiality). *(Internal link is to the wiki's existing leadership concept where relevant; the
  doctrine itself is Aquinas's "grace of headship.")*
- **Christ's threefold human knowledge** (Q9–Q12): besides the divine knowledge, Christ's soul has
  (1) **beatific** knowledge — He saw the divine essence "from the first moment of His conception,"
  though without *comprehending* the infinite (Q9 A2; Q10 A1); (2) **infused/imprinted** knowledge,
  analogous to the angels' "morning/evening knowledge" (Q9 A3; Q11); and (3) **acquired/empiric**
  knowledge from the active intellect — a point on which Aquinas explicitly **corrects his earlier
  self** (the *Sentences* commentary) to affirm that Christ's acquired knowledge truly grew (Q9 A4;
  Q12 A2). Christ learned from no man and from no angel (Q12 A3–A4).

## The consequences of the union (TP Q13–Q19)

- **Christ's defects** (Q14–Q15): He voluntarily *assumed* (did not *contract*) human bodily
  defects — hunger, thirst, pain, real sorrow, fear, and ordered anger — to satisfy for sin, prove
  His true humanity, and give example. But He had **no sin, no [[original-sin|*fomes peccati*]]**
  (the disordered "tinder" of concupiscence), **and no ignorance**, since these are incompatible
  with the fullness of His grace and cannot be ordered to satisfaction. He was at once **wayfarer
  (*viator*) and possessor (*comprehensor*)** — passible in the senses, beatified in the soul's
  height (Q15 A10).
- **[[communicatio-idiomatum|Communicatio idiomatum]]** (Q16): because there is one Person,
  properties of either nature may be predicated of the one Person concretely — "God is man" and
  "Man is God" are both true — but not abstract-of-abstract ("the Godhead is the manhood" is false),
  and qualified statements ("Christ is a creature," "this Man began to be") must carry the rider
  *in His human nature* lest they collapse into Arianism. See [[communicatio-idiomatum]].
- **Two wills and two operations** (Q18–Q19): against **monothelitism** (and Apollinaris,
  Eutyches), Aquinas affirms **dyothelitism** — a divine and a human will in Christ — and two
  corresponding operations (dyoenergism), citing Constantinople III. The human will (and the
  sensitive appetite) genuinely shrank from death yet was without contrariety to the divine will:
  Gethsemane ("not as I will, but as Thou wilt") "shows two wills — the human, which shunned death,
  and the Divine, which accepted death" (Q18 A6). Christ's human acts are **meritorious**, for
  Himself and — through the grace of headship — for us, as Adam's sin reaches us by carnal
  generation so Christ's merit reaches us by spiritual regeneration in baptism (Q19 A3–A4).

## Subjection, priesthood, adoption, predestination, adoration, mediation (TP Q20–Q26)

- **Subjection** (Q20): "the Father is greater than I" is true of Christ *as man*, equal *as God*
  (Augustine).
- **[[priesthood-of-christ|Priesthood]]** (Q22): Christ is **both priest and victim** (*sacerdos et
  hostia*); His sacrifice expiates both the stain of sin and the debt of punishment; His priesthood
  is **eternal** in its abiding intercession and is **after the order of [[melchizedek|Melchizedek]]**
  (not the Levitical line) — prefigured by Melchizedek's offering of bread and wine, his being
  outside one tribe, and his being "without genealogy" (Heb 7).
- **Adoption** (Q23): God's adoptive sonship by grace is an act of the whole Trinity; Christ,
  however, is the **natural** Son, not an adopted son — adoption belongs to those who are *not*
  natural sons.
- **Predestination** (Q24): Christ as man is predestined; His predestination is the **exemplar and
  cause** of ours.
- **Adoration** (Q25): the one Person of Christ receives **one** adoration. Christ's humanity is
  adored with **latria** (the worship due God alone) because we adore the Person *through* it; the
  **image of Christ** and the **cross** receive latria (the honor of the image passing to its
  prototype, and the true cross also as a relic touched by His blood); the **Mother of God**
  receives **hyperdulia** (a veneration higher than the **dulia** due other saints, but not latria);
  the **relics of the saints** are venerated, against Vigilantius (Jerome). This latria/dulia/
  hyperdulia taxonomy is the same treated under [[religion-virtue|religion]] (II-II).
- **[[christ-as-mediator|Mediation]]** (Q26): Christ alone is the **perfect Mediator**, and is so
  *as man* — "He is Mediator, as man" (Augustine), having beatitude in common with God and mortality
  in common with men. Prophets, priests, and angels are mediators only "dispositively and
  ministerially"; the Holy Spirit, being equal to God, is no mediator. See [[christ-as-mediator]].

## Historical-critical origins: exaltation vs. incarnation ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

Where Aquinas and the Fathers expound the *settled* doctrine, [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]
([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) reconstructs its **historical
emergence**. He distinguishes two early Christological types: an **[[exaltation-christology|
exaltation Christology]]** "from below" (the human Jesus elevated to divine status at the
[[resurrection|resurrection]]) and an **incarnation Christology** "from above" (a pre-existent
divine being who "comes in the flesh"). On his reading incarnation Christology was *not* the
original view but developed as the moment of Jesus's divinity was pushed back from resurrection
to baptism to conception to pre-existence — though already present in [[paul-apostle|Paul]] (the
1 Cor 8:6 creed; Phil 2's pre-existent being; an [[angel-of-the-lord|angel Christology]],
Gal 4:14) "before our earliest Gospel was written." Its fullest New Testament form is the
Johannine [[logos-christology|Logos]] (John 1:1–18): "what preexisted was the Logos of God …
It was only when the Logos became a human being that Jesus Christ came into existence." Ehrman
sees the same incarnational language drawing on Jewish [[two-powers-in-heaven|Wisdom]] tradition
in Col 1:15–20 and Heb 1:2–4. (A historical-critical account, complementary to but methodologically
distinct from the conciliar/scholastic exposition above.)

## Hermeneutical and doctrinal frame

Aquinas reads the conciliar tradition as binding and labels the rejected positions **heresies, not
mere opinions**, repeatedly invoking the ecumenical councils (Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople
II–III) as the rule. His method is the *disputatio*: each article distinguishes the sense in which
a proposition is true from the sense in which it is heretical (the recurring move of "words spoken
amiss lead to heresy," Jerome). The Christology is structurally Chalcedonian — two natures, one
Person, *without confusion, change, division, or separation* — expressed through the
nature/suppositum distinction.
