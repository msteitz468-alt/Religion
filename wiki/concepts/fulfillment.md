# Fulfillment

## Definition and scope

"Fulfillment" (Greek *plēroō*) names the relation the [[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]] asserts
between the Jewish scriptures and the person and work of [[jesus|Jesus]]. In [[rt-france|R. T.
France]]'s NICNT commentary it is not one theme among others but the governing one: France
states that he has argued elsewhere that "the central theme of Matthew's gospel is
'fulfillment'" (Introduction IV).

The concept as France develops it is deliberately *broader* than predictive verification. He
holds that Matthean fulfillment covers not only the explicitly predictive portions of the Old
Testament but "its historical characters, its narratives, and its cultic patterns, even the law
itself" (citing Matt 5:17; 11:13). He describes it as "a matter of tracing lines of
correspondence and continuity in God's dealings with his people," discerned in incidental
textual details as much as in the grand design.

France distinguishes the *concept* from its most visible literary vehicle. The eleven
[[formula-quotations|formula-quotations]] are the technique; fulfillment is the theological
programme that generates them, and France notes further fulfillment statements that fall outside
the traditional list (3:3; 11:10; 13:14-15; 15:7-9; 21:42) as well as unspecified appeals to
scripture at 26:54, 56.

## Fulfillment as typology, not prediction

The load-bearing move in France's account is the substitution of pattern-recognition for
single-event prediction. Commenting on Isa 7:14 he writes that where modern readers expect one
specific fulfillment, "Matthew's typological interest leads him rather to find patterns which
will recur repeatedly throughout God's dealings with his people." His working definition, offered
as a defence of loose fit, is that "typology depends on meaningful associations rather than exact
correspondences." See [[typology|typology]].

France is unusually candid about the cost of this position, and states the difficulties in their
strongest form before answering:

- On Hos 11:1 (quoted at Matt 2:15) he says plainly that the verse refers to the exodus: "It is a
  statement about the past, not a prediction of the future," and acknowledges the charge that
  Matthew's use is therefore illegitimate. His answer is that "that is the essence of typology,
  which depends not on predictions but on transferable 'models' from the OT story," noting that
  the Old Testament itself had already made the exodus a symbol of greater deliverance (Isa
  43:16-21; Jer 31:31-34). See [[hosea|Hosea]].
- On Isa 7:14 he concedes that Isaiah's prophecy, "uttered to Ahaz in about the year 735 B.C., is
  not about an event in the distant future" but times the Assyrian devastation. He nonetheless
  argues that Isaiah's own thought moves progressively from the virgin's child through Isa 8:8,
  8:10, 9:6-7 to 11:1-5, so that the verse points beyond the eighth-century crisis "not only in
  Matthew's typological scheme but also in Isaiah's intention." See [[isaiah|Isaiah]].
- He notes that two of Matthew's infancy typologies "do not fit neatly together" — Jesus as new
  [[moses|Moses]] (escaping a royal infanticide, fleeing from and returning to Egypt) and Jesus as
  new [[israelites|Israel]] (going down to Egypt and coming back) run in opposite directions — and
  he lets the tension stand rather than harmonizing it.

France defends the method against the charge of anachronism by observing that Matthew "was not
writing for modern exegetical scholars," and that the significance he finds lies "not necessarily
in what the original authors of the OT texts had in mind but in what can be perceived in their
writings with Christian hindsight." He adds that readers who have studied Qumran and rabbinic
interpretation "recognize that they are on familiar ground in Matthew," both in his actual
interpretive methods and in his creative discovery of fulfillment patterns.

## The fulfillment of the law (Matt 5:17-20)

France treats 5:17-48 as a single coherent unit, "the most extensive discussion of this issue
anywhere in the gospel tradition," which raises acutely the question of Jesus' messianic authority
in relation to the Torah and its authorized interpreters. He reads Matthew as confronting two
opposite tendencies at once: an antinomian one in vv. 17-19 (that Old Testament laws no longer
matter, "in line with Paul's 'freedom from the law' teaching") and a scribal-literalist one in v.
20, illustrated by the [[antitheses|antitheses]] of vv. 21-47. See [[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on
the Mount]] and [[old-law|old law]] / [[new-law|new law]].

France names the hinge explicitly: "The key to this issue must be what is meant by Jesus
'fulfilling' the law and the prophets." His argument runs:

1. *katalyō* ("abolish"), used of dismantling buildings (24:2; 26:61; 27:40), means with reference
   to an authoritative text "to declare that it is no longer valid, to repeal or annul." The issue
   is therefore not Jesus' personal practice but his attitude to the law's authority. France holds
   it "not unlikely that there were in fact some who did suppose that Jesus was against the law,"
   citing the sabbath disputes (12:1-14; see [[sabbath|sabbath]]) and the persistence of the charge
   against his followers (Acts 6:11, 13-14; 21:28).
2. He rejects the reading of *plēroō* as "obey, do, keep the requirements": "'Fulfill' … would not
   be the natural way to say that, and such a sense would not answer the charge of aiming to
   'abolish.'"
3. He surveys every Matthean use of *plēroō* — ten formula-quotation occurrences (denoting "the
   coming into being of that to which Scripture pointed forward, whether by direct prediction or
   understood typologically"), 26:54, 56; 13:14 (*anaplēroō*); 3:15; 13:48; 23:32 — and finds that
   all but one carry the sense of reaching a destined conclusion.
4. The decisive grammatical observation: "this verse does not speak of Jesus 'fulfilling the law,'
   but rather of his 'fulfilling the law and the prophets.'" The law is therefore fulfilled in the
   same forward-pointing mode as prophecy.
5. The decisive supporting text is 11:13, "All the prophets and the law prophesied until John":
   "The law is thus linked with the prophets as looking forward to a time of fulfillment which has
   now arrived." France holds that "not only the prophets but even the law itself 'prophesied,'"
   and says this statement "helps to explain the 'fulfillment of the law' as it is expounded in
   5:17-48."

France's paraphrase of 5:17: "Far from wanting to set aside the law and the prophets, it is my
role to bring into being that to which they have pointed forward, to carry them into a new era of
fulfillment." His core formulation is that "the authority of the law and the prophets is not
abolished. They remain the authoritative word of God. But their role will no longer be the same" —
disciples must discern, in light of Jesus' teaching, how to apply those texts in the new situation.

On the jots and tittles of 5:18 he argues that the second "until" is "not speaking of the time of
their abandonment but of their intended goal": they "are there to be fulfilled, not discarded …
not lost, but taken up into the eschatological events to which they pointed forward." Where the
antitheses leave particular regulations aside — divorce rules when [[divorce|divorce]] does not
happen, oath rules when swearing is ruled out, retribution limits when even resistance is
renounced — France asks whether this is after all to abolish them and answers: "No, it is rather to
bypass them, to leave them behind as no longer needed."

Two further formulations of his push the account further than mere continuity. On the *lex
talionis* he writes that "the 'fulfillment' of the law (v. 17) here consists in leaving it behind
in favor of something of a different order altogether, the [[righteousness|righteousness]] of the
kingdom of heaven." His terminal gloss on 5:17 in this range: "It is in the promotion of this
standard of perfection, going far beyond the literal requirements of the OT law, that Jesus
'fulfills' it." France notes that this reading "has gained a considerable degree of assent in
recent decades, over against the older view of a legally conservative Matthew."

France reads 7:12's "this is the law and the prophets" as echoing 5:17 and thereby closing the
main body of the discourse, what follows being "a coda calling for decisive response."

## Continuity and discontinuity

France states that "elements of continuity and of discontinuity are equally prominent in Matthew's
theology of 'fulfillment.'" He locates Matthew's conviction in the claim that the future of the
[[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]] lies not in the institutions of Judaism but in "a newly
constituted people of God focused not on national origin but on allegiance to Jesus the Messiah."
Commenting on 8:11-12 he calls the east-and-west saying "one of the clearest statements of
Matthew's understanding of the relation between Israel and the new community," combining continuity
(the patriarchs remain at the head of the table) with discontinuity (newcomers admitted, "sons of
the kingdom" excluded).

A methodological corollary France insists on: "to establish what OT expressions referred to in
their original context is not to determine the way Jesus or Matthew may have developed such
language." He appeals to what he calls a "consistent NT hermeneutic which understands the
nationalistic and territorial promises of the OT in terms of a new supranational people of God,"
noting that W. D. Davies himself has expounded that theme. See [[gentiles|Gentiles]].

## Fulfillment in the ministry narratives

- France argues that the Isaianic Servant functions for Matthew as "a more holistic model for
  Jesus' ministry as a whole," not merely an explanation of his suffering and death — evidence of
  "a more wide-ranging meditation on that scriptural model than elsewhere in the NT."
- He reads the third triad of [[miracles|miracle]] stories in Matt 9 as supplying the factual basis
  for 11:5 and, since 11:5 rests partly on Isa 35:5-6, as demonstrating "the fulfillment in Jesus'
  ministry of that prophecy of eschatological deliverance."
- On Isa 8:23–9:1 quoted at 4:15-16 he notes that Matthew's aorist tenses (against the LXX's
  imperative and future) make clear that "the prophecy has now found its fulfillment in Jesus,"
  and that where Isaiah's darkness-and-light imagery originally concerned Assyrian devastation,
  Matthew "takes it in a more eschatological sense."
- On the baptism (3:15) France notes that "fulfill," normally used in the quotation formula for
  completing a scripturally authenticated pattern, suggests the baptism has a role in carrying out
  Jesus' specific mission. See [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]].
- On the Isaiah quotation at 13:14-15 France raises but leaves open a "refulfilment" hypothesis:
  Isaiah's prophecy was fulfilled in his own day for Judah, and "now it is being 'refulfilled' or
  brought to completion in Jesus' contemporaries." He judges the compound verb *anaplēroō*
  "probably best taken here as a stylistic variant without difference of meaning," while allowing
  that Matthew may have meant "again" or "completely."

France places the whole conception within a wider New Testament motif of "incredulous wonder" at
the privilege of living at the moment of fulfillment (Heb 11:13, 40; John 8:56; 1 Pet 1:10-12),
contrasting Pss. Sol. 17:44 and 18:6, whose beatitudes concern a still-future blessing.

## Open questions and unresolved points

- Whether *anaplēroō* at 13:14 signals a second or completed fulfillment, or is merely stylistic:
  France gives both and prefers the latter without closing the question.
- Whether Isa 7:14 points beyond Ahaz's day in Isaiah's own intention or only in Matthew's
  typological scheme: France argues for the former but presents it as an argument, not a datum.
- France's fullest argument for the fulfillment thesis is referred to an earlier book of his own
  and is not reproduced in the commentary; this page rests on the commentary's statements of the
  position, not on that argument.
