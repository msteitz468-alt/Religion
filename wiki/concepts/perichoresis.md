# Perichoresis (*Circumincessio* / *Circuminsessio*)

**Perichoresis** (Greek *περιχώρησις*; Latin *circumincessio* [passing into one another] or *circuminsessio* [dwelling in one another]) is the patristic and medieval theological concept denoting the mutual indwelling and interpenetration of the three divine persons (or, in [[karl-barth|Barth]]'s terminology, the three [[modes-of-being|modes of being]]) in the [[trinity|Trinity]].

## Origin and Definition

The concept was given its settled doctrinal form by **John of Damascus** (*Ekdosis* I, 8 and 14): the divine modes of being "mutually condition and permeate one another so completely that one is always in the other two and the other two in the one" ([[barth-church-dogmatics-i1-s8-12|*CD* I.1]], ll. 3659–61). Earlier formulations appear in Fulgentius: "On account of their natural oneness, the whole Father is in the Son and the Holy Spirit, also the whole of the Holy Spirit is in the Father and the Son. No one of them is outside of any of the others" (ll. 3680–83).

The concept rests on two possible groundings (both legitimate, per Barth, and ultimately saying the same thing): (1) the unity of the divine essence; (2) the relations of origin as such — since the three modes are constituted by their mutual relations, each necessarily implies the others.

## Latin Terminology

The Latin Church preferred *circuminsessio* (spatial: dwelling in one another, *immanentia*, *inexistentia*) over *circumincessio* (temporal: passing into one another), since "instead of a temporal sequence the Latin Church adopted a spatial juxtaposition" (ll. 3690–91). The Greek *περιχώρησις* could suggest a cycle or rotation; the Latin term corrects toward permanent mutual indwelling.

## Theological Function

Perichoresis does double work in trinitarian doctrine:

1. **Confirms the distinction** in the modes of being: "none would be what it is (not even the Father) without its co-existence with the others" (ll. 3694–95). The three are not identical; their mutual indwelling presupposes their genuine distinctness.

2. **Relativises the distinction**: "none exists as a special individual, but all three 'in-exist' or exist only in concert as modes of being of the one God and Lord who posits Himself from eternity to eternity" (ll. 3695–97).

Josef Pohle called the doctrine "the final sum of the two factors under discussion" — the unity-in-trinity and the trinity-in-unity (l. 3698).

## Connection to *Opera Ad Extra*

In [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1-s8-12|*CD* I.1]] §9.3, Barth connects perichoresis directly to the principle [[opera-trinitatis|*opera trinitatis ad extra sunt indivisa*]]: "to the involution and convolution of the three modes of being in the essence of God there corresponds exactly their involution and convolution in His work" (ll. 3889–90). The external works of God (creation, reconciliation, redemption) are undivided because the modes of being that act in them are always mutually indwelling.

In §10.2 Barth invokes perichoresis as the doctrinal guard against Patripassianism and other forms of Modalism: "the doctrine of perichoresis, which admits of misuse in a one-sided emphasis on the involution of the three modes of being, also contains the further element … the understanding of the involution as a convolution, the presupposition of the eternal independence of the three modes of being in their eternal communion" (ll. 4905–13).

## Hermeneutical Note

The concept is formally Trinitarian, but the same term *perichoresis* was used in Christology (from Maximus Confessor onwards) to describe the mutual indwelling of the two natures in Christ. The Trinitarian usage antedates and is analytically distinct from the Christological usage.
