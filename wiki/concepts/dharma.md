# Dharma

**Dharma** (from *dhṛ*, "to hold fast, to make secure") is one of the most expansive and contested
concepts in Indian religion. It encompasses **duty, religion, religious merit, morality, social and ritual
obligation, the law, and justice** — at once the way things are and the way they should be. "Dharma holds
the universe together; dharma, rather than love, is what makes the world go 'round"
([[wendy-doniger|Doniger]], *[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 8).

Its cosmological ancestor is **[[rita|ṛta]]** (Vedic cosmic order), which the later concept of ritual
dharma in the legal codes absorbed. In [[buddhism|Buddhism]] the same word denotes the Buddha's **teaching**
and the **phenomena** of experience — a different but cognate usage.

## The Trivarga: Three Aims of Life

In the classical Hindu framework, dharma is the first and most important of the **three aims of human life**
(*trivarga*):
- **Dharma** — duty, piety, social and ritual obligation
- **Artha** — wealth, political power, material success
- **Kama** — pleasure, desire (broadly sensual — music, food, perfume, sex)

Each aim has its canonical text: *Manu's Dharmashastra* (dharma); *Kautilya's Arthashastra* (artha, c. 100
CE though incorporating earlier material); *Vatsyayana's Kama-sutra* (c. 2nd–3rd c. CE). There are many
more texts devoted to dharma than to the other two, reflecting its preeminence and complexity.

**The fourth aim — moksha.** The Upaniṣads introduced a **fourth aim** — [[moksha|moksha]] (Release from
rebirth) — as an interloper into the three worldly aims. The texts on the Trio generally resist it: Ashvaghosha
uses the trio, not the quartet; the Kama-sutra gives moksha "very short shrift." The problem of squaring
the householder's three aims with the renunciant's fourth is a structural tension throughout Hinduism. See
[[moksha|Moksha]] for the five solutions Doniger identifies.

## Universal Dharma vs. Sva-dharma

Dharma operates at two levels that are in permanent tension:

**Sanatana/sadharana dharma (universal dharma)** — a general moral ethic that applies to all four classes
(*[[varnas|varnas]]*). Manu lists it differently in different places: "Nonviolence, truth, not stealing,
purification, and the suppression of the sensory powers" (Manu 10.63). The general thought is "a vague
social ethic."

**Sva-dharma (one's own dharma)** — the unique path laid out for an individual at birth, determined
primarily by the class and eventually the *jāti* (caste) into which one was born. "Karma in its fifth
meaning" (see [[karma|karma]]): the innate activity one was born to perform — a king's sva-dharma to rule
and fight, a potter's to make pots. In the logic of the texts, "the particular rule generally overrides the
general rule; sva-dharma trumps general dharma."

**The violence paradox.** The most acute form of the universal/particular tension: if the general dharma
gives "pride of place to nonviolence" (ahimsa), and if a warrior's sva-dharma is to kill — how does he
reconcile the two? The *Bhagavad Gita* addresses this directly: Arjuna's dharma as a Kshatriya is to fight
the battle; acting without attachment to the results (*karma-yoga*) resolves the paradox. Manu makes it
explicit: "As in parliamentary rules of order, the particular rule generally overrides the general rule."

## Key Texts of Dharma

**Manu's Dharmashastra** (*Manusmṛti* or *Mānava-dharma-śāstra*, c. 100 CE) — 2,685 verses by "Manu" (the
mythological ancestor of the human race, "the wise one"), covering psychology, caste, sex, money, law,
purification, renunciation, worldly aims, and social practice. Claims to be an eyewitness account of
creation. Doniger's summary: "The claims made about the author himself give us a hint of what to expect."

**Kautilya's Arthashastra** (c. 200 CE; possibly earlier material) — political science for a king. Advises
the king to impersonate gods and stage religious charades; to use wandering ascetics as spies; to predict
deaths by engineering them. "Kautilya makes Machiavelli look like Mother Teresa."

**Vatsyayana's Kama-sutra** (c. 2nd–3rd c. CE) — pleasure as a fully legitimate aim; charts the
relationship between all three aims; gives "short shrift" to moksha while also covertly including it.

## The Four Ashramas (Life Stages)

The four **ashramas** represent a later, temporal resolution of the trivarga/moksha tension — assigning
different aims to different life-stages. Originally four *simultaneous options for any time in life*:

1. **Brahmacharya** — the chaste student, studying the Veda
2. **Gṛhastha** — the householder, married, with family
3. **Vānaprastha** — the forest-dweller, semi-retired from household duties
4. **Saṃnyāsa** — the renouncer, fully withdrawn

By Manu's time (c. 100 CE), these had been **serialized** — stages to be lived in sequence — rather than
lifestyle options. The forest-dwelling stage (stage 3) became increasingly problematic as a compromise
between the householder and the renouncer that satisfied neither fully.

## "Dharma Is Subtle" — The Mahābhārata's Deconstruction

The Mahābhārata's central phrase is **"dharma is subtle"** (*dharmo hi sukṣmaḥ*). Where the
Upaniṣads codified dharma into shastric rules, the Mahābhārata constantly shows those rules
collapsing:

> "Right and wrong [dharma and adharma] do not go about saying, 'Here we are'; nor do gods,
> Gandharvas, or ancestors say, 'This is right, that is wrong.'"

The Mahābhārata "deconstructs dharma, exposing the inevitable chaos of the moral life." Every
available moral choice turns out to be the wrong one:

- **Mandavya at the stake**: The god Dharma himself has karma — he punishes Mandavya with impalement
  for sticking blades of grass in butterfly tails as a child. Mandavya curses Dharma to be born as a
  human man in the womb of a Śūdra woman (Vidura). "The moral law is stupid… so the moral law must
  undergo its own expiation."
- **Origin of class colors**: In the Mahābhārata's own etiology, the four *varṇas* do not come from
  divine creation (as in the Puruṣa-sūkta) but from karmic fall: originally everyone shared Brahmin
  dharma; the other classes "voluntarily" took up different occupations, and their karma stained them
  different colors. "It's their own damn fault." This is "a major transition from authoritative decree
  to apologia."
- **The Gita's "karma without kāma"**: the resolution the Gita offers is itself a double bind —
  you must perform your sva-dharma (which for Arjuna means killing kinsmen) but with the
  *attitude* of a renunciant, unattached to the results. "As in parliamentary rules of order, the
  particular rule generally overrides the general rule."

## Āpad-dharma: The Escape Clause

One of Doniger's most important observations about the shastric system is its built-in **escape
clause**: the doctrine of *āpad-dharma* ("emergency dharma" or "distress dharma"). The term *āpad*
means "adversity, distress, extreme need." The escape clause states:

> In times of extreme adversity (*āpat*, *āpad*), it is permissible to perform acts otherwise
> forbidden — including eating forbidden foods, performing acts normally restricted to another
> caste, or overriding ritual prohibitions.

**Scope of the escape clause:** Manu discusses it in multiple registers:
- A Brahmin starving in a famine may eat forbidden foods
- A man may steal food to survive
- At the extreme, a Brahmin may perform the duties of a Kṣatriya or Vaiśya

This is not a marginal provision. It appears in virtually every dharma-śāstra. Its function is to
make the system livable by providing a legitimated exit from its most crushing demands.

**The safety valve model:** Doniger reads āpad-dharma as the mechanism by which the shastric system
maintained its ideological hold. Instead of making strict rules that would force mass non-compliance,
Manu built in a "safety valve" — an acknowledged gap between norm and practice. The gap is explicit
in the texts: "The dharma-śāstras acknowledge that they are describing what ought to be, not what is."

**Manu as meditation, not law code:** Doniger's revisionist reading of Manu: it is not a **law code**
applied by courts, but a **meditation on what a law code should be**. Inscriptions and court records
from the same period show actual legal practice diverged substantially from Manu's norms. "People in
ancient India may have been no more constrained by the dharma-śāstra than people in medieval Europe
were constrained by Leviticus."

## The Three Śāstras and Their Tensions

The three texts of the *trivarga* (see above) are not in harmony; they reflect sharply different social
visions:

**Manu's Dharmashastra (c. 100 CE):** Emphasizes hierarchy, ritual purity, caste discipline, and
male control of women. Manu's solution to women: protect them always ("in childhood by the father,
in youth by the husband, in old age by the sons; a woman should never be independent"). Manu's
vegetarianism is hedged: he acknowledges that meat-eating has always been normal but lists five
"slaughterhouses" (butcher, fisherman, innkeeper, tax collector, goldsmith) who are "the first to
perish" — without calling for universal vegetarianism.

**Kautilya's Arthashastra (c. 200 CE, incorporating older material):** "Kautilya makes Machiavelli
look like Mother Teresa." Politics as pure instrumental calculation; no appeal to ritual purity;
the king manipulates religion (staging miracles, using ascetics as spies). The arthashastra values
*artha* (power/wealth) as the primary aim; dharma is instrumentalized.

**Vatsyayana's Kama-sutra (c. 2nd–3rd c. CE):** More liberal than Manu on almost every contested
issue. On sexuality: acknowledges and classifies *tritiya prakriti* ("third nature" — a person who
identifies with neither male nor female, or performs same-sex erotic acts). The Kama-sutra treats
these acts as simply another erotic category, to be described and navigated. It does not condemn
them as Manu does. On women: treats educated courtesans (*gaṇikā*s) as agents and advisors, not
objects. On the trivarga: gives moksha "very short shrift."

**The divergence summarized:** Manu forbids what the Kama-sutra describes with clinical
non-judgment; both acknowledge what Kautilya ignores as irrelevant to power. The three śāstras
are not a unified system but three competing social imaginaries coexisting under the umbrella of
*trivarga* taxonomy.

## *Tritiya Prakriti*: "Third Nature"

The Kama-sutra's concept of *tritiya prakriti* ("third nature" or "third gender") is one of the
more liberal moments in classical Sanskrit literature. It acknowledges that some people are neither
male (*puruṣa*) nor female (*strī*) in the standard sense, and classifies various erotic identities
and practices — including what would now be called homosexual acts — as varieties of the third
nature. The Kama-sutra describes specific acts performed between two people of the third nature
without condemnation or legal consequence.

Manu, by contrast, assigns **penances** (*prāyaścitta*) for same-sex intercourse (though notably
mild compared to his punishments for other transgressions — losing one's caste takes considerably
more). This divergence between the two texts is a site of ongoing scholarship.

Later Sanskrit traditions (including some Puranas and *nibandha* texts) will map *tritiya prakriti*
onto the *hijra* social category — though the mapping is imperfect.

## Manu's Caste Taxonomy: Fantastical Miscegenation

Manu's account of caste origins differs from the Mahābhārata's (which treated the other varnas as
a karmic fall from Brahminhood). Manu's system is:

1. The four *varṇas* (Brahmin, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, Śūdra) are created by Brahma
2. The sub-castes (*jāti*s) arise from **miscegenation between varnas** — each combination produces
   a new caste type (a Brahmin man + Vaiśya woman → one jati; a Śūdra man + Brahmin woman → the
   "Caṇḍāla," the lowest, who faces the harshest restrictions)

Doniger calls this a "fantastical" system — nobody actually knows which jati came from which cross;
the system is ideology rather than social description. Its function is to naturalize caste hierarchy
by anchoring it in cosmology while also explaining its complexity (thousands of jatis) as the product
of historical accumulation.

**The four "addictions" (vyasanas):** Manu identifies four vices that threaten social order: dice,
women, hunting, and drinking. The king must avoid them; the man of good standing must control them.
These are the shastric counterparts to the Buddhist/Jaina precepts against intoxication and gambling.

## Hermeneutical Notes

The word *dharma* is one of the great translation problems of Indology. The wiki uses it untranslated, since
no English word captures its range. The Buddhist usage — *Dhamma* as the Buddha's teaching and as
phenomena — is related but distinct: see [[buddhism|Buddhism]] and the Pali canon.
