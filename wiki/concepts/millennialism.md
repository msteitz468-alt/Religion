# Millennialism (Chiliasm)

Doctrines concerning the **thousand-year reign** of Revelation 20 — and [[augustine|Augustine]]'s
decisive reinterpretation of it in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XX.

## Chiliasm / Millenarianism

The literal expectation of a future, earthly thousand-year kingdom of the risen saints. Augustine names
its adherents **Chiliasts** (Greek) or **Millenarians** (Latin) and reports — and rejects — the carnal
form, in which the saints "enjoy the leisure of immoderate carnal banquets… such as not only to shock the
feeling of the temperate, but even to surpass the measure of credulity." He notes that he himself "once
held this opinion" (in a non-carnal version).

## Augustine's amillennial reading

Augustine reads the **thousand years** symbolically as the **present age of the Church**, between Christ's
first and second comings — not a future earthly reign. The "thousand" is the cube of ten, a number of
perfection denoting the fullness of this age. Within it:

- the **first resurrection** (John 5) is the soul's present regeneration; the saints "even now" reign with
  Christ, "the Church even now [being] the kingdom of Christ";
- **Satan is bound** — restrained from seducing "the nations from which the Church is gathered" — and at
  the end **loosed** "for a short season" (3½ years) for the final assault;
- **Gog and Magog** (Book XX) are not literal barbarian nations but all the peoples in whom Satan is shut
  up, rising in the last persecution of "the whole city of the devil" against "the whole city of Christ."

This reading — that the millennium *is* the Church age — became the standard **amillennial** position of
Western Christianity, against both literal chiliasm and later premillennial schemes. See
[[last-judgment]].

## Pelikan: The "Already / Not Yet" Transformation

[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 3) frames the decline of literal chiliasm not as a crisis of "disappointment" over the delayed parousia but as a **shift within the already/not-yet polarity** — "an eschatology that cannot be classified as either 'futuristic' or 'realized.'" Early millenarianism (Papias, Justin, Irenaeus, drawing on "unwritten tradition") was at first "a mark neither of orthodoxy nor of heresy, but one permissible opinion among others"; it waned as the church's confession migrated, in his memorable phrase, "from the categories of cosmic drama to those of being, from the Revelation of St. John the Divine to the creed of the Council of Nicea." The creeds acted as a conservative brake, continuing to confess Christ's future coming and the [[bodily-resurrection|resurrection of the body]] even as Platonizing theology pressed toward the immortality of the soul. [[origen|Origen's]] absorption of eschatology into a cosmic succession of worlds ([[apokatastasis]]) served as "a cautionary tale" of private speculation overreaching.

## Ezekiel 40–48 and Millenarian Readings (Block, NICOT)

[[block-ezekiel-25-48-nicot|Block, Ezekiel 25–48 (NICOT)]] engages the literalist millenarian tradition directly. He notes that "a more literalist millenarian understanding [of Ezek 40–48] has had a long history in Christian circles" — the reading that Ezekiel's temple will be physically built in a future millennial kingdom — and argues against it: "this view is weakened considerably by the absence of eschatological language" (no "on that day" or "in the latter days"; *ʿôlām* never distinctly eschatological); the prince (nāśîʾ) "functions primarily, if not exclusively, as a liturgical personage, without a hint of a Davidic connection"; and "the description of the temple is not presented as a blueprint for some future building… Nowhere is anyone commanded to build it." The vision's stylized features (perfect square city, contrived numerology, land division "disregard[ing] topographic and historical realities") cast "doubt on any interpretation that expects a literal fulfillment." On the cultic calendar of ch. 46 he adds an explicitly anti-millennialist note: "to posit a reinstitution of millennial sacrifices on the basis of Ezekiel's cultic ordinances is unwarranted."

On **Rev 20:7–10 and Gog**, Block observes that John "clearly borrowed from Ezekiel's last frame" (Rev 19:17–21) and then, in Rev 20, "fix[es] the conflict after the protracted period… referred to in idealistic terms as one thousand years," transforming Ezekiel's foreign foe into "a satanic and diabolical force" with victory "placed in the hands of the Messiah." He notes the "camp of the saints" "seems to contradict Ezekiel's picture of unwalled and undefended towns, but one should not necessarily impose consistency on these texts."
