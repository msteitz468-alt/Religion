# Suttee (Sati)

**Suttee** (from Sanskrit *satī*, "good woman"; related to *sat*, "being, truth") denotes the
practice of a Hindu widow being burned alive on her dead husband's funeral pyre. The *sati* is the
woman who performs it; **suttee** is the Anglo-Indian English term for the act.

The word *sati* shares its Sanskrit root with [[gandhi|Gandhi]]'s coinage **satyagraha** ("holding
to truth") — "a remarkable semantic kinship between the vocabulary of a woman's ultimate sacrifice
and a man's political resistance" (Doniger, paraphrased).

## Definition and Social Logic

From the **Hindu** standpoint, a sati is the *opposite* of a widow:

- A widow is a bad woman: it is a wife's duty to keep her husband alive; his death signals her
  failure and is a source of shame and guilt
- A sati is a good woman: she remains a *wife* always and never a widow, since her husband is not
  regarded as dead until cremated, and she accompanies him into the next life

From the **Euro-American** standpoint, such women are widows being murdered or committing suicide.
The gap between these frameworks shaped every debate about the practice under British rule.

## Historical Overview

Suttee was not a Vedic or ancient practice. Key historical points:

- **Early attestations**: some queens commit suttee in the Mahabharata; the Greek author Diodorus
  Siculus mentions it in the Punjab (1st c. BCE); the earliest definitively dated inscription is
  510 CE at Eran, Madhya Pradesh
- **Rajput *jauhar***: prophylactic group immolation of women before a husband's expected death in
  battle (to prevent capture); most famously at Chitorgarh. Sati stones — memorials to women who
  died this way — are found across India
- **Bengali Brahmin surge**: from 1680 to 1830, suttee was unusually high among Bengali Brahmins,
  likely due to the **Dayabhaga** inheritance system under which widows were entitled to their
  husbands' share of family wealth — wealth that reverted to the sati's in-laws after her death
- **Colonial data**: in 1823, recorded suttees included 234 Brahmin women, 25 Kshatriyas, 14
  Vaishyas, and 292 Shudras — women of all castes could and did perform it

## Mythology: The Two Stories Misapplied

Every ritual requires a myth, and the mythology used to justify suttee draws on two stories that
are *not actually about suttee*:

1. **The goddess Satī**: she self-immolated in protest when her father Daksha failed to invite her
   husband Shiva to his sacrifice — *not* on her husband's funeral pyre (Shiva never dies)
2. **Sita's fire ordeal**: she entered a fire to prove her chastity after Rāvana's abduction — *not*
   her husband's pyre; and she survived (the fire felt like sandalwood). This myth was used to claim
   that a true *sati* would feel no pain

Both myths were later *adapted* into support for widow immolation, though this adaptation was
relatively late. Other mythological women were also cited as paradigmatic satis.

## "Did She Jump or Was She Pushed?"

Doniger (Ch. 22) surveys eyewitness accounts of suttee, which split sharply:

- **Version A** (coerced): women who tried to run away, were dragged back to the pyre, held down
  with bamboo poles and weighted with heavy logs
- **Version B** (willing): women who underwent immolation with "the greatest intrepidity," their
  "countenance animated with pleasure at the moment of ascending the fatal pile"

The voice most wanted — the sati's own — is structurally absent: those who survived to tell the
tale were those who escaped (giving a bias toward the coercion narrative), while those who died
cannot speak.

Doniger argues against reducing the practice to either **crass materialism** (all satis were
murdered for property) or **false consciousness** (all were victims of patriarchal ventriloquism).
The women were diverse individuals who made various choices for various reasons:

- Some were murdered for land or money or family honor
- Some sacrificed themselves for religious reasons
- Some committed suicide out of guilt, despair, or terror
- Some resisted and escaped; some tried to resist and failed

## Gender Analysis

The core dynamic is **male control of female sexuality**: a widow is a "loose cannon" who might
dishonor her family by infidelity to her dead husband. Suttee eliminates the danger by eliminating
the widow. Yet this patriarchal logic cannot account for *why* the abuse of women takes this
particular ritual form in India — that requires the Hindu mythology of fire, sacrifice, and the
ideal wife.

Doniger's synthesis: "once again we need a Zen diagram, allowing for the intersection of Materialist,
feminist, and religious concerns." The sati is not simply a victim or a fanatic; she is "a complex
individual who acted under a complex set of constraints and possibilities."

## Suttee Under the Raj

The British response to suttee is itself a case study in colonial ambivalence:

### British Sanction (1813)
An 1813 circular **legalized "voluntary" suttee** under specific conditions (the woman not less than
sixteen years old, not pregnant, not intoxicated, not coerced). British officials were instructed to
assist suttees in being performed "correctly" according to what they believed Hindu law required.
Numbers *increased* after 1813: from 378 suttees in 1815 to 839 in 1818.

### The Slow-Burning Fire (1828)
Captain H. D. Robertson, Collector of Poona, learned that an elderly widow had almost escaped when
the pyre burned too slowly. He determined that "Hindu scriptures" stipulated slow-burning grass and
decided that mandating this text would discourage suttee by increasing the agony. One woman still
committed suttee despite his attempts to dissuade her.

### The 1829 Ban
Governor-General William Bentinck banned suttee in 1829, partly due to [[rammohun-roy|Rammohun
Roy]]'s campaign. But Doniger argues: "the British law probably facilitated more women's deaths
than it saved, and its main effect was to stigmatize Hinduism as an abomination in Christian eyes."
The debate turned what had been an exceptional practice into a symbol of the total oppression of
Indian women.

### Post-Independence
Suttee did not end with the 1829 ban. At least forty widows burned since 1947, most ignored until
the **suttee of Rup Kanwar** at Deorala, Rajasthan, on September 4, 1987 became a *cause célèbre*
— which itself, by giving suttee celebrity, may have inspired copycat cases.

## European Romanticization

Europeans romanticized suttee in ways that reveal their own projections:
- **Voltaire** (*Zadig*, 1747) imagined a hero proposing that widows be required to spend an hour
  with a young man before deciding to immolate themselves
- **Richard Wagner**: Brünnhilde's ride onto Siegfried's pyre in *Götterdämmerung* is explicitly
  modeled on suttee; an early libretto draft includes garbled Vedanta (via Schopenhauer)
- **Goethe** ("The Pariah," 1797): used the transposed-heads myth alongside suttee imagery
