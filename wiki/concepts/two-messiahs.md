# Two Messiahs (Qumran)
The Qumran community expected **two distinct Messiahs** at the end of days — a **priestly Messiah** (Messiah of Aaron) and a **royal Messiah** (Messiah of Israel) — along with, in some contexts, a **Prophet** (*navi*) who prepares the way for them. This dual messianism is one of the most distinctive features of Qumran eschatology and reflects the community's priestly self-understanding.

## Primary Sources

### Community Rule (1QS IX:10-11)
> "They shall be governed by the first directives in which the men of the Community began their instruction, until the coming of the **Prophet** and the **Messiahs of Aaron and Israel**."

Three figures expected: (1) the Prophet (Deut. 18:15-18 — a prophet like Moses), (2) the Messiah of Aaron (priestly), (3) the Messiah of Israel (Davidic/royal). This is the clearest tri-partite expectation.

### Messianic Rule (1QSa II:11-22) — The Messianic Banquet
The Messianic Rule describes the ordering of the eschatological banquet:
- The **Priest** (Messiah of Aaron) leads: he blesses the bread and wine **first**
- The **Messiah of Israel** is seated among the congregation but defers to the Priest
- The order explicitly places priestly precedence over royal

This is a remarkable inversion of typical royal messianism — the priest takes precedence over the king. It reflects the community's Zadokite priestly identity: the high-priestly figure is the supreme authority even in the eschatological age.

### Damascus Document (CD XIX–XX; XII:23; XIV:19; XIX:10-11)
The Damascus Document repeatedly uses the phrase "until the arising of the **Messiah of Aaron and Israel**" — apparently a dual reference to both figures. The death of the Teacher is the temporal marker after which the Messiah(s) are expected.

### Blessings (1QSb)
Two distinct blessing formulae for the two Messiahs:
- **Messiah of Aaron** (the Priest): blessing for "the sons of Zadok, the Priests" — renewal, splendour, service in the Temple of the Kingdom
- **Messiah of Israel** (Prince of the Congregation): blessed with Isaiah 11 language — "spirit of counsel and might," "with the breath of his lips he shall slay the wicked," "righteousness shall be the girdle of his loins"

The **Messiah of Israel** is identified as the **"Prince of the Congregation"** (*Nasi ha-Edah*), the Davidic royal figure who will fight the eschatological war and judge the nations.

### Testimonia (4Q175)
Four proof-text groups, each pointing to an expected eschatological figure:
1. **Deut. 5:28-9 + 18:18-19**: The Prophet like Moses — "I will raise up for them a Prophet from among their brethren"
2. **Num. 24:15-17**: "A star shall come out of Jacob and a sceptre shall arise from Israel" — the Davidic Messiah
3. **Deut. 33:8-11**: Blessing of Levi / Urim and Tummim — the Priestly Messiah
4. **Josh. 6:26** (from the Psalms of Joshua, 4Q379): The Cursed Man of Belial — the anti-figure

## The Priestly-Royal Hierarchy
The Qumran schema consistently places priestly Messiahship above royal:
- The Priest blesses the bread before the king at the banquet
- The "Messiah of Aaron" is listed before the "Messiah of Israel" in virtually all formulations
- The community Rule's final authority is the "sons of Zadok, the Priests"

This likely reflects the community's origins in a priestly dispute (Zadokite vs. Hasmonaean high priesthood) and its continued self-understanding as a priestly remnant awaiting the restoration of legitimate Zadokite leadership.

## The "Anointed of Aaron and Israel" in CD
Some scholars read CD's "Messiah of Aaron *and* Israel" as a **single figure** — an eschatological priest-king who combines both offices. Vermes does not firmly resolve this; the evidence is ambiguous. 1QS clearly has three distinct figures; CD's language is more compressed.

## Relationship to Early Christian Messianism
Qumran dual messianism illuminates the background to early Christian debates about Jesus as both priest and king:
- **Hebrews** develops an explicitly priestly Christology drawing on Ps. 110:4 (Melchizedek) — the same tradition that generates the heavenly Melchizedek figure (11Q13)
- The combination of Davidic king and Zadokite priest in a single figure is unusual; early Christian texts resolve the Qumran dual-messiah structure by collapsing both into Jesus
- **Revelation** depicts a Lamb who is both priest (in the heavenly sanctuary) and king (rider on white horse) — dual messianic roles merged

## 4Q521 and the Question of a Singular Messiah
VanderKam (2010) notes an important nuance regarding **4Q521 (Messianic Apocalypse)**. While the text references "his messiah" (singular), the miraculous deeds — "he will heal the wounded, revive the dead and bring good news to the poor" — are performed by **God (the Lord)**, not the messiah. This is a significant distinction: 4Q521's messiah is a singular figure, but the *agent* of resurrection and healing is God himself. The passage draws on Isa 35:5-6 and Isa 61:1 and parallels Luke 7:22 (// Matt 11:4-5). The parallel breaks down at the agent: in the NT, Jesus performs these deeds; in 4Q521, God does. See [[dead-sea-scrolls-vanderkam]].

## Messianic Banquet and Regular Meals
VanderKam follows Schiffman's interpretation: the regular communal meals at Qumran (with priest leading, blessing of bread and wine, at least 10 men) were **noncultic pre-enactments** of the eschatological messianic banquet described in 1QSa 2.11-22. The messianic banquet statute applies to *every* meal of 10+ men (1QSa 2.21-22), not just a future eschatological event. In the recurring meals, someone played the role of the Messiah of Israel.
