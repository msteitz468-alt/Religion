# Superstitio

*Superstitio* (Latin; Greek equivalent often *deisidaimonia*) was the Roman category for an illegitimate, excessive, or foreign religious cult—as opposed to *religio*, the proper, civic, ancestrally sanctioned worship of the gods. The distinction was fundamental to Roman religious self-understanding and provides the earliest Roman frame through which Christianity was categorized.

**Source for this entry**: Robert Louis Wilken, *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, Introduction and Chapters I–II (range ll. 300–2182 of the OCR'd text). Chapter III ("The Piety of the Persecutors") opens the sustained treatment of *superstitio* but falls outside the present scope; this page will be expanded when that chapter is ingested.

## The Term in Roman Sources

Pliny the Younger used *superstitio* to characterize what he found when investigating the Christians in Pontus (c. 112 CE): "All Pliny found was a *superstitio*, a foreign cult" (Wilken, l. 276). He later described Christianity to Trajan as "a depraved *superstitio* carried to extravagant lengths" (opening of Ch. III, ll. 567–569 within range). Wilken notes that "the use of the term 'superstition' (*superstitio*) would be less significant were it not that two other Roman writers living at the same time also employed it to refer to the Christians"—namely Tacitus and Suetonius (ll. 571–574). The convergence across three independent authors indicates that *superstitio* was not Pliny's personal epithet but a conventional Roman administrative-religious label for Christianity.

Wilken states that *superstitio* "appears in two other contemporary writers, Tacitus and Suetonius, referring to Christianity" (ll. 14–15 within Ch. II opening). The full analysis of the term's content and the contrast with *religio* is developed in Chapter III; this entry records what is established in the range read.

## Superstitio vs. Religio: The Basic Roman Contrast

The Roman contrast between *religio* and *superstitio* turned on civic legitimacy, antiquity, and moderation. *Religio* was the ancestral, publicly regulated worship of the gods of Rome—integral to the life of the city. Cicero defended the intertwining of religion and civic office: "the most distinguished citizens safeguard religion by the good administration of the state and safeguard the wise conduct of religion" (*Dom.* 1; Wilken, ll. 213–215). Rome's official priesthoods (augurs, pontifices, etc.) were held by leading political figures precisely because "the Romans thought that the official cults were an integral part of the public life of the city" (ll. 209–211).

*Superstitio*, by contrast, marked a cult as (a) foreign and not ancestrally Roman, (b) excessive or immoderate in devotion, and/or (c) practiced privately and secretly, apart from public civic worship. A foreign cult became suspect not necessarily because of its content but because it disrupted the civic-religious order.

## Why Christianity Was Classed as a Superstitio

From Pliny's perspective, Christianity bore the classic markers of *superstitio*:

1. **Foreign origin**: Christianity was a novel oriental cult with no ancient Roman pedigree. The elder Pliny's *Natural History*, written a generation after Jesus's death, did not even mention the movement (Wilken, Introduction, ll. 145–150 within range).

2. **Secret, private practice**: Christians met "before dawn on a fixed day" for private rituals—not public civic worship. Celsus later described them as an "obscure and secret association" (*c. Cels.* 8.17; Wilken, ll. 473 within range), a charge that carried the *superstitio* connotation of hiding unacceptable practices.

3. **Refusal of civic cult**: Christians refused to participate in the sacrifices and offerings to the gods that were woven into the fabric of Roman civic life—temple rites, the emperor's supplications, festivals. This refusal is what made them visible to merchants in the sacrificial meat trade (Wilken, ll. 38–43) and what Pliny's test (offering wine and incense to the emperor's statue, cursing Christ) was designed to expose.

4. **Association with suspected crimes (*flagitia*)**: Foreign cults were routinely suspected of harboring immoral practices. Pliny expected to find evidence of crimes when he investigated the Christians; he found none, but the suspicion was structural to the *superstitio* category. Wilken discusses the precedent of the Bacchic suppression of 186 BCE (Livy 39.18): Roman officials banned the Bacchanalia from Italy because their nocturnal secret rites—"the pleasure of wine and feasts," ecstatic dancing, alleged sexual immorality—shocked Roman sensibilities (ll. 80–100 within range). Similar charges of ritual debauchery and infanticide circulated about Christians in later decades (Minucius Felix, *Octavius* 9.5–6, cited ll. 154–173 within range).

5. **Obstinacy (*contumacia*)**: Pliny found that Christians who persisted in confessing Christianity were guilty of at minimum "stubbornness and unshakeable obstinacy" (*contumacia*)—a kind of contempt of a magistrate's authority that was itself a punishable offense independent of the *superstitio* charge (ll. 324–332 within range).

## Relation to the *Hetaeria* Category

The two Roman labels Pliny applied to Christianity—*superstitio* and *hetaeria* (political club)—were distinct but reinforcing. *Hetaeria* categorized the Christians as a potentially disruptive private association (a civic-political concern); *superstitio* categorized their cult as an illegitimate foreign religion (a civic-religious concern). Wilken structures his book around these two terms: "Political club will occupy us in [Chapter II] and 'superstition' in chapter 3" (ll. 23–24 within Ch. II opening).

## Reception and Significance

The *superstitio* label shaped Roman administrative responses to Christianity for two centuries. Because it framed Christianity as an illicit foreign cult rather than a legitimate *religio*, Christians were vulnerable to prosecution not for any specific crime but for the *nomen* (the name itself)—a point Pliny articulated when he asked Trajan whether "the mere name of Christian . . . is punishable, even if innocent of crime" (Ep. 10.96; Wilken, ll. 318–320 within range). Trajan's answer implicitly confirmed that the name sufficed.

Christian apologists responded by inverting the charge: Tertullian (*Apologeticum*) argued that Christianity was not a *superstitio* but a legitimate *religio*—indeed a superior one. He borrowed the associational vocabulary of the *collegium* to present Christianity as a morally serious society, not a clandestine cult. "A similar process can be seen in the characterization of Christianity as a superstition" shaping Christian self-presentation (Wilken, ll. 554–556 within range).
