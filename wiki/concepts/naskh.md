# Naskh (Abrogation)

**Naskh** (نسخ, "abrogation," "cancellation") is the Islamic doctrine that a later divine ruling can
**supersede** an earlier one — the science of *an-nasikh wa-l-mansukh* ("the abrogating and the
abrogated"). It is at once a **legal** mechanism (resolving apparent conflicts among Qur'anic
commands) and a **hermeneutical/chronological** one (it presupposes knowing which verses are earlier
and later, hence the Meccan/Medinan ordering). It is one of the pivot points where the Islamic
doctrine of a perfect, eternal scripture meets the evident fact that the [[quran|Qur'an]]'s commands
*changed* over the twenty-odd years of revelation.

## Qur'anic Basis

The doctrine grounds itself in the Qur'an's own statements that God substitutes verses:

- "For whatever verse We cancel or cause (the messenger) to forget, We bring a better or the like"
  (Q 2:106).
- "When We substitute one verse for another — and God knows best what He sends down — they say, You
  are simply an inventor" (Q 16:101).
- "God will delete or confirm what He will; and with Him is the 'mother' of the Book" (Q 13:39); cf.
  Q 87:6f.

The classic worked example: the command to spend much of the night in prayer (sura 73:1–4) is
abrogated by the long closing verse (73:20) once the Medinan community's public responsibilities made
night-long vigils impractical.

## The Three Standard Types

Later jurists distinguished:

1. **Abrogation of ruling but not wording** (*naskh al-hukm duna al-tilawa*) — the most common; the
   verse remains recited as Qur'an but its legal force lapses.
2. **Abrogation of wording but not ruling** (*naskh al-tilawa duna al-hukm*) — the ruling stands but
   the words are no longer in the *mushaf* (e.g. the disputed "stoning verse" Umar insisted had been
   Qur'an).
3. **Abrogation of both wording and ruling**.

Because abrogated-but-retained verses are *still recited*, [[bell-watt-introduction-to-the-quran|Bell
& Watt]] note this as paradoxical **confirmation of the text's accuracy**: later scholars did not
"tidy up" the Qur'an to match their own theories.

## Hermeneutical and Critical Frames

- **Juristic** — abrogation is primarily a tool of *fiqh*; al-Shafi'i (d. 820) and later al-Suyuti's
  *al-Itqan* treat it at length, and it was extended to the *sunna* and used to argue that Jewish and
  Christian law was abrogated by the Qur'an. See [[tafsir|Tafsir]].
- **Critical** — [[bell-watt-introduction-to-the-quran|Bell & Watt]] argue the Qur'an's own language
  of substitution implies a *broader* "revision" than the jurists' doctrine captures, and connect it
  to the [[muhammad|Satanic Verses]] episode (Q 22:52, where *yansakhu* abrogates what "Satan threw
  in") — though there *naskh* removes a passage entirely rather than retaining it.
