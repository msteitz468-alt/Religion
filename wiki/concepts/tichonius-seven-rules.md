# Tichonius's Seven Rules (*Liber Regularum*)

Seven hermeneutical rules formulated by [[tichonius]] the Donatist in his *Book of Rules* (*Liber Regularum*) and preserved and expounded — with qualifications — by [[augustine]] in [[on-christian-doctrine]], Book III, Chs. 30–37 (lines 3170–3576). Augustine describes them as "as it were, keys to open the secrets of Scripture" (line 3174), while cautioning that they do not cover *all* scriptural obscurity and that the book must be read watchfully on account of Tichonius's Donatist errors (lines 3211–3213).

Tichonius himself claimed in the preface to his *Liber Regularum* (quoted by Augustine at lines 3197–3203):

> "There are certain mystical rules which hold the key to the secret recesses of the whole law, and render visible the treasures of truth that are to many invisible. And if this system of rules be received as I communicate it, without jealousy, what is shut shall be laid open, and what is obscure shall be elucidated, so that a man travelling through the vast forest of prophecy shall, if he follow these rules as pathways of light, be preserved from going astray."

Augustine accepts the rules as of "very great assistance in understanding Scripture" but corrects the overreach: they illuminate *many* but not *all* obscure passages (lines 3206–3210).

---

## Rule 1: Of the Lord and His Body (Ch. 31, §44, lines 3216–3229)

Scripture sometimes speaks of Christ and the Church as a single person, so that the text shifts seamlessly between head and body without signaling the transition. Knowing this allows the reader to discern which clause applies to Christ and which to the Church.

Augustine's example: Isaiah 61:10 (LXX), where a single speaker says *"He hath decked me as a bridegroom with ornaments, and adorned me as a bride with jewels"* — one figure, two referents (lines 3227–3229). The grounding principle: *"Ye then are Abraham's seed"* (Gal 3:29) unites Christ and believers as one seed (line 3218–3219).

---

## Rule 2: Of the Lord's Bipartite Body / The True and Mixed Church (Ch. 32, §45, lines 3231–3253)

Tichonius called this rule "the twofold division of the body of the Lord," but Augustine prefers "the true and the mixed body of the Lord" or "Concerning the mixed Church" (lines 3232–3236). Scripture sometimes addresses or describes the genuine members of the Church and then, without signaling the shift, speaks of hypocrites mixed among them.

Example: Song of Songs 1:5 — *"I am black, but comely, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon"* (line 3240–3241). The Church declares itself *both* at present — the tents of Kedar (Ishmael's line, the reprobate) and the curtains of Solomon — because "the good fish and the bad are for the time mixed up in the one net" (line 3243, citing Matt 13:47–48). Isaiah 42:16 shifts mid-sentence from the good part ("I will bring the blind by a way that they knew not") to the reprobate ("They shall be turned back"), without marking the change (lines 3246–3253).

---

## Rule 3: Of the Promises and the Law (Ch. 33, §46, lines 3270–3290)

Also nameable as "of the spirit and the letter" or "of grace and the law." Augustine treats this less as a rule for resolving local obscurities than as a large theological question in its own right (line 3272–3273). He criticizes Tichonius here: Tichonius held that works are God's reward for faith but that faith itself is *our own* and not given by God — a position Augustine regards as proto-Pelagian error, corrected by Eph 6:23 ("love with faith, from God the Father") and Rom 12:3 ("God hath dealt to every man the measure of faith") (lines 3276–3285).

Augustine: "The efforts of Tichonius to clear up this point were good, but not complete" (line 3275). Tichonius "had not come into contact with this heresy [Pelagianism], which has arisen in our time" (lines 3279–3280).

---

## Rule 4: Of Species and Genus (Ch. 34, §§47–49, lines 3292–3404)

When Scripture speaks of a specific city, nation, or person, its language may *overstep* the particular (the species) and apply to the universal (the genus). Tichonius uses logical vocabulary: species = a part, genus = the whole of which it is a part (lines 3292–3294).

Augustine's examples:
- Jerusalem, Tyre, Babylon, Judea, Egypt, Assyria: prophecies about them "pass over the limits of that particular country, and apply more fitly to the whole" (lines 3311–3316).
- Solomon: things said of him "reach far beyond him, and which are only properly understood when applied to Christ and His Church" (lines 3318–3320, citing 2 Sam 7:14–16).
- Ezekiel 36:17–23 (lines 3326–3370): the prophet begins speaking of carnal Israel's punishment (species) and slides, without marking the transition, into a prophecy of the New Testament gathering of all nations (genus) — "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you…A new heart also will I give you…" — which Augustine reads as prophecy of baptismal regeneration now "imparted to all nations" (lines 3355–3359).

Key caution: "When Scripture, having up to a certain point been speaking about the species, makes a transition at that point from the species to the genus, the reader must then be carefully on his guard" (lines 3322–3324).

---

## Rule 5: Of Times (Ch. 35, §§50–51, lines 3405–3446)

Temporal quantities in Scripture often cannot be taken at face value, either because of the rhetorical figure of **synecdoche** (part for whole, or whole for part) or because of the use of **legitimate numbers** (seven, ten, twelve, and their multiples) that signify universal or spiritual quantities rather than literal durations.

Examples of synecdoche:
- The Transfiguration: Luke says "after eight days," Matthew and Mark say "after six days" (lines 3412–3416). Synecdoche resolves both as correct: one counts the bracketing partial days, the other counts only full intervening days.
- The three days and three nights of Christ in the earth (Matt 12:40): the partial day of crucifixion and the partial night of resurrection are each counted as full units (lines 3418–3422).

Examples of legitimate numbers:
- "Seven times in the day do I praise Thee" (Ps 119:164) = continuous praise (lines 3435–3436).
- Seventy (7×10) = the whole sojourn of the Church among aliens — Augustine reads the 70 years of Jeremiah 25:11 "in a spiritual sense for the whole time during which the Church is a sojourner" (lines 3438–3440).
- 144 (12×12) in the Apocalypse signifies "the whole body of the saints" (lines 3440–3443, citing Rev 7:4).

---

## Rule 6: Of Recapitulation (Ch. 36, §§52–54, lines 3448–3526)

Scripture narratives sometimes appear to follow chronological order but actually loop back to earlier events without announcing the return. Failure to recognize recapitulation produces mistaken chronologies.

Examples:
- Genesis 2:8–9 followed by 2:15 (lines 3453–3480): the planting of the garden and the description of its trees appear to follow God's placing man in the garden, but actually *precede* it — the narrative recapitulates the garden's formation before re-stating man's placement there.
- Genesis 10:20, 31, 32 followed by 11:1 (lines 3481–3497): the statement "the whole earth was of one language" in 11:1 seems to occur after the nations were divided by tongues (ch. 10), but is actually a recapitulation going back to the pre-Babel situation, leading forward to the Tower narrative.
- Luke 17:29–32 (lines 3498–3526): "In that day, he which shall be upon the house-top…" appears to require action at the moment of Christ's revelation; but this is a recapitulation — the "day" is the entire gospel age, the admonition applies *now* so that each person may receive reward when the Lord is revealed.

---

## Rule 7: Of the Devil and His Body (Ch. 37, §55, lines 3528–3545)

As Rule 1 treats Christ and His body (the Church) as sometimes expressed under a single person, Rule 7 does the same for Satan and his body (the wicked). The devil is "the head of the wicked, who are in a sense his body, and destined to go with him into the punishment of everlasting fire" (lines 3529–3530), mirroring Christ as head of the Church destined for glory.

Consequence for interpretation: statements made about the devil may in their full truth apply not to Satan personally but to his body — those manifestly wicked and those secretly belonging to him while "mixed up with the Church" until death or final judgment (lines 3535–3537).

Example: Isaiah 14:12 LXX, *"How he is fallen from heaven, Lucifer, son of the morning!"* — understood of the devil, spoken in the figure of the king of Babylon. But the following phrase, *"He is ground down on the earth, who sendeth to all nations"* (Isa 14:12 LXX) — Augustine notes it applies better to the devil's *body* than to Satan himself, since it is his body that is "beaten small like the dust which the wind blows from the face of the earth" (lines 3540–3545).

---

## Augustine's Overall Assessment

> "All these rules, except the one about the promises and the law, make one meaning to be understood where another is expressed, which is the peculiarity of figurative diction; and this kind of diction, it seems to me, is too widely spread to be comprehended in its full extent by any one." (lines 3546–3549)

The rules are valuable but partial. They must be used with caution because of Tichonius's Donatist theological errors and his overreach in claiming the rules unlock *all* of Scripture. See [[figurative-interpretation]] for the broader hermeneutical framework in which Augustine situates these rules.
