The "Servant Songs" is the name given (following Bernhard Duhm, 1892) to four poems in Isaiah 40-55 that describe a figure called "the Servant of the LORD" (*ʿeḇed YHWH*). These four passages — Isaiah 42:1-9, 49:1-13, 50:4-11, and 52:13-53:12 — constitute the most detailed OT description of the Messiah's atoning work and are among the most-discussed passages in the history of biblical interpretation, Jewish and Christian.

The songs move from calling (42) to commissioning (49) to obedient suffering (50) to vicarious, atoning death and vindication (52-53). The climax in 52:13-53:12 is the locus of the most sustained Christian Christological interpretation of any OT text.

## The Four Servant Songs

### First Song: Isaiah 42:1-9

> "Here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen one in whom I delight; I will put my Spirit on him, and he will bring justice to the nations. He will not shout or cry out, or raise his voice in the streets. A bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not snuff out. In faithfulness he will bring forth justice; he will not falter or be discouraged till he establishes justice on earth. In his teaching the islands will put their hope." (42:1-4)

Features:
- YHWH speaks directly, presenting the Servant to the world
- The Servant is filled with YHWH's Spirit (connecting to 11:2, the Branch of Jesse)
- Mission: *mišpāṭ* (justice, right order) to the nations — a universal mission, not merely to Israel
- Method: gentleness, not force — "a bruised reed he will not break"
- Scope: the Servant is appointed as "a covenant for the people and a light for the Gentiles" (42:6)

### Second Song: Isaiah 49:1-13

> "Listen to me, you islands; hear this, you distant nations: Before I was born the LORD called me; from my mother's womb he has spoken my name.... He said to me, 'You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will display my splendor.' But I said, 'I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing at all.' Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and my reward is with my God." (49:1, 3-4)

Features:
- The Servant speaks in first person about his divine calling from the womb
- Named "Israel" (v. 3) — the source of the debate about identity
- Yet his mission is explicitly *to* Israel: "to bring Jacob back to him and gather Israel to himself" (49:5) — the Servant cannot be identical with Israel if his mission is to restore Israel
- Mission extends beyond Israel: "I will also make you a light for the Gentiles, that my salvation may reach to the ends of the earth" (49:6)
- A distinct and universal Servant emerges — associated with Israel's name and calling but exceeding Israel's actual capacity

### Third Song: Isaiah 50:4-11

> "The Sovereign LORD has given me a well-instructed tongue, to know the word that sustains the weary. He wakens me morning by morning, wakens my ear to listen like one being instructed. The Sovereign LORD has opened my ears; I have not been rebellious, I have not turned away. I offered my back to those who beat me, my cheeks to those who pulled out my beard; I did not hide my face from mocking and spitting." (50:4-6)

Features:
- The Servant speaks again, describing his daily formation by YHWH
- First mention of physical suffering — beating, beard-pulling, mocking, spitting
- The Servant's suffering is chosen, not forced: "I have not been rebellious"
- Confidence in YHWH's vindication despite the suffering (50:8-9)

### Fourth Song: Isaiah 52:13-53:12

The climax and the most significant OT passage for NT Christology. Structured in five stanzas:

**52:13-15** (YHWH speaks): The Servant will be "high and lifted up" — the exact phrase used of YHWH in 6:1 and 57:15, used nowhere else of a human being; yet his appearance was disfigured; kings will be silenced before him

**53:1-3** (The "we" group speaks): "Who has believed our message? To whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" — the Servant is identified with the "arm of the LORD," a phrase for YHWH's saving power (51:5, 9; 52:10). He had no attractive form; he was despised and rejected; "a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief"

**53:4-6** (Confession): "Surely he took up our pain and bore our suffering, yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted. But he was pierced for *our* transgressions, he was crushed for *our* iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was on him, and by his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned — every one — to his own way; and the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of *us all*."

The substitution is stated six ways: our pain / our suffering; our transgressions; our iniquities; the punishment for our peace; our healing by his wounds; our straying / the iniquity of us all.

**53:7-9**: He was "like a lamb led to slaughter" — passive, willing submission; "made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death, though he had done no violence, nor was any deceit in his mouth"

**53:10-12** (YHWH speaks again): "Yet it was the LORD's will to crush him and cause him to suffer, and though the LORD makes his life an offering for sin (*ʾāšām*), he will see his offspring and prolong his days." — the death is divinely planned; the *ʾāšām* (guilt offering) is the precise Levitical term for an offering made to atone for specific transgression; "he will see the light of life" — vindication beyond death; "he bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors."

## The Identity Question

The Servant Songs have generated three major interpretive categories:

### 1. Collective Israel

**Claim**: The Servant is the nation of Israel collectively — personified as a servant who suffers in the exile and is restored. Support: the Servant is explicitly named "Israel" in 49:3.

**Problems**:
- The Servant's mission in 49:5-6 is explicitly *to* Israel and Jacob — a nation cannot serve itself
- The "we" of 53:4-6 are those on whose behalf the Servant suffers — they are the people, and the Servant is other than them
- Israel as a nation has done violence and used deceit; the Servant in 53:9 had done neither
- The logic of substitution — bearing another's sin — is impossible if the Servant is the one whose sin is being borne

### 2. Historical Individual

**Claim**: The Servant is a specific historical figure — proposals include Isaiah himself, Jeremiah, Cyrus, Hezekiah, Moses, Zerubbabel, or an unknown figure.

**Problems**:
- The Servant's suffering in 53:4-6 bears the sin of "my people" — no mere human being can atone for others' sins on this scale
- The "high and lifted up" language of 52:13 (used only of YHWH in 6:1 and 57:15) hints at a divine-human identity that goes beyond any historical candidate
- No historical figure fits the specific details: voluntary, non-resistant suffering; death as an *ʾāšām* (guilt offering); vindication after death; making "many righteous" by bearing their iniquities

### 3. Messianic (Christological) — Oswalt's Position

**Claim**: The Servant is a figure yet future from the prophet's perspective who is identified with Israel's calling and name but exceeds Israel in his capacity for obedient suffering and atoning sacrifice. The NT identifies this Servant as Jesus of Nazareth.

**In favor**:
- The Servant's mission is *to* restore Israel — requires distinction from Israel
- The "arm of the LORD" identification (53:1 = 51:5, 9; 52:10) hints at divine identity
- "High and lifted up" (52:13) — language used only of YHWH elsewhere in Isaiah
- The *ʾāšām* (guilt offering) language requires the kind of priestly substitution that no ordinary human could provide
- The post-suffering vindication ("he will see his offspring and prolong his days," 53:10) implies life beyond death — confirmed in the NT by the resurrection

**Oswalt's "both/and" formulation**: The Servant is not merely an individual replacing the collective Israel; the relationship is circular. God calls Israel to servanthood (chs. 40-48); Israel cannot realize that calling (ch. 48); the Servant takes what Israel is called to be and fulfills it vicariously (chs. 49-53); as a result, Israel can become what she was called to be (chs. 54-66; see 54:17). The circle: Israel → Servant → Israel-as-servant.

### 4. Jewish Interpretive Tradition

Jewish tradition before the destruction of the Temple (70 CE) shows evidence of messianic readings of Isaiah 53 (Targum of Jonathan, some Dead Sea Scrolls fragments). After 70 CE and especially after the Christian appropriation of ch. 53, the dominant rabbinic interpretation became collective — the Servant as the suffering people of Israel. This is the dominant Jewish reading today, though it faces the same internal textual problems as the collective interpretation above.

## The Fourth Song in the History of Interpretation

### NT Appropriation

Isaiah 53 is quoted or clearly alluded to more than any other OT passage in the NT's description of the passion:

| NT Text | Isaiah 53 Verse | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Matt 8:17 | 53:4 ("he took up our infirmities") | Jesus' healing ministry |
| Luke 22:37 | 53:12 ("numbered with transgressors") | Jesus quoting his own destiny at the Last Supper |
| John 12:38 | 53:1 | Disbelief in Jesus fulfilling prophecy |
| Acts 8:32-35 | 53:7-8 | Philip's first evangelistic text to the Ethiopian |
| Romans 4:25 | 53:12 | "Delivered over for our trespasses" |
| Romans 10:16 | 53:1 | Israel's unbelief in the gospel |
| 1 Pet 2:22-25 | 53:4-9 | Christ's example for suffering servants |

### 1 Peter and the Origin of the Servant Christology (Jobes)

[[karen-jobes|Jobes]] (BECNT 1 Peter, 2005) argues that **1 Peter is the source of the familiar
Christian identification of Jesus with the Isaiah 53 Suffering Servant**. Although Isa. 53 is alluded
to across the NT, only Peter develops the identification at length (1 Pet. 2:21–25, weaving in 53:4,
5, 6, 9, 12; cf. the programmatic 1:10–12). Decisively, Jobes (with Achtemeier and Goppelt) notes
that Peter **exegetes Jesus' death in light of Isa. 53, not vice versa** — the sequence of 2:21–25
follows the *passion narrative* of Jesus rather than the order of Isaiah 53, showing that the
historical death of Jesus is primary and Isaiah is the interpretive lens. (The Servant identification
is *implied* in Acts 8:32–35 but not developed there.) This is part of what Jobes calls Peter's
"early and creative" Christology — emphases close to the crucifixion that "quickly dropped out" of
later Christian thought (R. P. Martin) — and it rests on Peter's distinctive hermeneutic that the
"Spirit of Christ" was already present and speaking to Israel's prophets (1:10–12). The Servant
Christology thus enters the wiki not only as Isaiah's revelation (Oswalt) but as Peter's apostolic
appropriation.

### Patristic and Medieval

The Servant was almost universally read Christologically from Justin Martyr onward. The "arm of the LORD" (53:1) was applied to the divine-human nature of Christ; the *ʾāšām* (53:10) grounded theories of satisfaction atonement (Anselm). Bernard of Clairvaux preached the Servant's passion extensively. Aquinas distinguished the "merit" of Christ's suffering (satisfying divine justice) from its "efficacy" (producing justification).
