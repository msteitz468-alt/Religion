# Pietas

**Pietas** is the central religious virtue of [[roman-religion|Roman religion]] — the dutiful, reverent
**fulfillment of one's obligations** to the gods, the family (especially the father and ancestors), and the
state/fatherland. It is far broader than English "piety": it names a whole **ethic of right relationship**,
of giving each their due. Wilken's [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|*The Christians as the Romans Saw
Them*]] (Ch. III, "The Piety of the Persecutors") makes *pietas* the interpretive key to why Romans
persecuted Christians — not from cruelty or cynicism, but from a genuine religious sensibility.

## Meaning and Semantic Range

Roman religion was a religion of **duty and reciprocity** rather than belief or feeling: the gods grant Rome
prosperity (the *pax deorum*) in return for **correct, faithful observance** of cult. *Pietas* is the
disposition that renders this observance — and extends to honoring parents and serving the *res publica*.
Its model is **"*pius* Aeneas"** of [[virgil-aeneid|Virgil's *Aeneid*]], who carries his father and the
household gods from burning Troy and subordinates every personal desire to the gods' command and Rome's
destiny.

The word originally designated honor owed to one's own family — children to parents, all to ancestors —
and expanded to loyalty to Roman custom, inherited law, and "the fatherland," before acquiring its cultic
sense of "reverence and devotion to the gods and to the ritual or cultic acts by which the gods were
honored" (Wilken). The familial sense was never displaced: "Separation of the concept of piety into a
familial and a cultic half is clearly a product of modern sensibilities; in antiquity piety formed a unity"
(C. Koch, *Pietas*). The Greek equivalent was *eusebeia*, with the same public/cultic range.

## Roman Religion as Practice, Not Belief

Romans did not speak of "believing in the gods" but of "having gods, just as a city might 'have laws or
customs.'" Religion was a matter of **practice** — cult, sacrifice, ritual, public ceremony — not private
conviction. The word *pietas* appears on imperial coins beside altars and sacrificial animals; military
calendars set holidays for public vows (*vota publica*). Tacitus's account of rebuilding the Capitol
(*Hist.* 4.53) shows the seamless fusion of the religious and civic: diviners, garlands, Vestal Virgins,
soldiers with auspicious names, and the praetor all join in sacrifices to the Capitoline triad — at once
"an act of piety toward the gods" and "a public occasion involving the populace as a whole."

## Divine Providence (*Providentia*) and the Social Order

The animating theological principle was *providentia* — that the gods govern human affairs and history.
"If one were to single out one idea that captured the religious sense of the Romans it would be divine
providence" (Wilken; see [[divine-providence]]). Hence piety was politically necessary: the gods preserved
Rome, insured the succession of emperors, and sustained the earth's fertility. Cicero: "the disappearance
of piety towards the gods will entail the disappearance of loyalty and social union among men as well, and
of justice itself" (*Nat. D.* 1.4). Piety is the structural glue of civilization, not an optional private
virtue.

## Religious Conservatism

The criterion of religious truth was **custom and tradition** — the practices of the ancestors (*mos
maiorum*). "To be pious in any sense, to be respectable and decent, required the perpetuation of cult"
(MacMullen). New cults were inherently suspect. Dio Cassius's Maecenas advises Augustus to "abhor and
punish" those who "distort our religion with strange rites," since innovators "persuade many to adopt
foreign practices, from which spring up conspiracies, factions and political clubs" (52.36.2).

## Superstitio as the Antithesis of Pietas

*Superstitio* was the negation of *pietas* — "groundless fear of the gods," irrational, foreign,
destabilizing, "the seed from which atheism springs" (Plutarch). Foreign cults (Egyptian, Gaulish,
Jewish) were labeled so; the Jews were "a people prone to superstition and the enemy of true religion"
(Tacitus, *Hist.* 5.13). When Pliny, Tacitus, and Suetonius all called Christianity a *superstitio* they
invoked a precise category, not casual prejudice. See [[superstitio]].

## "We Too Are a Religious People": The Persecutors' Self-Understanding

Wilken's central revisionist argument: the persecution of Christians expressed genuine *pietas*, not
capricious cruelty. The persecutors saw themselves as defending the religious foundations of civilization
against a movement that refused the gods, scorned sacrifice, and held aloof from the civic life sustained
by divine providence. A Roman official at the trial of the Scillitan martyrs gave the persecutors' own
self-description: **"We too are a religious people."** When Tacitus called Christians "enemy of mankind"
(*odium humani generis*) he meant they "were an affront to his social and religious world." Roman games,
processions, and banquets were religious events; refusing them was a religious as much as a social
offense. This is the heart of [[roman-persecution-piety-or-prejudice|the controversy over whether Roman
persecution sprang from piety or prejudice]].

## Cross-Tradition Note and the Christian Counterclaim

*Pietas* is the orthopraxic counterpart to belief-centered virtues elsewhere in the wiki: where
[[justification|faith]] is central to Pauline Christianity, Roman religion centers on **doing the right
thing in the right way**. The Latin word underlies English "piety" and "pity," and was taken up and
transformed by Christian writers into devotion to God. By the mid-second century apologists contested the
*superstitio* charge on Roman religious grounds: [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr]], aware Christianity was
viewed as superstition, countered, "We cultivate piety, justice, philanthropy, faith and hope."
