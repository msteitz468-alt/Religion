A **Nazirite** (Hebrew: *nāzîr*, from *nāzar*, "to separate" or "abstain") is a person who undertakes a vow of consecration to YHWH, involving three specific abstentions: no wine or fermented drink, no cutting of the hair, and no contact with a corpse. The practice is formally legislated in Numbers 6 and appears most prominently in narrative form in the Samson story (Judg 13–16) and in the dedication of Samuel (1 Sam 1).

## The Nazirite Legislation (Numbers 6)

Numbers 6:1-21 provides the standard procedure:

**Abstentions during the vow**:
1. No wine, strong drink, wine vinegar, grape juice, fresh or dried grapes — nothing from the vine
2. No razor shall touch the head; the hair grows uncut for the duration
3. No contact with the dead, even the corpse of an immediate family member

**Duration**: Normally voluntary and for a limited period, taken for a specific purpose. Not a permanent state for ordinary Israelites.

**Termination**: At the end of the vow period, the Nazirite presents burnt offerings, sin offerings, and peace offerings, then shaves the consecrated hair and places it in the fire of the peace offering. The priest performs additional rites, and the Nazirite is formally released. The shaving transfers the consecration back to YHWH in a symbolic final act.

The combination of these abstentions creates a form of purity analogous to (though distinct from) priestly purity. The long hair is the most visible sign — the external marker of the internal consecration.

## Samson: Nazirite from Birth

Samson represents the most theologically significant instance of the Nazirite in the Hebrew Bible. His case is exceptional in three ways that mark it off from Numbers 6:

1. **Divinely determined, not voluntarily assumed**: The messenger of YHWH announces the Nazirite calling to Samson's *mother* before his conception (Judg 13:3-5). No vow is taken; it is imposed by divine decree.

2. **From birth until death, not for a limited period**: The mother is told "he will be a Nazirite of God from birth until the day of his death" (13:7). The consecration is permanent and coterminous with his life. This parallels Samuel (1 Sam 1:11) but is stated even more emphatically.

3. **The calling frames a destiny**: "He will begin to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines" (13:5). The Nazirite separation is not an end in itself but is ordered toward a specific redemptive mission.

[[barry-webb|Webb]]'s key interpretive move: Samson's Naziriteship is the structural parallel for Israel's covenantal identity. As Samson is set apart from other men by his calling as a Nazirite, Israel is set apart from other nations by YHWH's covenant. As Samson repeatedly violates the terms of his consecration (contact with corpses, Philistine women, and ultimately the revelation of his hair's significance), Israel repeatedly violates the terms of its covenant. Samson's story is Israel's story in miniature.

### The Hair and Its Significance

The uncut hair is the only Nazirite sign that cannot be imposed on the mother (unlike the dietary laws). It is the one marker that belongs uniquely to Samson himself, and it becomes the narrative pivot of the Delilah episode. Delilah, after four failed attempts, finally extracts from Samson the secret: "No razor has ever come upon my head, for I have been a Nazirite to God from my mother's womb. If my head is shaved, my strength will leave me" (16:17).

When Delilah shaves his head, the text does not say YHWH removes Samson's strength directly — it says "he did not know that YHWH had left him" (16:20). The departure is YHWH's, not merely the hair's. The hair was the covenant sign; the violation of the sign is the breach of the covenant. But even this is not final: the hair begins to grow again (16:22), and Samson's final prayer is answered (16:28-30).

## Samuel as Nazirite

Hannah's vow in 1 Samuel 1:11 dedicates the son she prays for as a Nazirite: "no razor shall touch his head." Samuel is consecrated to YHWH's service at Shiloh from the time he is weaned. His Naziriteship is not explicitly developed in the narrative beyond the hair reference, but it establishes the pattern of a specially called figure set apart from birth for YHWH's purposes — a pattern continued in the OT (Jeremiah: "before you were born I consecrated you," Jer 1:5) and NT.

## The Nazirite in the New Testament

**John the Baptist** (Luke 1:15): "He will be great before the Lord, and he shall not drink wine or strong drink, and he will be filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother's womb." The abstinence from wine and the prenatal consecration place John structurally in the Nazirite tradition, specifically in parallel with Samson (Judg 13:4-5). [[barry-webb|Webb]] notes that Luke 1:15 is one of the clearest NT allusions to the Samson birth narrative.

**Paul's vow (Acts 18:18; 21:23-26)**: Paul shaves his head "because of a vow" at Cenchreae, and in Acts 21 he participates in the purification rites of four men completing Nazirite vows in the Jerusalem temple. This confirms that the Nazirite institution continued in Second Temple Judaism and was practiced in the early church by Jewish Christians.

**Jesus**: Jesus is not presented as a Nazirite (the "Nazoraean" of Matthew 2:23 is generally understood as a reference to his hometown Nazareth, not the Nazirite institution). However, his abstinence from wine at the Last Supper ("I will not drink again of this fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom," Matt 26:29) has been read in the patristic tradition as a Nazirite-like consecration for the passion.

## The Nazirite as Theological Category

The Nazirite practices a heightened form of the holiness that all Israel is called to embody (Lev 11:44-45; 19:2; 20:7-8). The connection to priestly purity is not accidental — the Nazirite abstentions overlap with those governing the high priest (Lev 21:1-11). For the duration of the vow, the Nazirite participates in the holiness ordinarily reserved for those who serve at the altar.

This suggests a democratizing impulse: any Israelite, regardless of tribe, can undertake the concentrated holiness of a Nazirite vow. In Samson's case, this is extended to a divinely imposed, lifelong, tribal-mission form that makes him a representative figure for all Israel.
