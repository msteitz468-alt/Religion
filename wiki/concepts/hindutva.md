# Hindutva

**Hindutva** ("Hindu-ness") is the ideological framework of Hindu nationalism — the claim that Hindu
religious-cultural identity is coextensive with Indian national identity, and that non-Hindus can
belong to the nation only as guests or subordinates. The term was coined by **V. D. Savarkar** in 1922.

## Definition and Core Claims

Hindutva is not identical with Hinduism as a religion; Savarkar's formulation was explicitly
civilizational rather than theological. Its core claims:

1. India (*Bharat*) is fundamentally a **Hindu nation** — its culture, history, and identity are Hindu
2. Non-Hindus (primarily Muslims and Christians) are outsiders or guests whose loyalties lie elsewhere
3. The **"abuse of history"** in the Hindu-nationalist direction: ancient Hindu civilization was pure,
   powerful, and self-sufficient before Muslim and later British conquest; restoration of Rāma-rāj
   (Rāma's ideal reign) is a political aspiration
4. **Saffronization**: the process of infusing government, education, and culture with Hindu-nationalist
   norms; named for the ocher robes of Hindu renouncers, now associated with the Hindu right

## The RSS and its Worldview

The **RSS** (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, "National Volunteer Corps"), founded 1925, is the organizational
core of Hindutva. Its second leader, **M. S. Golwalkar** (1906–1973), articulated the Hindutva worldview
with particular clarity in 1939:

> *"The non-Hindu peoples in Hindustan… must not only give up their attitude of intolerance and
> ungratefulness towards this land… but must… stay in the country wholly subordinated to the Hindu
> Nation, claiming nothing, deserving no privileges, far less any preferential treatment — not even
> citizen's rights."*

Doniger notes that Golwalkar's statement **simultaneously makes a historically defensible claim** (the
first half, about Hindu tolerance and generosity) **and contradicts it** (the second half, using pride
in tolerance to justify intolerance). The political schizophrenia is structural.

The RSS is also the parent organization of the **BJP** (Bharatiya Janata Party), India's Hindu-nationalist
political party, and of organizations including the **VHP** (Vishwa Hindu Parishad) and **ABVP** (student
wing). **Hanumān** has become the RSS's unofficial mascot — the martial monkey god whose devotion to
Rāma models the ideal Hindu nationalist's devotion to the nation.

## Arya Samaj as Precursor

The [[arya-samaj|Arya Samaj]] (founded 1875 by Dayananda Sarasvati) is a direct ideological precursor.
Its **cow protection movement** of the 1880s–1890s generated communal violence against Muslims as early
as 1893. Its *shuddhi* (reconversion) ceremony and "Vedas only" exclusivism anticipated the Hindutva
insistence on a pure, pre-Islamic Hindu identity. The link from Arya Samaj → Hindutva → BJP is direct.

## Key Political Deployments

The Hindutva deployment of Hindu mythology as political program generates several controversies:

- **[[babri-masjid-ayodhya|Babri Masjid / Ayodhya]]**: the claim that Babur's Mosque was built over
  Rāma's birthplace; the 1992 demolition by a BJP-led mob; the aspiration to rebuild a Rāma temple
- **Ram Setu**: resistance to the Sethusamudram canal project on the grounds that it would destroy
  "Rāma's Bridge"; government withdrawal of its own archaeological finding after political pressure
- **Textbook revision**: attempts to rewrite Indian history textbooks to assert that beef-eating was
  introduced by Muslims (Doniger documents that ancient Hindus ate beef); that the Aryan Migration
  theory is false; that the caste system never existed
- **Saffronization of education**: Delhi University controversies, including the successful campaign to
  remove A. K. Ramanujan's essay "Three Hundred Ramayanas" from the BA curriculum (2008, after a mob
  ransacked the history department)
- **Suppression of alternative Ramayanas**: the Hindu right insists there is **one** Rāmāyaṇa — any
  Dalit, tribal, or feminist retelling of the epic is attacked as blasphemy

## "Abusing History"

Doniger's central diagnosis (Ch. 25): **the abuse of history** is the use of a selectively constructed
past to justify present political agendas. Because the Hindu tradition is so vast and contradictory,
"you could easily use history to argue for almost any position in contemporary India." Hindutva exploits
this by cherry-picking a sanitized, monolithic "Hindu tradition" that excludes the tradition's own
internal diversity (women, Dalits, meat-eaters, erotic art, syncretism with Islam) — ironically
deploying a **Christian idea of orthodoxy** (a single correct teaching, a "papacy") to impose on a
tradition that never had such a structure.

Doniger's counterargument: **the hope lies in Hinduism's historically demonstrated pluralism** — the
bhakti movements that included women and Dalits; individual pluralists like Ashoka, Akbar, Kabir, Gandhi;
the very absence of a pan-Hindu magisterium. The goal of Hindutva — to establish a Hindu orthodoxy — is
both historically false to the tradition and practically opposed to it.
