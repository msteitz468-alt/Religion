# Knowability of the Word of God

A central epistemological concept in [[karl-barth|Karl Barth]]'s *[[barth-church-dogmatics-i1|Church Dogmatics]]* I.1 (§6), addressing the question of how — and on what ground — men can know the [[word-of-god|Word of God]].

## Definition and Thesis

Barth's controlling thesis (*CD* I.1, §6, lines 8567–8570): "The reality of the Word of God in all its three forms is grounded only in itself. So, too, the knowledge of it by men can consist only in its acknowledgment, and this acknowledgment can become real only through itself and can become intelligible only in terms of itself."

The question is phrased as one of *possibility*, not reality: "How *can* men know the Word of God?" The reality of such knowledge is already given in Church proclamation and the life of the Church; the question is what grounds that possibility (lines 8649–8661).

## Key Distinctions

**Knowledge vs. Cognizance**: Knowledge (proper) is distinguished from mere cognizance. "Cognizance becomes knowledge when man becomes a responsible witness to its content" (line 8623) — when the truth of an object becomes a "necessary and inward determination" of the knower.

**Possibility vs. Actuality**: Barth insists only the *possibility* of knowledge of the Word can be investigated from the human side. The *actuality* is the event of God's free grace — it "can only take place, and it can do so only as a miracle" (lines 10131–10135).

**Church anthropology vs. general anthropology**: The question of knowability is not about *man in general* but about the man concretely addressed by God's Word within the Church (lines 8663–8679). There is no "man in general" who faces the Word of God.

## The Grounding: In the Word Itself, Not in Man

Barth's central claim is that the possibility of knowing the Word of God lies *in the Word itself* — not in a native human religious capacity (*contra* Schleiermacher and the liberal theological tradition) and not even in a supernaturally infused capacity that becomes man's permanent possession (*contra* what Barth calls "indirect Christian Cartesianism" — Wobbermin, Schaeder).

The Word addresses man as creature, sinner, and one who waits (§5 content). What the Word addresses is precisely "an inconceivable *novum* compared to all his ability and capability" (line 8901–8902). Proof text: 1 Cor. 2:6–16.

The corresponding human possibility is therefore "not one we can exhibit but only one to which we can point" (lines 10908–10910) — like faith itself, or the Word, or the child in the manger at Bethlehem.

## The Concrete Form: Acknowledgment

If knowledge of the Word is possible, it consists concretely in *acknowledgment* (*Anerkennung*). Barth develops this through nine dimensions (lines 9340–9483) corresponding to the nine determinations of the Word of God in §5:

1. Knowledge — since the Word is primarily rational/speech
2. Personal relation — acknowledgment of God as person
3. Submission to the Word as Lord
4. Recognition of the Word's contemporary presence (*hic et nunc*)
5. Obedience to the Word's power
6. Decision — even disobedience "acknowledges" the Word
7. Halting before the mystery and concealment of the Word
8. Continual movement between aspects of the Word, never resolved in synthesis
9. Pure acknowledgment: meaning and basis lie wholly outside the acknowledger in the Word itself

At this frontier, "by taking place as an experience this experience ceases to be an experience" (lines 9484–9485). The final ground of acknowledgment is not human at all.

## Faith as the Locus of Knowability

In §6.4 Barth identifies *faith* as "the making possible of knowledge of God's Word that takes place in actual knowledge of it" (lines 10360–10362). The knowability of the Word is therefore located in faith — not as a human possession or achievement, but as the event in which God's Word makes itself known.

Three specifications:

1. **The object is primary**: Faith lives from the Word as its object, not from the quality of its own act. The Word makes faith faith.
2. **Conformity to God (*analogia fidei*)**: In faith, man is in "conformity to God" — not by an *analogia entis* (analogy of being, Roman Catholic) but by the *analogia fidei* of Rom. 12:6: the likeness of the known in the knowing, of the Word in the human word of confession (lines 11228–11234).
3. **Grounded solely in the object**: Man's faith is not self-created but given through the Word; it cannot be "put on inventory" or stored as a possession.

## Rejection of Competing Accounts

Barth explicitly rejects:

- **Schleiermacher / liberal theology**: Knowability grounded in a universal human religious capacity or "feeling of absolute dependence."
- **The "religious a priori"** (Troeltsch, ca. 1910): A Kantian faculty for religious experience native to man as such.
- **Indirect Christian Cartesianism** (Wobbermin, Schaeder): Knowability grounded in the believing ego's "own personal experience of faith" as an "opposite pole" — even if this is conceived as God-given, it still relocates the ground from the Word to the believer's consciousness.
- **The *sacrificium intellectus***: Sacrificing the intellect to make room for the Word is still "only a last desperate attempt to make the knowledge of God a work of man" (lines 11407–11408).

## Assurance and Expectation

The assurance with which the knowability of the Word can be affirmed is the assurance of *faith* — and therefore the assurance of expectation, not possession. "He is not sure of himself but of the Word of God, and he is not sure of the Word of God in and of himself but in and of the Word. His assurance is his own assurance, but it has its seat outside him in the Word of God" (lines 10215–10219).

This "trembling assurance" (line 10287) is paradoxically the strongest assurance, because it rests not on human certainty but on the Word's own self-certainty.
