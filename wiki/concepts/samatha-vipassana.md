# Samatha and Vipassanā (Serenity and Insight)

**Samatha** (Pali; Sanskrit *śamatha*, "serenity" / "tranquillity") and **vipassanā** (Pali;
Sanskrit *vipaśyanā*, "insight" / "clear seeing") are the two fundamental meditative orientations
in [[buddhism|Buddhism]], treated in detail by [[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa]] in the
[[visuddhimagga|*Visuddhimagga*]].

## The Fundamental Distinction

| | Samatha | Vipassanā |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Concentration; suppression of hindrances | Liberating insight into impermanence, suffering, not-self |
| **Object** | A fixed meditation subject (*kammaṭṭhāna*) | Formations as they arise (*saṅkhāra*) |
| **Achievement** | Access concentration → full absorption → [[jhana|jhāna]] | Insight knowledges → path knowledge |
| **Result** | Temporary suppression of defilements | Permanent elimination (cutting off) of defilements |
| **Liberation?** | No — jhāna alone does not liberate | Yes — insight culminating in path knowledge |

The critical Theravada position: **serenity alone does not liberate**. It can produce supernormal
powers and favorable rebirths, but without insight it does not cut the root of craving. This is
illustrated by the pre-Buddhist meditators Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta, who had achieved the
highest immaterial attainments but not Nibbāna. The Buddha surpassed them by adding *vipassanā*.

## Samatha: The Mechanics

Samatha meditation works by concentrating the mind on one of the **forty meditation subjects**
(*kammaṭṭhāna*) until the mind enters **[[jhana|jhāna]]** (meditative absorption). The
*Visuddhimagga* (Chs. III–IX) details all forty subjects, their suitability to different temperaments,
and the progressive stages of absorption.

Samatha suppresses defilements by **substitution** — concentrating on a wholesome object temporarily
prevents the arising of the hindrances. But since it does not see the impermanent/painful/not-self
nature of the formations themselves, the root (craving rooted in ignorance) remains.

## Vipassanā: The Mechanics

Vipassanā takes as its object the **formations themselves** (*saṅkhāra*) — mentality-materiality
as it arises and passes. The meditator sees their three characteristics:
- **Impermanent** (*anicca*) — they arise and pass; do not last even a moment
- **Painful/unsatisfactory** (*dukkha*) — what arises and passes is a threat, a terror, a burden
- **Not-self** (*anattā*) — there is no controller, no owner, no core

This seeing is direct, experiential knowledge — not conceptual understanding. The progressive
development through the insight knowledges (Chs. XVIII–XXI) is the *vipassanā* path.

## The Coupling at the Path Moment

At the moment of the noble path (*magga*), **both are present and coupled** — neither exceeds the
other. Paṭis II 98: "When he emerges from defilements associated with agitation, his concentration
has cessation as its domain. When he emerges from defilements associated with ignorance, his insight
has cessation as its domain. So serenity and insight have a single nature in sense of emergence,
they are coupled together, and neither exceeds the other." The path is thus *both* serenity and
insight, not one or the other.

## The Dry-Insight Path (*Sukkha-vipassaka*)

A meditator who develops only access concentration (not full absorption) and goes directly into
vipassanā is called a **bare-insight / dry-insight worker** (*sukkha-vipassaka*). The
*Visuddhimagga* acknowledges this path (XXII.112) — it yields the same liberation as the
jhāna-based path but produces a path associated with the first jhāna's factors regardless.

## Tradition-Specific Debates

Within [[theravada|Theravada]], the question of whether jhāna is required is actively contested:
- Classical Theravada (following the *Visuddhimagga*) treats jhāna as the ideal basis
- Modern *vipassanā* movements (Mahasi Sayadaw, Ledi Sayadaw tradition) emphasize the dry-insight
  path as accessible to laypeople
- The Burmese Pa-Auk tradition requires deep jhāna as prerequisite for systematic insight

Within [[mahayana|Mahayana]], the *śamatha-vipaśyanā* pair (Sanskrit) is fundamental to Madhyamaka
practice: stabilizing the mind before undertaking the analysis of emptiness (*śūnyatā*).

In [[vajrayana|Vajrayana]], the two are integrated into the tantric path via *tummo* and other
practices; the coupling is instantaneous rather than sequential.
