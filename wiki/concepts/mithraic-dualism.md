# Mithraic Dualism

> **Concept page.** The specific kind of dualism operative in the Roman cult of
> [[mithras|Mithras]], as analyzed by Samuel Laeuchli in
> [[laeuchli-groh-mithraism-in-ostia|*Mithraism in Ostia*]]. The central claim: it is **not**
> primarily a metaphysical cosmic dualism but a **social and cultic** one.

## Beyond the Iranian "Two Gods"

Laeuchli rejects two narrow definitions — dualism as "an extreme of an Iranian type of two gods,
one good and one evil," and dualism as merely "the metaphysical, religious, or philosophical
aspects of such duality" — because each "prevents the observer from grasping the dualistic
problem in the ancient world." Following Simone Pétrement, he treats an "extensive scale of
thought patterns — Plato, Valentinus, Mani — all three different from one another," and
distinguishes **five dualisms operating simultaneously** in antiquity:

1. **Metaphysical/cosmic** — Iranian two principles, but in Mithraism *qualified* ("only the
   scorpion is the product of Ahriman, and the bull is not evil").
2. **Social** — the exclusive initiation circle (Qumran, the mysteries, the *ekklesia*).
3. **Psychological** — escape from an evil world by ascent, magic, or ritual rebirth.
4. **Ethical** — asceticism and ritual preparation.
5. **Religious/philosophical** — mythologies of two worlds, "the poetry of the Fall and the
   patterns of salvation."

## A Qualified Break with Nature

Mithraism arose, on Laeuchli's account, when "the dualistic attack upon the natural experience of
man" drove him to seek roots beyond nature — the naturalistic cave-rite transplanted into the
urban house-sanctuary. But "**the break with nature was a qualified break**": the slain bull "was
not evil," and although there is even "an altar to Ahriman at Ostia," Ahriman is no supreme
principle. Mithraism therefore "stands between the speculative theology of the Gnostics and the
refined philosophy of Plotinus as a nonphilosophical Oriental cult with a myth it did not dare to
question." It "**must be held separate from the Gnostic one**" — the [[gnosticism|Gnostics]]
"asked questions which neither soldiers nor worshipers dared to raise."

## Social Dualism as the Key

The decisive form is **social dualism**: the arcane sanctuary as withdrawal from "the world,"
offering "both immortal life and a social unity" — but "not a radical withdrawal. The merchants
and slaves continued their business happily." "It was the genius of this urbanized, Romanized
Mithraism to offer man a new life by leaving him right where he was." Laeuchli glosses it as "a
social dualism related to the masonic ritual, which fits without conflict into the daily routine
of a businessman" — and marks the contrast with Christianity precisely here: "**Here lay the
issue between Christianity and Mithraism!**" (see [[christianity-mithraism-parallels]]).

## The Tauroctony as Encoded Dualism

The **tauroctonos** at the sanctuary's axis encodes this social dualism rather than a cosmic
war of gods: the bull-slaying "expressed the myth of a community which through initiation and
ritual separated itself from the rest of [[ostia|Ostia]]." Laeuchli identifies three ritual
identifications with the image — with **Mithra** the slayer (the soldier's *Invictus* ideal),
with the **bull** (natural for slaves: "from the blood arose new life"), and with the **total
event** (the communal salvific drama). See [[mithraism|the Mithraism page]] for the symbol's
ritual setting.
