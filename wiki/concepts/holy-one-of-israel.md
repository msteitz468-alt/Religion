*Qĕdôš yiśrāʾēl*, "the Holy One of Israel" — the book of [[isaiah|Isaiah]]'s signature
divine title, and (in Oswalt's reading) the theological center of the whole book.

## Definition and Distribution

The title's distribution is a brute textual fact: it is overwhelmingly Isaianic. Oswalt
gives the figures as 13 occurrences in chs. 1–39 and 16 in chs. 40–66 against "only 7 times
elsewhere in the Bible" (in the ch. 6 commentary he counts 26 in Isaiah vs 6 elsewhere —
both counts are his), and uses this even distribution across both halves as an argument for
the book's compositional unity.

## Content of the Title (Oswalt, attributed)

Oswalt argues holiness in Isaiah has two inseparable dimensions:

1. **Ontological otherness** — "the terrifying 'otherness' of God," expressed in the book's
   height-and-removal language ("high and lifted up," 6:1; 57:15). Holiness was originally
   "distinctness," in origin "a morally neutral word."
2. **Moral and ethical perfection** — "the primary characteristic that set this God apart…
   was his moral and ethical perfection," keyed to 5:16 ("the Holy God is sanctified in
   righteousness") and to Isaiah's cry "I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5): what crushes
   Isaiah "is not the recognition of his finitude… it is his uncleanness." Israel's
   distinctive, per Oswalt, is filling the common Semitic category of holiness with ethical
   content: "'You shall be holy as I am holy' (Lev 19) does not refer to ritual purity but
   rather to ethical behavior."

The trisagion of 6:3 he reads as "the strongest form of the superlative in Hebrew… Israel's
God is the most 'godly' of all the gods" — and notes (endnotes) that nothing in the context
supports the patristic Trinitarian reading, 1QIsaᵃ having only two "holies," probably by
haplography.

## Usage Within Isaiah (attributed to Oswalt)

- First occurrence at 1:4, where forsaking the Holy One is Israel's basic act of rebellion.
- Mockers weaponize the title at 5:19 — "a way of taunting Isaiah and deriding what they
  considered his pietistic excess"; by 30:11 the people wish to "take the Holy One of Israel
  from before us."
- "Refusal to trust in him is in fact a denial of his holiness"; the restored remnant will
  "rest upon… the Holy One of Israel in truth" (10:20).
- The song of ch. 12 climaxes in the title: "holiness is the sum total of the attributes of
  deity."
- At 37:23 the title names Sennacherib's real adversary — and its presence in the Kings
  parallel, being "highly characteristic of Isaiah but not of Kings," is one of Oswalt's
  arguments for Isaianic priority of the chs. 36–39 narratives.
