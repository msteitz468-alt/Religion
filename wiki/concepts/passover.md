**Passover** (Hebrew: *Pesakh*; Greek: *Pascha*) is the founding liturgical commemoration of Jewish faith, instituted in Exodus 12 to memorialize YHWH's deliverance of Israel from Egyptian slavery through the death of Egypt's firstborn. The word *pesakh* means "passed over" or, more precisely, "protected/shielded" (*pesakh* in Isa 31:5 means "to shield"). The Passover lamb, the unleavened bread, and the annual Seder are the primary vehicles through which the exodus becomes a constitutive present-tense act for each generation.

## The Original Institution (Exod 12)

### Structural Logic

[[james-bruckner|Bruckner]] demonstrates that Exod 12–13 is a chiastic structure braiding together three themes (protection of the firstborn, Passover lamb, Feast of Unleavened Bread):

> A — First Passover instruction to Moses (12:1–13)  
> B — Feast of Unleavened Bread instruction to Moses (12:14–20)  
> C — First Passover instruction to the people (12:21–27)  
> **D — First historical Passover-exodus event (12:28–42)** *(center)*  
> C′ — Perpetual Passover ordinance (12:43–51)  
> B′ — Perpetual Feast of Unleavened Bread (13:3–10)  
> A′ — Perpetual firstborn ordinance (13:11–16)  

The historical event (D) is the center; everything else is instruction framing the event and its perpetual commemoration.

### Ritual Details

- A year-old male lamb or kid, without blemish, selected on the 10th of Nisan and slaughtered on the 14th at twilight
- Blood applied to the doorposts and lintel of the house with hyssop
- Roasted whole (not boiled) and eaten with unleavened bread and bitter herbs
- Eaten in haste, with traveling clothes and staff in hand — "with trepidation" (*khippazon*)
- Leftovers burned; no bones broken

The blood: **Not apotropaic magic.** Bruckner insists the blood was "a sign for the people" (12:13) — their visible acceptance of YHWH's lordship and offer of protection. "God provided the blood; God does the protecting. The absence of blood on a doorframe would constitute a rejection of God's offer of grace."

### Sacrificial Category

The Passover lamb is classified as a *zebakh* ("slaughter for eating" — a fellowship or thank-offering eaten by the community), not a *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (sin offering). Bruckner: "Nothing was said about forgiveness or being made clean. The instructions for later Passovers never again mention the blood of the lamb... since this was a one-time event of deliverance." The NT's use of Passover imagery to describe Christ's death imports Levitical atonement imagery that is not native to the original Passover institution but does appear in the broader sacrificial framework. See [[sacrifice|Sacrifice and Atonement]].

### Inclusion and Exclusion

The perpetual Passover statutes (12:43–51) specify who may participate:
- **Excluded**: foreigners (*nekar*) who worship other gods; temporary residents (*toshab*); hired workers (*shakir*) with no commitment to the community
- **Included**: circumcised male slaves (12:44); landed immigrants (*ger*) who have joined the community permanently and been circumcised (12:48)

The table is not universally open; it constitutes the community of those who have accepted YHWH's lordship. "Participation is stated as nonnegotiable... comparable to someone who will not be baptized as a Christian or share in the Lord's Supper."

## Feast of Unleavened Bread

Intimately linked to the Passover, the Feast of Unleavened Bread (*Matzot*) lasts seven days beginning with the Passover night. Unleavened bread (*matsah*) commemorates the hasty departure — "there was no time for the bread to rise." The *seʾor* (sourdough starter) must be removed from the house; anyone eating *khamets* (leavened food) is "cut off" from the community (*karet*).

The feast is consistently described as a *personal* act of remembering: "On that day tell your son, 'I do this because of what the LORD did *for me* when I came out of Egypt'" (13:8) — not "what he did for them," but "for me." Even future generations say "for me." This liturgical first-person is a distinctive feature of the Seder.

## Jewish Observance

The **Seder** (lit. "order") is the Passover meal liturgy, structured to fulfill the Exod 13:8 obligation of personal narration to children. The Haggadah ("telling") guides the ritual: the four questions (asked by the youngest child), the narration of the exodus, the explanation of the symbolic foods, the psalms (Hallel, Pss 113–118), and the meals.

The structure follows a pattern: *describe the event* → *explain its meaning* → *celebrate with eating and drinking*. The Seder is present-tense: participants re-enter the exodus, not merely remember it. "This year we are slaves; next year may we be free."

Current Seder practice substitutes a lamb shank (or chicken) for the Passover lamb, since the sacrifice cannot be performed without the Temple. The Samaritans continue to roast an unskinned, uneviscerated whole lamb on the traditional site at Mount Gerizim.

## Christian Reception

### Synoptic Gospels

The Last Supper is set within the Passover feast context (Mark 14:12-16; Matt 26:17-19; Luke 22:7-13). Jesus reinterprets the cup ("This is my blood of the covenant") and bread over the Passover table, creating the Eucharist as a transformed Passover in which his death is the exodus event.

### John

John's Gospel schedules the crucifixion on the day of preparation for Passover (19:14), so that Jesus dies as the lambs are being slaughtered in the Temple. John 19:36 applies Exod 12:46 ("no bone of him shall be broken") to Jesus.

### Paul

Paul explicitly invokes the Passover: "Christ our Passover lamb has been sacrificed. Therefore let us celebrate the festival — not with the old yeast of malice and evil but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (1 Cor 5:7-8). The typological logic: the old *khamets* = bondage to sin; Christ's sacrifice = the one-time Passover deliverance; the Christian life = the perpetual Feast of Unleavened Bread in freedom.

### Hebrews

The author of Hebrews reads Passover and the Levitical atonement sacrifices together: "Christ our high priest entered the true holy of holies with his own blood" (Heb 9:11-14), combining Passover and Day of Atonement typologies. The NT uses of the Passover and Levitical sacrifice overlap significantly; keeping the categories distinct (as Bruckner does) is exegetically important.

## The Passover and the Covenant

The Passover institutionalized Israel's identity as a delivered, covenant people before the Sinai covenant was formally ratified. The blood covenant at Sinai (Exod 24:8, "the blood of the covenant") echoes the Passover blood but shifts the sacrificial purpose from protection to covenant confirmation. See [[covenant|Covenant]].
