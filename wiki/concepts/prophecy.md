# Prophecy

A theological-philosophical concept receiving its fullest medieval Jewish treatment in [[maimonides|Maimonides]]' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]], Part II, Chapters XXXII–XLVIII. Maimonides synthesizes Aristotelian faculty psychology, Neoplatonic emanation theory, and rabbinic tradition into a systematic account of how divine communication reaches human minds.

---

## Definition

> "Prophecy is, in truth and reality, an emanation sent forth by the Divine Being through the medium of the Active Intellect, in the first instance to man's rational faculty, and then to his imaginative faculty; it is the highest degree and greatest perfection man can attain: it consists in the most Perfect development of the imaginative faculty." (Ch. XXXVI)

Prophecy thus involves two faculties receiving the overflow of the [[separate-intellects|Active Intellect]]: intellect (rational faculty) first, then imagination. This double mediation is the foundation of the entire theory.

---

## The Three Views on Prophecy

Maimonides identifies three positions, paralleling the three positions on the eternity of the world (Ch. XXXII):

**1. Popular-religious view**: God selects any person He wishes — wise or ignorant, young or old — and inspires them, provided they are morally good. No preparation required.

**2. Philosophical view (al-Farabi / Avicenna)**: Prophecy is a natural human faculty brought to perfection by study and training. Given a fully prepared person, prophecy *must* result: "it is impossible that a man who has the capacity for prophecy should prepare himself for it without attaining it, just as it is impossible that a person with a healthy constitution should be fed well, and yet not properly assimilate his food." (Ch. XXXII)

**3. Maimonides' view (Scripture)**: Agrees with the philosophers on the necessity of preparation, but adds one crucial exception: even a fully prepared person may be withheld from prophecy by the will of God. "It is in that case the will of God [that withholds from him the use of the faculty]. According to my opinion, this fact is as exceptional as any other miracle." (Ch. XXXII). His Scriptural proof: Baruch son of Neriah prepared under Jeremiah but was told through Jeremiah, "Thou seekest for thee great things, do not seek" (Jer. xlv. 5).

The rabbinic dictum supports Maimonides: "The spirit of prophecy only rests upon persons who are wise, strong, and rich." (Ch. XXXII, quoting Sages). Fools and ignorant persons are as unfit for prophecy "as it is for an ass or a frog" — prophecy is impossible without study and training; once these create the *possibility*, whether it is actualized depends on God's will (Ch. XXXII).

---

## Prerequisites for Prophecy

Maimonides lists three kinds of perfection required (Ch. XXXVI):

1. **Physical perfection**: "The substance of the brain must from the very beginning be in the most perfect condition as regards purity of matter, composition of its different parts, size and position." A defective organ cannot be remedied by training; physical preparation must begin at birth (cf. Jer. i. 5, "Before I formed thee in the womb I knew thee").

2. **Intellectual perfection**: The rational faculty must pass "from a state of potentiality to that of actuality"; the intellect must be "as developed and perfect as human intellect can be"; thoughts directed to knowledge of God and the hidden laws of the universe.

3. **Moral perfection**: Suppression of all bodily pleasures — especially those of touch, which Maimonides (following Aristotle) calls "a disgrace to us" (Ch. XXXVI) — and of desires for domination, honor, and followers. The prophet must consider the multitude according to their true worth, engaging them only to guard against harm or derive necessary benefit.

When all three coincide with a fully active imaginative faculty influenced by the Active Intellect, "such a person will undoubtedly perceive nothing but things very extraordinary and divine, and see nothing but God and His angels. His knowledge will only include that which is real knowledge, and his thought will only be directed to such general principles as would tend to improve the social relations between man and man." (Ch. XXXVI)

---

## Three Classes of Persons Receiving the Active Intellect's Overflow

The Active Intellect's influence may reach persons differently (Ch. XXXVII):

1. **Only the rational faculty** → wise men and philosophers. Can attain truth but not prophecy.

2. **Both rational and imaginative faculties** → true prophets. The highest class.

3. **Only the imaginative faculty** (rational faculty undeveloped by neglect or natural defect) → statesmen, lawgivers, diviners, charmers, men of true dreams and secret arts. These may believe themselves prophets and produce "a strange mixture of true and imaginary things" — they are false prophets or pseudo-prophets.

The true prophet exceeds the philosopher in that his imagination is so perfected it can represent things not previously perceived by the senses, something only possible because the Active Intellect has first elevated his intellect. A person without intellectual perfection who claims visions is simply replaying retained impressions in a distorted way (Ch. XXXVIII).

---

## The Role of the Imagination

The imagination's highest function operates "when the senses are at rest and pause in their action, for then it receives, to some extent, divine inspiration in the measure as it is predisposed for this influence." (Ch. XXXVI). Hence:

- **Dreams** and **prophecy** differ in degree, not kind: "dream is the sixtieth part of prophecy" (Sages, cited Ch. XXXVI). The Sages also say "Dream is the nobelet (unripe fruit) of prophecy" — same fruit but fallen before fully developed.

- All prophecy comes through either vision (mareh/mahazeh/hazon) or dream: "If there be among you a prophet, I, the Lord, will make myself known unto him in a vision, in a dream will I speak to him" (Num. xii. 6; cited Ch. XXXVI).

- Emotional states suppress prophecy: "Inspiration does not come upon a prophet when he is sad or languid." Jacob received no revelation during mourning for Joseph; the exile caused cessation of prophecy because "there is no greater misfortune for man than to be a slave bought for money in the service of ignorant and voluptuous masters" (Ch. XXXVI).

---

## Modes of Prophetic Communication

Maimonides identifies four textual forms by which Scripture reports prophetic communication (Ch. XLI):

1. Prophet states explicitly he heard an angel in a dream or vision.
2. Prophet reports the angel's words without specifying dream/vision (assumes this is known).
3. Prophet says God spoke but notes it was in a dream or vision.
4. Prophet says God spoke without specifying dream or vision (assumes the principle is known).

**Key principle**: "Do not imagine that an angel is seen or his word heard otherwise than in a prophetic vision or prophetic dream" (Ch. XLII). Non-prophets (Hagar, Manoah) experienced only *bat-kol* ("prophetic echo"), not true prophecy.

Prophets also sometimes use wordplay and homonyms: the almond staff (*makkah shaked*) in Jeremiah points to "I will watch (*shoked*)" — entirely different in meaning from the visual image (Ch. XLIII).

---

## The Eleven Degrees of Prophecy

Maimonides enumerates eleven degrees (Ch. XLV). The first two are not true prophecy but steps toward it:

**(1) Spirit of the Lord** — divine assistance impelling a person to a great deed (delivering the oppressed, saving a community). Does not involve speech or revelation. Applies to the judges ("The Lord raised up judges for them, and the Lord was with the judge"), Samson, Saul, David after anointing, and Moses from manhood (who could not restrain himself from intervening against injustice even as a stranger in Midian).

**(2) Holy spirit** (*ruah ha-kodesh*) — a person awake, in full possession of senses, feels inspired to compose hymns, treat of science, discuss theology. David's Psalms, Solomon's Proverbs and Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon, Daniel, Job, Chronicles, Esther — all composed by holy spirit; their authors are "prophets" only in a looser sense. This is a degree below true prophetic dream.

**True prophecy (degrees 3–11)** (prophet introduces speech with "the word of the Lord came to me"):

**(3)** Sees an allegory in a prophetic dream, and its meaning is interpreted within the same dream (most of Zechariah's prophecies).

**(4)** Hears something clearly in a prophetic dream but does not see the speaker (Samuel at the beginning of his prophetic career).

**(5)** A person addresses the prophet in a dream (some of Ezekiel's prophecies).

**(6)** An angel speaks to the prophet in a dream: "And an angel of God said to me in a dream of night" (Gen. xxxi. 11). This applies to most prophets.

**(7)** In a prophetic dream it appears to the prophet that God is speaking: Isaiah "I saw the Lord, and I heard the voice of the Lord saying, Whom shall I send" (Isa. vi. 1, 8); Micaiah "I saw the Lord" (I Kings xxii. 19). Maimonides notes that strictly, in a vision one *knows* (hitpael of *yada*) but is not addressed by God — the speaking belongs to the dream mode.

**(8)** Allegorical figures are perceived in a prophetic vision while awake (Abraham in the vision "between the pieces," Gen. xv. 9–10).

**(9)** Words are heard in a prophetic vision: "And behold the word came to him, saying, This shall not be thine heir" (Gen. xv. 4).

**(10)** A man appears to the prophet in a prophetic vision and speaks to him (Abraham at the plain of Mamre, Gen. xviii.; Joshua at Jericho, Josh. v. 13).

**(11)** An angel speaks to the prophet in a prophetic vision (Abraham at the sacrifice of Isaac, Gen. xxii. 15). Maimonides considers this **the highest degree achievable by any prophet below Moses**.

## The Uniqueness of Moses

Moses' prophecy stands in a class apart, to the degree that Maimonides says "the term prophet is applied to Moses and other men **homonymously**" (Ch. XXXV). Four distinguishing characteristics:

1. Moses received prophecy directly, not through an angel — "Mouth to mouth do I speak with him" (Num. xii. 8); the voice came "from above the covering of the ark from between the two cherubim" (Exod. xxv. 22), **without the medium of the imaginative faculty** (Ch. XLV).

2. Moses' prophetic perception was superior to all prophets before and after: "And there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face" (Deut. xxxiv. 10).

3. Moses received revelation while fully awake and in possession of all faculties, not in dreams or visions; other prophets needed the suspension of the senses.

4. Moses' signs were performed publicly, "in the sight of all Israel" and before Pharaoh and his entire court — friend and foe alike. Other prophets' miracles were witnessed by few (Ch. XXXV).

Because Moses uniquely combined the highest prophetic perfection and the highest miracle-working, no other Law could have been given or will be given: "It is a principle in our faith that there will never be revealed another Law" (Ch. XXXIX).

---

## Pre-Mosaic and Post-Mosaic Prophecy

Prior to Moses, prophets such as Abraham, Noah, Shem, Eber, Enoch, and Methushelah received prophetic inspiration but did not proclaim "God sent me to you with a command." They taught by persuasion, philosophical argument, and example:

> "Abraham taught, and showed by philosophical arguments that there is one God, that He has created everything that exists beside Him... he trained his fellow-men in this belief, and won their attention by pleasant words as well as by acts of kindness. Abraham did not tell the people that God had sent him to them with the command concerning certain things." (Ch. XXXIX)

Post-Mosaic prophets exhort Israel to keep the Law of Moses and warn of consequences — they do not introduce new legislation.

---

## The Prophetic Act as Vision, Not Physical Event

A crucial hermeneutical principle (Ch. XLVI): prophetic acts described as journeys, commands obeyed, objects handled — Ezekiel digging in the Temple wall, Isaiah going naked, Hosea marrying a harlot, Jeremiah hiding the girdle at the Euphrates — all occurred within the prophetic vision only, not in physical reality. "God forbid to assume that God would make his prophets appear an object of ridicule and sport in the eyes of the ignorant, and order them to perform foolish acts." The narrative form omits the reminder "this was a vision" because it is a known principle.

---

## False Prophecy

The test of a true prophet is primarily **character and conduct**, especially regarding bodily pleasure (Ch. XL): "The best test is the rejection, abstention, and contempt of bodily pleasures: for this is the first condition of men, and a fortiori of prophets: they must especially disregard pleasures of the sense of touch." Zedekiah son of Maasiah and Ahab son of Kolaiah claimed prophecy while "committing adultery with the wives of their companions and followers" — "God exposed their falsehood" and Nebuchadnezzar burned them (citing Jer. xxix. 22–23, Ch. XL).

Divine laws (laws of Moses) are distinguished from human political legislations by their aim: divine law improves both the body politic *and* the theological and intellectual faculties of individuals. Human legislation improves social order only. Plagiarists steal true prophetic content but betray themselves by their conduct.

---

## Prophetic Language: Intermediate Causes and Divine Speech

Maimonides concludes the section (Ch. XLVIII) with the principle that all things produced have a causal chain back to the "will and decree of God." Prophets *omit* intermediate causes (natural laws, human freewill, animal desire, chance) and ascribe events directly to God. Thus "God said," "God commanded," "God sent" cover natural phenomena (snowmelt, storms), human political decisions (Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns), animal behavior (the fish swallowing Jonah), and chance events (Rebecca's betrothal). "It makes no difference whether these causes consist in substances, physical properties, freewill, or chance."

## Aquinas on prophecy (ST II-II Q171–178)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats prophecy as the first of the **gratuitous graces** (*gratiae
gratis datae*) — charisms given not for the recipient's own sanctification but **for the benefit of
others** ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] II-II Q171–178). His account converges with Maimonides at several
points and diverges sharply at others.

- **Nature (Q171).** Prophecy is primarily **knowledge** (a supernatural light enabling sure
  judgement of things beyond natural reason), with speech and miracle as secondary and confirmatory.
  It is **not a habit but a transient passion** — the prophetic light comes and goes (Elisha needing
  the minstrel) — a notable contrast with a fixed disposition. It extends to past, present, future,
  divine, and human things; **future contingents** are its most proper matter, but it is not confined
  to them (against a narrowing of prophecy to prediction). **Nothing false** can fall under true
  prophecy, though prophecies of denunciation express the order of *causes*, not God's final decree
  (hence Nineveh).
- **Cause (Q172).** Prophecy **cannot be natural** (2 Pet. 1:21) and is mediated through the
  **angels**; unusually, Aquinas holds it does **not require sanctifying grace or charity** (Balaam,
  Caiaphas prophesied), though disordered passions hinder it. **Demons** can produce a real but
  improper "prophecy" by manipulating the imagination — they cannot enlighten the intellect — and
  may speak truths (the Sibyls).
- **Manner and degrees (Q173–174).** Prophets do **not see God's essence** but receive an
  intellectual light, sometimes with new or rearranged images; intellectual vision alone ranks above
  imaginative. As in Maimonides, **Moses is the greatest prophet** — but Aquinas (with Augustine)
  holds Moses, like **Paul in his rapture, *did* see the divine essence** (Q174, A4), a stronger
  claim than the *Guide*'s. Post-apostolic prophecy continues for the **direction of action**, not
  for new doctrine.
- **Rapture (Q175).** **Paul was "caught up to the third heaven" and saw God's essence** — through a
  transient light of glory, withdrawn from his senses though not wholly separated from his body, and
  not knowing "whether in the body or out of the body."
- **The other charisms (Q176–178).** The **gift of tongues**, the **grace of the word** (preaching —
  women may teach privately but not publicly), and the working of **miracles** complete the
  gratuitous graces; prophecy ranks above tongues (knowledge of things over knowledge of words).

This Christian, Aristotelian-but-grace-centred account stands beside Maimonides' emanationist theory
above as a major cross-tradition comparison: both rank Moses supreme and tie prophecy to intellect
and imagination, but Aquinas grounds it in transient divine motion and angelic mediation rather than
the steady overflow of the [[separate-intellects|Active Intellect]], and uniquely admits the
beatific vision in rapture.
