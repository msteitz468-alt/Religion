# Eucharist

The central sacramental rite of Christianity, in which bread and wine (sometimes wine mixed with water) are consecrated and received as the body and blood of Jesus Christ. The term *Eucharistia* (Greek: εὐχαριστία, "thanksgiving") appears first as a technical term for the rite in the **[[didache|Didache]]** (late 1st/early 2nd c.) and [[first-apology-justin|Justin Martyr's First Apology]] (c. 155 CE), where it refers to both the prayer of thanksgiving and the consecrated food itself.

## Earliest Detailed Description: Justin Martyr

Justin Martyr (*First Apol.* Chs LXV–LXVI) provides the earliest surviving detailed account of the Eucharistic rite, combining a description of the post-baptismal first Eucharist (Ch. LXV) with a theological definition (Ch. LXVI) and a description of the regular Sunday Eucharist (Ch. LXVII).

### The Post-Baptismal Eucharist (Ch. LXV)

After baptism, the newly illuminated person is brought to the assembly (*ekklēsia*) of "the brethren." Common prayers are offered for all present and absent. After the prayers, "we salute one another with a kiss" (the *osculum pacis* / kiss of peace). Bread and a cup of wine mixed with water are brought to "the president of the brethren" (*proestos tōn adelphōn*). He "gives praise and glory to the Father of the universe, through the name of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, and offers thanks at considerable length (*epi polú*) for our being counted worthy to receive these things at His hands." All assent with "Amen" (Justin explains: "this word Amen answers in the Hebrew language to *genoito* [so be it]"). The deacons then distribute to all present and carry portions to the absent.

### The Theological Definition (Ch. LXVI)

"This food is called among us *Eucharistia* [the Eucharist], of which no one is allowed to partake but the man who believes that the things which we teach are true, and who has been washed with the washing that is for the remission of sins, and unto regeneration, and who is so living as Christ has enjoined."

The theological claim: "For not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word, and from which our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh."

Justin then cites the institution narrative: "the apostles, in the memoirs composed by them, which are called Gospels, have thus delivered unto us what was enjoined upon them; that Jesus took bread, and when He had given thanks, said, 'This do ye in remembrance of Me, this is My body;' and that, after the same manner, having taken the cup and given thanks, He said, 'This is My blood.'"

The Mithraic parallel is cited as demonic imitation: "which the wicked devils have imitated in the mysteries of Mithras, commanding the same thing to be done. For, that bread and a cup of water are placed with certain incantations in the mystic rites of one who is being initiated, you either know or can learn" (Ch. LXVI).

### The Sunday Eucharist (Ch. LXVII)

The regular weekly Eucharist is embedded in the full Sunday assembly. See [[first-apology-justin|First Apology, Ch. LXVII]] for the complete description. Key liturgical sequence: reading of apostolic memoirs or prophets → presidential homily → common prayer → presentation of bread and wine and water → presidential thanksgiving prayer (*epi polú*) → Amen → distribution by deacons → collection for the poor.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Proto-Orthodox Christianity

Justin's description is consistent with and anticipates later eucharistic theology without resolving its later controversies. His claim that the food is "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" by "transmutation" (*metaballo*) establishes a real identification between the consecrated elements and Christ's body and blood, but Justin does not theorize the mechanism. This language was later developed in different directions:

- **Catholic tradition** (transubstantiation): the substance of bread and wine is changed into the substance of body and blood at consecration — the most literal development of Justin's claim.
- **Eastern Orthodox tradition** (metabolē): transformation of the elements through the *epiclesis* (prayer invoking the Holy Spirit) — close to Justin's *metaballo*.
- **Lutheran tradition** (sacramental union / Real Presence): Christ's body and blood are truly present *in, with, and under* the bread and wine.
- **Reformed/Calvinist tradition** (spiritual presence): Christ is spiritually present; the elements are signs and seals, not substances.
- **Zwinglian/memorialist tradition**: The Eucharist is a commemorative rite — "do this in remembrance of me" is taken as defining the rite's meaning.

Justin's insistence that only the baptized who live according to Christ's teaching may receive the Eucharist is an early statement of **eucharistic discipline** (communion restricted to the initiated faithful), a practice maintained in Catholic and Orthodox traditions and variably practiced in Protestant churches.

## Key Interpretive Issues

**"Transmutation" (*metaballo*)**: Justin uses this word for the change in the eucharistic food, but does not explain what changes or how. The word was later used by Catholic theologians to argue for substantial change; Reformation interpreters argued it referred only to use or function. Justin's text does not resolve this.

**Wine and water**: Justin specifies "wine mixed with water" (*krasis oinou kai hydatos*) as the cup. This practice (mixing water with wine in the Eucharist) was standard in proto-orthodox tradition and became a theological point in later debates about the two natures of Christ (wine = divinity, water = humanity in some patristic readings).

**The *proestos* (president)**: Justin's term for the presider is not bishop, priest, or elder, but "president" — suggesting a functional office whose relation to developing episcopal structures is unclear. The president offers the thanksgiving prayer "according to his ability" (*hoson dynamis autō*), implying extempore prayer within a fixed structure, not a fixed eucharistic text.
