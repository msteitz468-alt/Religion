# Divine Impassibility (*Apatheia* of God)

The patristic axiom that God is *impassible* — incapable of suffering, change, or being acted upon from without. Distinct from [[apatheia|apatheia]] as the *human* spiritual ideal, divine impassibility is a predicate of God himself, absorbed into Christian doctrine from Greek philosophy and functioning as an unquestioned presupposition of the Trinitarian and Christological controversies. Drawn from [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Jaroslav Pelikan]], Vol. I, ch. 1.

## An Unargued Axiom

Pelikan observes that Christian theologians "customarily set down the doctrine of the impassibility of God as an axiom, without bothering to provide very much biblical support or theological proof." It "came into Christian doctrine from Greek philosophy," where *apatheia* meant freedom "of the changes and sufferings that characterize human life" — and stood in tension with the Old Testament's God of sovereign freedom and relational pathos (see [[divine-pathos]]).

Representative formulations: [[athanasius|Athanasius]] — God "stands in need of nothing, but is self-sufficient"; **Didymus the Blind** — the Spirit must be "impassible, indivisible, and immutable"; [[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinaris]] — "Anyone who introduces passion into the [divine] power is atheistic."

## Function in the Controversies

The axiom shaped everything downstream. It is why the [[communicatio-idiomatum|communicatio idiomatum]] and the language of "God crucified" were so fraught; why [[arianism|Arian]] and orthodox alike had to locate Christ's suffering carefully; and (Pelikan notes) it became a presupposition even of Anselm's later atonement theory. [[tertullian|Tertullian]], against [[marcion|Marcion]], drew the careful distinction that preserved it: "God may be wrathful, but he is not irritated." It is a prime exhibit in the [[hellenization-of-christianity|hellenization debate]].
