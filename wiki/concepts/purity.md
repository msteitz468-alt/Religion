# Purity

Ritual purity — the state that qualifies a person for contact with the holy, contracted or lost
through food, disease, bodily secretion, and contact — is the subject of the dispute at
[[matthew|Matthew]] 15:1-20, where a delegation from [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] challenges Jesus
over his disciples' failure to wash their hands before eating. This page records the treatment
in [[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the wiki's only ingested
source on the passage.

## The Weight of Purity in Pharisaic Religion

France states that "it is hard to exaggerate the significance of ritual purity for the Pharisaic
ideology," which most scribes supported and which became the basis of later rabbinic orthodoxy;
a large proportion of the halakhic tradition later codified in the Mishnah focuses on it,
especially the division *Ṭeharot*. See [[pharisees]].

France notes that the purity of God's people separated them from all others, so "the food laws
thus became a barrier to social intercourse between Jew and Gentile" — the cause of the long
and painful early-church dispute (Acts 10:9-48). See [[gentiles]].

He observes that the issue has been latent throughout Matthew's narrative — "the issue would
have been constantly present for one known as a healer" — through Jesus touching a leper,
contact with Gentiles, visiting pig-keeping territory, being touched by a woman with a
menstrual disorder, touching a corpse, and eating with tax collectors and sinners.

## Wenham (NICOT Leviticus) — uncleanness system and food laws (fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18)

*Attributed to [[gordon-wenham|Wenham]]; source [[wenham-leviticus-nicot]].*

**Structure.** Leviticus 11–15 explains kinds of uncleanness and their treatment, looking back to the priestly commission of 10:10 (distinguish holy/common and unclean/clean) and **preparing for ch. 16** (Day of Atonement “because of the uncleannesses of the Israelites”). Parallel: chs. 1–7 prepare for 8–10 as 11–15 prepare for 16.

| Chapter | Domain |
|---|---|
| 11 | Clean/unclean animals (food) |
| 12 | Childbirth (lochial discharge, not the birth-event as such; infant not declared unclean) |
| 13–14 | Skin and fungus “diseases” (*ṣāraʿat*—not simply modern Hansen’s disease); priest declares by symptoms |
| 15 | Bodily discharges |

**Food-law rationales Wenham rejects:** hygiene as the human authors’ primary motive; pure arbitrariness (except last resort); uncontrolled virtue-allegory; cultic/anti-Canaanite as *sole* cause.

**Rationale he accepts (Douglas):** clean animals exemplify **complete locomotion types** for their sphere; the divisions teach holiness-as-wholeness and **mirror election** (Israel separated among nations). NT holiness language (Christians as “saints”; 1 Pet 1:16 quoting the Levitical motto) continues the call without reinstating the food boundary for Gentiles (Acts 10/15).

**Skin disease:** often functions as living death / covenant exclusion; healing is from God, not priestly magic; full reentry (ch. 14) can require the full suite of offerings including a surprising **reparation** offering. Two birds // two goats of Yom Kippur as aggregation/elimination pair (Wenham following Davies et al.).

## Handwashing Is Not Biblical Law

France establishes a distinction that governs his whole reading. Handwashing was not an OT
requirement for ordinary Jews: "the only regular ritual handwashing required in the OT law is
that of the priests before undertaking their cultic duties" (Exod 30:18-21; 40:30-32) or before
eating sacrificial food (Lev 22:4-7). Subsequent scribal rulings extended the priestly
principle to ordinary food and non-priests, on the ground that Israel was a priestly nation —
but France is explicitly inconclusive on the timing: "it is uncertain how far this process had
advanced by the time of Jesus." He suggests ordinary people would have had no problem with the
disciples' practice, and that "this group of Jerusalem teachers are expecting of them a more
rigorous standard than was yet recognized in Galilee."

France's resulting formulation is precise and consequential: on handwashing Jesus stands in no
tension with the OT law and merely challenges the scribes' right to impose nonbiblical rules —
legitimate scribal debate. But "it is when Jesus goes on to question the very nature of purity
in itself that he, not his Pharisaic opponents, opens up the issue of the continuing validity of
the OT law." See [[old-law]], [[oral-torah]].

## The Counter-Charge: Qorban (15:3-9)

France notes that Mark has "Moses said" where Matthew has "God said," making explicit the
assumption every Jew shared that the laws come from God rather than simply from
[[moses|Moses]]; and that the argument assumes the commanded "honor" is more than verbal and
"includes making appropriate provision for one's parents in old age."

He explains *qorbān* (and the equivalent *qōnām*), from Mishnah tractate *Nedarim*, as a formula
dedicating food, money, or property to God — in practice to the temple treasury — so that
"anything so dedicated was thus placed out of reach of other people who might otherwise have a
claim on it, and the formula seems to have been deliberately used for this purpose." France
admits an unresolved mechanical question: "what is not so clear is how such a dedication could
be made without the donor also losing the right to his own property," though rabbinic
discussion shows it could somehow be achieved. He reports m. Ned. 9:1 debating whether the fifth
commandment sufficed to release such a vow, "with the balance of opinion tilting in favor of
release; apparently Jesus was aware of a more rigorous ruling," and cites m. Ned. 5:6, where a
man who had excluded his father by a *qorbān* vow had to give his courtyard to a friend so the
father could attend a grandson's wedding.

France diagnoses the scribal failure precisely: the approach "did not inquire whether the vow
was appropriate in the first place... and obstinately insisted on the validity of the vow
regardless of the consequences," lending itself to cynical manipulation. He defines the charge
of hypocrisy here "not so much of conscious deception as of a fatally distorted sense of
priorities in the service of God."

On the Isaiah citation France specifies the hermeneutic as typological rather than predictive:
Isa 29:13 was aimed at Isaiah's own contemporaries, and "about you" means the description fits
these scribes as well — "there is a typological correspondence between these two significant
phases in the response of God's people to his word through his prophet." See [[typology]],
[[formula-quotations]].

## The Pronouncement (15:11, 17-20)

France stresses that "the key pronouncement of v. 11 is thus presented as a deliberately public
declaration, not simply part of a private debate," addressed to the crowd though the Pharisees
can hear and are intended to. He classifies v. 11 as not particularly cryptic — "more in the
nature of a proverb or epigram (which also fall within the range of meanings of Greek
*parabolē*)."

France notes Matthew is more pithy than Mark: Mark specifies things "outside the person" and
"out of the person," whereas "Matthew, on the other hand, makes it clear that both the coming in
and the going out are via the mouth" — food and words respectively.

### France's verdict: Matthew does not stop short of Mark

France reports the common suggestion that Matthew draws back from Mark in two ways — omitting
Mark 7:19's "made all foods clean," and returning in v. 20 to handwashing — but he **does not
accept it**. Matthew "still records Jesus' key pronouncement (v. 11) and the following
commentary on it (vv. 18-19) no less explicitly than Mark." On the principle of externally
contracted defilement, well illustrated by the Levitical food laws, France states flatly: "it is
this principle which Jesus is here setting aside, no less explicitly in Matthew's rather
smoother version than in Mark's."

On the v. 20 return to handwashing, France's own verdict is that the frequent suggestion that
Matthew is anchoring the radical teaching back in a "safe" area misreads the structure: "I think
it more likely that his conclusion is governed by literary considerations, to provide an
inclusio for the pericope as a whole."

France argues that in the light of the post-Easter controversy over Christian food observance
"Matthew can hardly have been unaware of the radical significance of the principle that
defilement comes from inside, not from outside," and that the polemic of vv. 3-9 confirms he
knows relations with the scribes have broken down. He holds that Jesus' pronouncement provided
the theoretical basis for the mental revolution required of Jewish Christians, attributing the
delay in working it out to "natural religious conservatism" rather than "any lack of clarity in
Jesus' pronouncement."

He qualifies the scope: "not all ritual defilement in the OT was by means of food, of course;
one could also be defiled by disease" (especially skin disease), bodily secretions, and contact
with unclean things or people. The principle he distils is "It is not a matter of what you eat
but who you are" (comparing Rom 14:14, 17; 1 Cor 8:8; Heb 9:10), extending beyond food to any
external circumstance or contact.

On the vice list, France defines "heart" as, "generally in biblical literature, the seat of
thought and will (rather than of emotion, as in our idiom today)." He weighs translating
*blasphēmiai* as "blasphemies" but prefers the secular sense "slander," which "better fits the
overall character of the list." He argues that "bad thoughts," as the only purely mental item
and standing first, "serves to introduce all the items that follow" rather than extending
"murders." And he guards against anthropological over-reading: "the fact that all the items
listed as coming from the heart are bad does not mean that human beings are capable only of
evil" — the context is what produces uncleanness, and the heart can also produce good (12:34-35;
5:8; 22:37).

## The Unresolved Tension with 5:17

France does not harmonize. He marks 15:1-20 as the last substantial episode of the Galilean
public ministry, climactic because the opponents are a Jerusalem delegation and because Jesus'
pronouncement is "his most radical pronouncement on a matter of scribal concern (v. 11) which in
effect undermines a significant principle of the Mosaic law itself." He flags the irony that
this declaration sits in a pericope where Jesus accuses his opponents of undermining that same
law. He refuses the obvious escape route — a central/peripheral or moral/ceremonial division of
the law — because "that is to import a more recent categorization of the law into 'central' and
'peripheral' or into moral and ceremonial of which there is little trace in first-century
thinking."

And he leaves the tension standing: "it is not easy to see either in Jesus' own time or in that
of the gospel's first readers how this might have been squared with his assurance that he had
not come to abolish the law (5:17)." France suggests the pronouncement may be the first pointer
toward "a new, Christian, reevaluation of the OT laws which will find fuller expression in the
argument of the Letter to the Hebrews" — while noting Jesus gave his hearers no such basis at
the time. See [[fulfillment]], [[new-law]].

France links the two halves of ch. 15 causally: the turn toward the Canaanite woman and the
Gentile side of the lake is "a practical enactment of Jesus' radical attitude toward Jewish
purity laws which has just been declared in vv. 11-20."

## Second Temple Origins in Ezra–Nehemiah (Harrington NICOT)

*Source: [[harrington-ezra-nehemiah-nicot|Harrington, *The Books of Ezra and Nehemiah* (NICOT, 2022)]]. Full-body re-ingest 2026-07-18. All interpretive claims below are Harrington’s or scholars she names.*

[[hannah-harrington|Harrington]] argues that Ezra–Nehemiah is a **turning point** for purity language in Judaism:

1. **Impurity attached to types of people.** “The major innovation in the Second Temple period with regard to impurity is that it is no longer caused just by certain bodily processes or the behavior of human beings… For the first time, it is attached as an adjective to certain types of people” — gentiles and non-conforming Jews. The sinner, not only the sin, is impure.

2. **Holy seed.** *zeraʿ haqqōdeš* (Ezra 9:2) makes holiness a **biological** transmission, not only covenantal status (*ʿam qādôš*). Conversion is disallowed; mixture desecrates the sanctum that Israel herself has become (*maʿal*). See [[intermarriage-ezra-nehemiah]].

3. **Gentile impurity at Neh 13:9.** Contagious gentile impurity “surfaces here in Neh 13:9 for the first time in Judaism” when Nehemiah purifies Tobiah’s chamber: impurity “resides in the man himself,” not only in his actions. Harrington records:
   - **Klawans:** no gentile-impurity concept before 1st c. CE; sanctuary exclusion = profaneness not impurity; does not discuss Neh 13:9.
   - **Hayes:** follows Klawans; coins “genealogical pollution” for E-N; no *ritual* gentile impurity there.
   - **Olyan:** genealogical *and* ritual-like innovation (allied with Harrington).
   - **Harrington:** Hayes is right that 4QMMT’s primary concern is genealogical, **but ritual purity is also at stake**.

4. **Semantic expansions** she tracks from Leviticus into E-N: *maʿal* from sancta-goods to desecration of Jewish persons; *ṭumʾâ* permanently labels outsiders; *bādal* separates ethnically unauthorized persons; *niddâ* used metaphorically for moral impurity; *gāʾal* intensifies disgust at priestly genealogical pollution.

5. **Expansion of holy space** (Eskenazi, endorsed): purity buffer moves from temple → people → **city** (wall consecration; people “in the house of God” within walls, Neh 12:40). Ritual purification marks Passover boundary (Ezra 6:21) and wall dedication (Neh 12:30).

6. **ST reception:** Jub. 22:16-17 (eating with gentiles); 4Q266 (priests defiled among gentiles); 1QS v (outsider possessions impure); 4QMMT (pollute holy seed); Temple Scroll (foreign wife barred from pure food); Josephus / Herod inscription / Court of the Gentiles; rabbinic gentile-impurity and proselyte purification (m. Pesaḥ 8:8).

7. **NT reversal:** Acts 10:28 (association ban overturned); Harrington notes Klawans reads Peter as moral not ritual impurity, but counters that diaspora God-fearers and lack of Jerusalem temple agency complicate pure ritual readings.

Complements France’s Matthean reevaluation of purity without collapsing ST Jewish and Christian registers. See [[ezra-nehemiah]], [[nehemiah]], [[samaritans]].

## Coverage and Gaps

Coverage now includes France on Matt 15, Harrington on Ezra–Nehemiah as ST purity seedbed, and Zoharic densification. Levitical purity systems in their own right, full Qumran *halakha* beyond Harrington’s citations, rabbinic *Ṭeharot* in its own voice, and Islamic *ṭahāra* remain thin.

## Zoharic / Kabbalistic Reading (Pritzker Vol. VII)

*Source: [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol7|Matt Pritzker Vol VII]] (standards reingest 2026-07-18). Body ~3:42b–80a (Tazri’a–Aḥarei Mot) plus Shemini food laws.*

- ***Al mishkavi* ([42b]):** Assembly of Israel / Shekhinah lies in a foreign impure land and cannot unite with the King except in His palace.
- **Childbirth and seed:** *tselem*, right/left sex, Other Side deposits; face-to-face coupling ethics protect Coupling above.
- ***Tsara’at*:** Linked to evil speech (*ha-motsi ra*), Serpent, and “closing” (*segiru*); the priest opens with Compassion so Shekhinah can dwell; house affliction purges Canaanite impurity for Her sake.
- **Food laws (Shemini):** *zot ha-ḥayyah* as living Name vs ten impure crowns of sorcery; unclean food as permanent loathsomeness (*ve-nitmetem*).
- **Niddah / Serpent slime (~3:79a–b):** nail-filth and 1,405 species; new-moon atonement “above Me.”
- **Yom Kippur reset:** goats, Accuser→Advocate, sanctuary entry *be-zot* — annual theurgic restoration of purity conditions for presence.

Complements France’s NT reevaluation of purity without collapsing Jewish and Christian registers. See [[zohar|The Zohar]], [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]], [[holiness|Holiness]], [[leviticus|Leviticus]], [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]].
