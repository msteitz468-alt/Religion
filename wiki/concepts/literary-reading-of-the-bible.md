# Literary Reading of the Bible

The **literary reading of the Bible** is the modern interpretive approach that treats
biblical texts as works of conscious literary art and reads them with the tools of
literary criticism — narrative poetics, close reading, attention to style, structure,
characterization, and convention — rather than primarily as a quarry for historical
sources, doctrines, or redactional layers. It rose to prominence in the late 1970s and
1980s, associated above all with [[robert-alter|Robert Alter]] (*The Art of Biblical
Narrative*, 1981) and with Frank Kermode, and developed by readers such as J. P.
Fokkelman, Shimon Bar-Efrat, Meir Sternberg, and Adele Berlin.

## Core Commitments

- **The received text as a designed whole.** Without necessarily denying composite origins,
  the literary approach brackets the dissection of texts into hypothetical strata (the
  [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]], form criticism, the
  [[succession-narrative|Succession Narrative]], [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]]
  redaction) in favor of how the text as it stands produces meaning.
- **Close reading of the original language.** Meaning is carried by word-motifs
  (*Leitwörter*), repetition with variation, the granting and withholding of epithets,
  syntax and pacing, and the contrastive shaping of dialogue.
- **Type-scenes and convention.** Recurrent narrative templates (annunciation of birth,
  betrothal at a well, the hero's commissioning) are conventions the writer invokes and
  meaningfully varies.
- **Narrative reticence ("the gap").** Biblical narrative is terse and "fraught with
  background," withholding motive and inner life and compelling inference — as in the
  systematically blocked interiority of [[david|David]].

## Relation to Other Approaches

The literary approach is distinct from, and often in tension with, the **historical-critical**
methods that dominated 19th–20th-century scholarship (source, form, and redaction
criticism). Where the historical-critical reader asks *how the text came to be* and
*what really happened*, the literary reader asks *how the text means*. It also differs
from premodern confessional reading (the [[fourfold-sense|Quadriga]],
[[allegorical-reading|allegory]], PaRDeS) in being secular and synchronic, though it shares
with those traditions a conviction that the text rewards sustained, attentive interpretation
rather than dissection. It is not inherently anti-confessional and has been embraced across
the theological spectrum.
