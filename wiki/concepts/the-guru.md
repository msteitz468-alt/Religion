# The Guru

The concept of the **Guru** in Sikhism is layered and evolved across the tradition's history. McLeod identifies at least four distinct but continuous referents for the term: (1) the eternal/mystical Guru (Akal Purakh's voice); (2) the ten personal human Gurus from Nanak to Gobind Singh; (3) the Guru Granth (the sacred scripture); and (4) the Guru Panth (the corporate community). All four are understood as manifestations of a single continuous Guru-reality.

## The Eternal Guru: Akal Purakh's Voice

In Nanak's theology, the Guru pre-exists all human instantiation. "Prior to Nanak's time, the Guru, the 'voice' of [[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]], was mystically 'spoken' within an individual believer" (~L1004–1006). The [[sant-tradition|Sant tradition]] had already developed this concept: the devout Sant must "turn one's attention inward, there to focus it entirely upon the *shabad* or divine Word uttered in the *man* (the 'heart' or 'mind') by the mystical Guru, the 'voice' of the divine Being" (~L169–172). Akal Purakh is thus the eternal Guru, speaking through the creation: "The Word (*shabad*) is the message inscribed in creation, and the mystical 'voice' whereby it is 'spoken' is the eternal Guru. Akal Purakh is the eternal Guru, speaking through the creation which constitutes a visible form" (~L601–605).

## The Ten Personal Human Gurus

Nanak, "by his teachings, gave direct utterance to the words Akal Purakh was communicating to humankind and thus came to be acknowledged as the first human Guru" (~L1006–1009). He appointed a successor (Lahina, who became Angad), inaugurating a line of ten personal Gurus. Yet there was only one Guru passed through ten bodies: "Ten individual human bodies successively held it, but there was only one Guru, passed on to the next as ten torches are lit by a single flame" (~L1011–1015).

The doctrinal content of the Gurus' teaching remained constant through the first five successors: "All taught that liberation from the transmigratory cycle depended wholly upon disciplined remembrance of the divine Name in accordance with the instruction imparted by the Guru" (~L810–814).

### Doctrinal Development Through the Gurus

- **Gurus 1–5** (Nanak through Arjan): strictly devotional mode; the *pir* role (spiritual care of the community)
- **Guru Hargobind (6th)**: "tradition attaches to Guru Hargobind the responsibility" for the doctrine of [[miri-piri|miri-piri]] — the dual role of spiritual (*piri*) and worldly/temporal (*miri*) leadership (~L827–853). "No longer was the Guru to exercise the *piri* role of his five predecessors … The Panth was now dangerously threatened and the urgent need was for the Guru to assume the *miri* role also" (~L840–844).
- **Gurus 7–8** (Har Rai, Har Krishan): the affairs of the Panth "reverted to the earlier essentially devotional mode" (~L865)
- **Guru Gobind Singh (10th)**: inaugurated the Khalsa; extended the doctrine of Akal Purakh to emphasize divine justice and militant intervention in history

## The Guru Granth and Guru Panth

Following Guru Gobind Singh's death in 1708, "Sikh tradition affirms that shortly before he died the Guru conferred the role jointly upon the Guru Granth (the sacred scriptures) and the Guru Panth (the corporate community of his followers which orthodox Sikhs normally interpret as the Khalsa)" (~L988–992).

"Sikhs emphatically believe that the Guru's guidance is expressed by the sacred scripture and by the considered voice of the corporate Panth" (~L1001–1003). "Finally, with the death of Gobind Singh, the mystical Guru passed into the scripture and the corporate community, there to remain ever accessible to all who seek it" (~L1016–1019).

### The Guru Granth Sahib

Today "the Guru Granth Sahib is venerated beyond all else in Sikhism" (~L1040–1041). During the 18th century both the Adi Granth and the Dasam Granth were treated as the Guru Granth; the Tat Khalsa reformers of the Singh Sabha movement demoted the Dasam Granth, so that today "the term Guru Granth Sahib means the Adi Granth alone" (~L1073–1074). Only the Nihang sect dissents, insisting on equal status for the Dasam Granth (~L1078–1080).

### The Guru Panth

The Guru Panth's practical voice is difficult to elicit "partly because the nature of the corporate community has been disputed but more particularly because opinion concerning the Guru's intention has been divided" (~L1021–1025). *Hukam-namas* (letters of command) are occasionally issued by the SGPC claiming to be the Guru Panth's voice, "but such an occasion is a rare event" (~L1030–1034). In practice, "the Guru's voice is largely confined to the Guru Granth" (~L1035–1036).

## The Nand Lal Rahit-Nama on the Mystical Guru

Within the rahit-nama literature, Nand Lal's *Prashan-uttar* "concentrat[es] on an exposition of the doctrine of the mystical Guru and stress[es] the believer's obligation to practise nam simaran" (~L1702–1706). This confirms that the inner, mystical dimension of the Guru concept remained alive within Khalsa piety.

## Hermeneutical Note (Gurmat)

Within [[gurmat|Gurmat]], the Guru Granth Sahib is not merely a text but the living presence of the Guru. Reading and chanting its *bani* constitutes a direct encounter with the Guru's voice — which is ultimately [[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]]'s voice. This theological claim is the basis for the extreme veneration of the Granth: Non-Sikh observers sometimes suggest "that the Sikh attitude really amounts to idolatry. Sikhs are quick to respond to this, agreeing that certainly they pay the sacred volume extreme reverence but emphasizing that this reverence never proceeds to the point of actually worshipping it" (~L1052–1056).
