The **Gemara** (Aramaic: גמרא, from the root *g-m-r*, meaning "to study" or "to complete") is the expansive, dialectical commentary on the [[mishna|Mishna]] that comprises the bulk of the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]].

When the Mishna was compiled by [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi]] (c. 200 CE), it was a highly condensed, aphoristic legal code. The ensuing generations of scholars, known as the **Amoraim**, dedicated themselves to analyzing this text. The record of their debates, analyses, and rulings forms the Gemara.

## Function and Methodology

The Gemara does not simply accept the Mishna at face value. It rigorously interrogates the text using the following methods:
- **Identifying Sources:** Seeking the biblical verses that undergird a Mishnaic ruling.
- **Reconciling Contradictions:** Resolving apparent conflicts between different Mishnaic statements or between the Mishna and outside traditions (*Baraitot*).
- **Logical Extension:** Applying the principles of the Mishna to new cases and evolving circumstances.
- **Lexicography:** Defining ambiguous terms.
- **[[aggadah|Aggadic]] Digression:** Weaving theological, ethical, or folklore narratives into legal discussions.

This rigorous dialectical process is known as *Shakla V'Tarya* (give and take).

## Two Gemaras

Because the Amoraim operated in two major geographic centers, two distinct Gemaras were produced:
1. **The Babylonian Gemara:** Produced in the academies of Babylonia (e.g., Sura, Pumbedita). Written in Jewish Babylonian Aramaic. It is longer, more dialectical, and ultimately achieved universal authority in Judaism. When one says "The Talmud" without qualification, they mean the Babylonian Gemara.
2. **The Jerusalem Gemara:** Produced in the Land of Israel (Tiberias, Caesarea). Written in Jewish Palestinian Aramaic. It was concluded abruptly due to Roman persecution (c. 400 CE) and is more concise and less heavily edited.

The Mishna and the Gemara together constitute the Talmud.
