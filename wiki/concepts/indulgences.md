# Indulgences

An **indulgence** is the remission, granted by the Church's authority, of the **temporal punishment**
still owed for sins whose guilt has already been forgiven through [[sacrament-of-penance|penance]].
[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s account ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Supplement XP Q25–Q27) is the
mature scholastic statement of the doctrine that, two centuries later, would become the flashpoint of
the Protestant Reformation when Luther attacked its abuse in 1517.

## Aquinas's doctrine (Supplement XP Q25–Q27)

- **Validity.** Indulgences "hold good both in the Church's court and in the judgment of God, for the
  remission of the punishment which remains after contrition, absolution, and confession." Aquinas
  surveys three opinions on their efficacy and refuses the minimizing "pious fraud" view (that they
  merely entice devotion without truly remitting) as **"very dangerous."**
- **Basis: the treasury of merit.** Their power rests on the **"oneness of the mystical body"**: the
  superabundant satisfactions of Christ and the saints (which "exceed the entire debt of punishment
  due to those living at this moment") form a **common treasury** at the Church's disposal — a direct
  application of the head-and-members logic of the [[atonement]]. The relevant distinction is between
  the *guilt* of sin (remitted in penance) and the *temporal punishment* (which the indulgence
  remits from this treasury).
- **Who may grant them.** The **Pope** has plenary power to dispense the treasury; **bishops** may do
  so within limits; even a non-priest with jurisdiction may grant an indulgence — because the power is
  one of **jurisdiction**, not of orders, and the grantor's personal moral state is irrelevant.
- **Who benefits.** Only those in a state of **charity** with true contrition and confession; a
  person in mortal sin gains nothing. Indulgences can also be applied to the souls in
  [[purgatory|purgatory]].

## The Reformation controversy

Aquinas's doctrine assumes the whole Catholic edifice — the guilt/punishment distinction, satisfaction,
purgatory, the treasury of merit, papal jurisdiction. By the early 16th century the *practice* of
selling indulgences (e.g. the Tetzel campaign funding St. Peter's) provoked **Martin Luther's
Ninety-Five Theses** (1517), which attacked both the abuse and, increasingly, the underlying theology —
the treasury of merit, papal authority over purgatory, and the satisfaction scheme. For the Reformers,
forgiveness is by faith in Christ's finished work, leaving no "temporal punishment" for the Church to
remit. The dispute over indulgences was thus one of the proximate triggers of the Reformation and a
permanent dividing line between [[catholicism|Catholicism]] (which reformed the practice and reaffirmed
the doctrine at Trent) and [[protestantism|Protestantism]]. This is properly a candidate
`controversies/` page (efficacy of indulgences) if expanded.
