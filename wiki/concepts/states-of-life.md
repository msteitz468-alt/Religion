# States of Life and Christian Perfection (Aquinas)

The [[summa-theologica|*Summa*]]'s Secunda Secundae concludes with the **states of life** (II-II
Q183–189) — the conditions of perfection, the episcopate, and the religious (monastic/consecrated)
life. It is Aquinas's account of *where* Christian perfection is pursued, building on his teaching
that perfection consists essentially in [[charity-aquinas|charity]].

## What a "state" is (Q183)

A **state** (*status*) is, properly, a **durable condition** bearing on a person's freedom or
servitude — not a mere rank, office, or degree of virtue. The Church has a diversity of states by
reason of **perfection** (beginners, the proficient, the perfect), **action** (different duties), and
**beauty** (its ordered variety).

## Christian perfection (Q184) — the core

Perfection consists **essentially and primarily in charity** — "above all these things have charity,
which is the bond of perfection" (Col. 3:14) — and only **secondarily** in the other virtues. The
crucial distinction:

- Perfection lies **primarily in the precepts of charity** (love of God and neighbour), which **admit
  no upper limit** and bind everyone;
- It lies **instrumentally** in the **evangelical counsels** — **poverty, chastity, and obedience** —
  which are not perfection itself but **means** that remove the hindrances to charity. Abbot Moses:
  fasting and vigils are "means to perfection, not perfection." Christ's "sell all… and follow me"
  (Mt. 19:21) names the means ("sell all") and the perfection itself ("follow me").

A **state of perfection** requires a **perpetual obligation undertaken with solemnity**, not merely
the act of perfect love. **Bishops and religious** are in the state of perfection (the bishop as
*perfecter*, the religious as one being *perfected*); a parish priest, lacking the solemn perpetual
bond, is not, however holy.

## The episcopal state (Q185)

To **desire** the episcopate is presumptuous (it covets height and honour), praiseworthy only when
prompted by charity; yet to **refuse it absolutely** when duly called is also unlawful (against
charity and humility). A bishop may not resign without papal authority, but may flee persecution if
his flock is provided for; he may hold personal property (poverty being a counsel, not a precept), but
holds the **Church's goods in trust**, gravely sinning by serious misuse.

## The religious life (Q186–189)

- **The religious state** (Q186) is, "by antonomasia," a **holocaust** — the offering of one's whole
  self to God through the **three vows** of poverty, chastity, and obedience, of which **obedience is
  chief** (it offers the will itself and contains the others). Only violations of the three vows or
  of one's specific rule are mortal sins as such.
- **What is competent to religious** (Q187): teaching and preaching by delegation, charitable
  business with permission, manual labour (not a stricter obligation than for seculars), living on
  alms and even **begging** for a useful end (study, building) — Christ himself being poor.
- **The kinds of religious orders** (Q188), Aquinas's theoretical **defence of the mendicants**:
  orders differ chiefly by **end**. **Active-life** orders (charitable service), **military** orders
  (defence of worship and the common good), and **study/preaching** orders are all legitimate. The
  **hierarchy** (Q188, A6): orders devoted to **teaching and preaching** are **highest**, because the
  active life *flowing from the fullness of contemplation* surpasses simple contemplation — the
  [[contemplative-and-active-life|*contemplata aliis tradere*]] principle, which places the Dominican
  model at the apex. **Strictness of observance is not the chief criterion**; ordering the observances
  rightly to their end is. **Common possession** does not diminish perfection (poverty is a means);
  and the **solitary life** is more perfect only **for those already perfected** by community life.
- **Entrance into religion** (Q189): **no prior formation in the commandments is required** — sinners
  may enter directly (Matthew the publican), and entrance remits sins "like a second baptism."
  Children may be received for education; one may enter even **against parents' wishes** unless they
  are in genuine necessity ("we should more obey the Father of spirits"). Famously (Q189, A10),
  **long deliberation is not required and is a sign of weakness** — the decision admits no genuine
  doubt, since to doubt the counsel is to disparage Christ who gave it: "Hasten — cut the rope rather
  than loosen it" (Jerome).

## Cross-tradition note

Aquinas's theology of vows, counsels, and the consecrated life is a rich comparison point for the
renunciant and monastic institutions of other traditions — Buddhist and Jain [[renunciation]] and
[[jain-asceticism|asceticism]], the Hindu *sannyasa*, and the [[temple-dwelling-monks|monastic]]
orders generally.
