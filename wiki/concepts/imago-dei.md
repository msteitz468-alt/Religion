*Imago Dei* ("image of God," Latin) refers to the claim in [[genesis|Genesis 1:26–27]] that humanity was created *bĕtsalēm ʾelōhîm* (in the image of God) and *kiḏmûtēnû* (after our likeness). The passage is:

> "Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness... So God created humankind in his image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them." (Gen 1:26–27)

The *imago dei* is the most theologically contested clause in the creation narrative, generating radically different interpretations across Jewish and Christian tradition.

## The Hebrew Terms

- **Tselem** (צֶלֶם, "image"): Used in contexts of concrete representations — statues, idols, physical likenesses. It carries a more material or physical connotation.
- **Demut** (דְּמוּת, "likeness"): An abstract quality of resemblance. It is commonly read as qualifying *tselem* — not a perfect image but a resemblance.

The combination of the two terms appears to be a deliberate pairing: humanity bears a genuine likeness (*tselem*) to God, but that likeness is qualified (*demut*) — it is resemblance, not identity.

## Major Interpretive Positions

**Functional/royal image** (dominant in recent scholarship, e.g., J. R. Middleton, Phyllis Bird): The image of God designates humanity's *role* — to exercise dominion over creation as God's royal representative. In ancient Near Eastern royal ideology, a king was the "image of god" on earth, ruling in the deity's name. Genesis democratizes this language: all humans, not just kings, bear the divine image.

**Substantial/faculty interpretation** (patristic, medieval, dominant pre-20th century): The image resides in a specific human faculty: reason (Origen, Aquinas), the rational soul, or the immortal spirit. Body is usually excluded; image is a spiritual or intellectual endowment.

**Relational interpretation** (Karl Barth): The image consists not in a faculty but in the capacity for relationship — with God and with other humans. "Male and female he created them" (1:27b) is read as the human-relational analogy of God's own inner relationship.

**Holistic interpretation** ([[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]], following Clines): The image/likeness terms designate the **whole person** as God's image-bearer, not a specific component. "To be human is to bear the image of God" — the statement is about the entire human being in their unity of body, soul, and spirit. This reading resists the Platonic tendency to locate the image in a spiritual component separate from the body.

## Hamilton (NICOT) — densification (2026-07-18 reingest)

Attributed to [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]], *Genesis 1–17* (NICOT); source page [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot]].

### Holistic person; not a defined faculty

On Gen 1:26 Hamilton insists the verse does not define *what* the image is (conscience, soul, original righteousness, reason, capacity for prayer, posture, etc. are “subjective inferences”). “Gen. 1:26 is simply saying that to be human is to bear the image of God. This understanding emphasizes man as a unity. No part of man, no function of man is subordinated to some other, higher part or activity” (raw L2791 / Gen 1:26).

*ṣelem* as divine image appears at 1:26, 27 (twice), 9:6; related 5:3. Elsewhere often illicit idol images; Ps 39/73 more abstract. On *demût*, he reports the prevailing view (likeness tones down concrete “image”) and the reverse (intensifies reflection); against Clines’s *beth essentiae* (“make man *as/to be* our image”), he prefers standard “in/according to” (Exod 25:40 parallel; *beth essentiae* usually property of subject, not object) (raw L2781–2789 / Gen 1:26).

### Democratized royalty; dominion as responsible care

Egyptian/Mesopotamian “image of God” language applied to the king, not the canal digger. Hamilton: “In God’s eyes all of mankind is royal. All of humanity is related to God, not just the king. Specifically, the Bible democratizes the royalistic and exclusivistic concepts of the nations that surrounded Israel” (raw L2767–2777 / Gen 1:26). Dominion (*rāḏâ*) is royal language of “responsible care,” not exploitation: “Even in the garden of Eden he who would be lord of all must be servant of all” (raw L2791–2795 / Gen 1:26). Enuma elish’s anthropology (man as afterthought-servant of the gods) is set over against Gen 1’s regal nobility: the anthropologies “could not be wider apart” (raw L2811+ / Gen 1).

### Male and female both image-bearers

Narrator’s “his image” (sg.) undercuts reading “our” as a divine council of many gods. “God created in his image a male and a female ʾāḏām. Both share the image of God.” Not a bisexual primal human; sexuality is gift, foreign to God’s person yet within his will for image-bearers; the phrase bridges to the fertility blessing — emphasis here on procreators more than companions (raw L2797–2801 / Gen 1:27). Dual mandate: procreation and dominion; fertility not dependent on rites; *kāḇaš* “subdue” ~ settlement/agriculture parallel to “till and keep” (2:5, 15); dominion does not include killing for food until 9:3 (raw L2803–2809 / Gen 1:28).

On “Let us make,” Hamilton surveys six options and prefers Hasel’s “plural of fullness” / Clines’s “duality within the Godhead” (God speaking to the Spirit of v. 2) — not “trinitarian monotheism” as Moses’ intention, but not too primitive for plurality-within-unity hints (raw L2755–2767 / Gen 1:26).

### Image persists after the Fall (Gen 5; Gen 9)

- **Gen 5:1–3**: *sēper tôlĕdōt ʾādām*; prolegomenon // 1:26–28 (blessing effective in multiplication). Genealogy concerned with firstborn and with **transmission of divine image and likeness generation to generation**. “There is no evidence that the events of ch. 3 have scarred this transmission” — not unawareness of the Fall, but emphasis on grace (longevity as blessing); “the transmission of the divine image may be one way in which the writer is stressing his point about the operations of divine grace” (raw L1008–1026 / Gen 5:1–20).
- **Gen 9:6**: capital sanction for murder grounded in the image still present. “There is no evidence here that sin has effaced the divine image. … this verse has become the scriptural locus classicus for capital punishment” (R4 / Gen 9:2–6).

### Complementary creation panels; NT sketch

Hamilton pairs 1:1–2:3 (divine origins, endowments, authority — “you shall,” 1:29) with 2:4ff. (mundane origins, under authority — “you shall not,” 2:17) as *kenegdô* complements; neither more truthful than the other. Divine image might have suggested *tôlĕdôt ʾĕlōhîm*, but that phrase is absent; the only “sons of God” in Genesis are 6:1–4 (raw L355).

NT appropriation (Hamilton’s sketch): (1) Christ as *eikṓn* of God (2 Cor 4:4; Col 1:15) — substantial identity, never *homoíōsis* “likeness” for Christ; (2) man as image (1 Cor 11:7 male; Jas 3:9 all men after likeness); (3) believer conformed to Christ’s image (Rom 8:29; 2 Cor 3:18; Col 3:10). Clines: “In Christ man sees what manhood was meant to be…” (raw L669–681). Gal 3:28 “male and female” from Gen 1:27 in a soteriological-equality context, not a hierarchy debate (raw L705).

## Key Debates

**Does the Fall damage or destroy the image?**: Gen 5:3 (Seth born "in the likeness and image of Adam") and Gen 9:6 (murder prohibited because humans are made in God's image) suggest the image persists after the Fall. [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] (above) reads Gen 5 and 9 as showing the image **unscarred in transmission** and still grounding law after the Flood — an emphasis on grace rather than a denial of Gen 3. The Christian tradition is divided: Catholic theology speaks of the image as *wounded* but not destroyed; Protestant Reformers often spoke of it as *totally corrupted*; Eastern Orthodoxy distinguishes *image* (retained) from *likeness* (lost, to be recovered through theosis).

**Is the image gendered?**: Gen 1:27 states "male and female he created them" in immediate parallelism with "in the image of God he created them." This implies the image encompasses both sexes fully — neither male nor female alone is the image. [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] and feminist scholars alike cite this against any reading that makes the male the primary or full image-bearer.

**"Let us make" (Gen 1:26) — who is speaking?**: The cohortative plural has been read as: (a) divine council (polytheistic background), (b) plural of majesty, (c) Trinitarian self-deliberation, (d) rhetorical device emphasizing the solemnity of humanity's creation. Hamilton’s preferred option (above) is “plural of fullness” / duality within the Godhead, not full trinitarian monotheism as Mosaic intention.

**[[maimonides|Maimonides']] philosophical reading**: In the *Guide for the Perplexed*, Maimonides argues *tselem* refers to the intellect — humanity shares the divine intellectual form. Any interpretation that attributes a bodily image to God is for him a form of idolatry.

## Ancient Near Eastern Background

In Mesopotamian royal ideology, the king was the *tsalam* of the deity — the god's living representative on earth, installed to exercise dominion. Egyptian pharaohs were similarly described as divine images. Genesis 1 either adapts this royal language and democratizes it (all humans bear the image) or polemically inverts it (no king is uniquely divine; all humans share that dignity equally). Hamilton’s democratization claim and Enuma elish contrast are the load-bearing ANE moves in the NICOT commentary (above).

## Relationship to NT Christology

The NT applies *imago dei* language to Christ: Paul calls Christ "the image (*eikōn*) of the invisible God" (Col 1:15), the "exact imprint (*charaktēr*) of his nature" (Heb 1:3). Humanity's image is being "transformed into the same image from glory to glory" (2 Cor 3:18). This Christological development reads the original *imago dei* as pointing toward its fulfillment in the new humanity formed in Christ. Hamilton’s three-category NT sketch (Christ / all humanity / believers conformed to Christ) is summarized under his densification above.
