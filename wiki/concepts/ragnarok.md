# Ragnarök

**Ragnarök** ("the Doom/Fate of the Gods") is the [[norse-religion|Norse]] **end of the world** — the
prophesied final battle in which the **gods and their monstrous enemies destroy one another**, the cosmos
sinks in fire and flood, and a **renewed world** rises from the ruin. It is the most distinctive feature of
Norse religion: a mythology in which **the gods themselves are doomed and know it**.

## The Prophecy (in detail)

Foretold in the *Völuspá* (in the [[poetic-edda|Poetic Edda]]) and in Snorri's [[prose-edda|Prose Edda]]:

- **The signs.** A terrible winter (*Fimbulwinter*) of three years without summer; the breakdown of kinship
  and morality; the sun and moon devoured by wolves; the stars vanish; the earth quakes and **all bonds and
  fetters break**.
- **The enemies loose.** The bound wolf **[[fenrir|Fenrir]]** breaks free; the world-serpent **[[jormungandr|
  Jörmungandr]]** rises from the sea; **[[loki|Loki]]** and the fire-giant **Surtr** lead the giants; the
  ship **Naglfar** (made of dead men's nails) sails with the hosts of the dead.
- **The last battle.** On the plain Vígríðr the gods and the *einherjar* of [[valhalla|Valhalla]] march out
  to their doom. **[[odin|Odin]] is swallowed by Fenrir** (avenged by his son Víðarr); **[[thor|Thor]] kills
  Jörmungandr but dies of its venom**; **[[tyr|Týr]]** and the hound Garmr kill each other; **[[heimdall|
  Heimdall]] and Loki** slay one another; **[[freyr|Freyr]]** falls to Surtr, who then **engulfs the world in
  fire**.
- **The renewal.** The earth sinks into the sea — and **rises again, green and new**. A few gods survive
  (Víðarr, Váli, Thor's sons, and the returned **[[baldr|Baldr]]**), and two humans, **Líf and Lífþrasir**,
  who sheltered in the world-tree, repeople the earth. A new sun (the old one's daughter) shines.

## Significance

Ragnarök gives Norse religion its **tragic, heroic grandeur**: the gods fight on **knowing they will lose**,
making courage in the face of certain doom the supreme value. Its **destruction-and-renewal** structure
invites comparison both with the Zoroastrian/Abrahamic final consummation ([[frashokereti|Frashokereti]], the
new creation) and with the cyclical world-ages elsewhere — though uniquely here **the gods themselves die**.
The Christian coloring of the surviving "rebirth" verses is debated.
