# Covenantal Nomism

**Covenantal nomism** is the term coined by E. P. Sanders (*Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, 1977) to
describe the soteriological pattern he argued was common to most of second-temple Judaism. It became
the foundation of the [[new-perspective-on-paul|New Perspective on Paul]] and is a key background
category for reading both Paul and the [[james|Letter of James]]. The label joins two ideas:
- **Covenant**: Israel's place in God's people rests on God's gracious election, not on human
  achievement. Judaism is therefore a religion *of grace*, not (as older Christian polemic claimed)
  a religion of legalistic works-righteousness.
- **Nomism** (*nomos* = law): obedience to the Torah is the *grateful response* to that electing
  grace — the means of *staying in* the covenant, not of *getting in*.

Sanders's slogan: one does not obey the law to *get in* (one is already in by grace/birth) but to
*stay in*. This reframes Judaism as structurally similar to Christianity (grace first, obedience as
response), undercutting the Reformation contrast between Jewish "works" and Christian "faith."

## Moo's Two Qualifications

[[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC James, 2000) accepts that covenantal nomism is "in its main lines... an
accurate and helpful picture of Jewish soteriology in the NT period," but enters two important
adjustments:

1. **Diversity.** Like most sweeping paradigms, covenantal nomism underplays the real diversity of
   second-temple Judaism. Some Jews and some groups may well have been more "legalistic" than the
   generalized model allows; the literature shows wide variation in how covenant, grace, and law were
   integrated.
2. **Covenant membership ≠ guaranteed salvation.** The "get in / stay in" distinction applies to
   the *covenant*, but cannot simply be transferred to *salvation*. Belonging to the covenant (being
   born Israelite) did not by itself guarantee a favorable verdict; salvation was a decision God
   would make at the *final judgment*, taking obedience into account. So second-temple soteriology
   was, on Moo's reading, effectively **synergistic** about final salvation: human obedience had to
   *cooperate* with God's grace.

## Why It Matters for James

Moo finds the "point of intersection between Jewish views and James 2" precisely here. James's
pattern looks like covenantal nomism reinterpreted in *new-covenant* terms:
- **"Get in"** by God's grace — the new birth "through the word of truth" (1:18) and faith in Christ
  (2:1; like Abraham, 2:22–23).
- **"Stay in" / final vindication** through obedience to "the royal law" (2:8) — works taken into
  account at the judgment ("justified by works," 2:24).

This makes James appear *synergistic*, raising the question of its compatibility with Paul's
monergism. Moo's resolution: read James's "works" as themselves the *Spirit-produced fruit* of God's
grace (cf. Phil. 2:13), so that the apparent synergism of James is absorbed into the monergism of
Paul — faith alone brings one in, and true faith inevitably produces the works God honors at the
last judgment. See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on Justification]].

## Relation to the New Perspective

Covenantal nomism is the historical premise of the NPP: if Judaism was already a religion of grace,
then Paul was not opposing Jewish legalism but something else (ethnocentrism / the exclusion of
Gentiles, per Dunn and Wright). Moo and Schreiner accept that Judaism was broadly gracious but argue
Paul's critique runs deeper than ethnocentrism — to the law's inability to deliver what it demands
and to human inability (the *anthropological* argument). See
[[new-perspective-on-paul|New Perspective on Paul]] and [[justification|Justification]].
