# Jain Puja

## Definition

*Puja* in its broadest South Asian sense "signifies an act of devotion or obeisance directed towards a divinity and some kind of interaction with that divinity by means of making an offering to its iconic form" (ch. 7). In Jain practice, puja is directed at images of the [[tirthankara|fordmakers]] and is the most publicly visible aspect of Jain lay religion — though it is not universal, since the [[sthanakvasi|Sthanakvasi]] and [[terapanth|Terapanthi]] Svetambara sects define themselves in part by *rejection* of formal image worship. The majority of Jains, both [[svetambara|Svetambara]] and [[digambara|Digambara]], describe themselves as *murtipujaka* ("image-worshipping") or *mandirmargi* ("temple-attending"), and "there can be no doubt that the image cult represents one of the major historical continuities in Jain civilisation" (ch. 7).

## Historical Background

The first clearly datable textual evidence for a fordmaker image is a first-century BCE inscription of Kharavela. A shrine at Mathura dating from the second century BCE provides archaeological corroboration. The oldest portions of the Svetambara scriptures show no knowledge of image worship; references occur mainly in later, narrative portions of the canon. "A necessary historical conclusion from this evidence ... is that devotional worship of the fordmakers was from the very earliest times an important element of Jainism" (ch. 7).

Image worship may have evolved in connection with the cult of *yaksa* demiurges who inhabited sacred trees and pools, and the term *caitya* (originally a cult site around a sacred tree) came in Jainism to designate both image and temple. Votive tablets from Mathura (pre-common era) display auspicious signs and, later, images of fordmakers in meditation or *kayotsarga* posture — the two standard iconic positions still used today (ch. 7).

## Temple and Image Iconography

Fordmaker images "represent a clearly identifiable human type conforming to general Indian standards of idealised masculine physique" — no multiple arms or heads — "emblematic both of meditative calm and the power which arises from it" (ch. 7). Fordmakers are generally represented naked. Sectarian distinctions are visible: Svetambara images since the medieval period have been fitted with glass eyes and "clothed" with jewellery and finery; Digambara images are always austere.

Temple architecture emerged in the north and west of India in the early medieval period, influenced by both Hindu sacred architecture (the *sikhara* tower symbolising Mount Meru) and by literary descriptions of the fordmaker's preaching hall (*samavasarana*). A major example is the Dharna Vihara at Ranakpur, south Rajasthan, consecrated in 1441 — the largest Jain temple built in pre-modern India, with over 1,400 carved columns and 108 subsidiary shrines (ch. 7).

## Who May Perform Puja

Only lay people (or ritual specialists) can perform *dravyapuja*, the physical making of offerings involving contact with the image. Ascetics are restricted to inner worship (*bhavapuja*) — contemplating the image without contact — because their abandonment of normal washing renders them impure and their lack of possessions means they cannot own or buy offerings (ch. 7).

In Digambara temples in south India, lay people generally do not touch the image; any anointing puja is carried out by a priest called an *upadhye*. In Svetambara temples, lay people carry out ceremonies themselves, assisted by non-Jain *pujari* servants who clear up afterwards. "Neither the *pujari* nor the *upadhye* are in any way regarded as mediators or intercessors with the fordmakers, being solely ritual functionaries" (ch. 7).

## The Puja of Eight Substances (*Astaprakari Puja*)

The most common and most studied form of Jain image worship is the eightfold puja, in which eight substances are offered to the image. The eight substances are: "some sort of perfumed material such as camphor, flowers, rice, incense, light, sweets, fruit and water" (ch. 7). This eightfold form appears to have superseded an earlier five-substance ritual; medieval puja used a still wider variety of materials.

### Procedure (Svetambara Form)

The eightfold puja "should normally take place in the morning" in a state of purity, with clean unstitched clothes. On entering the temple, the worshipper utters the word *nisihi* ("abandonment"), "signifying a move from the profane world into the sacred space of the *samavasarana*" (ch. 7). A preliminary clockwise circumambulation (*pradaksina*) of the image three times precedes the offering.

**Contact phase (three substances):**
1. The image is bathed to remove accretions from the previous day and anointed with milk and water; the male worshipper is urged to imagine himself as Indra performing the lustration of the newborn fordmaker on Mount Meru.
2. *Gandh* — a mixture of camphor and sandalwood — is applied to the main parts of the image's body.
3. Flowers are offered, usually as a garland.

**Distant phase (five substances), performed from the main hall:**
4. Incense is waved toward the image.
5. Lamps (*arati*) are waved toward the image.
6. Rice is offered and arranged on a table facing the inner shrine into a doctrinal design: a *svastika* (its four corners signifying the four states of existence: human, animal, god, hellbeing), three dots above (the Three Jewels: right faith, knowledge, conduct), and above that a crescent with a single dot (the liberated souls at the summit of the universe).
7. Sweets are offered.
8. Fruit is offered.

The ritual concludes with utterance of *nisihi*, "contemplation of the image, inward worship and the muttering of prayers and sacred formulae" (ch. 7). Offerings are collected by temple staff as payment.

### Symbolic Interpretations

The substances have been subjected to layers of exegesis over centuries:

- Haribhadra (eighth century) read them as standing for the five Great Vows, devotion to one's teacher, knowledge, and austerity (*Ast* 3.6).
- Yasovijaya (17th c.) interiorised the offerings: the flower garland as forgiveness, incense as auspicious thoughts, the lamp as suppression of mental, vocal and physical activities (*JS* 29).
- Contemporary ascetic literature reads them as either attaining positive spiritual goals or eliminating negative ones: water = purity of the soul free from karma; sandalwood = quieting of mental and worldly turbulence; flowers = fragrance of the Three Jewels; incense = burning away karmic matter; lamp = attainment of enlightenment; rice = use of human birth religiously; sweets = abandonment of worldly pleasures; fruit = attainment of deliverance (ch. 7).

Dundas notes that "a wider variety of meanings can be and is assigned to each of the eight substances by Jain worshippers" and it is more appropriate to consider "the overall purpose of Jain puja as a totality" (ch. 7).

## Purpose of Puja

The fordmakers, having died and attained liberation, are by definition "outside human affairs and thus have no ability to respond to an act of worship" (ch. 7). Vattakera states: "What is to be given by all the excellent Jinas has in fact already been given by them, namely, the instruction in the threefold doctrine of faith, knowledge and behaviour" (*MA* 570).

The purpose of puja is internal, not transactional. "Ancient tradition ... is emphatic that worship of the fordmakers does not actually elicit a response from them but rather brings about an internal, spiritual purification in the worshipper" (*LV* p. 352; ch. 7). The image functions as an "aid" — not as a locus of divinity — but as a focal point for the qualities associated with the fordmakers: "just as the sight of somebody's son might bring to mind one's own son, so an image of a fordmaker brings about remembrance of the qualities of the fordmakers" (*Aparajita* on *BhA* 46; ch. 7). Since the soul that attained liberation embodied a state all souls (except *abhavya*) can reach, "worship of the image of the fordmaker represents an acknowledgement of the divinity of one's own innermost self" (ch. 7).

The various stages of the eightfold offering "thus represent a confirmation by the lay person of the renunciatory ideal which lies at the root of Jainism and an expression of willingness to further one's spiritual development" (ch. 7).

A key contrast with Hindu practice: food offerings to Hindu deities are returned as *prasad* (divine "goodwill"), assimilating the worshipper to the deity. No *prasad* is distributed after Jain puja because the liberated fordmaker cannot eat the offerings. The offerings are understood not as gifts expecting a divine boon but as acts of abandonment, "in a manner similar to the throwing of rice and money into the crowd of onlookers by an ascetic prior to initiation" (ch. 7).

Prayers are *not* directed at the fordmakers for mundane success such as business prosperity or luck in marriage — that is the province of the attendant goddesses (see Goddesses section below).

## Svetambara vs. Digambara Tensions on Image Worship

Both major sects practise image worship, but with differences:
- Digambaras in south India do not touch the image; puja involving anointing is conducted by the *upadhye* priest.
- Svetambara images are adorned with clothing and jewellery; Digambara images are always austere and unclothed.
- The non-image-worshipping [[sthanakvasi]] and [[terapanth]] Svetambara sects reject formal puja of iconic representations entirely, defining themselves against it. Their adherents are nonetheless numerically a minority compared to image-worshippers across both sects.

Early medieval monastic writers "sometimes attempt to stress the superiority of internal worship over the making of offerings" as leading to spiritual deliverance, while physical offerings lead "only" to rebirth in heaven (ch. 7). Yet in more recent times, monks have been "enthusiastic advocates of all the physical image-related practices in which they themselves cannot engage" (ch. 7), with figures like Muni Bhadrankaravijaya arguing that image worship integrates giving, morality, austerity, and meditation into one act.

## Goddesses (*Sasanadevata*)

The fordmakers themselves cannot grant worldly requests. This role is filled by attendant goddesses (*yaksi* / *sasanadevata*), each formally linked to a fordmaker but often developing independent cults. "Prayers are therefore not offered for mundane success ... This is the province of the various divinities like the goddess Padmavati who are linked to the fordmakers and whose representations are generally located outside the inner shrine" (ch. 7).

Jain goddesses were absorbed from local cults beginning at least by the early common era. The earliest fully iconic example of a fordmaker with attendant goddesses is an image from Akota (c. 550 CE). By the eighth century, a complement of twenty-four *yaksas* and *yaksis* was established (ch. 7). Key goddesses:

**Padmavati** — attendant of Parsva; a curer of snakebites and goddess of wealth and beauty. In Karnataka she is an object of intense Digambara devotion, worshipped on Fridays; her cult has an important centre at Hombuja in south Karnataka. She is "the goddess associated with Parsva, although there is not much evidence of the relationship before the tenth century" (ch. 7).

**Ambika** ("Little Mother") — attendant of the twenty-second fordmaker Nemi; particularly important among Svetambaras of Gujarat. Her legend tells of a brahman wife driven from her home for giving alms to a Jain monk who, after miracles and death by jumping into a well with her children, was reborn as Nemi's attendant goddess. She is depicted with two children and mangoes, "indicative of her role as a goddess associated with childbirth and prosperity" (ch. 7).

**Sarasvati** — the oldest known image of Sarasvati is Jain, dating from early in the first century CE, depicted in the posture of Mahavira's enlightenment with a sacred book, "her position as the tutelary deity of the Jain scriptures and the goddess who is invoked to help dispel the darkness of knowledge-concealing karma" (ch. 7).

**Laksmi** — the pan-Indian goddess of wealth, worshipped by Jains in common with Hindus, particularly at Divali.

Jain goddesses are "benevolent, vegetarian and unmarried, unlike the often inauspicious and meat-eating Hindu goddesses," embodying principles of non-violence and restraint (ch. 7). Their role is to make "some concessions to the more mundane aspirations of lay followers" — they do not belong to "any strictly religious or soteriological category, for that is the realm of the fordmakers alone" (ch. 7).

## Devotional Hymnody (*stotra*)

Jain puja is embedded in a rich tradition of devotional poetry. The hymn *Bhaktamara* ("Immortal Devotees," c. sixth century CE) by Manatunga, praising Rsabha in the Vasantatilaka metre, is used by both Svetambara and Digambara communities and "is essentially a prayer to the fordmaker for aid and support." According to legend each of its forty-four verses, on being composed, miraculously broke a chain on Manatunga's body. The verses became objects of worship in themselves, linked with esoteric mantra and yantra practice (ch. 7).

Medieval vernacular hymns in north and west India reached "a particularly impressive extent" and today the "production, performance ... and interpretation of vernacular hymns is an important area of ritual activity for women where their authority as knowledgeable transmitters of Jain teaching and ethical values is established" (ch. 7).

## Yearly Festivals

Jain festivals are "noteworthy for the muted and restrained demeanour of the participants" compared with Hindu festivals (ch. 7). Selected key observances:

**Paryusan** (*Svetambara*) — eight days within the four-month ascetic rain retreat (*caturmas*), called "Abiding," focused on repentance and fasting. The central event is the recitation of the *Kalpasutra* by monks. The final day, *Samvatsari* ("Annual"), involves "a ceremony of communal repentance and the seeking of pardon from all living creatures for any injury inflicted"; letters asking forgiveness are sent to friends and business associates; local authorities are asked to ban animal slaughter. Paryusan concludes with a communal meal (ch. 7).

**Dasalaksanaparvan** (*Digambara*) — "Festival of the Ten Religious Qualities," beginning immediately after Paryusan, lasting ten days. Revolves around recitation of the ten chapters of the *Tattvartha Sutra* and homilies on the ten religious qualities (*TS* 9.6: forbearance, gentleness, uprightness, purity, truth, restraint, austerity, renunciation, lack of possession, chastity). The most auspicious day is *Anantacaturdasi* (associated with the fourteenth fordmaker Ananta), involving universal fasting and puja with fourteen flowers. Ends with *Ksamapanda*, "Asking for Pardon" (ch. 7).

**Mahavir Jayanti** — Mahavira's birthday, celebrated by both sects on the thirteenth day of the bright half of Caitra (March/April).

**Divali** ("Row of Lights") — commemorates the final liberation of Mahavira and the lighting of lamps by kings of the Ganges area in tribute to "the light of knowledge which had gone out with his death." Puja is offered to Laksmi for worldly prosperity. The day after Divali commemorates Gautama's attainment of omniscience and marks the beginning of the new commercial year (ch. 7).

**Aksayatrtiya** ("Undying Third") — third day of the bright half of Vaisakha (April/May); commemorates Rsabha's initiation and the first act of religious giving (sugarcane juice given to Rsabha by Sreyamsa); celebrated by both sects. The most suitable location is regarded as [[mount-satrunjaya]] (ch. 7).

**Jnanapancami** (*Svetambara*) / **Srutapancami** (*Digambara*) — festival in honour of the scriptures; involves dusting and repairing books and commissioning manuscript copies (ch. 7).

**Maunekaadasi** ("Silence Eleventh") (*Svetambara*) — a day devoted solely to silence, falling on the eleventh day of the bright half of Margasirsa (November/December); "supposedly takes its origin from a request by busy merchants to have their own day to compensate for their inability to find the time to engage in religious vows and ceremonies" (ch. 7).
