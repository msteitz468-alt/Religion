# Dependent Origination

**Pratītyasamutpāda** (Sanskrit; Pali *paṭiccasamuppāda*), "dependent origination / conditioned
co-arising," is one of the most fundamental Buddhist teachings: **everything arises in dependence on
causes and conditions; nothing exists independently.** "This being, that becomes; from the arising
of this, that arises." It is the doctrinal hinge between the early teaching and
[[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] [[sunyata|emptiness]].

## The Early/Abhidharma Teaching

Classically the doctrine is spelled out as the **twelvefold chain (nidānas)** — ignorance →
volitional formations → consciousness → name-and-form → the six sense bases → contact → feeling →
craving → clinging → becoming → birth → aging-and-death — explaining how suffering and rebirth
(*saṃsāra*) arise and, when the chain is broken (beginning with ignorance), how they cease. The
Buddha presents dependent origination as the **middle path between eternalism and annihilationism**:
there is no permanent self that transmigrates (against eternalism), yet there is causal continuity
(against annihilationism) — the person across lives is "neither identical nor distinct." For
[[abhidharma|Abhidharma]], the *dharmas* are what stand in these dependence relations.

## Dasgupta's Analysis: the Chain Over Three Lives (Vol. I, Ch. V)

[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] reconstructs the twelve links as the answer the Buddha
reached by tracing decay-and-death *backward* — "What being there, is there birth?" — and adds detail the
wiki should track:

- **Bhava = karma.** Following **Candrakīrti**, Dasgupta reads *bhava* ("becoming/existence") as *the karma
  of a previous existence that produces rebirth* (*punarbhava-janakaṃ karma*) rather than bare "existence" —
  "the word was probably used loosely for *karma-bhava*." He stresses how close several links lie to the
  [[upanishads|Upaniṣadic]] formulae: *upādāna* ≈ "whatever one betakes oneself to, so does he work" (Bṛh.
  IV.4.5), *taṇhā* ≈ *kāma*, though the terms *bhava*, *upādāna*, *taṇhā* are not in the early Upaniṣads.
- ***Nāma-rūpa* and *viññāṇa*.** Dasgupta reads *viññāṇa* here as the **germ of consciousness that descends
  into the mother's womb** (citing *Madhyamaka-vṛtti*: "if consciousness did not descend into the womb,
  name-and-form could not be constituted there"), product of the dying man's *saṅkhāras* and prior
  consciousness — transmitted "like a reflected image, the lighting of one lamp from another, or the impress
  of a stamp on wax." Birth and death are not a great break but the same uniform momentary change.
- **The three-life scheme.** Per Buddhaghosa and the *Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha*: *avijjā* and *saṅkhāra* belong
  to the **past** life; the middle eight (*viññāṇa* → *bhava*) to the **present**; *jāti* and *jarā-maraṇa*
  to the **future** — "Five causes in the Past and now a fivefold fruit; five causes Now and a fivefold
  fruit to come."
- ***Hetu* vs. *paccaya*.** Dasgupta distinguishes the causal chain read as bare **succession** (*hetu*)
  from read as **ground** (*paccaya*): *avijjā* is the *paccaya* of *saṅkhāra* in nine ways (ground of its
  origin, movement, conglomeration, etc.), suggesting "the saṅkhāras were in some sense regarded as special
  manifestations of avijjā" — though, he cautions, the early texts do not develop this further.

## Tsong-kha-pa's Treatment (Lamrim Chenmo, Vol. I, ch. 21)

[[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] presents the twelve factors as "the best method for generating
disenchantment with cyclic existence" (see [[renunciation|the determination to be free]]). He
organizes them in three further classifications: by **four causes** (the *Rice Seedling Sūtra*'s seed
of consciousness sown by ignorance, moistened by craving); by **four groups** (projecting / projected
/ actualizing / actualized — *Compendium of Knowledge*); and by **three paths** (Nāgārjuna's
*Fundamental Treatise*: factors 1, 8, 9 are afflictions; 2 and 10 are [[karma|karma]]; the other seven
are suffering — "From the three arise the two; from the two arise the seven…"). The cycle completes
over a **minimum of two and a maximum of three** lifetimes. Because arhats retain vast projecting
karma yet are liberated by lacking afflictions, the analysis exposes the afflictions as the real
enemy. The Kadam master **Pu-chung-wa** built his entire graded path on the twelve factors, mapping
them onto the [[three-types-of-persons|three capacities]].

## Nāgārjuna's Reading: Dependence Entails Emptiness

[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]'s [[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] makes dependent origination the very meaning
of emptiness. The dedicatory verse glosses it with the **eight negations** ("neither cessation nor
origination, neither annihilation nor the eternal…"), and **MMK 24.18** — the text's most famous
verse — declares: *"Dependent origination we declare to be emptiness; it is a dependent concept;
just that is the middle path."* The reasoning (24.16–19): whatever arises through causes and
conditions takes its character *from* them, so it cannot have **intrinsic nature ([[svabhava|
svabhāva]])**; therefore *"there is no dharma whatsoever that is not dependently originated, and
hence no dharma whatsoever that is non-empty."* Conversely, only *because* things are empty
(dependent) can they arise and cease at all — so to deny emptiness is to make dependent origination,
and the whole Buddhist path, impossible (MMK 24.14).

Read as **conventional** truth, dependent origination describes how pots and persons arise; read as
**ultimate** truth, it states that no ultimately real thing ever arises (see [[two-truths|the two
truths]]).

## Cross-Tradition Note

Dependent origination is the Buddhist alternative to a first cause / creator and to self-existent
substance: a thoroughgoing **relationalism** in which independent, intrinsic existence is found
nowhere — the metaphysical ground of [[anatta|nonself]] and [[sunyata|emptiness]] alike.
