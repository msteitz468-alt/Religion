# The Passions of the Soul (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s **Treatise on the Passions** ([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]]
I-II, Q22–48) is the most extended analysis of the emotions in medieval philosophy — twenty-seven
questions, the single longest treatise in the *Summa*. A **passion** (*passio animae*) is a movement
of the **sensitive appetite** accompanied by a bodily change, aroused by an apprehended good or evil.

## The structure of the passions (Q22–25)

Passions belong to the sensitive appetite, which divides into two powers, yielding eleven passions
in contrary pairs:
- **Concupiscible** (the simple good/evil axis): **love / hatred**, **desire / aversion**, **joy /
  sorrow**.
- **Irascible** (the arduous good/evil axis): **hope / despair**, **fear / daring**, and **anger**
  — which uniquely has *no contrary*, because in anger the evil (an injury already received) is
  present and already includes its own contrariety (Q23, A3; *Rhetoric* ii).

Love is the **first and root** passion: desire is love in movement toward the absent good, and joy
is love at rest in the good possessed; every other passion is caused by some form of love (Q25;
Q27, A4; Q28).

## Passions are not in themselves evil (Q24)

Against the **Stoic** condemnation of all passion (*apatheia*), Aquinas sides with the Peripatetics:
the Stoics failed to distinguish the sensitive from the intellectual appetite. Passions are morally
good or evil only as they are subject to reason and will — "the passions of the soul, in so far as
they are contrary to the order of reason, incline us to sin; but in so far as they are controlled by
reason, they pertain to virtue" (Q24, A2–A3). A passion *consequent* upon a good will even
*increases* the goodness of an act ("my heart and my flesh have rejoiced in the living God," Ps.
83:3).

## Love and its effects (Q26–28)

Love arises from the apprehension of a connatural good (the **beautiful** adds to the good a
relation to the cognitive power — Q27, A1). Its effects (Q28): **union** (real and affective),
**mutual indwelling** (lover in the beloved and vice versa), **ecstasy** (being drawn out of
oneself), and **zeal** (intensity of love that brooks no rival).

## Sorrow and its remedies (Q35–39)

Sorrow (*tristitia*) is pain arising from interior apprehension; of all passions it is the most
harmful to the body (Q37, A4). Aquinas's celebrated, humane list of **five remedies for sorrow**
(Q38): (1) any **pleasure**; (2) **weeping and groaning**; (3) the **sympathy of friends**; (4) the
**contemplation of truth** ("the greatest of all pleasures consists in the contemplation of
truth"); and (5) **sleep and baths**. Sorrow is evil simply, yet a sign of goodness on the
supposition of a present evil, and can be virtuous (Q39).

## Hope, fear, anger (Q40–48)

- **Hope** (Q40): an irascible passion bearing on a *future*, *arduous* good that is *possible* to
  attain; despair is its withdrawal-contrary; experience, youth, and even drunkenness cause hope.
- **Fear** (Q41–44): with six species (Damascene); its bodily effect is **contraction** (heat
  retreats inward, leaving the extremities cold and trembling).
- **Anger** (Q46–48): a composite desire for **vengeance** upon an apprehended unjust injury;
  always presupposes an act of reason (denouncing the injury) and a relation of justice; produces a
  fervor of bile around the heart and, of all the passions, "most hinders the judgment of reason."

## Significance

The treatise gives Aquinas's moral theology a sophisticated philosophical psychology: the passions
are the raw material the moral [[human-act|human act]] governs and the virtues (treated next in the
*Prima Secundae*) perfect — not Stoic disturbances to be extirpated but powers to be ordered by
reason.
