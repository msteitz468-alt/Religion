# Peace (*Pax*) — the Tranquillity of Order

The concept of **peace** as developed by [[augustine|Augustine]] in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]]
Book XIX, the most influential Christian account of peace and political order in the Latin tradition.

## Definition

Augustine's governing formula: **"The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order"**
(*pax omnium rerum tranquillitas ordinis*), where "order is the distribution which allots things
equal and unequal, each to its own place." Peace is arranged as a hierarchy, from the body up to God:

- **peace of the body** — "the duly proportioned arrangement of its parts";
- **peace of the irrational soul** — "the harmonious repose of the appetites";
- **peace of the rational soul** — "the harmony of knowledge and action";
- **peace between man and God** — "the well-ordered obedience of faith to eternal law";
- **peace between man and man** — "well-ordered concord";
- **domestic and civil peace** — well-ordered concord of rulers and ruled;
- **peace of the heavenly city** — "the perfectly ordered and harmonious enjoyment of God, and of
  one another in God."

## The universality of the desire for peace

All things seek peace, even through war: "It is therefore with the desire for peace that wars are
waged… For every man seeks peace by waging war, but no man seeks war by making peace." Even robbers
keep peace among themselves to wage war on others; even the most savage creature seeks peace with its
own body and household. Pride's perversity "apes God" by seeking an unjust peace of its own, "but it
cannot help loving peace of one kind or other."

## The two cities and earthly peace

The [[two-cities|heavenly city]], "the part of it which sojourns on earth and lives by faith," **uses**
earthly peace as a pilgrim — obeying the laws by which "the things necessary for the maintenance of this
mortal life are administered," and adopting the diverse "manners, laws, and institutions" of the nations
"so long only as no hindrance to the worship of the one supreme and true God is thus introduced." "As
long as the two cities are commingled, we also enjoy the peace of Babylon" (citing Jer. 29:7). The name
**Jerusalem** is glossed as "Vision of Peace." Eternal peace — "the peace of this blessedness and the
blessedness of this peace" — is the [[supreme-good|supreme good]] and final end of the city of God.

## Justice and the commonwealth

Closely tied to peace is Augustine's redefinition of **a people**. Against Cicero/Scipio (*De Republica*),
who defined a republic by "a common acknowledgment of right," Augustine argues that where God is not
truly served there is no true justice, hence no right, hence no people in Cicero's sense — so Rome was
never a true republic. He offers an alternative, descriptive definition: **"a people is an assemblage of
reasonable beings bound together by a common agreement as to the objects of their love"** — a people is
known by *what it loves*, "superior in proportion as it is bound together by higher interests." See
[[two-cities]], [[gloria-love-of-praise]].
