# Analogia Fidei (Analogy of Faith)

The *analogia fidei* ("analogy of faith") is the principle, grounded in Romans 12:6
("whether prophecy, according to the analogy of faith," *kata tēn analogian tēs pisteōs*),
that Scripture is to be interpreted according to the rule or proportion of faith — and, in
[[karl-barth|Barth's]] usage, that theological knowledge is constituted by and measured against
faith's own object rather than any external standard.

## Biblical Origin

Paul's phrase in Romans 12:6 (κατὰ τὴν ἀναλογίαν τῆς πίστεως) governs the exercise of
the gift of prophecy: it is to be used "in proportion to faith." In the Reformed hermeneutical
tradition this became a principle for interpreting Scripture: obscure passages are to be read
in the light of clearer ones, all in conformity with the rule of faith established in the plain
sense of Scripture as a whole.

## Barth's Usage

In [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1|*Church Dogmatics* I.1]] §1.2 (lines 550–569), Barth employs
the term epistemologically: talk about God has "true content when it conforms to the being
of the Church, i.e., when it conforms to Jesus Christ ... according to the analogy of faith"
(Rom. 12:6). The standard by which dogmatics measures Christian utterance is not invented or
found externally, but "given with the Church ... as Jesus Christ is given, as God in His
revelation gives Himself to faith. But it is given" (lines 554–556).

The *analogia fidei* functions in Barth as the *epistemological counterpart* and explicit
*alternative* to the Roman Catholic [[analogia-entis|*analogia entis*]] (analogy of being).
Where the *analogia entis* holds that creaturely existence bears a structural resemblance to
divine being that can serve as the basis for natural knowledge of God, the *analogia fidei*
insists that the only legitimate analogy by which created speech reaches God is the analogy
constituted by God's own act of revelation and the faith that receives it. The movement is
exclusively from God to creature, not from creature to God.

"It is not possible for it [dogmatics] to begin by finding or inventing the standard by which
it measures. It sees and recognises that this is given with the Church" (lines 553–554). The
knowledge resulting is both certain (because its object, Jesus Christ, is given) and always
provisional (because human appropriation is fallible and partial — "we see in a mirror dimly,"
1 Cor. 13:12).

## Contrast with Analogia Entis

| | *Analogia fidei* | *Analogia entis* |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis** | God's act of revelation in Christ received in faith | Creaturely participation in divine being |
| **Direction** | God → creature | Creature → God |
| **Access** | Faith alone (*sola fide*) | Natural reason + faith |
| **Associated tradition** | Reformed / Barth | Roman Catholic scholasticism |

## Fuller Account in §6.4

In §6.4 of *CD* I.1 (lines 11228–11234), Barth elaborates the *analogia fidei* as the epistemological heart of the knowability of the Word of God:

> "Our reply to the Roman Catholic doctrine of the *analogia entis* is not, then, a denial of the concept of analogy. We say rather that the analogy in question is not an *analogia entis* but according to Rom. 12:6 the *analogia tēs pisteōs* [*analogia fidei*], the likeness of the known in the knowing, of the object in thought, of the Word of God in the word that is thought and spoken by man, as this differentiates true Christian prophecy in faith from all false prophecy."

This corresponds to Barth's treatment of the *imago Dei*: the image of God was "totally annihilated" by sin (not merely damaged, *contra* Brunner; lines 10952–10974). The "point of contact" for God's Word in man is not a residual creaturely property but the *rectitudo* (rightness) restored *in Christ*, which is "not real outside faith; it is real only in faith" (lines 10967–10968).

The *analogia fidei* is further grounded in the Pauline inversion: human knowledge of God is re-described as *being known by God* (Gal. 4:9; 1 Cor. 8:2–3; lines 11235–11271). The human act of knowing corresponds to (is analogous to, while remaining distinct from) the divine act of knowing man — not the reverse.
