The doctrine of divine attributes in Maimonidean philosophy is the systematic account of what kinds of predicates may legitimately be applied to God, and why positive *essential* attributes are philosophically inadmissible. The locus classicus is [[guide-for-the-perplexed]], Part I, Chapters LVII–LXI. For the related Islamic debate see [[divine-attributes-in-islam]].

---

## The Five Classes of Attributes (Maimonides' Taxonomy)

Maimonides identifies five classes of attributes that people attempt to apply to God, and subjects each to analysis. The taxonomy is set out in Chapter LVII–LVIII:

1. **Definition** — describing the essence through genus and differentia. Inadmissible for God, who has no genus and no differentiating property outside Himself.

2. **Partial definition** — describing part of the essence. Inadmissible, since God's essence is absolutely simple and has no parts.

3. **Quality (essential attributes)** — predicating moral or intellectual qualities (wise, powerful, living, knowing). Inadmissible as *essential* attributes. If such attributes inhered in God as real properties distinct from His essence, they would constitute a plurality within the divine being. The correct reading of such predications is either as attributes of action (class 5) or as negations (class 4).

4. **Negative attributes** — the only genuinely admissible class for describing God's essence. "We say that it is living, expressing thereby that it is not dead. We call such a being incorporeal, because we notice that it is unlike the heavens…" (Ch. LVIII). See [[negative-theology]] for the full treatment.

5. **Attributes of action** (*middot ha-pe'ulah*) — descriptions derived from effects God produces. "It is further necessary that we say that the existence, that is the essence, of this being is not limited to its own existence: many existences emanate from it… we say, on that account, it has power, wisdom, and will, i.e., it is not feeble or ignorant, or hasty, and does not abandon its creatures." (Ch. LVIII). These are permissible because they describe the relation between God's works and created beings, not an intrinsic property of the divine essence.

The conclusion: "every attribute predicated of God either denotes the quality of an action, or — when the attribute is intended to convey some idea of the Divine Being itself, and not of His actions — the negation of the opposite." (Ch. LVIII)

## The Argument Against Positive Essential Attributes

### Divine Simplicity

The foundational argument is that God's existence is not due to any cause, and therefore "existence and essence are perfectly identical; He is not a substance to which existence is joined as an accident." (Ch. LVII). Any positive essential attribute would introduce a distinction between the subject (the divine essence) and the predicate (the attribute). That implies composition, which contradicts absolute simplicity.

### The Idolatry Argument

Maimonides goes further than a merely logical objection:

> "I do not merely declare that he who affirms attributes of God has not sufficient knowledge concerning the Creator, admits some association with God, or conceives Him to be different from what He is: but I say that he unconsciously loses his belief in God." (Ch. LX)

His reasoning: if God's existence is absolutely simple, no partial knowledge of God is possible (we cannot know one thing about Him while being ignorant of another). Affirming positive attributes — treating God as a subject with distinguishable properties — is therefore not merely an imprecise description but a description of a non-existent being: "when we say that that essence, which is called 'God,' is a substance with many properties by which it can be described, we apply that name to an object which does not at all exist." (Ch. LX)

### Unity and Attributes

The problem of positive attributes is structurally connected to divine unity. If God truly possessed multiple positive attributes (wisdom, will, power, life), these would constitute real distinctions within the divine essence, implying a plurality analogous to that of compound substances. This would undermine monotheism itself: "you are led to assume that there is a plurality in God, and to believe that He, though one essence, has several unknown attributes." (Ch. LX)

The Mutakallimun who affirm real divine attributes face this self-refuting problem explicitly in their proofs for unity (Ch. LXXV): "according to their opinion, the Primal Cause includes many different elements. They represent its wisdom and its omnipotence as two different things, and again the omnipotence as different from the will. Consequently it would not be impossible that either of the two divine beings possessed several properties, some of which would be common to both, and some peculiar to only one of them."

## The Divine Names and the Tetragrammaton (Ch. LXI–LXIII)

Maimonides applies the doctrine of attributes to his treatment of the divine names. All names of God in Scripture, with one exception, are *appellatives* — names derived from God's actions or from attributes applied by convention:

- *Adonay* (Lord) — derived from the appellative "lord"
- *El*, *Elohim* — "chief," applied as designation of power in action
- *Shadday* — "he who is sufficient" (from *day*, "enough")
- *Jah* — implies eternal existence
- *Zaddik*, *hannun*, *rahum*, *dayyan* — all appellatives derived from actions

The sole exception is the **Tetragrammaton** (yod-he-vav-he), which alone is a true *nomen proprium* — a proper name that "does not denote any attribute of God, nor does it imply anything except His existence. Absolute existence includes the idea of eternity, i.e., the necessity of existence." (Ch. LXIII). Its derivation from *hayah* ("to be / to exist") is unique in that the same word appears twice in *Ehyeh asher Ehyeh* — "the Being whose existence is absolute" — making it a self-referential name of pure, uncaused existence.

The prophetic phrase *Ehyeh asher Ehyeh* (Exod. 3:14) was given to Moses as a philosophical proof-concept: "God taught Moses how to teach them, and how to establish amongst them the belief in the existence of Himself, namely, by saying *Ehyeh asher Ehyeh*… the Being whose existence is absolute." (Ch. LXIII)

The Zechariah prophecy "In that day will the Lord be One, and His name One" (Zech. 14:9) is interpreted eschatologically: at the end of days humanity will comprehend divine unity fully enough that God will be addressed by a single proper name indicating His essence alone, rather than by multiple appellative attributes.

## God as Intellectus, Intelligens, and Intelligibile (Ch. LXVIII)

An important corollary of the attributes doctrine concerns divine intellection. Maimonides adopts from Aristotelian philosophy the principle that intellect in act is identical with its object: the knower, the act of knowing, and the known collapse into one when knowing is actual rather than potential. Since God is pure intellect *always* in act — "there is in Him at no time a mere potentiality, He does not comprehend at one time, and is without comprehension at another time, but He comprehends constantly" — "He and the things comprehended are one and the same thing, that is to say, His essence."

This is not an admission of three distinct attributes in God (intellect, act, object). Rather it is the demonstration that in God the three distinctions we normally draw between them entirely dissolve, consistent with absolute simplicity.

## Distinction from the Islamic Debate

The debate over divine attributes (*sifat*) is central to Islamic Kalam. Maimonides explicitly distinguishes his position from the Mu'tazilah (who denied real attributes, a position early Jewish scholars such as the Geonim adopted) and from the Ash'ariyya (who affirmed them). His argument is not primarily theological but philosophical: he derives the denial of positive essential attributes from demonstrative (*burhan*) metaphysics, not from a dogmatic commitment.
