## Definition

In [[maimonides|Maimonides]]' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]] (Part I, Chs. XXVII–LVI), the treatment of divine names is embedded within a broader doctrine of divine attributes. The central claim is that every name or attribute applied to God in Scripture functions as either (a) a figurative expression pointing to one of God's *actions*, or (b) a purely homonymous term that shares nothing but its label with the same term when applied to creatures. No name or attribute picks out an essential quality of God's inner nature.

## Homonymity of All Divine Names and Attributes

Maimonides' most precise statement appears in Ch. LVI:

> "The terms Wisdom, Power, Will, and Life are applied to God and to other beings by way of perfect homonymity, admitting of no comparison whatever … they have only the same names, and nothing else is common to them." (Ch. LVI)

And likewise:

> "The term existence, when applied to God and to other beings, is perfectly homonymous." (Ch. LVI)

This is the culmination of the argument running through Chs. LI–LVI. Maimonides distinguishes five possible types of affirmative attributes (Ch. LII):

1. **Definition** — inadmissible for God, who has no prior causes by which to be defined.
2. **Part of definition** — inadmissible, as it implies composition of essence.
3. **Quality** — inadmissible, as quality implies an accident superadded to essence.
4. **Relation** — the least objectionable type, but still inadmissible because there is no genuine correlation between God and creatures, given the "important difference between them in respect to true existence, the greatest of all differences" (Ch. LII).
5. **Action** — the only admissible kind: "this kind of attributes is separate from the essences of the thing described, and, therefore, appropriate to be employed in describing the Creator" (Ch. LII).

The practical consequence: when Scripture applies names such as "merciful," "gracious," "longsuffering," or "wrathful" to God, these are all **attributes of action**, not of essence. They describe acts resembling acts that in human beings arise from those dispositions — they do not imply that God possesses those dispositions (Ch. LIV).

## The Thirteen Middot as Attributes of Action

Ch. LIV is built around Exodus 33–34 and Moses' petition to know God's essence and attributes:

> "'Show me now thy way, that I may know thee.' … God is known by His attributes, for Moses believed that he knew Him, when he was shown the way of God." (Ch. LIV)

God's reply was to reveal "all my goodness" — interpreted as the whole creation and the system of governance — and to enumerate the thirteen *middot* (Exod. 34:6–7). Maimonides insists:

> "They do not mean to say that God really possesses middot (qualities), but that He performs actions similar to such of our actions as originate in certain qualities … not that God has really such dispositions." (Ch. LIV)

The Sages' term *middot* (literally "qualities") is used for these divine acts precisely as it is used for human character types (Mishnah Avot 5:13–14): the terminology is descriptive of action-patterns, not of essence.

## The Language of Man (Leshon Benei Adam)

A governing hermeneutical principle invoked throughout Chs. XXIX, XXXIII, XLVI, and LIII is the rabbinic maxim that "the Torah speaks in accordance with the language of man" (*dibberah Torah bi-leshon benei adam*):

> "The language of the Torah is like the language of man … it is the object of the Torah to serve as a guide for the instruction of the young, of women, and of the common people." (Ch. XXXIII)

> "We apply to all such passages the principle, 'The Torah speaketh in the language of man,' and say that the object of all these terms is to describe God as the most perfect being, not as possessing those qualities which are only perfections in relation to created living beings." (Ch. LIII)

This principle governs the entire catalogue of corporeal expressions in Scripture. Physical organs are attributed to God only to indicate the *actions* normally performed by those organs (Ch. XLVI):

> "These organs of the body are figuratively ascribed to God, who is above all imperfection, to express that He performs certain acts: and these acts are figuratively ascribed to Him to express that He possesses certain perfections different from those acts themselves." (Ch. XLVI)

## The Attributist Error and the Mutakallimun

Chs. LI–LIII also criticize the *Attributists* — identified with Kalam-style theologians (the *Mutakallimun*) — who affirm divine unity with the lips while implicitly positing plurality:

> "Those who believe that God is One, and that He has many attributes, declare the unity with their lips, and assume plurality in their thoughts. This is like the doctrine of the Christians, who say that He is one and He is three." (Ch. L)

The error is traced to taking scriptural attributive language literally rather than figuratively:

> "The adherence to the literal sense of the text of Holy Writ is the source of all this error." (Ch. LI)

## Incorporating Onkelos and Jonathan

The homonymous-term chapters (Chs. XXVII–XLVIII) repeatedly invoke Onkelos the Proselyte and Jonathan son of Uzziel as exemplars of the non-corporeal interpretive method. Onkelos systematically paraphrases all expressions of divine motion, perception, and corporeal action — translating "the Lord came down" as "the Lord manifested Himself" (Exod. 19:20), rendering "his feet" (Exod. 24:10) as "the throne of his glory," and paraphrasing "God heard" as "it was heard before the Lord" (Ch. XLVIII). Maimonides regards this as demonstrating that the incorporeality of God is not merely a philosophical refinement but the straightforward understanding of the tradition's greatest interpreters.

## Hermeneutical Method

The interpretive framework operative across these chapters is best described as **figurative / allegorical reading in service of negation** (anticipating the fuller [[negative-theology]] doctrine). The procedure is:

1. Identify expressions ascribed to God that, taken literally, imply corporeality or psychical dispositions.
2. Show that the terms are homonymous (equivocal) — the word carries one sense when applied to humans, another (or no positive sense at all) when applied to God.
3. Re-read the expression as pointing to a divine *action* or *perfection*, not to a divine quality.

This is the operation of the "language of man" principle: prophetic speech adopts human idiom for pedagogical and communicative purposes, not ontological description.

## Absence of the Tetragrammaton in This Range

**Note (faithfulness):** The chapters in this range (XXVII–LVI) do not yet address the Tetragrammaton (*Shem ha-Meforash*), the name *Ehyeh asher Ehyeh* ("I am that I am"), or the explicit distinction between the proper name and descriptive names (*shemot ha-nikhnasim*). Those discussions occur in later chapters of Part I (outside this range). This page covers only the doctrine of divine attributes and homonymous names as presented in Chs. XXVII–LVI.
