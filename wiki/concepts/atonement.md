# Atonement

**Atonement** is the reconciliation of humanity to God effected by the saving work of
[[jesus|Christ]], pre-eminently His Passion. [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s account
([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Tertia Pars Q48–Q49) is the most systematic of the scholastic
treatments. It develops and qualifies Anselm's satisfaction theory, and
is the bridge between the patristic models and later Catholic and Protestant soteriology. Aquinas's
distinctive move is that the Passion does not save in *one* way but in **four concurrent modes** —
merit, satisfaction, sacrifice, and redemption — all reducible to a single instrumental efficient
causality under the Godhead.

## The four modes of the Passion's efficacy (ST III Q48)

Aquinas gathers them in one summary (Q48 A6 ad 3):

> "Christ's Passion, according as it is compared with His Godhead, operates in an efficient manner;
> but in so far as it is compared with the will of Christ's soul it acts in a **meritorious**
> manner; considered as being within Christ's very flesh, it acts by way of **satisfaction**…
> inasmuch as we are freed from the servitude of guilt, it acts by way of **redemption**; but in so
> far as we are reconciled with God it acts by way of **sacrifice**."

1. **Merit** (A1): Because grace was given to Christ as **[[incarnation|Head of the Church]]**, His
   meritorious acts overflow to His members — "the head and members are as one mystic person."
   Christ merited salvation "not only for Himself, but likewise for all His members."
2. **Satisfaction** (A2) — the *locus classicus* of the **satisfaction theory**: "He properly atones
   for an offense who offers something which the offended one loves equally, or even more than he
   detested the offense." Christ's Passion was a **superabundant** satisfaction on three grounds:
   the "exceeding charity from which He suffered," the "dignity of His life… the life of one who was
   God and man," and "the extent of the Passion and the greatness of the grief endured." The dignity
   of Christ's flesh, deriving from the divine Person assuming it, is "of infinite worth" — hence the
   God-man alone can satisfy condignly for the infinite offense of sin (cf. [[hypostatic-union]];
   the "condign satisfaction" argument of Q1 A2).
3. **Sacrifice** (A3): "A true sacrifice is every good work done in order that we may cling to God in
   holy fellowship" (Augustine). Christ's voluntary suffering from charity was "a true sacrifice,"
   in which (uniquely) the one Mediator is at once the offerer, the offered, the one to whom it is
   offered (as God), and one with those for whom it is offered. See
   [[priesthood-of-christ|priest and victim]].
4. **Redemption** (A4): Sin held humanity in a twofold bondage — the bondage of sin itself and the
   debt of punishment. The Passion is "a price at the cost of which we were freed from both." Aquinas
   insists, against the patristic "ransom to Satan" tradition, that **the price was paid to God, not
   to the devil** (see [[ransom-to-whom|the ransom controversy]]). Christ is the Redeemer immediately
   *as man*; the Trinity is the first and remote cause; the sufferings of other saints aid the Church
   "by way, not of redemption, but of example."

Underlying all four is **instrumental efficient causality** (A6): God is the principal cause; Christ's
humanity is "the instrument of the Godhead," so that all His actions and sufferings "operate
instrumentally in virtue of His Godhead for the salvation of men."

## Effects of the Passion (ST III Q49)

Deliverance from sin (the Passion is the "universal cause of the forgiveness of sins," applied to
individuals through faith and the [[sacraments]] — [[baptism]] and [[sacrament-of-penance|penance]] —
which "derive their power from Christ's Passion"); deliverance from the power of the devil (who, by
slaying the sinless Christ, "exceeded his power" and justly lost his captives — Augustine);
deliverance from the debt of punishment; **reconciliation** with God (not that God "began anew to
love us," but that "the source of hatred was taken away"); and the opening of the gate of heaven,
closed by original and personal sin.

## Death, descent, and resurrection

- **The death** (Q50): The hypostatic union was **not** dissolved at death — the Word remained
  united to both the separated soul and the body ("My God, why hast Thou forsaken Me?" means the
  Father left Him to His persecutors, not that the union broke).
- **The descent into hell** (Q52): Christ's soul descended locally only into the **limbo of the
  Fathers** (*limbus patrum*) — the just detained through original sin, not personal guilt — whom He
  delivered, leading them to glory at His Resurrection; His *effect* reached all four regions of hell
  (the lost, purgatory, the limbo of children, the Fathers), but He did not deliver the lost, the
  children in original sin, or (wholesale) the souls in [[purgatory|purgatory]]. This is the
  scholastic "harrowing of hell."
- **The Resurrection** (Q53, Q56): the **efficient and exemplar** cause — not the *meritorious*
  cause (Christ being no longer a wayfarer) — of the resurrection of both bodies and souls. The
  division of labor: "He was delivered up for our sins, and rose again for our justification" — the
  Passion removes evil and wins forgiveness; the Resurrection is "the beginning and exemplar of all
  good things," the cause of newness of life. See [[bodily-resurrection]].

## The patristic background: Athanasius's "exchange" / physical model

Long before Anselm and Aquinas, [[athanasius|Athanasius]]'s [[on-the-incarnation|*On the
Incarnation*]] (c. 318) frames the saving work not as juridical satisfaction but as the reversal of
**corruption** (*phthora*) through the Word's solidarity with human nature. Because humanity, made in
the [[imago-dei|Image]], had fallen toward non-being, only the Word who made all could "recreate"
it. The Word assumes a real, mortal body and "surrendered His body to death instead of all," so that
"in His death all might die, and the law of death thereby be abolished" (§8). The death of the one
body, indwelt by "the Word who was above all," is "a sufficient exchange for all" (§9) — the body
both pays "the debt" owed by all to death (§20) and, being deathless by the Word's indwelling,
abolishes corruption from within. This is often called the **"physical" or "participatory" theory of
redemption**: salvation works by the union of humanity-in-general with the immortal Word, illustrated
by the **great king honoring a whole city by dwelling in one of its houses** (§9). Athanasius also
keeps the older **Christus Victor / ransom** motifs (Heb 2:14: the Word destroys "him that had the
power of death, that is, the devil," §§10, 20) and the **curse-bearing** of the cross (Gal 3:13,
§25), but his governing category is incorruption restored, not penalty satisfied. This contrasts
instructively with Aquinas's later **satisfaction** framing and with the patristic "ransom to Satan"
strand Aquinas rejected (see [[ransom-to-whom]]).

## Reception and significance

Aquinas's "satisfaction" refines Anselm's: where Anselm framed the alternative as *aut poena aut
satisfactio* (punishment or satisfaction), Aquinas folds satisfaction, merit, sacrifice, and
redemption into one work and grounds them in charity and instrumental causality, avoiding any notion
that the Father needed to be made loving. The Reformers transformed this inheritance into **penal
substitution** (Christ bearing the *punishment* due to sin), a development Catholic theology
distinguishes from Aquinas's *satisfactio*. The motif of Christ as both priest and victim feeds
directly into the [[transubstantiation|eucharistic]] theology of the Mass as the re-presentation of
the one sacrifice.

## Pelikan: Early Soteriology as a Cluster of Images

[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 3) stresses that ante-Nicene soteriology was **a cluster of images, not a doctrine**: it is impossible "to ascribe exclusive or even primary force to any one theory." He distinguishes three atonement themes — Christ as **teacher/example**, as **sacrifice/satisfaction**, and as **victor** — answering to three definitions of salvation's content: **revelation of truth, forgiveness and justification, and immortality and [[apotheosis-divinization|deification]]**. Beneath them lies an unresolved question: did Christ's work *accomplish* the reconciliation, or *disclose* one "there all along"? Pelikan argues the **[[christus-victor|Christus Victor]]** motif (with the [[ransom-to-whom|ransom to the devil]] and the [[descent-into-hell|descent into hell]]) was far more central than later Western dogmatics, focused on satisfaction, has recognized. The word "satisfaction" was introduced by [[tertullian]] (from the discipline of penance) and first applied to Christ's death by Hilary.
