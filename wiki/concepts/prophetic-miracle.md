# Prophetic Miracle (*Muʿjiza*)

In Islamic theology, a *muʿjiza* (lit. "that which renders [opponents] incapable") is a supranormal event granted by God to a prophet as a sign authenticating his prophethood. The theological problem the concept must solve is twofold: (1) how such events are metaphysically possible without violating God's omnipotence or the coherence of the natural order, and (2) how they function as evidence for prophetic truth-claims. The locus classicus for the philosophical defense of miracle in the *kalām* tradition is the Seventeenth Discussion of [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]].

---

## Metaphysical Precondition: The Denial of Necessary Causation

Prophetic miracles become metaphysically unproblematic once the necessity of causal connections is denied. On the [[occasionalism|occasionalist]] account, the observed sequences of nature ("fire burns," "decapitation kills") are not logically necessary but *habitually regular* — God's customary practice of creating the same pairings. Since God creates each event by direct will, He can simply decline to create the habitual effect on any given occasion, or create an entirely different outcome. As Ghazālī states:

> "it is within [divine] power to create satiety without eating, to create death without decapitation, to continue life after decapitation." (§1)

The philosophers' denial of prophetic miracles rests on their commitment to necessary causation. Once that commitment is dismantled, the miracles are not impossible but merely unusual departures from divine habit.

## Scriptural Examples Cited by Ghazālī

**Abraham and the fire** (*Qurʾān* 21:69): The paradigmatic miracle in the Seventeenth Discussion. If two similar pieces of cotton both burn when placed in fire, how can Abraham be cast into fire and remain unburned while the fire remains fire? Ghazālī addresses this directly in §§9–19. The philosophers hold that the only way to explain this is either to remove heat from the fire (making it no longer fire) or to change Abraham's body into stone — both of which they deem impossible. Ghazālī offers two alternative approaches (see below).

**The staff of Moses changing into a serpent** (*Qurʾān* 7:107 etc.): Cited at §19 as explicable on the principle that "matter is receptive of all things" — earth changes into plants, plants into blood, blood into sperm, sperm into an animal through a developmental process that God can compress into a shorter time:

> "Why, then, should the opponent deem it impossible that it lies within God's power to cycle matter through these stages in a time shorter than has been known?" (§19)

**Raising of the dead**: Cited alongside the staff at §19 on the same principle of accelerated material transformation.

## Two Approaches to the Miracle's Metaphysical Mechanism

The Seventeenth Discussion offers two distinct approaches (§10) to explaining how miracles occur; both are consistent with occasionalism, but they differ in what they concede to the philosophers:

### First Approach (§11)
Deny that the cosmic principles (whether God directly or the intermediary "bestower of forms") act by necessity rather than by will. If God voluntarily creates the burning each time fire and cotton meet, He is equally free not to create it. The miracle requires no additional explanation beyond God's choice:

> "If, then, it is established that the Agent creates the burning through His will when the piece of cotton comes into contact with the fire, it becomes rationally possible [for God] not to create [the burning] with the existence of the contact." (§11)

### Second Approach (§18)
Concede, for argument's sake, that fire habitually burns similar pieces of cotton identically. Nevertheless, God or His angels can introduce a special *quality* into either the fire or the prophet's body that prevents the normal transfer of heat:

> "there would come about either from God or from the angels a quality in the fire which restricts its heat to its own body so as not to transcend it … or else there will occur in the body of the prophet a quality which will not change him from being flesh and bone [but] which will resist the influence of the fire." (§18)

The analogy given: a person who covers himself with talc can sit in a fiery furnace unharmed; someone who has never witnessed talc's properties would call this impossible. Ghazālī's point is that our ignorance of the full range of natural properties and divine powers does not entitle us to rule out such effects as impossible:

> "Among the objects lying within God's power there are strange and wondrous things, not all of which we have seen. Why, then, should we deny their possibility and judge them to be impossible?" (§18)

## The Role of Prophetic Attention and Divine Order

At §§20–22, Ghazālī addresses whether miracles proceed from the prophet's soul or from a separate divine principle. His answer ties prophetic miracle to the *order of the good*:

> "The time meriting its appearance … is when the prophet's attention is wholly directed to it and the order of the good becomes specifically [dependent] on its appearance so that the order of the revealed law may endure … But it does not emanate from Him except when the need for its existence becomes preponderant and the order of the good becomes specified therein. And the order of the good becomes specified therein only if a prophet needs it to prove his prophethood in order to spread the good." (§21)

This grounds the *muʿjiza* in divine providence rather than in the prophet's personal power: God (or His angels) is the true agent; the prophet's concentrated attention is the occasion; the purpose is authentication of the prophetic mission and the establishment of the revealed law (*sharīʿa*).

---

## Habitual Knowledge and the Epistemology of Miracle

Since observers have reliable knowledge of God's habitual causal sequences, miracles are recognizable *as* departures from the norm. God both creates the unusual event and simultaneously removes from the hearts of observers the habitual expectation of the contrary:

> "If, then, God disrupts the habitual [course of nature] by making [the miracle] occur at the time in which disruptions of habitual [events] take place, these cognitions … slip away from [people's] hearts, and [God] does not create them." (§17)

This explains why miracle functions as *evidence* for prophethood: it is recognizably extraordinary against the backdrop of God's consistent practice, and it occurs specifically at the moment the prophet requires authentication.

---

## Relationship to the Charge of Apostasy

Al-Ghazālī does not treat the philosophers' denial of prophetic miracles (via their commitment to necessary causation) as *kufr* in the Seventeenth Discussion. The three discussions in the *Tahāfut* where he levels that charge concern the eternity of the world, God's ignorance of particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection — not causation. The philosophers' error on causality is serious but is treated as a philosophical mistake (shared in different form with the Mu'tazila), not as apostasy. This is an important hermeneutical nuance for reading [[asharism|Ash'arite]] polemic against [[falsafa|falsafa]].
