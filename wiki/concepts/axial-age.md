# The Axial Age

The **Axial Age** (German *Achsenzeit*, "axis time") is **Karl Jaspers**'s name (1949) for
the period of roughly **800–200 BCE** during which, independently and in several
civilizations at once, a decisive transformation in human religious and philosophical
consciousness took place. [[john-hick|John Hick]] makes it the historical pivot of his
account of religion: it marks the shift from **archaic/cosmic religion** to the **religions
of salvation/liberation** that pluralism treats as comparably valid responses to
[[the-real|the Real]].

## The Simultaneous Breakthrough

Jaspers observed that in this band of time, in mutual isolation, the foundational figures
and movements of the enduring traditions appeared:

- **China** — Confucius and Laozi; the foundations of [[confucianism|Confucianism]] and
  [[daoism|Daoism]].
- **India** — the Upanishads and the Buddha; the turn from Vedic ritual to *moksha*-oriented
  [[hinduism|Vedanta]] and [[buddhism|Buddhism]].
- **Iran** — Zarathustra and the ethical dualism of [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]
  (dating debated, but often placed in this orbit).
- **Israel** — the classical prophets (Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah) and the ethical
  monotheism of post-exilic [[judaism|Judaism]].
- **Greece** — the tragedians and the philosophers (Socrates, Plato); rational inquiry into
  cosmos and the good.

## Pre-Axial vs. Post-Axial Religion (Hick)

Hick uses the periodization to define what religion *became*:

- **Pre-axial religion** is principally **cosmic maintenance** — ritual that keeps society
  and the natural order in harmony, validates the existing structure of life, and offers no
  radical critique of the human condition. (Much of [[mesopotamian-religion|Mesopotamian]],
  [[egyptian-religion|Egyptian]], early Vedic, and archaic Greek/Roman religion fits here.)
- **Post-axial religion** is **soteriological** — it diagnoses ordinary existence as
  somehow fallen, deluded, or unsatisfactory, and offers a *limitlessly better* state
  (salvation, liberation, *moksha*, *nirvana*, the Kingdom). Its keynote is the
  transformation **from self-centredness to Reality-centredness** that Hick makes the
  cross-tradition criterion of authentic religion (see [[religious-pluralism]]).

Hick stresses the qualifications: the axial age is a "fluid medium," not a clean break; it
was prepared by earlier movements and is everywhere mixed with surviving pre-axial
elements. Later developments — **Jesus and Christianity, Muhammad and Islam, Mahayana
Buddhism** — are read as continuations *within* the axial streams rather than fresh axes.
Judaism's roots (Abraham, the Exodus) predate the axis, but its distinctive understanding
of God was largely formed by the axial-age prophets and redactors.

## "Cosmic Optimism"

A further Hickian theme: post-axial traditions share a **cosmic optimism** — the conviction
that reality is ultimately to be trusted, that the universe is "to be welcomed" rather than
feared, whether expressed eschatologically (the Semitic faiths' future consummation) or in
"realised" form (the mystic's present liberation). This shared optimism is part of what
lets Hick treat the traditions as parallel responses to one benign Real.

## Reception and Critique

The axial-age thesis has been enormously influential (Eisenstadt, Bellah,
[[karen-armstrong|Karen Armstrong]]'s *The Great Transformation*) and persistently
criticized: the dates are elastic, the "simultaneity"
is loose, Egypt and Mesopotamia are arguably excluded unfairly, and the very category may
project a modern (and Western) valuation of interiority and ethical universalism back onto
diverse developments. It remains a useful heuristic rather than a settled historical fact.
