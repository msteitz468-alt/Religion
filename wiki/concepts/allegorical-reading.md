# Allegorical Reading

**Allegorical reading** is the interpretive practice of taking the surface narrative or
wording of a text as a coded sign of a second, "deeper" meaning — moral, philosophical,
spiritual, or mystical — that the literal sense conceals and discloses at once. It is the
hermeneutical move shared, under different names and warrants, across the Greco-Roman,
Jewish, Christian, and Islamic exegetical traditions. This page treats allegory as a
*cross-tradition family*; the tradition-specific instances (**Remez**, **theōria /
allegoria**, **ta'wil / bāṭin**) are aliased and described below.

Per the wiki's naming convention, "allegorical reading" is the canonical English title
covering Remez, Theoria, and Ta'wil as tradition-specific cases. For the Jewish fourfold
scheme see [[pardes|PaRDeS]]; for the Christian fourfold scheme see
[[fourfold-sense|Fourfold Sense (Quadriga)]].

---

## Definition and the Allegory / Typology Distinction

Allegory in the strict sense treats the letter as a *symbol* of an idea or reality on a
different ontological plane — the events need not have happened, or their happening is
incidental to the meaning. This distinguishes it from [[typology|typology]], which holds
that real historical persons and events (Adam, the Exodus, the bronze serpent)
*prefigure* later real persons and events within a single line of salvation history.
Typology preserves the historicity and the horizontal, time-bound link; allegory tends to
read vertically, from letter to timeless meaning. In practice the two blur — patristic
exegetes used both, and "allegory" is often used loosely for any non-literal reading.

The Greek root *allēgoria* ("speaking otherwise") enters exegesis from grammatical and
philosophical interpretation of Homer, where Stoic and Middle-Platonic readers rescued the
scandalous myths of the gods by reading them as physics or ethics. This pagan technique
was the direct methodological ancestor of scriptural allegory.

## Tradition-Specific Instances

| Tradition | Term | Sense disclosed | Exemplary practitioner |
|-----------|------|-----------------|------------------------|
| Hellenistic Judaism | *allēgoria* | philosophical/psychological truth | [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] |
| Rabbinic/Kabbalistic Judaism | **Remez** (רֶמֶז, "hint") | philosophical allusion; in Kabbalah, the gateway toward Sod | Maimonidean rationalists |
| Christianity | **theōria / allegoria** | Christ and the Church beneath the Old Testament | [[origen|Origen]], [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement]] |
| Islam | **ta'wil**, the **bāṭin** (esoteric) sense | the inner reality beneath the *ẓāhir* | Sufism and Shi'ism |

### Jewish — Philo and Remez
[[philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]] is the founding figure of sustained scriptural
allegory: the patriarchs become stages of the soul's progress (Abraham = the soul's
migration toward virtue; Sarah = generic virtue), Eden the rational faculty, the migration
narratives the ascent of the mind. Within later Jewish interpretation, allegory is the
**Remez** ("hint") level of [[pardes|PaRDeS]] — the philosophical-allusive reading that the
Maimonidean rationalists used (e.g., reading the Garden narrative as the active intellect),
and which the kabbalists appropriated and redirected toward the mystical [[pardes|Sod]].

[[maimonides|Maimonides]]' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the Perplexed*]] is the most
systematic deployment of this register: Scripture's literal sense is "silver" and its inner sense
"gold" (Prov. 25:11; Guide Introduction), and the whole of Part I allegorizes the Torah's
[[anthropomorphism|anthropomorphic]] language away. His governing rule is *dibberah Torah
bi-leshon benei adam* ("the Law speaks in the language of man"). Critically, he **limits**
allegory: a literal text may be read figuratively *only* where reason has *demonstrated* the
literal sense impossible (so divine corporeality is allegorized, but the creation account is held
literal because [[eternity-of-the-world|eternity]] is undemonstrated — Guide II:25). This
disciplined, demonstration-gated allegoresis parallels the Islamic *[[zahir-batin|batin]]* he
inherited from [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]] and [[avicenna|Avicenna]].

### Christian — Theoria and Allegoria
[[origen|Origen]], building on [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] and
[[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]], systematized a threefold sense (somatic /
psychic / pneumatic — body, soul, spirit) corresponding to the Pauline body–soul–spirit
schema, with the "spiritual" sense as the true goal of reading. Paul's own appeal to
allegory (Gal 4:24, "these things are *allēgoroumena*" — Hagar and Sarah as two covenants)
gave the method apostolic warrant. The Antiochene exegetes (Theodore of Mopsuestia,
Diodore) pushed back, preferring *theōria* — a disciplined contemplation anchored in the
historical sense — against what they saw as Alexandrian excess that dissolved the letter.
This tension was eventually domesticated into the [[fourfold-sense|fourfold sense]], where
allegory becomes one regulated sense among four.

### Islamic — Ta'wil and the Batin
The exoteric (*ẓāhir*) / esoteric (*bāṭin*) distinction underwrites *ta'wil*, the
esoteric interpretation practiced in Sufism and especially in Shi'ism (where the Imams are
the authoritative interpreters of the inner sense — foundationally the sixth Imam
[[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]]). The Sufi reading the inner meaning of
the Qur'an is the structural counterpart of the kabbalist reading [[pardes|Sod]]. See
[[tafsir|Tafsir]] for the parallel exoteric, tradition-based commentary against which
*ta'wil* is defined, and the *bi'l-ra'y* (reason-based) vs. *bi'l-ma'thur*
(tradition-based) divide. [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]] (and the [[fusus-al-hikam|Fusus al-Hikam]]) is the
towering figure of esoteric, mystical Qur'anic reading. A distinct but related use of *ta'wil* is the
**rationalist** one: the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] and [[al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]] read the
anthropomorphic verses figuratively not to disclose a mystical *batin* but to protect God's
incomparability ([[tanzih-tashbih|tanzih]]) — allegory in the service of reason rather than gnosis.

## The Recurring Controversy: Letter vs. Spirit

Every tradition that develops allegory also develops a critique of it — the worry that the
spiritual sense floats free of the text and licenses the interpreter to find anything. The
medieval Jewish *peshat* revolution (Rashi, Rashbam, Ibn Ezra) recovered the plain sense
against midrashic and allegorical dominance; the Antiochenes checked Alexandrian allegory;
the Reformers' *sensus literalis* polemic and, later, the historical-critical method, were
in part reactions against the perceived arbitrariness of the fourfold spiritual senses.
The standing question — *what disciplines allegory and keeps it from arbitrariness?* — is
answered differently in each tradition: by the rule of faith and ecclesial consensus
(Christianity), by the chain of tradition and the authority of the interpreter
(rabbinic Derash; the Shi'i Imams), or by the claim that the text itself signals its
deeper sense (the Zohar's "beautiful maiden").
