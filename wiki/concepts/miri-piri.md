## Definition

*Miri-piri* (from Persian *mīr*, "prince/temporal lord," and *pīr*, "spiritual guide") designates the doctrine of the Guru's dual authority — temporal (*miri*) and spiritual (*piri*) — symbolized by two swords worn simultaneously. The doctrine holds that the Sikh Guru is both a temporal sovereign and a spiritual preceptor, with neither domain subordinate to the other.

## Origin under Guru Hargobind

The doctrine is associated with [[guru-hargobind|Guru Hargobind]], the sixth Guru. McLeod describes the shift:

> "Under the sixth Guru, Hargobind, the Panth radically shifted direction. The Mughal rulers were proving hostile and in order to protect itself the Panth took up arms under the direct leadership of the Guru." (McLeod ~L239–241)

McLeod characterizes this shift as historically explicable rather than theologically unmotivated: the influx of Jat converts — "overwhelmingly from the villages" — whose commitment was "a matter of loyalty to the leader rather than deep awareness of the teachings of the divine Name," combined with "disturbed circumstances in the Punjab," created the conditions in which armed self-defence became necessary (McLeod ~L28–38).

## The Theological Problem

Miri-piri creates an apparent tension with Guru Nanak's foundational teaching that external features and worldly engagement "must necessarily stand squarely in the way of liberation through the divine Name" (McLeod ~L898–901). How can a Guru who bears arms and holds temporal authority continue the interior, *nam simaran*-centred spirituality of Nanak?

## The Sikh Resolution

McLeod records the standard Sikh theological answer:

> "The theological answer is the same one as is given for the introduction of changes by Guru Amar Das or of worldly activities by Guru Hargobind. The answer is that there is only one Guru, successively inhabiting ten human bodies, and what Gobind Singh decreed at the end of the seventeenth century was what Nanak would have decreed in the same circumstances more than 150 years earlier." (McLeod ~L907–919)

This "one Guru in ten bodies" doctrine makes each Guru's innovations expressions of what the eternal Guru would always have taught given the same historical circumstances.

## Development under Guru Gobind Singh

The miri-piri principle reached its institutional apex with [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]]'s founding of the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] in 1699. The Khalsa was explicitly conceived as a fighting order whose members were to be the "chosen messengers of Akal Purakh" against Mughal power (McLeod ~L810–812). The Five Ks (*panj kakke*) gave miri-piri tangible, visible form: the *kirpan* (sword) is one of the five obligatory articles and encodes the temporal-martial dimension within the most intimate sphere of the Khalsa Sikh's body.

## Continuing Significance

After Guru Gobind Singh's death the corporate Panth — particularly the Khalsa — maintained miri-piri through the institution of the *gurmata* (will of the Guru): decisions of the assembled *Sarbat Khalsa* held "in the presence of both sacred scriptures" (Adi Granth and Dasam Granth) were binding on all members, making communal governance a form of the Guru's temporal authority (McLeod ~L1662–1673). Akal Takhat in Amritsar served as the biannual seat of this dual authority.
