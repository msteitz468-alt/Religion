# The Two Truths

The **doctrine of two truths** distinguishes **conventional truth** (*saṃvṛti-satya*, also
*vyavahāra*) from **ultimate truth** (*paramārtha-satya*). It is shared across Buddhist scholasticism
but **redefined** at each stage; in [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] it becomes the indispensable key to
emptiness. "The Dharma teaching of the Buddha rests on two truths" ([[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]]
24.8).

## The Abhidharma Version

For [[abhidharma|Abhidharma]]:
- A statement is **conventionally true** if acting on it reliably leads to successful practice — our
  commonsense beliefs about persons, chariots, towns.
- A statement is **ultimately true** if it corresponds to reality without asserting or presupposing
  any **conceptual fiction** — i.e., if it speaks only of the simple, real **dharmas** (which have
  [[svabhava|intrinsic nature]]). "No statement about a chariot could be ultimately true (or false)."

On this picture the conventional is wholly *reducible* to the ultimate: all facts about chariots and
people can in principle be restated as facts about dharmas.

## The Madhyamaka Redefinition

[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]] keeps the two truths but empties the ultimate. Since **nothing** has
[[svabhava|svabhāva]], what Abhidharma called "ultimately true" (statements about dharmas) turns out
to be **merely conventional** too. Candrakīrti gives three etymologies of *saṃvṛti* — "concealing,"
"mutual dependence," and "worldly convention" — and the ultimate truth becomes one of two things
(MMK 24, Akutobhayā):

1. that ultimate reality contains **no arising entities whatsoever** (it is utterly empty); or, more
   radically,
2. that **there is no such thing as the way reality ultimately is** — "the ultimate truth is that
   there is no ultimate truth," the very idea of a mind-independent ultimate nature being incoherent.

Two consequences are central to Madhyamaka:

- **The ultimate cannot be taught apart from the conventional** (MMK 24.10): "without relying on
  customary usage, the ultimate is not taught; without realizing the ultimate, nirvāṇa is not
  attained." Conventional truth is "the cup the thirsty must use" (Candrakīrti).
- **"All is possible when emptiness is possible; nothing is possible when emptiness is impossible"**
  (MMK 24.14): only because things are empty (and so dependently arising) can suffering, the path,
  and liberation function at all. The charge that emptiness destroys the Dharma rests on confusing
  the two truths.

## Significance

The two truths answer the **nihilism objection** and the **self-refutation objection** (if "all is
empty," is *that* claim empty? — yes, but it is asserted only conventionally). They also frame
**saṃsāra and nirvāṇa**: ultimately indistinct (MMK 25.19), conventionally utterly different.
Misreading them is the snake wrongly grasped (MMK 24.11). Later Tibetan and East Asian Buddhism
build whole systems on graded versions of the distinction.
