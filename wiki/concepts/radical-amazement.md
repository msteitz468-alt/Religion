# Radical Amazement

**Radical amazement** is [[abraham-joshua-heschel|Abraham Joshua Heschel]]'s term (developed
in [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]], Part I) for **wonder that does not
cease when an explanation is given** — a permanent, irreducible astonishment at the sheer
fact that there are facts at all. It is the foundational stance of religious awareness in
Heschel's philosophy of [[judaism|Judaism]].

## Definition and Distinctions

For Plato and Aristotle wonder is the *beginning* of philosophy — a spur to knowledge that
ceases once the explanation arrives. Heschel inverts this: "To the prophets wonder is a
form of thinking … it is not the beginning of knowledge but an act that goes beyond
knowledge; it does not come to an end when knowledge is acquired." Radical amazement is
thus distinct from mere curiosity, which is satisfied by information.

Its scope is total. Where ordinary perception or cognition fastens on "a selected segment
of reality," radical amazement "refers to all of reality; not only to what we see, but
also to the very act of seeing as well as to our own selves." The decisive object of
amazement is not the relations between things but "the fact that even the minimum of
perception is a maximum of enigma" — "the most incomprehensible fact is the fact that we
comprehend at all."

## As Civilizational Diagnosis

Heschel frames the decline of wonder as a crisis: "As civilization advances, the sense of
wonder declines … Mankind will not perish for want of information; but only for want of
appreciation … What we lack is not a will to believe but a will to wonder." Jewish
liturgy, on his reading, is built to keep wonder alive — the daily blessings train one "to
experience commonplace deeds as spiritual adventures." He cites the rabbinic claim that
"the earning of bread is a greater wonder than the division of the Red Sea" (Pesahim 118a).

## Relation to Other Concepts

Radical amazement is the seed from which [[awe]], the sense of [[the-ineffable]], and
ultimately faith grow: in Heschel's chain, "the beginning of awe is wonder." It is part of
his broader [[depth-theology]] method, which probes the pre-conceptual acts out of which
belief arises rather than the propositional content of creeds. It also grounds his critique
of natural theology: "We are in greater need of a proof for the authenticity of faith than
of a proof for the existence of God."
