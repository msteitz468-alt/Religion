# Divorce

Divorce is the subject of the third of the [[antitheses|antitheses]] at [[matthew|Matthew]]
5:31-32 and of the fuller controversy at 19:3-12. This page records the treatment in
[[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the wiki's only ingested
source on the passage.

A note on location: although 19:3-12 is the fuller passage, France's editorial decision is to
give his main discussion at 5:31-32 — "it seems better to deal with the issue more generally
here" — asking the reader of 19:3-12 to refer back. The substance below therefore comes from
his comment on the antithesis.

## The Old Testament Basis

France stresses that Deut 24:1-4, the only pentateuchal passage directly on divorce, "was not
concerned with the rightness or wrongness of divorce in itself, nor with permissible causes of
divorce, but only with the aftermath of a divorce which is assumed to have taken place." He
notes that the "quotation" at 5:31 "is not in fact a direct quotation of the Deuteronomy text
but an inference from it" — Deut 24:1 does not instruct the husband to provide a certificate but
presupposes one.

France flags a gap in the OT itself: the assumption that husbands have the right to divorce, and
the basis of the certificate, "is not spelled out either here or anywhere else in the OT" —
though at 19:7 Jesus' questioners take Moses to have *commanded* both. See [[old-law]],
[[moses]].

Divorce was a male prerogative requiring no legal hearing, merely the husband's decision;
France reports (citing Josephus, *Ant.* 15.259) that "Jewish law made no provision for a woman
to initiate divorce," and that a whole Mishnah tractate, *Giṭṭin*, covers the valid certificate.

### Malachi 2:16 — "I hate divorce" (Verhoef, NICOT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[pieter-verhoef|Verhoef]], [[verhoef-haggai-malachi|*The Books of
Haggai and Malachi* (NICOT)]], on [[malachi|Mal.]] 2:10–16 — the OT's most explicit
condemnation of divorce.*

Verhoef calls 2:10–16 "the most difficult pericope in the book," with v. 15a
"grammatically and syntactically incomplete." His key readings:

- **Marriage as a covenant witnessed by God** (2:14). God "acted as witness between you
  and the wife of your youth"; the marriage is a "covenant of God," with God as witness,
  guarantor, and — for the faithless — prosecutor.
- **2:16 — God as subject.** Against the MT's third-person pointing ("he hated") and
  against the LXX/Targum conditional reading ("if one divorces out of hate," which for
  Verhoef "undermines all that the prophet is seeking to convey"), Verhoef (with Rudolph)
  repoints to a **participle with suppressed pronoun: "I hate, I am hating"** — God the
  continual, habitual hater of divorce (*šallaḥ*, "sending away").
- **The elevated view of marriage.** "Nowhere else in the OT is there such an elevated
  view of marriage as in Mal. 2:10–16; nowhere else is divorce condemned in such explicit
  terms." The OT elsewhere *permitted* divorce (Deut. 24:1–4); Malachi provides "the
  ultimate in the OT revelation." Verhoef (citing Koole) notes that in [[matthew|Matt.]]
  19 Christ appealed to Gen. 2:24, but "Christ's answer is already stated in Mal. 2:14–16:
  I hate divorce" — anticipating the NT position (with the exceptions of 1 Cor. 7:15 and
  Matt. 19:9).
- **The v. 15 crux.** Verhoef reads "residue of spirit" as intelligence/sound judgment:
  the person seeking "godly offspring" for the kingdom of God has spiritual insight and
  will not violate marriage as a divine institution.

## The Rabbinic Dispute: Grounds, Not Legitimacy

France states that the rabbinic debate was over "not the legitimacy of divorce in itself, which
everyone seems to have taken for granted," but its permissible grounds, arising from Deut 24:1's
"something shameful" (*ʿerwat-dābār*) and 24:3's "disliked" (*śānāʾ*). See [[oral-torah]].

Reporting m. Giṭ. 9:10, France records the spectrum of positions:

- **In Shammaite interpretation**, only "unchastity" warranted divorce — the hardline position.
- **In Hillelite interpretation**, divorce was permitted for something as trivial as spoiling a
  meal.
- **R. Akiba** allowed it simply because the husband preferred someone else.

France holds that "in practice it seems clear that the Hillelite position prevailed among most
Jews," citing Josephus's *Life* 426-27 ("I sent away my wife, being displeased with her
behavior") as typical, his aside at *Ant.* 4.253 ("for whatever cause"), and Sir 25:26.

## France's Core Reading: Jesus Queries Divorce Itself

France's central argument is that Jesus does not enter the grounds debate at all. Jesus raises
the issue "not to discuss the grounds ... but in order to query the assumption that any divorce
could be acceptable in the first place," sweeping aside the trouble-shooting provisions "with
the assertion that the original divorce itself was not permissible." France diagnoses the
quarrel as one about method: current ethical teaching "is basing its standards on an assumption
of failure" — Moses' provision only for "your hardheartedness" (19:8) — "rather than on God's
original purpose for marriage."

His verdict on Deut 24:1-4 is that Jesus' teaching "ideally makes it obsolete," and that "in
opposing current divorce legislation Jesus is rescuing Deut 24:1-4 from misuse for a purpose for
which it was never intended" — trouble-shooting, not a positive ethical basis.

This fits France's broader taxonomy of the antitheses: in the third and fourth (divorce, oaths)
"Jesus declares that the actions which the OT law presupposes and for which it provides
regulation should never have occurred in the first place." Asked whether that abolishes those
regulations, France answers: "it is rather to bypass them, to leave them behind as no longer
needed." He allows a residual practical role — "in the real world it may be that there will
still be divorce, untruthfulness, and judicial retribution, and for these purposes the
regulations of the OT law may retain a practical role. But insofar as they are needed, that is a
mark of the failure of the more radical ethic which Jesus here sets out." See [[fulfillment]].

## The Exception Clause

France states the problem squarely: Mark 10:2-12, Luke 16:18, and 1 Cor 7:10-11 are "clear-cut;
divorce is simply forbidden," while "Matthew, both here and in 19:9, apparently blurs the stark
opposition" by inserting *parektos logou porneias* (19:9: *mē epi porneia*). See
[[synoptic-problem]].

He defines *porneia*: strictly relations with a prostitute ("fornication"), but wider in usage;
applied to a married woman it most likely means adultery or the discovery of premarital
intercourse with another, "or more likely to either or both" (citing Sir 23:23; Hermas *Mand.*
4.1.5). He holds that the phrase *logos porneias* "is probably intended to recall the
*ʿerwat-dābār* of Deut 24:1," which was also the basis of the Shammaite position — concluding
that "Matthew's Jesus thus appears to hold a Shammaite position — though a particularly strict
one," since *porneia* is more clearly limited to actual sexual misconduct than the wider range
of immodest acts some Shammaites included.

### Positions on the clause

**Position 1 — pragmatic adaptation (reported, then challenged).** France states the view he
will oppose: "It is often assumed that this represents the beginning of a process of pragmatic
adaptation by a church which found Jesus' absolute ethic unworkable in practice," ending in a
modern liberalism functionally equivalent to the Hillelite position.

**Position 2 — France's proposal: the marriage was already dissolved.** France argues that
adultery sets up a rival "one-flesh" union which in Jewish thought so violated the original that
continuation was unthinkable and the marriage "was officially dissolved" (m. Yebam. 2:8; Soṭah
5:1; Ketub. 3:5). Ending such a marriage was therefore "not so much 'divorce' ... as the
necessary recognition that the original marriage no longer existed." On that view, "Matthew is
merely making explicit what was assumed by Mark and Luke to be already obvious to their
readers": Mark and Luke forbid the voluntary breaking of a marriage hitherto intact, assuming
*porneia* had already destroyed it.

**France offers this as an argument, not a certainty** — he presents it in the form "it can be
argued," and the hedge should not be dropped. He supports it by appeal to Joseph at 1:18-19,
whose dilemma "was not over whether to repudiate Mary or not, but only over whether this duty
should be carried out publicly or privately" — the betrothal's validity was not in question
until the angel intervened.

On "makes her the victim of adultery" France offers two compatible senses — either the husband's
repudiation of an intact marriage is itself equated with adultery, or her subsequent remarriage
places her in an adulterous relationship "since the original marriage remains valid in the sight
of God" — with a third nuance, that of branding her unjustly as an adulteress.

## Divorce Without Remarriage: Rejected

France firmly rejects the modern reading that Jesus permitted divorce after adultery but forbade
remarriage. It "does not fit the Jewish context," where the certificate explicitly granted
remarriage (m. Giṭ. 9:3: "You are free to marry any man"); without that permission "it was not
divorce," and "divorce and the right to remarry are thus inseparable." He adds that "the Jewish
world knew nothing of a legal separation which did not allow remarriage," and that there is
nothing in Jesus' words in Matthew, Mark, or Luke suggesting he meant to create one.

## Coverage and Gaps

Coverage rests on two Christian commentaries (France on Matthew; Verhoef on Malachi's OT
condemnation of divorce). Patristic, canon-law, Reformation, and modern
denominational treatments of divorce and remarriage are unrepresented, as is Jewish *gittin*
law in its own voice and Islamic *ṭalāq*.
