# Traducianism and Creationism (Origin of the Soul)

The patristic debate over how the individual soul comes to be — a question entangled with [[original-sin|original sin]], since the mode of the soul's origin governs how Adam's sin could be transmitted. Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. XIII.

## The Three Theories

- **Creationism** (the prevalent Greek view): each soul is "created independently by God at the moment of its infusion into the body." Held by Hilary, Ambrose, Jerome, and — consistently with his denial of inherited guilt — [[pelagius|Pelagius]].
- **Pre-existence** (the Origenist view): the soul pre-existed the body "as a penalty for its sins"; held by Didymus and Priscillianists, and formally condemned in the sixth century.
- **Traducianism** (associated with [[tertullian]]): each soul is generated from the parents' souls along with the body — "every soul is a twig cut from the parent-stem of Adam." [[augustine|Augustine]] leaned this way "with many hesitations" because it "fitted in best with his teaching about [[original-sin|original sin]]," but he confessed himself permanently baffled by the question.

The choice is consequential: creationism makes the transmission of [[original-sin|original sin]] hard to explain (Pelagius pressed exactly this), whereas traducianism supplies a natural vehicle for it.
