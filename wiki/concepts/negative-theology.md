Negative theology (*via negativa*; in Hebrew, sometimes expressed as *shillul ha-middot*, negation of attributes) is the doctrine that God can be known — and must be described — only by negating what He is not, never by affirming positive essential properties. The positive attributes one might affirm of God either (a) denote qualities of His actions, not His essence, or (b) tacitly reduce to negations of their opposites. The fullest treatment of this doctrine in the Jewish philosophical tradition appears in Maimonides' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]], Part I, Chapters LVII–LX.

---

## Maimonides' Doctrine

### The Metaphysical Foundation

Maimonides grounds the via negativa in a metaphysical claim about divine simplicity. Because God's existence is not due to any cause, "existence and essence are perfectly identical; He is not a substance to which existence is joined as an accident, as an additional element" (Ch. LVII). All positive attributes would introduce plurality into what is absolutely simple: they would imply a subject with properties distinguishable from it. Therefore:

> "God exists without possessing the attribute of existence. Similarly He lives, without possessing the attribute of life; knows, without possessing the attribute of knowledge; is omnipotent without possessing the attribute of omnipotence; is wise, without possessing the attribute of wisdom: all this reduces itself to one and the same entity; there is no plurality in Him." (Ch. LVII)

The same logic applies to divine unity itself: "God's unity is not an element superadded, but He is One without possessing the attribute of unity" (Ch. LVII).

### The Two Classes of Attributes

Maimonides identifies exactly two admissible classes of divine description:

1. **Attributes of action** (*middot ha-pe'ulah*): Descriptions derived from the effects God produces in the world — that He is merciful, wise, powerful — understood as referring to His works, not to properties inhering in His essence. These are permissible when used in prayer or Scripture because they are indirect: they describe what emanates from God, not what He is.

2. **Negative attributes**: Statements that exclude what God is not. "It is living, expressing thereby that it is not dead. We call such a being incorporeal, because we notice that it is unlike the heavens… we say it is the first, expressing thereby that its existence is not due to any cause." (Ch. LVIII). When we say God has power, wisdom, and will, "we mean that it is not feeble or ignorant, or hasty, and does not abandon its creatures" (Ch. LVIII).

### Negation as Epistemic Progress

Maimonides argues that degrees of knowledge of God correspond to the *number of things successfully negated* by proof:

> "by each additional negative attribute you advance toward the knowledge of God, and you are nearer to it than he who does not negative, in reference to God, those qualities which you are convinced by proof must be negatived." (Ch. LIX)

He illustrates this with the "ship" parable (Ch. LX): ten persons approach the concept of a ship, each adding one more negation — not an accident, not a mineral, not a plant, not a body joined by nature, not flat, not spherical, not pointed, not round, not solid. The tenth person, by successive negation, arrives nearly at the correct concept without any positive definition. "In the same manner you will come nearer to the knowledge and comprehension of God by the negative attributes."

Conversely, every positive assertion is an epistemic step backward: "every time you establish by proof the negation of a thing in reference to God, you become more perfect, while with every additional positive assertion you follow your imagination and recede from the true knowledge of God." (Ch. LIX)

### The Danger of Positive Predication

Affirming positive attributes does not merely fall short — Maimonides argues it amounts to a loss of belief in God altogether:

> "I do not merely declare that he who affirms attributes of God has not sufficient knowledge concerning the Creator, admits some association with God, or conceives Him to be different from what He is: but I say that he unconsciously loses his belief in God." (Ch. LX)

If a being with truly simple, absolute existence were said to have attributes combined with extraneous elements, "it would in no way be an existing thing," and "when we say that that essence, which is called 'God,' is a substance with many properties by which it can be described, we apply that name to an object which does not at all exist." (Ch. LX)

### The Limit of Knowledge

The via negativa yields no positive knowledge of the divine essence — only approach by exclusion. Even this approach has a ceiling:

> "All we understand is the fact that He exists, that He is a Being to whom none of His creatures is similar, who has nothing in common with them, who does not include plurality…" and even this resemblance to a steersman governing a boat "is not a real relation, a real simile, but serves only to convey to us the idea that God rules the universe." (Ch. LVIII)

The epistemological conclusion is cited from Psalms: "'Silence is praise to Thee' (Ps. 65:2). It is a very expressive remark on this subject: for whatever we utter with the intention of extolling and of praising Him, contains something that cannot be applied to God, and includes derogatory expressions: it is therefore more becoming to be silent, and to be content with intellectual reflection." (Ch. LIX)

### Liturgical Application

The doctrine has direct liturgical consequences. Maimonides invokes the Talmudic story of Rabbi Haninah, who rebuked a prayer-leader for multiplying divine epithets: "There was once an earthly king, possessing millions of gold coin; he was praised for owning millions of silver coin: was this not really dispraise to him?" (Ch. LIX). The only authorized departure from silence is the three epithets Moses used and which the Men of the Great Synagogue introduced into prayer; all further elaboration is, at minimum, theologically confused, and at worst "real heresy" or "profanity and blasphemy" (Ch. LIX).

### God as Intellectus, Intelligens, Intelligibile (Ch. LXVIII)

One aspect of the via negativa connects to Maimonides' adoption of the Aristotelian doctrine that intellect in action is identical with its object. God is pure intellect always in act, never in potentiality: therefore "He and the things comprehended are one and the same thing, that is to say, His essence." The triad *intellectus* / *intelligens* / *intelligibile* collapses into one in God — which coheres with the denial of all positive plurality. This is not a positive description of what God is but a demonstration that the distinctions we would normally draw between knower, act, and known cannot be applied to God.
