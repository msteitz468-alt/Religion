# Apostolic Succession

The doctrine that the unbroken line of bishops descending from the apostles guarantees the identity of the Church's present teaching with the original apostolic revelation. In its formative second-century use it was primarily an *epistemic* guarantee — a way of certifying which teaching is authentically apostolic against Gnostic claims to secret tradition — before it hardened into a doctrine of sacramental and jurisdictional legitimacy. Drawn primarily from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. II.

## Primitive Hints

- **[[clement-of-rome|Clement of Rome]]** (*1 Clem.* 42) seems to imply that the hierarchy succeeding the apostles inherited the gospel message they had been commissioned to preach.
- **[[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius]]** placed immense stress on loyalty to the [[monepiscopate|episcopate]] precisely because he regarded the bishop as "the appointed guarantor of purity of doctrine."
- *2 Clement* (17) inculcates obedience to the presbyters because their task is to preach the faith, and their instructions are "identical with those of Christ Himself."

## Irenaeus: The Classic Formulation

For **[[irenaeus]]**, the identity of the Church's oral tradition with the original revelation "is guaranteed by the unbroken succession of bishops in the great sees going back lineally to the apostles" (*Haer.* 3.2.2; 3.3.3; 3.4.1). The apostolic tradition is *public*, not secret — "visible in the Church for all who cared to look for it" — which is exactly the point against the Gnostics. The bishops are "Spirit-endowed men who have been vouchsafed an infallible charism of truth" (*charisma veritatis certum*, *Haer.* 4.26.2).

## Tertullian

**[[tertullian]]** made apostolic foundation the surest test of authentic doctrine: the churches that mattered "were founded by, and were continuously linked with, the apostles" (*De praescr.* 21, 32), with their "otherwise inexplicable unanimity" a further guarantee. He was emphatic "that no secret tradition existed" (*De praescr.* 28).

## Later Development

Kelly traces how the theory of apostolically founded sees "faded" through the third and fourth centuries, its place taken by "a growing consciousness of the magisterial authority of the Catholic Church." The [[romans|Roman]] church increasingly regarded itself as "in a special sense the appointed custodian and mouthpiece of the apostolic tradition." This consciousness later issued in the [[consensus-patrum|appeal to the consensus of the Fathers]] and, in [[vincent-of-lerins|Vincent of Lérins]], in a formal canon of catholic tradition.
