# Dogma (Barth)

## Definition

For Karl Barth, **dogma** is "the agreement of Church proclamation with the revelation attested in Holy Scripture" (*Church Dogmatics* I.1, §7, line 12175–76). This is a relational and eschatological concept, not a propositional one. It names the *correspondence* between the Church's speech and the Word of God — a correspondence that is never simply in hand, never demonstrable, and therefore always the object of inquiry rather than a settled possession.

## Three Levels: Dogma, Dogmas, Dogmatic Propositions

Barth draws a careful three-level distinction (lines 12289–12305):

- **Dogma** (in the primary sense): the agreement itself, never possessed by the Church; "not itself a proposition, not proclaimed by any church" (line 12291–92). It is "the epitome of all possible propositions of this kind" — the *essence* of which dogmas and dogmatic propositions are at best phenomena. Dogma is a *concept of relation*.
- **Dogmas**: the concrete doctrinal propositions the Church acknowledges and confesses in its symbols and creeds. These are venerable and normative; they *aim at* and *strive after* the truth of revelation but do not simply possess it. Quoting Isidore of Seville via Thomas Aquinas: *Articulus est perceptio divinae veritatis tendens in ipsam* — "An article is a perception of divine truth that inclines toward it" (line 12280).
- **Dogmatic propositions**: the outputs of the scientific work of dogmatics, aiming at dogmas and through them at dogma.

## Dogma as Eschatological Concept

Barth explicitly labels dogma an "eschatological concept" (line 12342). If dogma were actually achieved — if the agreement of Church proclamation with the Word of God were demonstrated — "dogmatics would be at an end along with the *ecclesia militans*, and the kingdom of God would have dawned" (lines 12334–38). The real results of dogmatics are therefore "new questions" (line 12329), not settled answers. This rules out any confident claim to *have* dogma; it remains the regulative goal of an inquiry that can never be complete before the eschaton.

## Dogma as Command, Not Merely Proposition

Barth argues that the biblical use of "dogma" (Hebrew and Greek Old Testament; Luke 2:1; Daniel 6; Ephesians 2:15; Colossians 2:14) originally meant a *decree* or *command* (lines 12183–85), and that the development of the term in the Church toward *doctrinal proposition* was a "false development" (line 12367). If revealed truth (*veritas revelata*) is a command rather than a proposition, it requires obedience — a decision — not merely contemplation. "A command is what it is only in the act of the one who gives it and the one who obeys or disobeys it" (lines 12497–98). Barth therefore insists the relation implicit in the concept of dogma must be understood "as the relation between command and obedience and only then and in this way as also the relation between doctrine and instruction" (lines 12536–38), following the Anselmian principle *credo ut intelligam* (line 12544).

## Contrast with Roman Catholic Dogma

Roman Catholic dogmatics defines dogma as *veritas a Deo formaliter revelata et ab Ecclesia . . . definita* (a truth formally revealed by God and officially defined by the Church — line 12206, citing Deneffe). For Barth, this equation of revealed truth with Church-defined propositions involves two errors:

1. It identifies the Word of God with the word of the Church. "The Word of God is above dogma as the heavens are above the earth" (lines 12229–30).
2. It treats revealed truth as a *neutral proposition* — something that can be "had" and "contemplated" without requiring decision or obedience. Barth argues this materialises and depersonalises revelation: "Can one have it in abstraction from the person of Him who reveals it and from the revelatory act of this person in which it is given to other persons to perceive?" (lines 12390–92).
