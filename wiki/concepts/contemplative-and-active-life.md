# The Contemplative and Active Life (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] closes the moral theology of the [[summa-theologica|*Summa*]]'s Secunda
Secundae (II-II Q179–182) with the division of human life into the **active** and the
**contemplative** — the framework within which he then treats the [[states-of-life|states of life]].

## The twofold division (Q179)

Human life is adequately divided into the **active** (ordered to external works and the service of the
neighbour) and the **contemplative** (ordered to the consideration of truth), figured by the
scriptural pairs **Martha and Mary** and **Leah and Rachel**. The division is taken from the
**intellect**, whose two operations — doing and contemplating — give the two lives their character.

## The contemplative life (Q180)

The contemplative life is **essentially in the intellect** but **motivated by the will/charity** —
one contemplates because one loves — and it **terminates in delight**, which in turn intensifies love
(Q180, A1, A7). The **moral virtues** belong to it not essentially but **dispositively**, by quieting
the passions that disturb contemplation. Its objects rise in four steps: the moral virtues →
preparatory acts → contemplation of God's **effects** → contemplation of the **divine truth itself**,
which is its summit. In this life the vision of God's essence is impossible **except in rapture**
(Paul's rapture being the highest degree of present-life contemplation; see [[prophecy]]).

## The active life (Q181)

The active life consists in the exterior works of the moral virtues directed to the neighbour;
teaching and preaching can belong either to the active or to the contemplative life depending on
whether they flow from interior contemplation.

## Which is greater? (Q182)

The **contemplative life is simply more excellent** than the active — Aquinas gives eight Aristotelian
reasons (it is proper to man's highest part, more continuous, more self-sufficient, loved for its own
sake, a foretaste of heavenly beatitude) capped by a ninth from Scripture: Mary "hath chosen the best
part, which shall not be taken away from her" (Lk. 10:42). It is also **greater in merit**, since it
rests on the love of God, the root of merit. Yet:

- The active life can be **more urgent** in a case of present necessity;
- The active life **both hinders and helps** contemplation — it hinders by its external occupation,
  but helps by **calming the passions** ("train in the camp of action before holding the fortress of
  contemplation," Gregory);
- In the order of *generation* the active life **precedes** the contemplative (it disposes to it),
  but by nature the contemplative is **prior as end**.

### The "mixed life"

Highest of all is the life that, out of the **fullness of contemplation**, overflows into the active
work of teaching and preaching — "**better to give to others the fruits of one's contemplation than
merely to contemplate**." Being called from contemplation to action is "not by subtraction but by
addition" (Augustine). This *contemplata aliis tradere* principle is the theological self-portrait of
the [[thomas-aquinas|Dominican]] vocation and grounds Aquinas's ranking of the teaching-preaching
orders at the summit of the [[states-of-life|religious life]].

## Cross-tradition note

The active/contemplative distinction and the praise of contemplation invite comparison with the
contemplative ideals of other traditions — the Buddhist [[samatha-vipassana|meditative path]], the
Hindu contrast of [[karma-yoga]] and *jnana*, and monastic contemplative traditions generally.
