# Use and Enjoyment (Uti/Frui)

## Definition

Augustine's foundational distinction in [[on-christian-doctrine]] between two modes of relating to things: *frui* (enjoyment, to enjoy) and *uti* (use, to use). The distinction is simultaneously ontological, soteriological, and hermeneutical — it structures the whole of Book I and grounds Augustine's account of how Scripture is to be read.

> "There are some things, then, which are to be enjoyed, others which are to be used, others still which enjoy and use." (Bk. I, ch. 3, line 348)

## Augustine's Account

### The Distinction Defined

**To enjoy** (*frui*) is to rest in something for its own sake, finding happiness complete in it:
> "For to enjoy a thing is to rest with satisfaction in it for its own sake." (Bk. I, ch. 4, line 357)

**To use** (*uti*) is to employ something as a means toward the proper end:
> "To use, on the other hand, is to employ whatever means are at one's disposal to obtain what one desires, if it is a proper object of desire; for an unlawful use ought rather to be called an abuse." (Bk. I, ch. 4, lines 358–359)

Things that are objects of enjoyment "make us happy." Things that are objects of use "assist, and (so to speak) support us in our efforts after happiness, so that we can attain the things that make us happy and rest in them" (Bk. I, ch. 3, lines 349–351).

### The Threefold Division of Things

Augustine divides all *res* (things) into three classes (Bk. I, ch. 3):
1. **Things to be enjoyed** — the Triune God alone
2. **Things to be used** — everything else, including the temporal dispensation for salvation, the created world, and other human beings (as means, not ends)
3. **Things which enjoy and use** — human beings ourselves, who are capable of both enjoying God and using other things

### The Traveler Analogy

Augustine's most memorable illustration: we are wanderers in a foreign country, wishing to return to our homeland (God). We must use conveyances — ships, carriages — to make the journey. If we become enchanted by the beauty of the journey itself and refuse to press on, we fail to reach home:
> "Such is a picture of our condition in this life of mortality. We have wandered far from God; and if we wish to return to our Father's home, this world must be used, not enjoyed." (Bk. I, ch. 4, lines 367–369)

### God Alone as the Object of Enjoyment (Bk. I, ch. 5)

The Triune God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — is the sole proper object of enjoyment. God is the supreme, unchangeable Good: "The true objects of enjoyment, then, are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, who are at the same time the Trinity, one Being, supreme above all" (Bk. I, ch. 5, lines 378–379).

Augustine also distinguishes God from all temporal goods on the axis of *changeability*: "a wise intelligence... was, previous to its attaining wisdom, unwise. But wisdom itself never was unwise" (Bk. I, ch. 8, lines 441–442). God alone is unchangeable wisdom, and what is unchangeable is properly to be rested in.

### Enjoyment of Human Beings and Neighbors

Human beings fall in the category of *things to be used*, not enjoyed — but with nuance. We are to love other persons, but not *in* them (resting in them as the ultimate end), but *in God*:
> "Now he is a man of just and holy life who forms an unprejudiced estimate of things, and keeps his affections also under strict control, so that he neither loves what he ought not to love, nor fails to love what he ought to love." (Bk. I, ch. 27, lines 754–756)

When you enjoy a person *in God*, it is God you truly enjoy: "But when you have joy of a man in God, it is God rather than man that you enjoy. For you enjoy Him by whom you are made happy, and you rejoice to have come to Him in whose presence you place your hope of joy" (Bk. I, ch. 33, lines 899–901). Augustine cites Paul's "Let me have joy of thee *in the Lord*" (Philemon 20) as the model.

### God's Use of Us (Bk. I, chs. 31–32)

God neither enjoys nor uses us as we use things. His use "has no reference to His own advantage, but to ours only; and, so far as He is concerned, has reference only to His goodness" (Bk. I, ch. 32, lines 874–876). God uses us not for gain but because He is good, and we exist and are good only insofar as He gives it to us.

### The Error of Enjoying What Should Be Used

The central moral/spiritual danger is *misuse* — treating temporal things as ultimate ends. Enjoyment of what should be used produces *cupiditas* (disordered love); proper use oriented toward God produces *caritas*. See [[charity-and-cupidity]].

## Hermeneutical Dimension

The uti/frui distinction is not only theological but interpretive. The whole temporal dispensation of Scripture and salvation is for *use*:
> "The whole temporal dispensation for our salvation, therefore, was framed by the providence of God that we might know this truth and be able to act upon it; and we ought to use that dispensation, not with such love and delight as if it were a good to rest in, but with a transient feeling rather, such as we have towards the road." (Bk. I, ch. 35, lines 954–959)

Even Christ's incarnate form is a *use*-object — a way and road — not a resting-place. The interpreter who mistakes the road for the destination has misread Scripture's function (Bk. I, ch. 34).
