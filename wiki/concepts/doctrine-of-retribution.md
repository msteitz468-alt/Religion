# Doctrine of Retribution

The **doctrine of retribution** is the conviction that God apportions prosperity and
adversity according to moral desert. In the form that the [[book-of-job|book of Job]]
interrogates, [[john-hartley|John E. Hartley]] names it the **doctrine of double
retribution** and defines it as the belief "that the righteous are always blessed and that
the wicked experience untold hardship, leading to premature death" (Hartley, *Job*, NICOT,
pp. 48–49). The doubling is the point: the doctrine makes two symmetrical claims, one about
the righteous and one about the wicked, and both are put under pressure by Job's case.

Everything on this page below the definition derives from a single ingested source
([[hartley-job-nicot|Hartley, *The Book of Job*, NICOT]]) and is stated in attributed
voice accordingly.

## Origin and Ancient Setting

Hartley argues against dating Job late on the strength of its engagement with retribution,
noting that "the doctrine of retribution was well entrenched in different ages, as attested
by Proverbs, Ezekiel, Kings, and Chronicles" — and adding the canonical-historical
observation that "even centuries later Jesus still had to combat this belief (e.g., Luke
13:1–5)." His conclusion is methodological: "it is fallacious to assume that Job was written
quite late primarily because it challenges the doctrine of retribution" (p. 18). The Luke
citation is Hartley's own cross-canonical framing, not a claim of the book of Job.

Within Israel's [[wisdom-literature|wisdom tradition]] Hartley treats retribution as the
received orthodoxy that the friends articulate and Job's experience contests.

## What the Book of Job Does to the Doctrine — Hartley's Precise Formulation

Hartley is emphatic that the book **corrects rather than abolishes** the doctrine, and his
formulation should not be overstated in either direction:

> "While the book of Job does not categorically reject the doctrine of retribution, it
> endeavors ardently to correct erroneous applications of that doctrine, especially the
> views that suffering is conclusive proof that the sufferer has sinned, that the righteous
> always prosper, and that the wicked are swiftly punished for their evil deeds."
> (p. 545)

And in the Introduction: "this work does not reject the doctrine of retribution, but rather
corrects misguided applications of that doctrine" (p. 22). His sharpest negative verdict is
aimed at the doctrine's *load-bearing* use, not its existence: "earthly retribution is not
the backbone of divine justice, as the book of Job itself demonstrates" (p. 164).

**Hartley's division of labour between the parts of the book.** "While the dialogue
establishes the position that suffering does not necessarily imply sin, the epilogue
recounts Job's great prosperity as Yahweh's free gift, not as a reward earned for his
faithful bearing of suffering" (p. 545).

**His counter-argument to reading the epilogue as anticlimactic.** Hartley reports that
"many modern interpreters … dislike the epilogue as anticlimactic and unrealistic," and
answers with a *reductio*: "The epilogue, however, preserves an essential statement of
Yahwistic faith: fear of Yahweh leads to an abundant life. If fear of Yahweh led inevitably
to hardship and suffering, such a faith would be sadistic. Pain would be the highest
expression of devotion and the heroes of that faith would be those who endured the greatest
suffering. Such is not the case, as the epilogue teaches" (pp. 544–545).

## The Disinterested-Piety Hinge

The most consequential move in Hartley's account is his linkage of retribution theology to
the prologue's test of motive. Because the friends urge Job to repent in order to escape
suffering and recover blessing, Hartley holds that "they unsuspectingly tempt him to use God
for personal gain, the essence of sin. Therefore, if Job followed their counsel, he would
confirm the Satan's proposition that human beings are totally self-serving in their worship
of God" (pp. 48–49). Retribution theology, applied pastorally, thus becomes the very
temptation the book was written to defeat.

Hartley says this of two friends by name. Of [[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz]]:
"Unfortunately, and obviously without realizing it, Eliphaz sides with the Satan against God
in offering this counsel, for he seeks to motivate Job to serve God for the benefits that
piety brings. **His error is not in his doctrine, but in his inability to counsel Job
rightly**" (p. 129). Of [[zophar-the-naamathite|Zophar]]: "Zophar unwittingly aligns himself
with the Satan's position found in the prologue by encouraging Job to seek God for personal
gain" (p. 204).

**The *ḥinnām* thread.** Hartley tracks the Hebrew word *ḥinnām* ("for nought, without
cause") as the spine of the book, turning three times:
- **1:9** — the challenge of [[satan|the Satan]]: "He doubted whether any person would fear
  God for nothing or without reason (Heb. *ḥinnām*)… The Satan's question insinuated that
  all good deeds spring from selfish motives" (p. 73).
- **2:3** — Yahweh's reversal: the trial "proved to be without cause, i.e., the Satan's
  accusations about Job were groundless… Job trusted God with a pure heart filled with love
  for God, not for the benefits God had bestowed upon him" (p. 80).
- **9:17** — Job's own turn of the word: "now Job perceives that God has afflicted him
  *ḥinnām*, without cause. Whereas the Satan doubted Job's motive for being righteous, the
  trial leads Job to doubt the justice of God's actions" (p. 176).

Hartley reads the book's resolution in the same key: in yielding to God, "Job reveals beyond
any doubt that he serves God out of love, not for material gain or prestige" (p. 50). See
[[job-42-6|Job 42:6]] for the disputed recantation itself.

## The Friends' Versions (character register)

The positions below belong to the **characters in the dialogue**, as Hartley reports them.
They are not Hartley's own theology and are not the book's teaching.

**[[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz]]** — grounded in a night vision and in experience. Hartley:
"Eliphaz's central premise is that everyone is guilty of error. From this premise he derives
two basic arguments. First, he boldly articulates the law of retribution, i.e., that the
righteous prosper and the wicked suffer hardship in this life and face a premature death
(4:7–21). Second, he lauds God's greatness and his compassionate care in delivering his own
from sorrow (5:9–26)" (p. 104). Eliphaz refines the scheme with a disciplinary stage:
"Eliphaz understands discipline or reproof as an intermediate step in the doctrine of
retribution… **Pain is the instrument of discipline long before it becomes the fulfillment of
a curse**" (p. 125, on 5:17). In his second speech, Hartley says, Eliphaz "wants to impress on
Job that the doctrine of retribution is an inviolable, moral law."

**[[bildad-the-shuhite|Bildad]]** — argues from the traditions of the fathers, and receives
Hartley's harshest verdict. "There are no exceptions to retribution. The blameless are always
blessed by God and the wicked always punished. Any circumstances to the contrary are either
illusory or momentary… In Bildad's opinion it is unequivocally true that God does not pervert
justice. **He thus equates justice with double retribution**" (p. 164). Hartley continues: "A
prisoner of tradition, Bildad refuses to allow any experience, particularly Job's, to temper
his doctrine… **Bildad's error lies in building his entire theology on the premises of the
doctrine of retribution**" (p. 164). In the second cycle Hartley notes that Bildad presses
retribution as operative *in the present*.

**[[zophar-the-naamathite|Zophar]]** — the dogmatist who draws the conclusion the others
leave implicit. "It is clear that Zophar has taken the final step in the logic of the
doctrine of retribution, concluding that Job is suffering for some hidden sin. For him it
means that Job's suffering is justly deserved. Therefore, Job's complaint against God is
untenable… There remains only one way for Job to escape his plight—the way of repentance"
(p. 204).

## Job's Counter-Case (character register)

Hartley records Job's objections as arguments, not as the narrator's verdict. Job observes
that "the wicked enjoy repose without any hindrance from God" (12:6); he charges at 9:22–24
that God "unjustly makes an end of the blameless and the wicked"; and at 14:21 he undercuts
a specific escape clause — the passage "counters any view of retribution that argues that a
wicked person who is successful is punished through the misfortunes that beset his children"
(p. 240). Hartley's most detailed treatment is of Job 21, where Job attacks delayed and
transferred retribution on moral grounds: punishment must fall on the offender himself, since
"after his death a person has no interest in the fate of his household" (p. 317).

**A precision Hartley repeats:** Job is not portrayed as abandoning retribution as such but
as denying its exceptionless and immediate application — questioning God's *method* is not
the same as abandoning the doctrine. Hartley notes at 12:10 that "Job is questioning, without
denying God's sovereignty, the proposition that God immediately carries out retribution on
all evildoers" (p. 209).

## Elihu's Variant — Discipline Rather Than Punishment

Hartley presents [[elihu|Elihu]]'s speeches as a genuine reshaping: "Although Elihu's
approach is close to that of the three friends, he differs from them in that he does not
assume that all suffering is punishment for past sins. He teaches that misfortune may befall
a person in order to awaken him to some wrongful attitude or unconscious error… Another major
difference in his teaching is the emphasis that suffering may be an expression of God's mercy
more than his wrath" (p. 427). Hartley calls this "a significant reshaping of the doctrine of
retribution," crediting Elihu with accepting "the possibility that the righteous may suffer"
(p. 486).

**[TENSION — recorded, not resolved.]** Hartley's own commentary on Elihu's *second* speech
qualifies that claim. On 34:12 he writes that the line "is a reiteration of Bildad's thesis…
This teaching means that whatever misfortune a person experiences is justly deserved. There
are no exceptions, for God makes no mistakes" (p. 454); and on 34:10–11 that "God judges every
deed and renders to its doer a just reward" (pp. 453–454). Hartley does not harmonize the two
observations. Both are recorded here without adjudication; see
[[elihu-speeches-authenticity|the Elihu speeches controversy]] for the related source-critical
dispute.

## Key Text–Concept Connections

- [[book-of-job|Job]] — the primary locus; the doctrine is the dialogue's contested premise.
- [[theodicy|Theodicy]] — Hartley lists "the question of theodicy" among the book's six
  themes alongside "qualifications to the doctrine of double retribution" (p. 48).
- [[wisdom-literature|Wisdom Literature]] — the tradition in which Hartley locates both the
  doctrine and Elihu's reshaping of it.
- [[job-42-6|Job 42:6]] — the recantation whose reading conditions what the book is taken to
  conclude about retribution.
- [[satan|The Satan]] — the prologue's challenge that the friends' counsel would, on
  Hartley's reading, inadvertently vindicate.
- [[sheol|Sheol]] — the terminus that, for Job, forecloses any post-mortem settling of
  accounts.
