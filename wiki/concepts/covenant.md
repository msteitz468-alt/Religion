*Covenant* (Hebrew: *berît*; Greek: *diathēkē*; Latin: *foedus*) is the central relational-legal category of the Hebrew Bible: a solemn, binding agreement between parties that establishes obligations, grants promises, and is often sealed by oath, ritual, or sign. Covenant is not merely a contract (a bilateral exchange of benefits) but a constitutive relationship that creates a new bond between the parties.

## Types of Covenant

**Royal grant** (unconditional): God grants something to a human party unilaterally, based on that party's prior faithfulness or as an act of pure grace. The Abrahamic covenant (Gen 12:1-3; 15) and the Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7) fit this pattern — God's promises are not conditioned on continued human obedience.

**Suzerain-vassal** (conditional): A more powerful party (the suzerain) establishes a covenant with a less powerful party (the vassal), imposing obligations and offering protection. The Sinai covenant (Exodus 19–24) and Deuteronomy's covenant form fit this pattern. See [[suzerain-vassal-treaty|Suzerain-Vassal Treaty]].

**Parity**: An agreement between roughly equal parties. The covenants between Abraham and Abimelech (Gen 21:27) and between Jacob and Laban (Gen 31:44) fit this pattern.

## The Abrahamic Covenant(s)

The Abrahamic covenant in Genesis is the foundational covenant of the Pentateuch. It appears in two major formulations:

**Genesis 15** (commonly assigned to J or pre-J): God and Abram enter a covenant through a vision and an animal-splitting ritual (*berît bên ha-bĕtārîm*, "covenant between the pieces"). Abram falls into a deep sleep; God alone passes between the pieces as a smoking firepot and torch. The covenant promises: (1) the land of Canaan; (2) innumerable descendants. The one-sided passage of God through the pieces signals the covenant's **unconditional** character — God alone binds himself. Abram's faith is reckoned to him as righteousness (15:6). [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] notes this verse's centrality for Paul's arguments in Romans and Galatians.

**Genesis 17** (P): God reaffirms the covenant to Abram, now renamed Abraham. The sign of the covenant is **circumcision** (*mûl*), to be performed on every male in the household on the eighth day. The covenant's scope expands: Abraham will be the father of many nations (*ʾaḇ hamôn gôyim*). The formulation is solemnly bilateral — Abraham must keep the covenant — but the promises remain unconditional in substance.

[[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] reads Gen 15 and Gen 17 as two stages of a single covenant, not two separate covenants. P's Gen 17 is a reaffirmation and expansion of J's Gen 15, not a replacement.

## The Sinai / Deuteronomic Covenant

The Sinai covenant is formally ratified in Exodus 24. The ratification involves six stages: (1) Moses recites the "book of the covenant" (Exod 20:22–23:33) orally; (2) the people assent unanimously ("everything the LORD has said we will do"); (3) Moses writes the covenant down; (4) an altar and twelve stone pillars are erected for the twelve tribes; (5) young men offer burnt offerings and fellowship offerings; (6) Moses sprinkles half the blood on the altar and half on the people — "This is the blood of the covenant" (Exod 24:8). The seventy elders then eat a communion meal in the visible presence of God (Exod 24:9-11).

[[james-bruckner|Bruckner]] emphasizes that grace precedes law in this structure: "The law was never the means of that salvation. God's delivering grace and saving action in bringing them 'up, out of the land of Egypt' preceded the law." The Decalogue's preamble ("I am the LORD your God who brought you out of Egypt," Exod 20:2) makes this explicit.

[[peter-craigie|Craigie]] and M. G. Kline argue that [[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]] follows the formal structure of a second-millennium Hittite **suzerain-vassal treaty** (preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, succession document). On this reading, the Sinai covenant is a legal relationship of the vassal-type: Israel owes exclusive loyalty (*ʾahavah*, love) to YHWH as suzerain. Covenant violation brings the curses of chs. 27–28; faithfulness brings the blessings.

The [[shema|Shema]] (Deut 6:4-5) is the Deuteronomic covenant in concentrated form: YHWH is Israel's suzerain (*ʾeḥad*, alone/one), and the demanded response is total love.

## The Talmudic Covenant

The [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] and [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] tradition understands the Sinai covenant as having two dimensions: the Written Torah and the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] — both given at Sinai, both binding. Circumcision, the sign of the Abrahamic covenant, is the entry rite for Jewish males into the covenant community. The covenant's obligations (613 commandments in rabbinic reckoning) govern all of life.

## Covenant Renewal at Shechem (Joshua 24)

The Shechem covenant renewal is the climactic act of the book of Joshua and the Deuteronomistic History's inaugural covenant ceremony outside the Sinai/Moab sequence. Its structure closely follows the [[suzerain-vassal-treaty|suzerain-vassal treaty]] form identified by Craigie and Kline in Deuteronomy:

1. **Preamble** (24:2): "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel" — the suzerain identifies himself
2. **Historical prologue** (24:2-13): YHWH recounts his acts from Terah through the conquest — in the first person throughout. Every act is divine; Israel's role is passive
3. **Stipulations** (24:14-15): "Fear the LORD and serve him"; choose this day whom you will serve
4. **Covenant affirmation** (24:16-18, 21-22): The people assent
5. **Witness** (24:22, 27): The people are themselves witnesses; a stone is set up as a witness
6. **Sanctions** (24:19-20): "If you forsake the LORD… he will turn and do you harm"

Joshua's warning — "you cannot serve the LORD, for he is a holy God; he is a jealous God" (24:19) — is among the most remarkable statements in the Former Prophets. It appears to counsel *against* the covenant the chapter just establishes. [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] reads it not as contradiction but as a warning about the absolute demands of covenant with the Holy One: what Israel is undertaking is not manageable religion but binding relationship with a God who will hold them to their word. The book's final note — "Israel served the LORD all the days of Joshua and the elders who outlived him" (24:31) — is the narrator's affirmation that the seemingly impossible was achieved, however temporarily.

## The New Covenant

Jeremiah 31:31–34 predicts a "new covenant" (*berît ḥadāšâ*) in which the Torah will be written on the heart rather than stone tablets. The NT reads this as fulfilled in Jesus' death (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25; Heb 8–10). [[darrell-bock|Bock]] reads Acts as the narrative of the new covenant community's formation through the Spirit, inaugurated at Pentecost. How the new covenant relates to the Abrahamic and Mosaic covenants — whether it replaces, fulfills, or renews them — is a major point of Christian theological controversy.

## Cross-Tradition Tensions

The covenant concept raises several unresolved inter-tradition controversies:
- Whether the Abrahamic covenant's promises are conditional (Jewish covenantal nomism) or unconditional (evangelical Protestant reading) — E. P. Sanders' work on covenantal nomism is relevant here
- Whether the Mosaic covenant is fulfilled and superseded in the new covenant, or whether it remains in force for Jewish people
- Whether covenant membership is constituted by circumcision (physical descent) or by faith (Paul's argument in Galatians 3–4)
