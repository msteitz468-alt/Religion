# Natural Law (and the Kinds of Law)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s **Treatise on Law** ([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I-II,
Q90–108) is the foundational text of the Western **natural-law** tradition. The core (Q90–97) is
ingested here; the treatments of the Old and New Law (Q98–108) remain to be ingested.

## The definition of law (Q90)

"Law is **an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and
promulgated**" (Q90, A4). Each element answers an article: law belongs to **reason** (the rule and
measure of acts, A1); it is ordered to the **common good** (A2); it is made by the whole people or
their public vicegerent (A3); and it must be **promulgated** to bind (A4).

## The four kinds of law (Q91)

1. **Eternal law** — the plan of divine wisdom governing all things, "the type of the order of
   things directed towards an end" existing in the mind of God (Q91, A1; cf. Q93).
2. **Natural law** — "the natural law is nothing else than **the rational creature's participation
   of the eternal law**" (Q91, A2). By the light of natural reason the rational creature discerns
   good and evil, sharing in the eternal reason.
3. **Human law** — the particular determinations devised by human reason from the precepts of
   natural law (Q91, A3; Q95).
4. **Divine law** — the revealed law (Old and New), needed to direct man to his supernatural end, to
   provide certainty, to reach interior acts, and to forbid all evils (Q91, A4). (Aquinas also
   discusses the *fomes* / "law of sin," A6.)

## Natural law in detail (Q94)

The **first, self-evident precept** of natural law is "**good is to be done and pursued, and evil is
to be avoided**" (Q94, A2) — practical reason's first principle, paralleling "being" as the first
concept of speculative reason. All other precepts are founded on it, following the order of the
**natural inclinations**:
- to **self-preservation** (shared with all substances);
- to **procreation and the rearing of offspring** (shared with animals);
- to **know the truth about God and to live in society** (proper to rational beings).

The natural law is **the same in all** as to its general first principles, but can fail in
particular conclusions (Q94, A4 — Aquinas cites Caesar's report that the Germans did not reckon
theft wrong). Its first principles are **indelible**, but secondary precepts "can be blotted out from
the human heart" by evil persuasion or vicious custom (Q94, A6).

## Human law (Q95–97)

Human law **derives from natural law** in two ways (Q95, A2): by **conclusion** (as "one must not
kill" follows from "do harm to no one" — such laws share natural-law force) and by **determination**
(specifying what natural law leaves open, e.g. the particular mode of punishment — such laws have
force from human enactment alone). This maps onto the *ius gentium* vs. civil law distinction.

A just human law **binds conscience** (Q96, A4); but an **unjust** law — contrary to the human good
(in end, author's competence, or proportionate burden) — "is no longer a law but a perversion of
law" (Augustine: *lex iniusta non est lex*) and does not bind in conscience, except to avoid scandal
or disturbance. A law contrary to the **divine** good must never be obeyed ("We ought to obey God
rather than men," Acts 5:29).

## Significance

Aquinas's synthesis — anchoring positive human law in a natural law that is reason's participation
in the eternal law — became the backbone of the Catholic moral and legal tradition, the Scholastic
theory of just and unjust law, and (via Suárez, Grotius, and the early-modern jurists) of secular
natural-rights theory.
