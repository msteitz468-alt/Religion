# Antichrist (ἀντίχριστος)

## Definition

The Greek compound *antichristos* (ἀντίχριστος) appears **only** in the Johannine Epistles in the entire New Testament: 1 John 2:18 (twice), 2:22; 4:3; 2 John 7. It combines *anti* (against/instead of) + *christos* (anointed one = Christ). The word can mean either "one opposed to Christ" or "one claiming to be Christ instead of Christ"; the Johannine usage primarily implies the former.

The term was developed but not invented by John; 1 John 2:18 assumes the readers have already "heard that the antichrist is coming" — indicating a prior eschatological tradition. The Letters of John give the concept its only direct NT definition.

## Johannine Usage

### Singular: The Eschatological Antichrist

"As you have heard that the antichrist is coming..." (2:18a). This refers to an expected end-time figure of opposition to Christ — an idea present in Jewish and Christian eschatological thinking, though not under this specific name in earlier sources. The author acknowledges the tradition but does not develop it; his interest is in the plural sense.

### Plural: The Secessionists as Antichrists

"...even now many antichrists have come" (2:18b). This is John's distinctive move: the expected singular antichrist has been revealed, *proleptically*, in the plural form of the false teachers who have already appeared. Their presence is a sign that "it is the last hour."

Who are the antichrists? Any person or spirit that:
- Denies that Jesus is the Christ (2:22)
- Denies the Father and the Son (2:23)
- Does not acknowledge that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:3; cf. 2 John 7)
- Claims to have come from God but fails the christological test (4:1–3)

"Any such person is the deceiver and the antichrist" (2 John 7). The term functions as the most severe possible characterization — not merely as "wrong about doctrine" but as the embodiment of cosmic opposition to Christ.

### The Christological Test

The criterion for identifying the spirit of antichrist is explicitly Christological: "Every spirit that acknowledges that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God, but every spirit that does not acknowledge Jesus is not from God. This is the spirit of the antichrist" (4:2–3). The confession is an ongoing claim about the reality of the incarnation — that Jesus Christ continues to exist as "having come in the flesh" — not merely a historical report.

## Key Exegetical Issues

**What does "come in the flesh" mean?** The phrase targets the Docetic / Cerinthian denial that the divine Christ truly and lastingly united with the human Jesus. The secessionists may have held that Christ merely "seemed" to be incarnate, or that the divine power descended at baptism and departed before the crucifixion. "Come in the flesh" is the anti-Docetic formula: the Word truly became flesh (John 1:14) and remained so.

**"Spirit of antichrist" (4:3)**: The false prophets are not merely wrong individuals; they are inhabited or moved by a spiritual power opposed to God. The author is not merely offering an epistemological criterion but making a statement about the spiritual reality at stake.

**The expected singular vs. present plural**: Marshall notes this is not contradiction: the many present antichrists are forerunners and manifestations of the expected singular opponent; their appearance is itself a sign of the eschatological hour.

## The Man of Lawlessness (2 Thessalonians 2:3–12)

The figure most frequently conflated with the Antichrist in later tradition is Paul's "man of lawlessness" (*ho anthrōpos tēs anomias*, 2 Thess 2:3) — called also "the son of destruction" (*ho huios tēs apōleias*, v. 3) and "the lawless one" (*ho anomos*, v. 8). This figure is never called "antichrist" by Paul; the identification is a post-canonical development.

The man of lawlessness is characterized by:
- **Temple desecration and self-deification**: "He takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (2:4). Operating within a **grammatico-historical** and **historical-critical** framework, Weima reads this as a literal reference to the Jerusalem temple, using the presupposition of its standing as evidence the letter is pre-70 CE. In contrast, **allegorical / ecclesiological** frameworks (from John Chrysostom and the Reformers to Beale) read "the temple of God" figuratively as referring to the church itself, forecasting an apostasy within the Christian community.
- **Danielic background**: Utilizing rigorous **grammatico-historical intertextuality**, Weima shows the figure draws heavily on Daniel 10–12. The self-exaltation formula closely parallels Dan 11:36 (the king of the north who exalts himself above every god); the temple desecration parallels Dan 11:31 (Antiochus IV's defilement); "the son of destruction" echoes Dan 12:10.
- **Satanic empowerment**: His coming is "in conformity with the work of Satan, with every kind of power, sign, and false wonder" (2:9), deceiving those who rejected the gospel.
- **Eventual destruction**: Christ destroys him "by the breath of his mouth" at the parousia (2:8; Isa 11:4 allusion).

**The restrainer**: Before the man of lawlessness can be revealed, *to katechon* / *ho katechōn* (the restraining thing / person) must be removed. Weima's identification relies on **grammatico-historical exegesis** linking 2 Thess 2 back to Daniel 10-12: the archangel Michael, whose "stand aside" in Dan 12:1a LXX triggers the subsequent tribulation. See [[restrainer-2-thess-2|The Restrainer of 2 Thessalonians 2:6–7]].

**Historical vs. futurist readings**: 
- **Preterist / Historical-critical framework**: Some scholars (Rigaux, Bornkamm) read the man of lawlessness as a reference to an already-historical figure such as Caligula (who threatened to install his image in the temple, AD 39–40). 
- **Modern critical (Pseudepigraphy)**: Theorists who assume post-70 pseudepigraphy treat the temple reference as *vaticinium ex eventu* (prophecy after the fact) and a stylized apocalyptic trope rather than a historical expectation.
- **Grammatico-historical (Futurist)**: Most contemporary conservative scholars (Weima, Marshall, Wanamaker) read him as a genuinely future eschatological figure anticipated by Paul within a **pastoral-consolatory framework**, intended to comfort persecuted believers rather than offer a strict eschatological timetable.

## The Beast of Revelation (Osborne)

Revelation never uses the word *antichrist*, but the **beast** of chapters 13 and 17 became the
richest source for the later Antichrist figure. [[grant-osborne|Osborne]] (BECNT, 2002) reads the
beast as **the final Antichrist** and **the final, unholy Roman Empire** — a Nero-like figure leading
the "earth-dwellers" in a last pogrom against God's people. Key features:
- **The "false trinity."** The dragon (Satan), the beast from the sea, and the false prophet/beast
  from the earth (16:13) are a deliberate **parody** of Father, Son, and Spirit — part of Revelation's
  theme that Satan can only *imitate* what God has already done. The beast's "mortal wound that was
  healed" (13:3, 12, 14) imitates the death and resurrection of Christ and likely reflects the **Nero
  *redivivus*** legend.
- **The mark and the number "666" (13:16–18).** Osborne reads the number **symbolically** — the
  number of humanity/imperfection (falling short of the divine 7, trebled) — and the "mark" as a
  parody of God's sealing of the saints (7:3), i.e., a sign of *ownership/allegiance*, not a literal
  technology. The best-known concrete proposal is **gematria for "Nero Caesar"** (Hebrew letters), a
  reading aided by the variant "616."
- **Daniel background.** As with Paul's man of lawlessness, the beast draws on Daniel 7 (the hybrid
  beasts) and the self-exalting little horn.
- **Interpretive-school dependence.** Whether the beast is *Nero* (preterist), *successive empires/
  the papacy* (historicist/Reformers), *all anti-Christian power* (idealist), or *a future Antichrist*
  (futurist) follows from one's method; Osborne's eclectic-futurist reading takes it as a final
  eschatological figure prefigured by Nero and Rome. See [[revelation-interpretive-schools|The Four
  Interpretive Schools of Revelation]] and [[revelation|Revelation]].

## Later Christian Development

The Johannine concept of antichrist became absorbed into a wider tradition of eschatological expectation, often merged with other figures:
- The "man of lawlessness" of 2 Thessalonians 2:3–4 (see above)
- The beast of Revelation 13
- The false prophet of Revelation 16:13; 19:20

None of these are called "antichrist" in their own texts, but later Christian theology (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Jerome, Augustine) identified them under the Johannine label. The tradition of a singular end-time Antichrist (capital A) who would appear before Christ's return is a development that goes beyond the Johannine epistles' own usage, which is primarily applied to present, identifiable false teachers.

The Protestant Reformation applied "antichrist" to the papacy (Luther). The specific christological focus of the Johannine usage was often lost in these polemical applications.
