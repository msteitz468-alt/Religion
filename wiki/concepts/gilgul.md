# Gilgul (Reincarnation / Transmigration of the Soul)

**Gilgul** (Hebrew: גִּלְגּוּל, "rolling" or "cycle"; often *gilgul neshamot*, "transmigration of souls") is the Kabbalistic doctrine that a soul may return to earthly life in a new body to complete unfinished spiritual work, rectify past failings, or fulfill a specific mission (*tikkun*). It is one of the most distinctive developments of medieval Jewish mysticism and receives its first extensive, systematic treatment in the Zohar.

## Origin and Core Development in the Zohar

The Zohar’s most important discussion appears in the embedded composition **Sava de-Mishpatim** ("Old Man of Mishpatim"), included in Pritzker Edition Volume V (commentary on Parashat Mishpatim, Exod 21–24). In this narrative, a mysterious old donkey-driver engages Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Yose with riddles and then reveals profound secrets. The old man links the biblical law of levirate marriage (*yibbum*, Deut 25:5–10) with the soul’s need for repair across lifetimes.

Key ideas introduced or developed here:
- A soul that has not completed its task or has committed certain sins may be sent back (*gilgul*) in another body.
- Levirate marriage can serve as a mechanism for the soul of a childless man to return through his brother’s wife so that the “name” and spiritual root may be rectified.
- The process is not punishment in a simple sense but an opportunity for *tikkun* (repair/cosmic restoration). The soul “rolls” through bodies until it achieves the required rectification.
- The famous parable of the **beautiful maiden** (Torah): Torah is depicted as a hidden princess who gradually reveals herself to the worthy devotee. Different levels of meaning (including the secrets of gilgul) correspond to stages of intimacy with the divine.

The Sava’s treatment is the first sustained exploration of reincarnation in Jewish literature; earlier sources contain hints, but the Zohar (via the “Old Man”) makes it a central theosophical and theodical principle.

The volume also includes *Sifra di-Tsni’uta* ("Book of Concealment"), whose dense reflections on the upper divine structure provide the metaphysical backdrop for soul dynamics.

## Relation to Other Zoharic Soul Teachings

Previous Zohar material (Vols. I and III) already presents a **tripartite soul** (*nefesh* – *ruaḥ* – *neshamah*) rooted in the sefirot (Malkhut, Tif’eret, Binah) and describes the soul’s post-mortem journey through the cave of Machpelah to the lower Garden of Eden, the “dew of resurrection,” and the idea that “the righteous do not die.” Gilgul extends this by adding the possibility of return to embodied life for further repair. ## Tradition-Specific Portraits

**Kabbalistic (Zoharic and later)**: Gilgul becomes a major theodical tool. Suffering, childlessness, untimely death, and apparent injustice can be explained as the working out of prior-life debts or as the soul’s voluntary return to elevate sparks or complete a mission. In Lurianic Kabbalah (16th c. Safed), the doctrine is systematized and expanded: souls are composed of “sparks” from the primordial shattering; gilgul and *ibbur* (temporary “impregnation” by another soul) are mechanisms for gathering the scattered lights and repairing the cosmos (*tikkun olam*). Later Hasidism popularizes the idea in stories of *tzaddikim* who recognize reincarnated souls and help them.

**Rabbinic Judaism (pre- and non-Kabbalistic)**: Classical rabbinic literature is largely silent or resistant to reincarnation. The emphasis remains on this-worldly reward and punishment, resurrection at the end of days, and the world-to-come. Some medieval rationalists (e.g., Maimonides) reject gilgul outright.

**Modern Jewish thought**: Gilgul is central to many contemporary mystical and Neo-Hasidic currents. It is sometimes psychologized or universalized. Orthodox authorities are divided; some accept it as part of esoteric tradition, others treat it as non-binding or allegorical.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Gilgul addresses the problem of theodicy without impugning divine justice: what appears as undeserved suffering or inexplicable fate may be the continuation of a soul’s story across bodies. It also democratizes spiritual opportunity — every person, in whatever circumstances, may be participating in a larger arc of repair. At the same time, it intensifies personal responsibility: one’s actions affect not only this life but the soul’s future embodiments and the collective *tikkun* of the sefirotic world.

The doctrine is intimately tied to the Zohar’s central drama of Shekhinah’s exile and redemption. The soul’s wanderings mirror the Shekhinah’s own “rolling” through the worlds until union is restored.
