# Gilgul (Reincarnation / Transmigration of the Soul)

**Gilgul** (Hebrew: גִּלְגּוּל, "rolling" or "cycle"; often *gilgul neshamot*, "transmigration of souls") is the Kabbalistic doctrine that a soul may return to earthly life in a new body to complete unfinished spiritual work, rectify past failings, or fulfill a specific mission (*tikkun*). In the wiki’s sources it receives its first extensive Jewish treatment in the Zohar’s *Sava de-Mishpatim* stratum (Pritzker Vol. V).

## Origin and Core Development in the Zohar

The Zohar’s classic discussion is the independent composition **Sava de-Mishpatim** ("Old Man of Mishpatim"), in Parashat Mishpatim (Exod 21–24; Zohar **2:94b–114a** zone; Pritzker Vol. V). Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Yose meet a donkey-driver whose riddles seem empty; he proves a master of wisdom and reveals soul mysteries by decoding the laws of slavery and related verses. Matt’s preface (Vol. V) frames this as the first extensive *gilgul* treatment in Jewish literature, linking it to levirate marriage (*yibbum*) and noting interpretive density that can border on parody of *Yevamot*.

**Standards reingest (2026-07-18)** densifies the doctrine from sequential reading of the Sava body (digests A–B), with short quotes and folio loci. Prior 2026-06-07 filing remains scaffolding.

### Opening: riddles and soul hierarchy (2:95a–96a)

At the Tower of Tyre the old man poses three riddles, including “Who is a beautiful maiden without eyes, her body hidden and revealed?” (2:95a). From Lev 22:12–13 he reads the **supernal soul** as “daughter of a priest,” with a hierarchy of soul levels: **nishmeta, ruaḥ, and nefesh** (2:95b). Souls fly from a treasure-house; when drawn with the evil impulse they may find “structure of an alien man” and leave imperfect. A **scales** image governs destinies: “scale of righteousness… scale of deceit. The scales never subside, and souls ascend, entering and returning” (2:95b). World is conducted by the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil; from certain tormented souls issue “the pious of the nations… and bastard scholars who take precedence over an ignorant, renowned high priest” (2:95b–96a).

### Slave-girl, Lilith, preexistence (2:96b–97b)

Exod 21:7–8 (*If a man sells his daughter as a slave-girl*) is read of the soul “in revolving migrations of evil deeds.” Tormented souls include infants whom **Lilith** “delights over… torments and removes… while he is suckling at his mother’s breast” (2:96b). Protection is the garment of the holy name **Eloah** (*be-vigdo vah* re-read as “with his garment upon her”). Souls are fashioned before descent; some go down “unwillingly.” *If for his son he designates her* maps to the **Palace of Love** and paternal kisses of the King (Gen 29:11 typology) (2:97a–b). Food, clothing, and conjugal rights (Exod 21:10) become three **supernal names** (flows of YHVH/Elohim, Eloah, YHVH Tseva’ot) not to be diminished for the first soul (2:97b).

### Torah-as-maiden parable (2:99a–b)

The Sava’s troubadour parable: a beautiful maiden hidden in her palace gradually reveals herself to the circling lover — window-glimpse, then **derasha**, then **haggadah** riddles, then face-to-face secrets (“complete man, husband of Torah”), then **peshat** “just like it is. One should not add or delete even a single letter” (2:99a–b). Progressive intimacy models layered Sod revelation (including gilgul secrets).

### Levirate, sling, Male/Female worlds (2:100a–106a)

“All souls enter a cycle.” Naked spirits “whirl like a stone in a sling” (1 Sam 25:29) until a redeemer rebuilds via the mate’s vessel — “new creature… new spirit and a new body” (2:100a). **Neshamah** from the mighty tree / river from Eden and **ruaḥ** from a smaller tree join as **ner**, *lamp of YHVH* (Prov 20:27). Three-spirit problem (vessel-spirit, naked returner, redeemer’s) is resolved so the original vessel-spirit is reserved for the **first body**; first body may suffer in **Adamah** / **Tevel** until restoration (Dan 12:2 *admat afar*) (2:100a–b). Former mate becomes mother and brother father — “World… upside-down” (2:100b–101a). Failure to increase inverts Joseph/Yesod rung: uprooted from **World of the Male** into **World of the Female** (2:101a).

Exod 21:2–5 “proves” the system: six years = Male; seventh = Female; refusal brands to the **Other Side**; Jubilee opens another transmigration; childless *If he came by himself* = sling/rock/lone serpent-spirit until redeemer (2:105b–106a). *Ba’al teshuvah* can breach every entrance (2:106a). Exod 21:5 (*I love my master…*) is read as prayer/supplication that can divert from transmigration into Heavenly Academy discipline and eventual redemption (2:109a–b).

### Close of the Sava (2:114a)

The old man names himself: “I, **Yeiva Sava**, have stood before you to arouse these words.” Companions return to Rabbi Shim’on, who marvels they survived a “supernal lion” unrecognized (2:114a). Song 8:6 seals love/jealousy of Assembly of Israel.

### Main-body Mishpatim (after Sava)

After the Sava closes, main-body **Commentary on Mishpatim** (from 2:121a) continues holiness/law theurgy (people of holiness, mishpatim balancing Gevurah-Torah, “Secret of Secrets” body of the King) but is a distinct register from the Sava’s gilgul narrative. See [[zohar|The Zohar]] Vol. V structure.

## Relation to Other Zoharic Soul Teachings

Previous Zohar material (Vols. I and III) already presents a **tripartite soul** (*nefesh* – *ruaḥ* – *neshamah*) and post-mortem journey (Machpelah, lower Garden, resurrection dew; “the righteous do not die”). Gilgul extends this by return to embodied life for repair. Terumah in the same volume densifies the three-level afterlife chain (nefesh at the grave; ruaḥ in the earthly Garden ascending on Sabbaths/festivals; neshamah to its source) as theurgic parallel to the Dwelling (2:141b–143a). See [[zoharic-afterlife|The Soul and the Afterlife (Kabbalah)]].

## Tradition-Specific Portraits

**Kabbalistic (Zoharic and later)**: Gilgul becomes a major theodical tool. Suffering, childlessness, untimely death, and apparent injustice can be read as prior-life debts or voluntary return. In Lurianic Kabbalah the doctrine is systematized (*ibbur*, sparks, *tikkun olam*). Later Hasidism popularizes recognition stories of reincarnated souls.

**Rabbinic Judaism (pre- and non-Kabbalistic)**: Classical rabbinic literature is largely silent or resistant. Emphasis remains this-worldly reward/punishment, resurrection, and the world-to-come. Some medieval rationalists (e.g. Maimonides) reject gilgul.

**Modern Jewish thought**: Central in many mystical and Neo-Hasidic currents; sometimes psychologized. Orthodox reception is divided.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

In the Sava, gilgul is not simple retributive punishment but a structure of **scales, redeemer, vessel, and worlds** (Male/Female) through which incomplete souls and incomplete bodies can still be accounted for at resurrection (“nothing will be lost and all will rise,” 2:105a zone). Cause of many transmigrations: refusal to “increase in the world” and uphold the covenant (2:105a). The doctrine intensifies responsibility and theodicy while democratizing repair. Soul wanderings parallel [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]’s exile and restoration.
