The concept of intellectual perfection as the **highest human end** — the possession of true metaphysical knowledge of God — is the culminating theme of [[maimonides|Maimonides']] [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]], developed in Part III Chapters LI–LIV.

## The Four Perfections (Ch. LIV)

Maimonides draws on "the ancient and the modern philosophers" to distinguish four kinds of human perfection, arranged hierarchically:

**First perfection (lowest): Perfection in property.** "The possession of money, garments, furniture, servants, land, and the like; the possession of the title of a great king belongs to this class." This is purely external and "imaginary": "when that relation ceases, he that has been a great king may one morning find that there is no difference between him and the lowest person" (Ch. LIV). It belongs to the possessor only in name.

**Second perfection: Bodily perfection.** "The perfection of the shape, constitution, and form of man's body; the utmost evenness of temperaments, and the proper order and strength of his limbs." This must be excluded as the chief aim because man shares it with all living beings and cannot compete with animals in raw strength (Ch. LIV).

**Third perfection: Moral perfection.** "The highest degree of excellency in man's character." Most of the commandments aim at producing this. But moral principles concern *relations to other people*; "imagine a person being alone, and having no connexion whatever with any other person, all his good moral principles are at rest, they are not required, and give man no perfection whatever" (Ch. LIV). Moral perfection is therefore a preparation for the fourth, not an end in itself.

**Fourth perfection (highest and true): Intellectual perfection.** "The possession of the highest intellectual faculties: the possession of such notions which lead to true metaphysical opinions as regards God. With this perfection man has obtained his final object; it gives him true human perfection; it remains to him alone; it gives him immortality, and on its account he is called man" (Ch. LIV). Unlike the other three perfections, which are partly shared or partly external, this belongs to the person alone: "'They shall be only thine own, and not strangers' with thee'" (Prov. 5:17, cited Ch. LIV).

The prophet Jeremiah is cited as expressing the same doctrine: "Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, let not the rich man glory in his riches; but let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me" (Jer. 9:22–23, cited Ch. LIV). Maimonides notes the Jeremiah passage ranks the perfections in reverse order of true value but in the order "the multitude" esteems them — rich first, mighty second, wise (moral) third.

## The Parable of the Palace (Ch. LI)

Maimonides presents an extended parable of a king in his palace to describe degrees of approach to God/knowledge:

- **Those abroad** (outside the country): people with no religion of any kind — "extreme Turks that wander about in the north, the Kushites who live in the south" — whom he regards as sub-rational. (Note: this reflects medieval geography and is a descriptive claim within Maimonides' text, not a factual judgment.)
- **Those in the country with backs to the palace**: people with religion but false doctrines, who "recede more and more from the royal palace the more they seem to proceed." "Under certain circumstances it may become necessary to slay them, and to extirpate their doctrines."
- **Those walking toward the palace but not yet seeing it**: the mass of observant people who keep the commandments but are theologically uninstructed.
- **Those circling the palace seeking the entrance**: those who study only the practical law (*halakhah*) and believe traditional principles without philosophical proof.
- **Those who have entered the ante-chamber**: those who investigate the principles of religion through reason.
- **Those in the inner palace with the king**: those who "have succeeded in finding a proof for everything that can be proved, who have a true knowledge of God... they have reached the goal, and are in the palace in which the king lives" (Ch. LI).

Applied to the curriculum: mathematics and logic correspond to circling the palace; physics to entering the hall; metaphysics to the innermost court. Prophets are those who "direct all their mind toward the attainment of perfection in Metaphysics, devote themselves entirely to God, exclude from their thought every other thing" (Ch. LI).

## Love of God Identical with Knowledge of God

Maimonides explicitly equates the two: "man's love of God is identical with His knowledge of Him" (Ch. LI). Divine service enjoined by the commandments must therefore be "preceded by the love of God." Intense love (*hashak*, as distinguished from *ahab*) means "a man's love is so intense that his thought is exclusively engaged with the object of his love" (Ch. LI).

## The Intellect as the Link to God and Providence

"The intellect which emanates from God unto us is the link that joins us to God. You have it in your power to strengthen that bond, if you choose to do so, or to weaken it gradually, till it breaks if you prefer this" (Ch. LI). Divine Providence is apportioned according to the degree of this intellectual-divine link: "Providence watches over every rational being according to the amount of intellect which that being possesses" (Ch. LI). When a wise man turns his thoughts to worldly matters, Providence proportionally diminishes; when he returns to contemplation of God, it is restored. This explains why "good and pious men are afflicted with great evils" — it occurs during moments of inattention, not by cosmic injustice.

The Patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob) and Moses are Maimonides' examples of those who achieved this union even amid worldly affairs — "when they were occupied in these things, only their bodily limbs were at work, whilst their heart and mind never moved away from the name of God" (Ch. LI).

## Death as a Kiss: Moses, Aaron, Miriam

Maimonides cites the rabbinic teaching that Moses, Aaron, and Miriam died "by the mouth of the Lord" (*pi ha-Shem*) — meaning their deaths were a "kiss." "The meaning of this saying is that these three died in the midst of the pleasure derived from the knowledge of God and their great love for Him" (Ch. LI). This "death which in truth is deliverance from death" is the soul's transition from embodied knowledge to its fullest, unimpeded state: "The intellect of these men remains then constantly in the same condition, since the obstacle is removed that at times has intervened between the intellect and the object of its action: it continues for ever in that great delight" (Ch. LI).

## Practical Training Toward Perfection

Maimonides gives a graduated practical program (Ch. LI):
1. First, concentrate during *Shema* and its first verse or paragraph of prayer only.
2. After years of this, extend concentration to the entire Torah reading.
3. Then extend to all blessings and prophetic readings.
4. Finally, ensure the mind is free of worldly thought during all religious acts.
5. Reserve worldly reflection for eating, bathing, conversation with family, and ordinary business.
6. When alone and awake, dedicate all inner time to intellectual contemplation of God.

The goal is described: "When we have acquired a true knowledge of God, and rejoice in that knowledge in such a manner, that whilst speaking with others, or attending to our bodily wants, our mind is all that time with God" — citing the Song of Songs: "'I sleep, but my heart waketh'" (Song 5:2, cited Ch. LI).

## Hesed, Mishpat, Tzedakah (Ch. LIII–LIV)

The three terms from Jeremiah's culminating verse are given precise definitions:

- **Hesed** (*loving-kindness*): "an excess in some moral quality... showing kindness to those who have no claim to it whatever." Applied to God: "the very act of the creation is an act of God's loving-kindness" — citing Ps. 89:3 (Ch. LIII).
- **Tzedakah** (*righteousness*): "giving every one his due, and showing kindness to every being according as it deserves" — but specifically moral conscience beyond legal obligation. Extends to faith in God: "he believed in the Lord, and he accounted it to him as righteousness" (Gen. 15:6, cited Ch. LIII).
- **Mishpat** (*judgment*): "deciding upon a certain action in accordance with justice which may demand either mercy or punishment" (Ch. LIII).

The practical implication of the Jeremiah passage for Maimonides is that intellectual perfection does not terminate in passive contemplation. The knowledge of God's ways and attributes — specifically His *hesed*, *mishpat*, and *tzedakah* "in the earth" — leads the perfected person to practice these same qualities: "the perfection, in which man can truly glory, is attained by him when he has acquired... the knowledge of God, the knowledge of His Providence, and of the manner in which it influences His creatures... Having acquired this knowledge he will then be determined always to seek loving-kindness, judgment, and righteousness, and thus to imitate the ways of God" (Ch. LIV).

The phrase "in the earth" in Jer. 9:23 is treated as a deliberate anti-Aristotelian theological assertion: it "rejects the theory of those who boldly assert that God's providence does not extend below the sphere of the moon" (Ch. LIV).

## Religious Acts as Means, Not End

A key subordinating move: "all such religious acts as reading the Law, praying, and the performance of other precepts, serve exclusively as the means of causing us to occupy and fill our mind with the precepts of God, and free it from worldly business" (Ch. LI). Performed mechanically — lips moving while the mind is on business — they are of no value: "we are then like those who are engaged in digging in the ground, or hewing wood in the forest, without reflecting on the nature of those acts" (Ch. LI). Similarly, moral perfection (the third perfection) and the religious acts producing it are "but preparations leading to [the ultimate aim]. Hear the opinion of our Sages... 'And all things that are desirable... are not to be compared to her [wisdom]'" (Bereshit Rabbah, cited Ch. LIV).
