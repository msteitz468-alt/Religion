# Speeches in Acts

The **speeches in Acts** — the missionary sermons, defenses, and addresses that make up roughly
**one-third of the book** — are a defining literary and historiographical feature of
[[acts|Acts]]. Whether they preserve what was actually said, or are free compositions of the author,
is one of the classic questions of Acts scholarship. As read by [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]]
(BECNT *Acts*, 2007), the speeches are faithful summaries fitted to their speakers and settings, and
they display a distinctive Old-Testament-fulfillment method. This is a hermeneutical concept: it
concerns *how* Acts presents its preaching, not only *what* the preaching says.

## The Historiographical Debate

Bock frames the question through two camps:

- **The Dibelius camp** (Dibelius 1956; Soards 1994; Haenchen) treats the speeches as "a literary
  device, a convention of historiography," with Luke responsible for their content "with only a
  minimal concern to reflect what originally took place."
- **The Dodd/Bruce/Marshall/Witherington camp** holds that Luke supplies the wording but is
  "summarizing tradition with a concern to report what was said." Their evidence: **Christological
  variation** across speeches (the heavy OT use of Acts 2 and 3 versus Acts 10; the shift from
  Messiah / Holy One / servant to Lord / judge); the **brevity** of the speeches, which "speaks for
  their character as summaries"; and, per Witherington, that the speeches are "tightly fitted into
  their settings."

**Bock's position** sides with the second camp. He debates the Thucydidean convention (*Pel. War*
1.22.1) — noting Polybius (12.25A–B) condemned inventing speeches — and concludes, in Bruce's
phrase, that Luke is **"Thucydidean but with 'considerable restraint'"**: not free Lukan invention
but careful summary. His verdict: **"Luke is a careful, ancient historian" who summarizes** — "Luke's
creativity is seen not in his creating an event but in his summarizing of it." (This continues
Bock's broader defense of the historicity of Acts against the skepticism of Dibelius, Conzelmann,
and Haenchen.)

## Dodd's Six-Theme Kerygma

For the content and structure of the missionary speeches (the *kerygmata*), Bock adopts **C. H.
Dodd's (1936) six-theme outline** of the apostolic preaching, first applied to Peter's Pentecost
sermon (Acts 2:14):

1. the age of fulfillment has dawned;
2. this has come through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus;
3. the resurrection has exalted Jesus (to God's right hand);
4. the Holy Spirit is the sign of the church's present power;
5. the consummation will come at Jesus's return;
6. an appeal to repent, with the offer of forgiveness and the Spirit.

## The OT-Fulfillment Method

Bock highlights the speeches' characteristic **hermeneutical technique** for reading the Old
Testament — the same techniques the wiki tracks under Bock's grammatical-historical, Luke-Acts-unity
reading:

- **Gezerah shewa (link-word) exegesis** — one of Hillel's rules, joining texts through shared
  vocabulary. In the Pentecost sermon Bock lists the linking words that bind the citations (Joel,
  Ps. 16, Ps. 110): "poured out" (2:17/33), "hades" (2:27/31), "Lord" (2:21/36), "corruption"
  (2:27/31), "right hand" (2:33/34). The same technique recurs in the Pisidian Antioch sermon
  (13:33–35, linking Isa. 55:3 and Ps. 16:10 through *ta hosia* / *ton hosion* and *diaphthoran*)
  and in James's use of Amos 9 (15:15–18).
- **"This-is-that" pesher form** — Peter's *touto estin* ("this is that which was spoken," 2:16)
  interprets present events as the fulfillment of prophecy, a form Bock compares to Qumran pesher.

Through these methods the speeches present the Christ-event as the fulfillment of promises "made
long ago," which Bock takes to be the central legitimating claim of Acts.

## The Major Speeches

Bock treats the principal speeches as a graded series addressed to different audiences: Peter's
**Pentecost sermon** (Acts 2, to Jews); Peter's **Cornelius sermon** (Acts 10, to a God-fearer's
household); Paul's **Pisidian Antioch synagogue sermon** (Acts 13, to Jews and proselytes); Paul's
**Areopagus address** (Acts 17, to pagan Gentiles — see [[natural-theology|natural theology]]);
Paul's **Miletus farewell** (Acts 20, his only speech exclusively to Christians); and the sequence
of **defense speeches** before the Sanhedrin, Felix, Festus, and Agrippa (Acts 22–26). Bock reads
this range of settings as itself evidence that the speeches are fitted to their occasions rather
than uniformly composed.
