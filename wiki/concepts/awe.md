# Awe (Yir'ah)

In [[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]]'s philosophy of [[judaism|Judaism]], **awe** (Hebrew
*yir'ah*) is the disposition that mediates between [[radical-amazement|wonder]] and faith.
"The beginning of awe is wonder, and the beginning of wisdom is awe." It is "an intuition
for the creaturely dignity of all things and their preciousness to God" — "a way of being
in rapport with the mystery of all reality."

## Awe versus Fear

Heschel sharply distinguishes awe from fear. Fear anticipates evil and makes us shrink;
awe draws us toward what inspires it and is compatible with love and joy. Citing the Hebrew
lexicon, he notes that "in regard to God, *yirah* is used in the Bible primarily in the
sense of awe." Both fear of punishment in this life and fear of punishment in the afterlife
are "considered inferior in Jewish tradition." Job — "Though He slay me, yet will I trust in
Him" — exemplifies piety motivated "not by fear but rather by awe." (Heschel notes the
School of Shammai held an opposite view of the relation of fear and love.)

## Awe as the Root of Faith and Religion

"Awe precedes faith; it is at the root of faith." Awe is epistemically generative:
"Knowledge is fostered by curiosity; wisdom is fostered by awe." Heschel observes that the
biblical religious person is called not "believer" but *yare hashem* ("one who is in awe of
the Lord"), so that *yirat hashem* is nearly equivalent to "religion" itself. Its loss is
corrosive: "Forfeit your sense of awe … and the universe becomes a market place for you."

## Relation to Other Concepts

Awe is one of the data of [[depth-theology]] and stands between [[radical-amazement]] and
the sense of [[the-ineffable]] in Heschel's account of how awareness of God arises. It is
closely tied to his reading of the [[kavod|glory]] (*kavod*) as the perceptible presence of
God in the world.
