# Word of God

The concept of the "Word of God" (*Wort Gottes*; Lat. *Verbum Dei*) is one of the central organizing categories of Protestant theology, denoting God's self-communication to humanity. In its biblical usage the phrase encompasses divine address through the prophets and apostles, written Scripture, and the person of Jesus Christ identified with the eternal Logos (John 1:1). In Reformation and Reformed theology it becomes the criterion by which all church proclamation is measured.

## Barth's Threefold Form

Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* I.1 (§§3–5) develops the doctrine of the Word of God as the foundation of all dogmatic work. Barth distinguishes three forms of the one Word:

1. **The revealed Word** — Jesus Christ himself, the primary and original form
2. **The written Word** — Holy Scripture as witness to revelation
3. **The proclaimed Word** — Church proclamation as promise of revelation

These are not three different Words but one Word in three related forms. Scripture is authoritative not as an inerrant text in itself but as it witnesses to revelation; proclamation is authoritative as it faithfully repeats the scriptural witness.

See also [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1]] for the full development of this schema in §§3–4.

## The Nature of the Word of God (Barth, §5)

In §5 of *Church Dogmatics* I.1 (lines 5763–8564), Barth develops the *nature* of the Word by asking not what it *is* (which would require a definition standing above it) but *how* it is — the attributes it displays in its three forms. The governing epistemological constraint: "This How is the attainable human reflection of the unattainable divine What" (line 6086). Three attributes are developed sequentially — speech, act, mystery — where each is an exegesis of the preceding, not a replacement for it.

### The Word as Speech

God's Word means, first and literally, that God speaks. "Speaking is not a 'symbol'" (Barth against Tillich, line 6094); speech corresponds to "the possibility which God has chosen and actualised at all events in His Church." Barth draws three implications:

**Spiritual nature**: Speech is "the form in which reason communicates with reason and person with person" (lines 6187–6189). The Word is primarily spiritual, then physical — there is "an upper and lower aspect ... which ... is not to be effaced or reversed" (line 6135). Against Rudolf Otto: "the Word of God — and at this point we should not evade a term so much tabooed to-day — is a rational and not an irrational event" (lines 6193–6194).

**Personal quality**: "God's Word is not a thing to be described nor a term to be defined. It is neither a matter nor an idea ... It is the truth as it is God's speaking person, *Dei loquentis persona*" (lines 6263–6265). The identification of God's Word with God's Son (Jesus Christ as the Logos, John 1:1; Rev. 19:12–13) means the Word cannot be systematized into a corpus of fixed propositions: "The only system in Holy Scripture and proclamation is revelation, i.e., Jesus Christ" (line 6318). God is "not bound" to the wording of Scripture but it to him; he retains "free control over the wording of Holy Scripture" (line 6380).

**Purposive character / address**: The Word is always directed to us, but not essentially so — God's love is free and unmerited. "God would be no less God if He had created no world and no man" (lines 6408–6409). The Word's being directed to us is pure grace. As address it has four dimensions: (1) it is a Word we cannot say to ourselves — "encounter with the Word of God is genuine, irrevocable encounter ... that can never be dissolved in union" (lines 6479–6480); (2) it is the Word of the Creator that "smites us in our existence"; (3) it is the Word of reconciliation; (4) it is the eschatological Word of the Redeemer, the "hidden Lord of all times."

### The Word as Act

The key thesis: "The Word of God is itself the act of God. It is act to a degree that everything else that we usually call act, event, practice, life, etc. ... can only seem to be very questionable as real act in comparison with it. The Word of God makes history in the supreme sense" (lines 6570–6576). The word/act distinction applicable to human speech — where word is mere self-expression and act is alteration of the external world — collapses when God speaks, because God is the Lord of history whose self-expression is itself absolute alteration.

Three implications:

**Contingent contemporaneity**: The three times of the Word (revelation, Scripture, proclamation) are differentiated not merely chronologically but by order. True contemporaneity across them cannot be achieved by immanent historical understanding (as Lessing sought to cross the "ugly, wide ditch" via inner experience) but only by God's free act. The formula: *illic et tunc* (there and then) becoming *hic et nunc* (here and now) — always contingently (lines 6811–6827). Heb. 13:8: "Jesus Christ the same yesterday and today."

**Power to rule**: The Word exercises lordship. Its promise, claim, judgment, and blessing are not empty declarations that await human response to become effective; they transform the situation of the hearer absolutely. "The promise of the Word of God is not as such an empty pledge ... It is the transposing of man into the wholly new state of one who has accepted and appropriated the promise" (lines 6995–6997). The world already stands under Christ's lordship whether it acknowledges it or not.

**Decision**: The Word is not universally present and ascertainable like created realities; it is always real "*suo modo, sua libertate, sua misericordia*" (in its own way, in its own freedom, in its own mercy; line 7245). As divine decision it creates, rather than responds to, the human decision of faith or unbelief. Not every calling (*klēsis*) results in election (*eklogē*); the Word can be heard and yet the choice that takes place therein may be the choice of gracelessness.

### The Word as Mystery

"Mystery" is not a fourth attribute alongside speech and act but a final qualification reminding that even the careful delimitations achieved so far do not make God's Word available to theological mastery. Drawing on the NT sense of *mystērion*: "Mystery is the concealment of God in which He meets us precisely when He unveils Himself to us, because He will not and cannot unveil Himself except by veiling Himself" (lines 7555–7557).

Three forms of the Word's mystery:

**Secularity**: The Word always comes in secular form — the Church is also a sociological entity, the Bible is also a Near Eastern historical document, "Jesus Christ is also in fact the Rabbi of Nazareth who is hard to know historically" (line 7570). "Revelation means the incarnation of the Word of God. But incarnation means entry into this secularity" (lines 7732–7733). This secularity is not a defect but constitutive: God veils himself in unveiling. The framework is Luther's *theologia crucis* against *theologia gloriae*: knowledge of God is always mediated through the suffering secularity of the crucified, never by direct access to divine majesty (*speculatio Maiestatis*).

**One-sidedness**: The Word does not come partly veiled and partly unveiled, but either veiled or unveiled, never in a synthesis we can achieve. "Our thinking can be realistic or idealistic but it cannot be Christian" (lines 8063–8064). Faith commits the synthesis to God and finds it there. The pattern recurs throughout Scripture: Law and Gospel, threat and promise, cross and resurrection can only be heard one at a time; the other is held only in faith.

**Spirituality**: The mystery of the Word is that it can only be heard through the Holy Spirit. "The Lord of speech is also the Lord of our hearing. The Lord who gives the Word is also the Lord who gives faith" (lines 8374–8376). No human method — no hermeneutical technique, no existential borderline experience (Tillich), no philosophical pre-understanding (Bultmann) — can guarantee the event of hearing. "Faith is the work of the Holy Spirit" (line 8516). The conclusion of §5: "It is on our lips and in our hearts as the mystery of the Spirit who is the Lord" (lines 8562–8563).

## Relation to Other Concepts

The Word of God as Barth develops it connects to:
- [[theology-of-the-cross]] — the secularity and hiddenness of the Word is the *theologia crucis* as a hermeneutical principle
- [[revelation]] — revelation (Jesus Christ) is the original form of the Word, presupposed by Scripture and proclamation
- [[trinity]] — the equation of Word and Son (Logos) grounds the threefold form in the immanent Trinity
- [[incarnation]] — the secularity of the Word is the consequence of the Word becoming flesh
- [[predestination]] — the Word as decision/act connects to election; not every calling results in election
