# Reconciliation

## Definition

In Christian theology, *reconciliation* (*katallage*, Greek; *reconciliatio*, Latin) designates the restoration of fellowship between God and humanity that had been broken by sin. The term derives from Paul: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Cor. 5:19); "we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son" (Rom. 5:10). In Barth's *Church Dogmatics*, reconciliation is given a specific structural role: it is the economic act *appropriated to the Son* (the second mode of being), distinguishing the Son's work from the Father's work as Creator and the Spirit's work as Redeemer.

## Barth's Structural Usage (Church Dogmatics I.1, §11)

Barth distinguishes three modes of God's economic work corresponding to the three modes of God's immanent being:

| Mode of being | Economic work | Problem addressed |
|---------------|---------------|-------------------|
| Father | Creation | Creature's dependence on Creator |
| Son | **Reconciliation** | Sinner's enmity against God |
| Holy Spirit | Redemption | Man's incapacity to receive revelation |

The distinction between creation and reconciliation is not quantitative but qualitative. The Creator-creature distinction involves infinite distance (*finitum non capax infiniti*) but not enmity; the problem reconciliation addresses is different: "sin and death lie between this world and our world" (*CD* I.1, line 5481). Reconciliation is required not merely because man is finite but because man is a sinner — *homo peccator non capax verbi divini* (man as sinner incapable of the divine Word, line 5346). The sinner stands not merely at a distance from God but in active enmity; reconciliation means God addressing this enemy as a friend.

**Reconciliation = revelation**: "To the extent that God in the fact of His revelation treats His enemies as His friends; to the extent that in the fact of revelation God's enemies already are actually His friends, revelation is itself reconciliation" (*CD* I.1, lines 5433–5436). Reconciliation does not come *after* revelation as a result; the revelatory act *is* the reconciling act.

**Reconciliation vs. redemption**: Reconciliation is present and accomplished; redemption remains eschatological. "Alongside this New Testament understanding of reconciliation (if connected with it in the closest possible way) there stands a second understanding of salvation ... which we can sum up in the concept of redemption or liberation or deliverance" (*CD* I.1, lines 5439–5441). Reconciliation designates what has been accomplished in and through the event of God's revelation in Jesus Christ; redemption designates the consummation that remains a future hope, the Spirit's work. See [[redemption]].

**Why reconciliation requires the Son's full deity**: A "superhuman or semi-divine" reconciler would not match the seriousness of the problem. "Who can reveal God except God Himself? ... If He reveals God, then irrespective of His creaturehood He Himself has to be God. And since this is a case of either/or, He has to be full and true God without reduction or limitation" (*CD* I.1, lines 5309–5317). The same argument applies to reconciliation: if the Reconciler were not fully God, the enmity between God and man would not be genuinely overcome.

## Reception and Cross-Tradition Perspectives

- **Anselm (satisfaction theory)**: Reconciliation requires a satisfaction for sin offered by one who is both human (to represent humanity) and divine (to offer a satisfaction of infinite worth). Barth's structural use of "reconciliation" as the Son's appropriated work is compatible with Anselm's framework, though Barth does not develop atonement theory in CD I.1.
- **Luther**: Reconciliation through the cross; Barth cites Luther extensively in §11 to argue that the God revealed *sub contraria specie* (under the opposite form) at the cross is nonetheless the fully divine God, not a semi-divine mediator.
- **Ritschl (liberal Protestantism)**: Barth explicitly targets Ritschlian reductions that emphasize the *beneficia Christi* (benefits of Christ) without the "antecedently in Himself" — the eternal being of the Son as Son. If reconciliation means only Christ's historical "for us" without an eternal grounding, "man is thus thought of as indispensable to God ... this destroys God's freedom in the act of revelation and reconciliation, i.e., it destroys the gracious character of this act" (*CD* I.1, lines 6078–6079).
- **Later Barth (CD IV)**: *Church Dogmatics* IV bears the title "The Doctrine of Reconciliation" — in CD I.1 we have the structural groundwork; in CD IV the fully developed account.
