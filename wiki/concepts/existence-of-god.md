Maimonides' philosophical proofs — drawn from Aristotelian physics and metaphysics — for three
propositions he regards as the bedrock of rational theology: that God *exists*, that God is
*One*, and that God is *incorporeal*. These proofs appear in Part II, Introduction through
Chapter XXIII of the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]].

## The Twenty-Five Propositions

Before mounting his proofs, [[maimonides|Maimonides]] enumerates twenty-five propositions
accepted by Aristotle and the Peripatetics as demonstrated. A selection of the most
consequential for the proofs:

- **Prop. I–II**: No actual infinite magnitude, and no co-existing infinite multitude of finite
  magnitudes.
- **Prop. III**: An infinite causal series is impossible — "if, e.g., one Intelligence were the
  cause of a second, the second the cause of a third, the third the cause of a fourth, and so on,
  the series could not be continued ad infinitum."
- **Prop. VII**: Everything that moves is divisible, therefore corporeal; what is indivisible
  cannot move and cannot be corporeal.
- **Prop. XII**: A force distributed throughout a finite corporeal object is itself finite.
- **Prop. XVI**: Incorporeal beings cannot be numbered except as causes and effects; they
  cannot be counted by spatial or quantitative discreteness.
- **Prop. XIX**: A thing whose existence is owed to external causes has only the *possibility*
  of existence; it does not exist necessarily.
- **Prop. XX**: What exists by necessity of its own essence can have no external cause.
- **Prop. XXI**: Anything composed of two elements depends on its composition; its existence
  is not necessitated by its own essence alone.
- **Prop. XXIII**: Whatever exists potentially, and whose essence includes a state of
  possibility, may at some time fail to exist.

## The Withheld Twenty-Sixth Proposition

Maimonides sets the twenty-sixth proposition — "Time and motion are eternal, constant, and in
actual existence" — aside as an unproved hypothesis. He admits it *provisionally*:

> "We do not accept — namely, the proposition which affirms the Eternity of the Universe, but
> we will admit it for the present, because by doing so we shall be enabled clearly to
> demonstrate our own theory." (Introduction)

He regards Aristotle's eternity-proofs as "not conclusive" and as "only apparent and plausible"
(Ch. XV), but equally dismisses the Mutakallimūn's *a priori* impossibility claim. His
hermeneutical move is to prove existence, unity, and incorporeality on *both* hypotheses —
eternity and creation — so the theological core does not depend on settling the cosmological
question.

## Hermeneutical Method

This section of the *Guide* operates through **demonstrative syllogism from granted premises**
rather than from Scripture. Maimonides explicitly states that his purpose is not to copy
philosophical books but to use philosophical demonstrations to remove doubts about Scriptural
passages (Ch. II note). The proofs are Aristotelian in form; the conclusions are Jewish
theological commitments. The *Guide* in this range operates at the *sod* (hidden/rational) level
of meaning: philosophical truth that elucidates the esoteric sense of Torah.

## The Four Philosophical Proofs of God's Existence

### First Proof: From the Motion of the Spheres

By Propositions XVII, I, II, XII, IX, and XXVI (admitted hypothetically), Maimonides eliminates
all possible loci for the Prime Mover of the celestial spheres:

1. The mover cannot be a corporeal body *outside* the sphere (would itself require a mover → 
   infinite regress, violating Prop. II).
2. The mover cannot be a force *distributed throughout* the sphere (any finite body contains
   only a finite force by Prop. XII; cannot produce infinite circular motion).
3. The mover cannot be an *indivisible force residing in* the sphere as the soul resides in a
   body (such a force moves accidentally; accidental motion must cease, Prop. VIII; the sphere
   would stop).

Conclusion: "the efficient cause of the motion of the sphere… is neither itself corporeal nor
does it reside in a corporeal object; it must move neither of its own accord nor accidentally; it
must be indivisible and unchangeable (Prop. VII and Prop. V). **This Prime Motor of the sphere
is God, praised be His name!**" (Ch. I)

### Second Proof: From the Series Motum/Motor

Following Aristotle, all members of a chain of moved-movers ultimately require a terminus that
is a mover but not itself moved: "a motor must exist without being at the same time a motum, and
that is the Prime Motor, which, not being subject to motion, is indivisible, incorporeal, and
independent of time" (Ch. I).

### Third Proof: From Contingency and Necessary Existence

From the observable fact that transient things exist, Maimonides argues (Ch. I):

> "Since there are undoubtedly beings of a temporary existence, there must also be an eternal
> being that is not subject to destruction, and whose existence is real, not merely possible."

If a being's necessity derives from an external cause, that external cause is itself the truly
necessary being (Prop. XIX). Therefore "there must be a being which has absolutely independent
existence, and is the source of the existence of all things" (Ch. I). Since its existence admits
no external cause (Prop. XX), it includes no plurality (Prop. XXI), and therefore cannot be
corporeal or a force in a body (Prop. XXII). **This being is God.**

### Fourth Proof: From the Transition from Potentiality to Actuality

Everything that passes from potentiality to actuality requires an external agent (Prop. XVIII).
The series cannot regress infinitely. There must therefore exist:

> "a cause of the transition of an object from the state of potentiality to that of actuality,
> which is constant, and admits of no potentiality whatever. In the essence of this cause nothing
> exists potentially, for if its essence included any possibility of existence it would not exist
> at all (Prop. XXIII): it cannot be corporeal, but it must be spiritual (Prop. XXIV); and the
> immaterial being that includes no possibility whatever, but exists actually by its own essence,
> is God." (Ch. I)

## Validity Under Both Hypotheses

Maimonides makes the independence of the proofs from the eternity question explicit:

> "Even if we were to admit the Eternity of the Universe, we could by any of these methods prove
> the existence of God; that He is One and incorporeal, and that He does not reside as a force in
> a corporeal object." (Ch. I)

And in Ch. II: "We have thus shown that whether we believe in the *Creatio ex Nihilo*, or in the
Eternity of the Universe, we can prove by demonstrative arguments the existence of God, i.e., an
absolute Being, whose existence cannot be attributed to any cause, or admit in itself any
potentiality."

## Proofs of Unity

**First argument for Unity**: Absolutely incorporeal beings cannot be numbered by
anything other than the cause-effect relation (Prop. XVI). Two Gods could not be
differentiated spatially or temporally. "The hypothesis that there exist two Gods is inadmissible,
because absolutely incorporeal beings cannot be counted (Prop. XVI), except as cause and
effect" (Ch. I).

**Second argument for Unity** (from the organic unity of the Universe): "The whole existing
world is one organic body, all parts of which are connected together." If two deities governed
different parts, either one would be inactive at times — implying potentiality and dependence —
or both must act together, in which case their combined action requires a unifying cause, which
is itself the true one God (Ch. I).

**Third argument**: If two beings each possessed "absolutely independent existence," that
independence would be an added property common to both, contradicting the absolute simplicity
required of a being that admits no composition (Ch. I).

## Proofs of Incorporeality

**First**: Every corporeal object is composed of matter and form (Prop. XXII); composition
implies dependence and therefore cannot characterise an absolutely independent being (Ch. I).

**Second**: Bodies are divisible and subject to accidents; nothing corporeal can be a unity in
the strict sense (Ch. I).

**Linked proofs**: Because the Necessary Existent admits no plurality (Prop. XXI), and
because all bodies are either distributed forces (Prop. XI) or composites (Prop. XXII), God can
be neither. "It is now clear that there must be a being with absolutely independent existence…
this being is God" (Ch. I).

## The Creation vs. Eternity Debate (Chs. XIII–XXIII)

Three positions on the universe's origin:

1. **Torah position** (First Theory): *creatio ex nihilo* — "God alone existed, and nothing
   else; neither angels, nor spheres, nor the things contained within the spheres." Time itself is
   created (Ch. XIII). Maimonides calls this "a fundamental principle of the Law of our teacher
   Moses; it is next in importance to the principle of God's unity" (Ch. XIII), attributing it
   also to Abraham.

2. **Plato's position** (Second Theory): God forms the universe from co-eternal primordial
   matter; the heavens are transient but their matter is eternal (Ch. XIII).

3. **Aristotle's position** (Third Theory): Universe is eternal, necessary, and unchangeable;
   time and motion have no beginning; the Prime Cause and its effect are inseparable (Ch. XIII).

Maimonides' critical judgment on Aristotle's proofs for eternity: "I am convinced… that what
Aristotle says on the Eternity of the Universe… cannot be proved, and that Aristotle never
intended to prove these things" (Ch. XV). He credits Aristotle himself with presenting the
eternity thesis as "most plausible" rather than demonstrated.

His own resolution (Ch. XVI): since neither creation nor eternity can be *demonstrated*, and
since the eternity arguments are not conclusive, he accepts creation "on the authority of
Prophecy, which can teach things beyond the reach of philosophical speculation." He then
argues (Chs. XVII–XXIII) that creation is *more philosophically defensible* than eternity,
particularly because:

- The variety of sphere-motions and the fixed positions of stars in their spheres cannot be
  explained by "fixed laws of Nature" but point to **design and will** (Chs. XIX, XXII).
- Aristotle's own account of the emanation of compound spheres from simple Intelligences is
  internally inconsistent (Ch. XXII).
- The properties of things in their fully developed state cannot be used to infer their
  condition at the moment of creation — the "orphan analogy" of Ch. XVII.

## Refutations of Aristotle's Eternity Arguments (Chs. XVII–XVIII)

- **Against "God would have changed from potentiality to actuality"**: An incorporeal being
  does not contain potentiality (Ch. XVIII). The Active Intellect, itself incorporeal, "acts at
  one time and does not act at another" without thereby undergoing a potentiality-actuality
  transition (Ch. XVIII, citing al-Fārābī's treatise on the Intellect).
- **Against "God's will is immutable"**: The true essence of divine will differs categorically
  from human will; "will" is used homonymously of man and God (Ch. XVIII).
- **Against "God's wisdom is eternal, so its products are eternal"**: We cannot understand why
  wisdom produced nine spheres rather than ten; by the same token we cannot rule out that wisdom
  caused the universe to exist at a particular time (Ch. XVIII).
