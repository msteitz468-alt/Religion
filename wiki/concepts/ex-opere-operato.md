# Ex Opere Operato (Sacramental Efficacy)

The principle that a sacrament's efficacy is assured **"by the work worked"** (*ex opere operato*) — by the divinely instituted act itself — rather than **"by the work of the worker"** (*ex opere operantis*), i.e. by the holiness of the minister. [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 6) traces its crystallization to [[augustine|Augustine's]] answer to the [[donatism|Donatists]].

## Origin in the Donatist Controversy

The [[donatism|Donatists]] made the validity of a sacrament depend on the purity of the minister: a *traditor* bishop's baptisms and ordinations were void. Against this, Augustine grounded the holiness of the church not in its members but in "the holiness of the grace dispensed in its sacraments." Citing [[optatus|Optatus]] — "the church is one, and its holiness is produced by the sacraments… not… on the basis of the pride of individuals" — he held that "baptism belongs to Christ, regardless of who may give it." The efficacy is therefore secured *ex opere operato*: the act, divinely instituted, conveys grace independently of the minister's worth. This is one expression of Pelikan's "[[grace|paradox of grace]]" — grace as **mediated**.

## Ambivalence

Pelikan notes the principle's double edge: it secures the **priority of divine initiative** (it is God, not the priest, who baptizes), yet it also opened the door for later medieval theology to relocate the decisive variable in "the perfection of man's readiness to accept the sacraments" (*ex opere operantis* on the recipient's side) — a tension that fed the Reformation disputes, where Augustine's anti-Pelagian doctrine of necessary grace was turned against his anti-Donatist doctrine of mediated grace.
