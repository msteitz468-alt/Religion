# Filioque

## Definition

*Filioque* is Latin for "and [from] the Son." The term refers to the clause added to the Western recension of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (381 CE), which originally stated that the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father" (*ek tou Patros ekporeuetai*, Jn. 15:26). Western churches came to confess that the Spirit proceeds "from the Father *and the Son*" (*ex Patre Filioque procedit*). The clause is the central doctrinal issue in the Great Schism between the Western (Roman Catholic) and Eastern (Orthodox) churches.

## Historical Development

The original Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (381) contained no *Filioque*. The addition emerged gradually:

- **6th-century Spain**: First interpolated into the creed at the Third Council of Toledo (589)
- **Carolingian Frankish church**: Adopted liturgically from the late 8th century
- **1014**: Received Roman endorsement when the *Filioque* was sung at the coronation Mass of the German Emperor Henry II in Rome
- **1054**: A major factor in the formal rupture between Rome and Constantinople (the Great Schism)
- **Councils of Lyons II (1274) and Florence (1439)**: Attempted reunions produced formulas affirming that the Spirit proceeds from Father and Son "as from one principle" (*tamquam ex uno principio*), which Eastern delegations later rejected

The Eastern position: the Spirit proceeds *ek monou tou Patros* — "from the Father alone." Patriarch Photius (9th c.) gave this position its classical theological defense.

## Theological Arguments

### Western Argument for the Filioque

1. **From revelation to immanence** (Barth's canonical formulation): "statements about the divine modes of being antecedently in themselves cannot be different in content from those that are to be made about their reality in revelation" (*Church Dogmatics* I.1, lines 9011–9013). In the economy of salvation, the Spirit is the Spirit of *both* Father and Son; this economic relationship must reflect God's immanent being. Augustine's foundational principle: "*Spiritus sanctus commune aliquid est Patris et Filii*" — "The Holy Spirit is something common to the Father and the Son" (*De Trinitate* VI.5.7).

2. **The Spirit as love/communion**: The Spirit is the act of mutual love between Father and Son — the "*communio consubstantialis*" (Augustine). If the Spirit is the bond of love between Father and Son, He must proceed from *both* as from their shared source. Barth: "The *Filioque* expresses recognition of the communion between the Father and the Son. The Holy Spirit is the love which is the essence of the relation between these two modes of being of God" (*CD* I.1, lines 9055–9057).

3. **One source, not two**: The *Filioque* does not mean the Spirit proceeds from two separate principles (which would be theological error). Lyons II and Florence insist: the Spirit proceeds from Father and Son "as from one principle." Barth: "not from two sources but from one source" — the *unitas* of Father and Son *as* Father and Son (*CD* I.1, lines 9358–9368).

### Eastern Argument Against the Filioque

1. **Textual basis**: John 15:26 states explicitly "the Spirit ... proceeds from the Father." No text equally clearly names the Son as a co-principle of procession.

2. **Monarche of the Father**: In Eastern theology the Father is the sole *arche* (source, principle) of divinity. The Son is begotten from the Father; the Spirit proceeds from the Father. To add the Son as a second source of the Spirit either introduces a second *arche* (threatening ditheism) or requires mediation (the Spirit proceeding through the Son from the Father — the *dia tou Huiou* formula).

3. **Canonical authority**: The Council of Ephesus (431) forbade "composing, writing, or devising any other faith" than the Nicene Creed. The Western addition thus lacks conciliar authority.

4. **Relation to tritheism**: Eastern theology accused the *Filioque* of dividing the divine unity and tending toward tritheism by positing the Father-Son dyad as a collective principle.

### Barth's Assessment

Barth sides decisively with the Western tradition on material grounds. He grants the Eastern position coherence as an interpretation of isolated biblical texts but argues that "this whole insight and outlook is lost when the immanent *Filioque* is denied. If the Spirit is also the Spirit of the Son only in revelation and for faith, if He is only the Spirit of the Father in eternity, i.e., in His true and original reality, then the fellowship of the Spirit between God and man is without objective ground or content" (*CD* I.1, lines 9074–9083). Without the *Filioque*, the eschatological work of the Spirit (redemption, divine sonship) becomes unmoored from the historical work of the Son (reconciliation) and the God-human relation in the Spirit bypasses the Mediator.

Barth also warns against the Eastern tendency he identifies in Gregory of Nyssa's "three torches" metaphor: a chain of derivation (Father → Son → Spirit) that, while avoiding a literal two-source reading, implicitly subordinates the Spirit to the Son and the Son to the Father, threatening "tritheism," which "was always the special danger in Eastern theology" (*CD* I.1, line 9170).
