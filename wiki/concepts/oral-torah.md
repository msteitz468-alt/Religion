The Oral Torah (*Torah she-be'al peh*) is the body of legal interpretation, commentary, and tradition that rabbinic Judaism holds was transmitted orally alongside the Written Torah (the Pentateuch) from Moses at Sinai. The claim that an authoritative oral tradition accompanies the written text is the foundational premise of the entire rabbinic enterprise — the basis upon which the [[mishna|Mishna]], the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]], and all subsequent rabbinic literature rests.

## Definition and Content

The Oral Torah encompasses:
- **Interpretive traditions**: How specific commandments are to be observed in practice (what "Sabbath rest" means, how circumcision is to be performed, etc.)
- **Hermeneutical rules**: The [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot of Rabbi Ishmael]] and other systems for deriving new law from scriptural text
- **Takanot and gezerot**: Rabbinic legislation (enactments and fences around the law) not explicitly in Scripture
- **Transmitted rulings** (*halakha le-Moshe mi-Sinai*): Specific practices attributed directly to the Mosaic transmission

The oral character was originally a defining feature: writing down the Oral Torah was forbidden, as it was held that the written and oral Torahs had different modes of transmission and authority. [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi's]] compilation of the [[mishna|Mishna]] (c. 200 CE) broke from this principle as an emergency measure to prevent the tradition's loss.

## Historical Development

The chain of transmission (*shalshelet ha-kabbalah*) presented in *Avot* 1:1: "Moses received the Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua; Joshua to the Elders; the Elders to the Prophets; the Prophets transmitted it to the Men of the Great Assembly."

The **Tannaim** (c. 10–200 CE) are the Mishnaic-era sages whose oral disputes and rulings are preserved in the Mishna. The **Amoraim** (c. 200–500 CE) comment on and develop Tannaitic rulings in the Gemara. Both generations understood themselves as transmitters and interpreters of an unbroken tradition from Sinai.

## The Foundational Controversy

The claim of a Sinaitic Oral Torah is the **central fault line** in ancient and modern Jewish religious identity. See [[oral-torah-authority|Oral Torah Authority: The Pharisees, Sadducees, and Karaites]].

- **Sadducees** (Second Temple period): Accepted only the Written Torah as binding; rejected oral traditions as human additions. Their literal reading of the Written Torah (e.g., "eye for an eye" = literal bodily punishment; "burning" = fire, not strangulation) was consistently opposed by the Pharisees.
- **Karaites** (from 8th century CE onward): Rejected the Talmud and rabbinic oral tradition entirely, returning to Scripture alone. At their peak they represented a significant fraction of world Jewry.
- **Modern historical-critical scholarship**: Does not accept the Sinaitic origin claim; reads the Oral Torah's development as a historical process of legal evolution and innovation from the Second Temple period onward.
- **Rabbinic Judaism**: The Oral Torah's Sinaitic authority is non-negotiable; its transmission is the chain that gives the entire rabbinic enterprise its legitimacy.

## Relationship to Written Torah

The Oral Torah is not simply "commentary" in the modern sense — it is understood as co-equal in authority to the Written Torah and in some respects primary. The Written Torah requires the Oral Torah to be intelligible and actionable: the Written Torah says "you shall slaughter your animals as I have commanded you" (Deut 12:21), but the method of slaughter (*shechita*) is nowhere specified in writing. Only the Oral Torah supplies it.

## Cross-Tradition Analogies

The concept of an authoritative interpretive tradition accompanying a foundational text has loose analogies in other traditions:
- **Christian apostolic tradition**: The claim that the church's interpretive tradition (especially in Catholic and Orthodox ecclesiology) carries authority alongside Scripture
- **Islamic ijmāʿ and hadith**: The tradition of prophetic practice and scholarly consensus as interpretive supplements to the Quran

These are analogies, not equivalents; the institutional and theological structures differ significantly.
