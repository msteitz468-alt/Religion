The Oral Torah (*Torah she-be'al peh*) is the body of legal interpretation, commentary, and tradition that rabbinic Judaism holds was transmitted orally alongside the Written Torah (the Pentateuch) from Moses at Sinai. The claim that an authoritative oral tradition accompanies the written text is the foundational premise of the entire rabbinic enterprise — the basis upon which the [[mishna|Mishna]], the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]], and all subsequent rabbinic literature rests.

## Definition and Content

The Oral Torah encompasses:
- **Interpretive traditions**: How specific commandments are to be observed in practice (what "Sabbath rest" means, how circumcision is to be performed, etc.)
- **Hermeneutical rules**: The [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot of Rabbi Ishmael]] and other systems for deriving new law from scriptural text
- **Takanot and gezerot**: Rabbinic legislation (enactments and fences around the law) not explicitly in Scripture
- **Transmitted rulings** (*halakha le-Moshe mi-Sinai*): Specific practices attributed directly to the Mosaic transmission

The oral character was originally a defining feature: writing down the Oral Torah was forbidden, as it was held that the written and oral Torahs had different modes of transmission and authority. [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi's]] compilation of the [[mishna|Mishna]] (c. 200 CE) broke from this principle as an emergency measure to prevent the tradition's loss.

## Historical Development

The chain of transmission (*shalshelet ha-kabbalah*) presented in *Avot* 1:1: "Moses received the Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua; Joshua to the Elders; the Elders to the Prophets; the Prophets transmitted it to the Men of the Great Assembly."

The **Tannaim** (c. 10–200 CE) are the Mishnaic-era sages whose oral disputes and rulings are preserved in the Mishna. The **Amoraim** (c. 200–500 CE) comment on and develop Tannaitic rulings in the Gemara. Both generations understood themselves as transmitters and interpreters of an unbroken tradition from Sinai.

## The Foundational Controversy

The claim of a Sinaitic Oral Torah is the **central fault line** in ancient and modern Jewish religious identity. See [[oral-torah-authority|Oral Torah Authority: The Pharisees, Sadducees, and Karaites]].

- **Sadducees** (Second Temple period): Accepted only the Written Torah as binding; rejected oral traditions as human additions. Their literal reading of the Written Torah (e.g., "eye for an eye" = literal bodily punishment; "burning" = fire, not strangulation) was consistently opposed by the Pharisees.
- **Karaites** (from 8th century CE onward): Rejected the Talmud and rabbinic oral tradition entirely, returning to Scripture alone. At their peak they represented a significant fraction of world Jewry.
- **Modern historical-critical scholarship**: Does not accept the Sinaitic origin claim; reads the Oral Torah's development as a historical process of legal evolution and innovation from the Second Temple period onward.
- **Rabbinic Judaism**: The Oral Torah's Sinaitic authority is non-negotiable; its transmission is the chain that gives the entire rabbinic enterprise its legitimacy.

## Relationship to Written Torah

The Oral Torah is not simply "commentary" in the modern sense — it is understood as co-equal in authority to the Written Torah and in some respects primary. The Written Torah requires the Oral Torah to be intelligible and actionable: the Written Torah says "you shall slaughter your animals as I have commanded you" (Deut 12:21), but the method of slaughter (*shechita*) is nowhere specified in writing. Only the Oral Torah supplies it.

## Cross-Tradition Analogies

The concept of an authoritative interpretive tradition accompanying a foundational text has loose analogies in other traditions:
- **Christian apostolic tradition**: The claim that the church's interpretive tradition (especially in Catholic and Orthodox ecclesiology) carries authority alongside Scripture
- **Islamic ijmāʿ and hadith**: The tradition of prophetic practice and scholarly consensus as interpretive supplements to the Quran

These are analogies, not equivalents; the institutional and theological structures differ significantly.

## France on Matthew and the Tradition of the Elders (NICNT, 2007)

The material above records the Oral Torah as rabbinic Judaism understands it. A second angle
enters with [[rt-france|R. T. France]]'s commentary on [[matthew|Matthew]] (NICNT, 2007), which
reads the gospel's conflict stories as an external, and hostile, first-century witness to the
scribal tradition in formation. What France offers is not a description of the Oral Torah in its
own voice but an account of what Matthew's Jesus is portrayed as contesting.

**The technical term.** France identifies "the tradition of the elders" (15:2) as "a technical
term for Pharisaic oral tradition, in contrast to the written Mosaic law," noting the irony that
Jesus counters with "your own tradition," and that codification into the Mishnah came only at
the end of the second century.

**Dating and evidentiary caution.** France reports that the two lengthy sabbath tractates
(*Šabbat* and *ʿErubin*), though not reaching final form until the end of the second century,
are "agreed to represent in principle the sort of discussions and rulings in which Pharisaic
scribes were engaged in Jesus' day." He is explicitly inconclusive elsewhere: on the extension
of priestly handwashing to ordinary food and non-priests, "it is uncertain how far this process
had advanced by the time of Jesus." He describes the move from the David narrative to the
priestly principle in Matt 12 in the tradition's own idiom — "in rabbinic terms, we move now
from haggadah to halakhah."

**Specific halakhic material France reports.** On the [[sabbath]]: exceptions were normally
allowed only where there was imminent danger of death (m. Yoma 8:6), plus assistance in
childbirth (m. Šabb. 18:3); later discussion at b. Šabb. 128b concludes that relief of animal
suffering should override the regulation, against the unequivocal Qumran prohibition at CD
11:13-14. On vows: *qorbān* (and the equivalent *qōnām*), from tractate *Nedarim*, is a formula
dedicating food, money, or property to God, so that "anything so dedicated was thus placed out
of reach of other people who might otherwise have a claim on it, and the formula seems to have
been deliberately used for this purpose." France reports m. Ned. 9:1 debating whether the fifth
commandment sufficed to release such a vow, "with the balance of opinion tilting in favor of
release; apparently Jesus was aware of a more rigorous ruling," and m. Ned. 5:6, where a man who
had excluded his father by such a vow gave his courtyard to a friend so the father could attend
a grandson's wedding — showing both that the vow was treated as irrevocable and that the son
retained use of the property. He acknowledges an unresolved mechanical question: "what is not so
clear is how such a dedication could be made without the donor also losing the right to his own
property." On [[divorce]]: the rabbinic debate concerned "not the legitimacy of divorce in
itself, which everyone seems to have taken for granted," but its permissible grounds, and m.
Giṭ. 9:10 preserves the Shammaite, Hillelite, and Akiban positions.

**France's account of the challenge.** He argues that Jesus' reply at 15:3 does not engage
handwashing at all but "puts in question the whole concept of religious authority which it
embodies," and diagnoses the scribal failure over *qorbān* precisely: the approach "did not
inquire whether the vow was appropriate in the first place... and obstinately insisted on the
validity of the vow regardless of the consequences." On the sabbath he holds that the combined
effect of "The Son of Man is the Lord of the sabbath" (12:8) and "It is permissible to do good
on the sabbath" (12:12b) is "to call in question the whole scribal industry of sabbath
regulation."

France nevertheless draws a boundary that limits the scope of the attack: challenging the
scribes' right to impose nonbiblical rules is, on his reading, legitimate scribal debate and
puts Jesus in no tension with the OT law itself. See [[old-law]], [[purity]].

*Register note*: this is a Christian commentary on a polemical gospel narrative. It is recorded
here as France's reading of Matthew's portrayal, not as a description of rabbinic tradition on
its own terms.
