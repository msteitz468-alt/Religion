# Revelation (Heschel's Theory)

Part II of [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]] develops
[[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]]'s constructive account of **revelation** — the
counterpart to his thesis of [[divine-pathos|God in search of man]]. Revelation is "a moment
in which God succeeded in reaching man; an event to God and an event to man." It is the
reversal of mysticism: "the mystic experience is an ecstasy of man; revelation is an ecstasy
of God."

## Event, Not Process

Revelation belongs to the category of the *unique event*, not the repeatable *process*:
"Prophetic inspiration must be understood as an event, not as a process … Processes are
typical; events are unique. A process follows a law, events create a precedent." This is why
Judaism is a **[[religion-of-time]]** — God "spoke through events in history," and the
patriarchs "are not principles to be comprehended but lives to be continued." Grasping
revelation requires a "category of uniqueness" that repeatable-process science lacks.

## The Paradox of Sinai and the Language of Understatement

For modern man the puzzle is "how is revelation possible?"; for the biblical man it was "how
can man endure the shattering presence of God?" The burning bush is the prototype: "to
reveal He must conceal, that to impart His wisdom He must hide His power." Sinai "did and did
not" bring God to earth — "both an event to God and an event to man." Because the reality
exceeds speech, revelation can only be told by **understatement** and *via negationis* — "we
can only say what it is not" (the paradigm is Elijah's "still small voice," 1 Kings 19). The
biblical account is therefore itself interpretation: "As a report about revelation the Bible
itself is a midrash." "God spoke" is not *less* than literal (mere metaphor) but *more* than
literal — a mystery; its language is **indicative, not descriptive**.

## Co-Revelation: "The Word of God and Man"

Revelation is not a monologue: "prophecy consists of a revelation of God and a co-revelation
of man." The prophet is neither a passive dictation-machine nor a self-generating genius; his
"response … turns revelation into a dialogue." Hence "the Bible is more than the word of God:
it is the word of God and man; a record of both revelation and response." God "does not
reveal Himself; he only reveals His way." Authority is not chronological — "We accept the
authority of the Pentateuch not because it is Mosaic, but because Moses was a prophet"; "the
act of revelation is a mystery, while the record of revelation is a literary fact."

## Continuous Understanding; the Torah in Exile

"Judaism is based upon a minimum of revelation and a maximum of interpretation … The Bible
is a seed, God is the sun, but we are the soil." The oral Torah and the sages' ongoing
understanding are constitutive; pure scripturalism (the [[karaism|Karaites]]) is judged a
failed experiment. The present Torah is itself partial and accommodated — "Just as the
Shechinah is in exile, so is the Torah in exile" — awaiting a messianic disclosure.

## A Two-Front Battle (Crux)

Heschel positions his theory explicitly between two rejected extremes: **fundamentalist**
verbal-dictation literalism ("every word literally valid, no distinction between the eternal
and the temporal") and **rationalist/critical** reduction of Scripture to myth or poetry.
"Philosophy of religion has to carry on a battle on two fronts." Biblical criticism may
correct our account of transmission but "the act of revelation remains beyond its scope."
This middle position is in tension with strict Orthodox verbal-inspiration doctrines (his
approving citation that Moses may have "ascribed his order to the authority of the Lord")
and with liberal denial of revelation alike.
