# Jhāna (Meditative Absorption)

**Jhāna** (Pali; Sanskrit *dhyāna*) is the Theravada term for the states of meditative **absorption**
— deep, stable, unified concentration in which the mind becomes fully absorbed in its object and
temporarily freed from the hindrances. The *[[visuddhimagga|Visuddhimagga]]* (Part II, Chs. III–XIII)
provides the most systematic classical account of the jhānas in Theravada.

## The Five Hindrances (*Nīvaraṇa*)

Before jhāna can be achieved, the five hindrances must be suppressed:
1. **Lust/sensual desire** (*kāmacchanda*)
2. **Ill will** (*byāpāda*)
3. **Stiffness and torpor** (*thīna-middha*)
4. **Agitation and worry** (*uddhacca-kukkucca*)
5. **Uncertainty/doubt** (*vicikicchā*)

These are suppressed — not permanently destroyed — by the power of concentration. Each is abandoned
by a specific jhāna factor that is its direct opposite.

## Access Concentration (*Upacāra-samādhi*)

Before full absorption (*appanā*), the meditator achieves **access concentration**: the hindrances
are suppressed, the counterpart-sign (*paṭibhāga-nimitta*) has appeared, and the mind is on the
threshold of absorption. This is sufficient as the basis for insight (*vipassanā*) in the
"dry-insight" (*sukkha-vipassaka*) path.

## The Four Material Jhānas

### Fivefold vs. Fourfold Counting

Theravada recognizes two counting systems:
- **Fourfold** (standard, from the suttas): four jhānas
- **Fivefold** (Abhidhamma elaboration): the first jhāna is split into two (with and without
  applied thought), yielding five material jhānas

The *Visuddhimagga* presents the fivefold system for analytical purposes; the fourfold is the
canonical norm.

### The First Jhāna

**Jhāna factors present**: applied thought (*vitakka*), sustained thought (*vicāra*), joy (*pīti*),
pleasure (*sukha*), one-pointedness (*ekaggatā*).

Entered by full absorption in the meditation object (the *nimitta*). Applied thought = directing
the mind to the object; sustained thought = holding it there; joy = the joyful interest in the
object; pleasure = the bodily/mental pleasure; one-pointedness = the stillness and unification of
mind. The hindrances are suppressed.

**Five kinds of mastery** (*vasiṭā*): adverting, attaining, resolving [duration], emerging,
reviewing — each must be mastered at every jhāna before ascending.

### The Second Jhāna

Applied thought and sustained thought abandoned. Factors: **joy, pleasure, one-pointedness**. The
mind is internally settled, "single-pointed" without effort.

### The Third Jhāna

Joy abandoned (joy is "coarse" at this stage). Factors: **pleasure, one-pointedness**, plus
**mindfulness and full awareness** (*sati-sampajañña*) as secondary features. The Noble Ones
describe the one with this attainment as "one who, with equanimity, is mindful."

### The Fourth Jhāna

Pleasure abandoned in favor of **equanimity** (*upekkhā*). Factors: **equanimity and one-pointedness**.
This is the "springboard" for the supernormal direct-knowledges (*abhiññā*) and for the immaterial
attainments. In-breath and out-breath cease at this stage (**bodily formation** (*kāya-saṅkhāra*)
= breathing, ceases at fourth jhāna).

## The Four Immaterial (*Arūpa*) Jhānas

Achieved by transcending all perception of form:

### Base of Boundless Space (*Ākāsānañcāyatana*)

Attending to "space is infinite" by removing the kasina-object and attending to the space it occupied.

### Base of Boundless Consciousness (*Viññāṇañcāyatana*)

Attending to "consciousness is infinite" — the previous jhāna's consciousness pervading everywhere.

### Base of Nothingness (*Ākiñcaññāyatana*)

Attending to "there is nothing" — the consciousness of the previous base is not seen as present;
non-existence of the previous object.

### Base of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception (*Nevasaññānāsaññāyatana*)

The subtlest attainment. Too subtle to be called "perception" since it is extremely refined; but not
"non-perception" since there is still a residual function. **Verbal formations** (*vacī-saṅkhāra*)
= applied thought and sustained thought, cease at the second jhāna; **mental formations**
(*citta-saṅkhāra*) = perception and feeling, cease only at the attainment of cessation, which
follows this base.

## The Attainment of Cessation (*Nirodha-samāpatti*)

Available only to non-returners and Arahants who have mastered all eight attainments. The
progressive suppression of consciousness through the jhānas (with the preparatory task at the base
of nothingness) leads to the **complete cessation of consciousness and its concomitants**. The meditator
is without consciousness but not dead: bodily formations continue, heat and life span remain.
Lasts as long as predetermined (up to seven days). Emergence is by the arising of fruition
consciousness. Toward what does the mind of one who has emerged tend? Toward Nibbāna:
"his consciousness inclines to seclusion, leans to seclusion, tends to seclusion" (M I 302).

## Jhāna and the Path to Liberation

A central debate in Theravada (implicit throughout the *Visuddhimagga*) is whether jhāna is
required for liberation or whether access concentration suffices. Buddhaghosa's text describes both:

- **Jhāna-based path**: the meditator uses one of the eight attainments as a "springboard" — enters
  the jhāna, emerges, and then takes the jhāna factors themselves or other formations as the object
  of insight. The jhāna colors the resulting path (the path takes on the characteristics of the
  jhāna used).
- **Dry-insight** (*sukkha-vipassaka*) path: the meditator uses only access concentration as the
  basis for insight, never achieving full absorption. This yields the first jhāna's path.

## Jhāna Factors and Path Factors

At the moment of the noble path, the jhāna factors and the path factors occur together. Their
number depends on which jhāna was used as the basis:

| Jhāna as basis | Path jhāna factors |
|---|---|
| First (or dry-insight / unrelated formations) | 5 (applied thought + sustained thought + joy + pleasure + one-pointedness) |
| Second | 4 |
| Third | 3 |
| Fourth | 2 |
| Fifth (equanimity-jhāna) or any immaterial | 2 (equanimity + one-pointedness) |
