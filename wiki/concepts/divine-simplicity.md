# Divine Simplicity (Aquinas)

**Divine simplicity** is the doctrine that God is in no way composite — that there is no
real distinction of parts, principles, or aspects within God. In [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] it
is the controlling negative attribute of the *Prima Pars*, treated at [[summa-theologica|*Summa
Theologiae*]] I, Q3, and presupposed by everything that follows. Because "we cannot know what God
is, but rather what He is not" (Q3, prologue), Aquinas proceeds *via negativa*, removing from God
every kind of composition.

## The negations (Q3, A1–A7)

1. **God is not a body** (A1). God is the unmoved first mover and pure act, but every body is in
   potentiality (a continuum is infinitely divisible); scriptural body-language is read by way of
   "virtual quantity" (Pseudo-Dionysius).
2. **No composition of matter and form** (A2). Matter is potentiality; God is pure act. As a
   self-subsisting form, God is individuated precisely by *not* being received in any matter.
3. **God is identical with His own essence/nature** (A3) — *suppositum* and nature coincide,
   since there is no individuating matter to distinguish "this God" from Godhead.
4. **Essence and existence are identical in God** (A4 — the pivotal article). "God is not only
   His own essence… but also His own existence." If existence differed from essence it would have
   to be caused by another (impossible for the first cause); and a thing that *has* existence but
   *is not* its existence would be a being by participation, not the first being. God is therefore
   ***ipsum esse subsistens*** — subsisting existence itself. (Hilary: "In God existence is not an
   accidental quality, but subsisting truth.")
5. **God is not in any genus** (A5). Genus-plus-difference implies potentiality and actuality;
   and "being cannot be a genus." Hence no definition of God is possible — God is "outside every
   genus" and the principle of every genus.
6. **No accidents in God** (A6). An accident is related to its subject as actuality to a
   potentiality; God has no potentiality. A corollary drawn here: "virtue and wisdom are not
   predicated of God and of us univocally" — grounding the doctrine of [[analogy]].
7. **God is altogether simple** (A7). The synthesis: God is "nowise composite, but altogether
   simple," since every composite is posterior to and dependent on its parts, has a cause, and
   mixes potentiality with actuality — none of which can be true of the first, uncaused, pure act.

A final article (A8) denies that God enters into composition *with* other things — refuting the
world-soul view (against Augustine's report), the Amalricians (God as formal principle of all
things), and "David of Dinant, who most absurdly taught that God was primary matter." God is the
being of all things "as their efficient and exemplar cause, but not as being their essence."

## Significance

*Ipsum esse subsistens* is the metaphysical engine of the whole treatise on the One God: God's
perfection (Q4), goodness (Q6), infinity (Q7), omnipresence (Q8), immutability (Q9), eternity
(Q10), and unity (Q11) are each derived from simplicity. Because God *is* His existence, He
lacks no perfection of being; because He is uncomposed, He is immutable and one.

## Comparison

Aquinas's doctrine closely tracks [[maimonides|Maimonides]]'s argument that in God "existence
and essence are perfectly identical" (the foundation of Maimonides' rejection of positive
essential attributes — see [[divine-attributes]]). Both reason from non-composition to the denial
of accidents in God. They diverge on consequences for religious language: Maimonides concludes to
a strict [[negative-theology|negative theology]] (affirmative essential attributes are
inadmissible), whereas Aquinas holds that perfection-terms can be affirmed of God
**analogically** and *substantially*, though imperfectly (see [[analogy]]).
