# The Trinity

The **Trinity** is the central Christian doctrine that the one God exists as three co-equal,
co-eternal persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — of one divine essence. This page records the
doctrine as systematized by [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] in the Treatise on the Most Holy Trinity,
[[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q27–43, which became the standard scholastic account.
(The concept should be extended with patristic and conciliar material — Nicaea, the Cappadocians,
Augustine's *De Trinitate* — as those sources are ingested.)

## Knowable only by revelation (Q32)

A foundational contrast with the *Prima Pars* treatise on the one God: whereas God's **existence**
is demonstrable by natural reason (the [[five-ways|Five Ways]]), the **Trinity of persons** is
**not** knowable by reason at all (Q32, A1). Natural reason reaches God only from creatures as
effects to their cause, and creative power belongs to the *unity of the essence*, common to all
three persons — not to the distinction of persons. Philosophers could know the appropriated
attributes (power, wisdom, goodness) but never the proper notions (paternity, filiation,
procession). To attempt a rational *proof* of the Trinity, Aquinas warns, both "derogates from the
dignity of faith" and "exposes [believers] to the ridicule" of unbelievers; reason can only defend
and illustrate the doctrine once it is revealed.

## The two processions (Q27)

Within God there are two **immanent** processions (not outward emanations), modeled on the two
spiritual activities of an intellectual nature:
- the **generation of the Son/Word** by way of *intellect* (the Word conceived by the Father), and
- the **procession of the Holy Spirit** by way of *will/love* ("nothing can be loved by the will
  unless it is conceived in the intellect," so the procession of Love presupposes the Word).

The procession of Love is not a generation, because it proceeds by impulse rather than by likeness
of form; hence the Spirit "proceeds rather as spirit."

## Relations, persons, notions (Q28–29, Q40–41)

The persons are constituted by **relations of origin** (paternity, filiation, spiration,
procession). Aquinas adopts **Boethius**'s definition of *person* — "an individual substance of a
rational nature" (Q29, A1) — reading "individual" as *incommunicability* (not material
individuation), so that a divine person is "a subsistent relation" in the one essence. (Cf.
Richard of St. Victor's amendment, "the incommunicable existence of the divine nature.")

## Appropriation (Q39)

Although the essential attributes are common to all three persons, Scripture and tradition
**appropriate** certain ones to particular persons by similitude — classically **power** to the
Father, **wisdom** to the Son, **goodness** to the Holy Spirit (also "from Whom / by Whom / in
Whom," Rom. 11:36). Appropriated attributes are *not* exclusively proper to the person to whom they
are appropriated — a guard against tritheism.

## Equality, co-eternity, circumincession (Q42)

Against Arian **subordinationism** (see [[arianism]]), Aquinas argues the persons are fully
co-equal and **co-eternal**: the Father generates the Son by nature (not will), the divine nature
was eternally perfect, and generation is non-successive — so the Son exists whenever the Father
exists. The persons mutually indwell one another — **circumincession** (Greek *perichoresis*) — in
essence, relation, and origin ("I am in the Father, and the Father in Me," Jn. 14:10; Hilary: "He
is God in God").

## Missions (Q43)

A divine person is **sent** when, besides its eternal procession, a new *temporal* effect relates
it to a creature. The **invisible mission** is tied to **sanctifying grace** and the special
indwelling of the Trinity in the rational creature: the Son's invisible mission is a sapiential
illumination that breaks forth into love; the Holy Spirit's is charity. The **visible missions** are
the Son in the Incarnation and the Spirit at the Baptism/Pentecost. The Father, being "from no
other," is never sent.

## Barth's Derivation of the Trinity from Revelation (*Church Dogmatics* I.1, §8)

[[karl-barth|Karl Barth]] offers the most systematic modern argument for grounding the Trinity not in speculation or proof-texts but in the *structure of revelation itself*. His thesis (§8, line 72–75): "God Himself in unimpaired unity yet also in unimpaired distinction is Revealer, Revelation, and Revealedness." The three questions that Scripture forces upon the reader — *Who* is the self-revealing God? *What* does He do? *What* does He effect in those to whom He reveals Himself? — receive answers that are "essentially identical" yet irreducibly distinct, generating the trinitarian problem (§8.1, lines 100–102).

Barth derives the three persons from three aspects of revelation: (1) *Unveiling/Form* (Son) — God "takes form" in His self-disclosure, consummated in the *humanitas Christi*; (2) *Veiling/Freedom* (Father) — the Deus revelatus is always simultaneously the Deus absconditus, who "even in taking form in the Son does not take form" (§8.2, lines 1419–1421); (3) *Impartation/Historicity* (Spirit) — revelation is given to specific persons in specific history, and the *pneuma* is "the miracle of the presence of real men at God's revelation" (§8.2, line 1719).

Barth explicitly rejects the vestigia Trinitatis tradition (§8.3) — the attempt to find traces of the Trinity in nature, culture, history, religion, or consciousness — as prone to replacing the Triune God of Scripture with a cosmic principle or deified humanity. See [[vestigium-trinitatis]].

Placing the Trinity *first* in dogmatics (before Scripture prolegomena or divine attributes) is Barth's programmatic move: "The doctrine of the Trinity is what basically distinguishes the Christian doctrine of God as Christian" (§8.1, lines 351–352).

## Barth's Systematic Account (§9, *Church Dogmatics* I.1)

In §9 Barth carries the constructive doctrinal argument through four sub-sections:

**Unity in Trinity**: The divine essence is not merely compatible with the threeness but *constituted* by it. We distinguish *alius-alius-alius* (one-another-another) but never *aliud-aliud-aliud* (one thing–another thing–another thing). All antitrinitarianism fails precisely *as* monotheism: Arianism "does violence to the divine unity by the very adoration it pays the creature" (ll. 2731); Modalism produces a mode of revelation different from the mode of existence so that "God in His revelation is not really God" (ll. 2754–56). Conclusion: "Only the substantial equality of Christ and the Spirit with the Father is compatible with monotheism" (ll. 2777–78).

**Trinity in Unity**: Barth proposes [[modes-of-being|*Seinsweisen*]] (modes of being) in place of *persona* — "the statement that God is One in three ways of being, Father, Son and Holy Ghost, means … that the one God … is what He is not just in one mode" (ll. 3125–30). The material content of the threeness comes from the doctrine of **relations of origin**: fatherhood (*paternitas*), sonship (*filiatio*), procession (*processio*). Anselm's formula: *In divinis omnia sunt unum, ubi non obviat relationis oppositio* (ll. 3394–95).

**Triunity**: The preferred term *Dreieinigkeit* (triunity) holds both numerals together (ll. 3584–91). The section expounds [[perichoresis|perichoresis]] (mutual indwelling of the modes, from John of Damascus) and [[opera-trinitatis|*opera trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt*]] (undivided outward works, from Augustine; confirmed at Florence). The distinction between works is possible only per *appropriationem*.

**Meaning of the Doctrine**: The Trinity answers the question of the *subject* of revelation — "who" reveals Himself. It guards against Subordinationism (which denies the revealer is fully God) and Modalism (which denies God really is what He reveals Himself to be): "As Father, Son and Spirit God is, so to speak, ours in advance" (ll. 4248–49).

## The pre-Nicene coinage and the road to Nicaea ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) traces the
*emergence* of Trinitarian language out of third-century polemic. Against
[[modalism|modalism]] (then apparently the majority view, held even by bishops of Rome),
**[[tertullian|Tertullian]]** became the first Christian author to use the word **Trinity**
(*trinitas*) — three "not in condition, but in degree … yet of one substance and of one
condition, and of one power, inasmuch as He is one God" — while Hippolytus coined **triad**.
Yet both still subordinated the Son to the Father, as did [[justin-martyr|Justin]], Novatian,
and [[origen|Origen]] — positions later judged heretical. The unresolved status of the Son
erupted in the [[arian-controversy|Arian controversy]], settled (provisionally) at Nicaea (325)
with *homoousios* and definitively at Constantinople (381). Ehrman frames the resulting
doctrine as the Trinitarian **[[ortho-paradoxes|ortho-paradox]]**: "three persons, all of whom
are God, but there is only one God … distinct in number but united in essence" — reached by
rejecting both modalism and Arian subordinationism at once.

## The Greek Patristic Apparatus: "One Ousia in Three Hypostases"

The Latin formula Aquinas inherited ("one *substantia*/*essentia* in three *personae*") and Barth's modern reworking both rest on a Greek conceptual settlement fixed in the 4th century by the [[cappadocian-fathers|Cappadocian Fathers]]: **one *ousia* in three *hypostases***. Johannes Zachhuber's [[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]] (2020) reconstructs this as a genuine philosophy — the **classical theory** — with two complementary versions: [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]]'s **abstract account** (the common divinity as univocally shared predicates; each person marked by a particularizing property / *idiōma*) and [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]]'s **concrete account** (a real universal nature subsisting only in its individual hypostases). The settlement answered two threats at once: [[eunomius|Eunomius]]'s anomoianism (which made the Son "unlike in substance") and Sabellian modalism (which collapsed the persons). For the full treatment — including the "why not three Gods?" problem, the abstract/concrete tension, and Zachhuber's argument that this is an *ontology of being-as-one* rather than a "turn to the person" — see [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|Ousia / Hypostasis (the classical theory)]]. Note the translation hazard the Latin tradition negotiated: Greek *hypostasis* (lit. "substance") maps to Latin *persona*, not to *substantia*.

## Ayres: "Pro-Nicene" Theology as the Doctrine's Real Settlement

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) argues that the developed
doctrine of the Trinity is the achievement not of Nicaea (325) but of
**[[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene theology]]** (360s–380s), defined by three marks: a clear
person–nature distinction, the Son's eternal generation *within* the one divine being, and the
[[opera-trinitatis|inseparable operation]] of the persons — all governed by
[[divine-simplicity|divine simplicity]] and divine incomprehensibility. On this reading the
clear Creator/creature boundary modern readers assume was "itself an achievement of the fourth
century." Ayres also rejects the [[de-regnon-paradigm|de Régnon paradigm]] (Greek East = three
persons first, Latin West = one essence first): [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]] and
[[augustine|Augustine]] alike reason from divine unity, so the typology "is inadequate and
misleading."
