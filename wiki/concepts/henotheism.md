# Henotheism (Kathenotheism)

***Henotheism***, or ***kathenotheism*** — terms coined by **Max Müller** for the religion of the
**[[rig-veda|Ṛgveda]]** — names the stage of belief in which **each god in turn is exalted as the
highest**, "a belief in single gods, each in turn standing out as the highest." When a Vedic poet adores
[[agni|Agni]], or [[indra|Indra]], or [[varuna|Varuṇa]], that god alone is for the moment supreme and
absorbs every divine attribute; the others shrink into insignificance until they in turn are addressed.

## Neither Polytheism nor Monotheism

[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] (Vol. I, Ch. II) presents henotheism as a **primitive
stage prior to both** polytheism and monotheism — one carrying a tendency toward each. The Vedic poets,
"children of nature," hypostatized whatever force of nature overawed them and adored it as supreme for the
moment; the exaggeration of a single god's power was a **monotheistic bias in germ**, while the
side-by-side existence of independent deities tended toward polytheism.

## Macdonell's Qualification

Dasgupta records the standard critique (Macdonell, *Vedic Mythology*): Vedic gods are *not* in fact
independent of one another — no religion brings its gods into more frequent combination, and even the
mightiest are made dependent on others (Varuṇa and Sūrya subordinate to Indra; Varuṇa and the Aśvins to
**Viṣṇu**). Henotheism is therefore "an **appearance** rather than a reality" — produced by
undeveloped anthropomorphism, the lack of any fixed head of the pantheon (no Vedic Zeus), and the singer's
natural tendency to magnify the god before him.

## Why It Matters

Henotheism is the matrix from which the Vedic **monotheistic tendency** grew — the abstraction of a
supreme Lord of beings (**[[prajapati|Prajāpati]]**, Viśvakarman, Hiraṇyagarbha) and ultimately the
[[brahman|Brahman]] of the [[upanishads|Upaniṣads]]. It is a key term for the wiki's comparative tracking
of how traditions move between the one and the many.
