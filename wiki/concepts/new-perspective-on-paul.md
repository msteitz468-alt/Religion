# The New Perspective on Paul (NPP)

The "New Perspective on Paul" is a school of NT Pauline interpretation that emerged in the late 20th century, challenging the Reformation-era reading of Paul's letters (especially Romans and Galatians) as a critique of legalistic Jewish meritocracy. The NPP argues that this Lutheran reading misunderstood Second Temple Judaism and consequently misread Paul's actual theological concerns. The debate is one of the most consequential in contemporary NT scholarship, with significant implications for the doctrine of justification, the church-Israel relationship, and ecumenical dialogue.

## Origins and Key Figures

**E. P. Sanders**, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977): The foundational NPP text. Sanders argued that Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of self-earned righteousness (Pelagian works-righteousness) but of "covenantal nomism": Jews understood themselves as *in the covenant by grace* (election, mercy) and maintaining their covenant standing by *obedience to the Torah*. The pattern is "getting in" by grace, "staying in" by works of the law. If this is so, then Paul was not responding to a Pelagian Judaism but to something else. Sanders's own conclusion: Paul argued "from solution to plight" — he started from Christ and concluded backward that the law was inadequate.

**James D. G. Dunn**, *The New Perspective on Paul* (essay, 1983; collected volume 2005) and *Romans* (WBC, 1988): Dunn coined the term "New Perspective on Paul" and provided the most exegetically detailed development. He argued that "works of the law" (*erga nomou*) in Paul's letters do not refer to human moral achievement in general (merit-earning) but specifically to the Jewish practices that served as *ethnic boundary markers* — circumcision, food laws, Sabbath observance — that distinguished Jews from Gentiles in the social world of the first century. Paul's objection to "works of the law" is not to moral effort but to ethnocentrism: the assumption that covenant membership requires Jewish ethnic identity. Justification by faith means membership in God's covenant people is open to Gentiles apart from these ethnic markers.

**N. T. Wright**, *The Climax of the Covenant* (1991), *What Saint Paul Really Said* (1997), *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013): Wright extended the NPP within an overarching "New Exodus" narrative framework. Key claims: (1) "Righteousness of God" in Paul means *covenant faithfulness* — God's faithfulness to his covenant promises to Abraham; (2) justification is primarily a *declaration of covenant membership* rather than a forensic acquittal in a law court; (3) Paul's theology is structured by the story of Israel from Abraham through exile to eschatological restoration in Christ. Wright also argued for *pistis Christou* ("faith of Christ" rather than "faith in Christ") as subjective genitive — referring to Christ's own faithfulness.

## Core NPP Claims

1. **Second Temple Judaism was not Pelagian**: Jews did not generally believe they earned salvation by accumulating meritorious deeds. Covenant membership was based on divine election; Torah-keeping maintained standing within the covenant.

2. **"Works of the law" = ethnic boundary markers**: Paul's critique of *erga nomou* targets circumcision, food laws, and Sabbath — the practices that divided Jews from Gentiles — not human merit-earning in general.

3. **Justification as covenant membership declaration**: The Pauline phrase "justified by faith" is primarily about who counts as a member of God's people, not about an individual's standing before a divine law court. Justification is the *declaration* of covenant membership.

4. **Righteousness of God = God's covenant faithfulness**: *Dikaiosynē theou* is a subjective genitive (God's own righteousness) referring to his faithfulness in keeping his promises to Abraham, not a gift of forensic status transferred to believers.

## Evangelical Critique (Schreiner's Position)

[[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (BECNT Romans, 1998; *The Law and Its Fulfillment*, 1993) is a leading evangelical critic of the NPP. His main arguments:

**On Sanders**: Even granting covenantal nomism, Paul's critique is not merely that the law fails to extend the covenant to Gentiles. His critique is that the law as a system — even a graciously entered covenant — cannot produce the righteousness God demands, because sin uses the law to provoke more sin (Romans 7:7–11). The problem is not merely ethnic exclusivism but the incapacity of human beings under the law to achieve what God requires.

**On Dunn's "works of the law" as boundary markers**: Schreiner argues this is too narrow. Paul uses *erga nomou* in contexts that go beyond circumcision/food/Sabbath to encompass moral law-keeping generally (Romans 2:14–15; Galatians 3:10–12). The indictment of Romans 1:18–3:20 is not merely that Jews have excluded Gentiles from the covenant, but that all humanity — Jew and Gentile — has failed to obey God morally. To confine the indictment to ethnic boundary markers does not adequately account for the scope of the argument.

**On Wright's righteousness of God as covenant faithfulness**: Schreiner agrees that God's righteousness *involves* covenant faithfulness but insists it cannot be *defined* as covenant faithfulness. The forensic dimension (declaration of the believer's righteous status) is irreducible. Key text: Philippians 3:9, where Paul contrasts "my own righteousness from the law" with "the righteousness from God on the basis of faith" — the second is a *gift given to Paul*, not merely a description of God's own attribute.

## Significance for Other Pauline Questions

The NPP debate affects interpretation of virtually every controversial passage in Paul:
- **Romans 9–11**: Is divine election individual or corporate/historical? The NPP tends toward corporate; the traditional reading toward individual.
- **Romans 7**: What "law" is in view and what is the "I"?
- **Galatians 2:16**: Is the Antioch incident about merit or table fellowship?
- **Romans 4 (Abraham)**: Is Abraham the paradigm of *individual* faith apart from works, or the father of a *multi-ethnic* covenant community?

## Contemporary Developments

The NPP has generated significant counter-responses (Carson, O'Brien, Seifrid, Schreiner, Moo — often called the "Old Perspective on Paul"), as well as mediating positions. N. T. Wright continues to develop his positions. Douglas Campbell's *The Deliverance of God* (2009) pushed beyond the NPP to question whether Romans 1:18–3:20 even represents Paul's own view (a "diatribal" opponent). The debate shows no signs of resolution.

## Hermeneutical Framework

The NPP represents an application of historical-critical method to the Jewish background of Paul. By correcting what they see as a caricature of Judaism, NPP scholars believe they can hear Paul more accurately within his own first-century context. Traditional Protestant interpreters (Schreiner et al.) respond that the Reformation reading is not based on a caricature but on careful exegesis of Paul's actual arguments.
