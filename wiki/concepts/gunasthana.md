# Gunasthana

## Definition

*Gunasthāna* (Prakrit: *guṇaṭhāṇa*), "stages of quality" or "stages of spiritual progress", is a list of fourteen graduated levels through which a soul can pass on the way from spiritual ignorance to final liberation. The framework delineates how every individual can, in a gradualistic manner, proceed over one life through a series of levels to final deliverance. The stages form a continuum capable of effecting karmic modification, but they are not regarded as mechanical or inevitable in their progression.

## Origin and Development

The fourteen-stage schema appears for the first time in Digambara texts at the beginning of the common era, although some of its constituent elements are earlier (Dundas, *The Jains*, ch. 6). It was subsequently accepted across the Digambara–Svetambara divide as a shared model of spiritual development.

## Structure of the Stages

The career of the individual begins at the lowest rung — superstition and ignorance (*mūḍhatā*) — and proceeds through the gaining of faith and true Jain values, designated as "correct view" (*samyagdṛṣṭi* or *samyaktva*), and the consequent performance of the full range of lay duties. From there, the career potentially leads to initiation as an ascetic which will then, after the practice of austerity, meditation and suppression of the passions, bring about the elimination of [[karma|karma]] and the attainment of enlightenment and subsequent deliverance. Non-Jain views are located in the lowest stages, designated as "falsehood" (*mithyātva*), demonstrating a willingness to encompass yet also transcend all other religious and philosophical paths.

Even in the highly advanced eleventh stage — where the passions have been calmed but not fully eliminated — it is possible to relapse back into the lower stages, confirming the non-mechanical character of the model.

## Practical Limitations

It is generally accepted that lay people and ascetics in the current world age cannot progress beyond the fifth and sixth stages respectively, which means the model has only theoretical value in practice. Nevertheless, it demonstrates general approval of the validity of the householder's role and its linkage to that of the ascetic. Some Jain writers today invoke the stages of quality as if they threw sociological light on how Jains envision their position in the world, but Dundas notes this aspiration exceeds what the model actually provides (ch. 6).

## Relation to Lay and Ascetic Paths

Interpreting the gunasthāna model as a smooth continuum would present the change from the lay to the ascetic vocation as a seamless transition. In reality, a radical reorientation of behaviour requires some motive factor — disgust with the world (*vairāgya*) — rather than smooth progression.
