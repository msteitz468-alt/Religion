# Patience (Kṣānti)

The third of the **[[six-perfections|six perfections]]**, and the antidote to **anger** — which
[[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] (citing [[shantideva|Śāntideva]]) calls the supreme destroyer of virtue:
"There is no sin like hatred / And no fortitude like patience." Anger "projects its own fruitions,
which are extremely unpleasant," and prevents the pleasant fruitions of its opposite.

## The Three Kinds (Lamrim Chenmo, Vol. II, structured on Asaṅga's *Bodhisattvabhūmi*)

1. **Patience of disregarding harm done to you.** Generated by analysis: harmdoers lack self-control,
   driven by their afflictions "like servants" (Āryadeva: "the afflictions are at fault, / Not the
   persons"); harm is impelled by causes, so anger is as senseless as anger at fire's heat; the harm
   you receive ripens your own past karma. Also: praise and fame are worthless ("Praise, fame, and
   honor / Do not cause merit, nor longevity"); the non-material mind cannot be harmed by words.
2. **Patience of accepting suffering.** Without accepting it, one adds "the suffering of worry" to the
   basic suffering. "If there is a remedy, / Why be displeased? / If there is no remedy, / What is the
   use of being displeased?" Suffering has five good qualities (it spurs liberation, dispels
   arrogance, makes one shun sin, delight in virtue, and feel compassion). Eight bases of acceptable
   suffering are enumerated from the *Bodhisattvabhūmi*. Build up gradually: "by becoming used to
   small harms / You will bear great harms as well."
3. **Patience of certitude about the teachings.** The "forbearance of conviction" toward eight
   objects — the qualities of the three jewels, the two selflessnesses, the powers of buddhas, the law
   of cause and effect, enlightenment, the training, and the knowable.

## The Enemy as Teacher

The reasoning is framed as "techniques for defeating your greatest enemy, anger," waged by "arguing
with your own afflictions … with discerning wisdom" — patience generated through **analytical
meditation**, a hallmark of Tsong-kha-pa's method. The Kadam masters illustrate it (Sha-ra-wa's three
geshes who heard abuse "no different from speaking to dirt and rocks").
