# Church Proclamation

In [[karl-barth|Karl Barth]]'s *Church Dogmatics* I.1, **church proclamation** is the primary category for the Church's constitutive act — the activity in virtue of which the Church is the Church. It encompasses both preaching (oral proclamation) and sacrament, with preaching accorded primacy.

## Definition

Barth defines proclamation not as the Church's self-expression but as the repetition of the divine promise: "Proclamation must mean repetition of the divine promise. On the basis of the Word which God has spoken to His Church attention is drawn in His Church, through men, to the Word which He wishes to speak to His Church" (*Church Dogmatics* I.1, lines 3004–3006). Real proclamation is distinguished from all self-expression precisely by its claim and its expectation: the claim to be God's own Word, and the expectation that God will make it so. Proclamation "both as preaching and sacrament does not cease to be representation, human service" (lines 3239–3240), but it is more than this — "when and where it pleases God, it is God's own Word" (line 3237).

## Contrast with Modernist/Liberal Theology

Barth's central polemic in §3 is against any account of proclamation as **self-expression** — man conversing with himself under religious or spiritual categories. He identifies this error in Schleiermacher, for whom proclamation is "the act of the community as such" or "the influence of the stronger on the weaker" through self-display and imitation (summarizing *Christian Faith* §§133–135, lines 2726–2736). In such an account, "man is finally conceived of as conversing only with himself" (line 2747). Barth extends the critique to Paul Tillich, whose "new sacramental situation" is similarly grounded in reaching "the depths of our own undivided, pre-objective being" (*Religiöse Verwirklichung*, 1930; lines 2737–2743). Proclamation as self-exposition "must in the long run turn out to be a superfluous and impossible undertaking" (lines 2823–2824).

## Contrast with Roman Catholic Theology

Roman Catholicism is "the church of the sacrament" whose dogmatics shrouds preaching in "Silentium altissimum" (profound silence, line 2898). The underlying theological reason is that Roman Catholicism conceives grace not as the connection between Word and faith but as "a divine being as cause and a divine-creaturely being as effect" — "a physical, not a historical, event" (lines 3021–3032). On this view sacrament, defined as acting *ex opere operato* (by virtue of the act itself, lines 3070–3074), necessarily becomes the centre, and preaching is reduced to "preliminary and ancillary exposition" (line 3077). Barth's Reformation counter: "the sacrament for the sake of preaching, not vice versa" (line 3133).

## Proclamation as the Material of Dogmatics

Church proclamation is the *raw material* of dogmatics: dogmatics asks "how the Church talked about God yesterday" in order to ask "how this should be done to-morrow" (line 3487). Proclamation is not derived from dogmatics (a "fatal confusion" to reverse the order, line 3574) — it receives its content from "the middle space between the particular text in the context of the whole Bible and the particular situation of the changing moment" (lines 3582–3584). Dogmatics is "concentrated care and concern of the Church for its own most proper responsibility" (lines 3433–3434), serving proclamation by raising the question of its faithfulness to the Word of God.

## Real Proclamation

Because proclamation claims to be God's Word, it is also measured by a criterion that transcends all human criteria: "Church proclamation and not dogmatics is immediate to God in the Church. Proclamation is essential, dogmatics is needed only for the sake of it" (lines 3937–3939). Real proclamation — in which God actually speaks through human words — is the event around which Barth structures the entire [[threefold-word-of-god|threefold form of the Word of God]].
