# The Word of God in its Threefold Form

[[karl-barth|Karl Barth]]'s doctrine of the threefold form of the Word of God is the structural backbone of his *Church Dogmatics* I.1. The thesis is that the one Word of God is given to the Church in three distinct but inseparable forms: (1) the **revealed** Word (Jesus Christ), (2) the **written** Word (Holy Scripture as witness), and (3) the **preached** Word (Church proclamation). These are not three different words but "three different forms of the Word of God" (line 5510), united by the one act of divine address.

## The Three Forms

### 1. The Word of God Preached

Real proclamation, the preached Word, is analyzed through four concentric circles (§4.1):

1. **Commission**: Proclamation rests on a command from God that "comes upon us ... absolutely from without ... which we can only receive and have in the act of receiving" (lines 4060–4062). The necessity of proclamation is not grounded in human circumstances or convictions but in God's positive command.
2. **Theme**: The object of proclamation presents itself as an object "over against us and the whole world of all our objects, certainly in the unavoidable medium of perceptual objectivity, but in this medium as the object which can never in any sense be our possession" (lines 4115–4118).
3. **Judgment**: The criterion that decides the truth of proclamation cannot be anticipated or controlled by humans — "it is the criterion which handles itself and is in no other hands" (lines 4168–4169).
4. **Event**: Most fundamentally, the Word of God is "the event itself in which proclamation becomes real proclamation" — "man's talk about God in which and through which God speaks about Himself" (lines 4263–4264).

The miracle of real proclamation is that human speech, without ceasing to be fully human, is also and "primarily and decisively God's own act" (line 4196). Barth rejects both the Roman Catholic doctrine that reduces this to *opus operatum* (mechanical sacramental causality) and any nominalistic reduction that makes it merely "human willing and doing characterised in some way" (lines 4184–4196). As a Christological analogy: "as Christ became true man and remains true man to all eternity, real proclamation becomes an event on the level of all other human events" (lines 4218–4221).

### 2. The Word of God Written

Holy Scripture is the concrete form of the Church's recollection of God's past revelation. It is "the bolt which in fact shuts out Platonic anamnesis" (lines 4594–4595) — it prevents the Church from grounding itself in a timeless self-reflection on its own spiritual depth. Scripture functions as the Canon — *kanon* meaning rod, ruler, standard — because "it imposed itself upon the Church as such, and continually does so" (lines 4884–4885) in virtue of its *content*: "The prophetic and apostolic word is the word, witness, proclamation and preaching of Jesus Christ. The promise given to the Church in this Word is the promise of God's mercy which is uttered in the person of Him who is very God and very Man" (lines 4903–4907).

Scripture is the Word of God in exactly the same dynamic sense as proclamation: "The Bible is God's Word to the extent that God causes it to be His Word, to the extent that He speaks through it" (lines 4988–4989), and "the little word 'is' refers to its being in this becoming" (lines 5005–5007). Barth criticizes both the Council of Trent (for equalizing oral tradition with written Scripture) and Lutheran orthodoxy (for positing a *vis hyperphysica* — a supernatural force inhering permanently in the biblical text — which treats the Bible's power on the analogy of physical forces rather than as a personal divine word, lines 5015–5043).

The true apostolic succession of the Church is not historical-juridical (Roman Catholic position) but scriptural: "The apostolic succession of the Church must mean that it is guided by the Canon, that is, by the prophetic and apostolic word as the necessary rule of every word that is valid in the Church" (lines 4740–4743). Scripture confronts the Church as a "free power" — it must be allowed to do so through "free exegesis for the sake of a free Bible" (lines 4851–4863).

### 3. The Word of God Revealed

Revelation is "originally and directly what the Bible and Church proclamation are derivatively and indirectly, i.e., God's Word" (lines 5344–5345). It is "the form that underlies the other two. But it is the very one that never meets us anywhere in abstract form. We know it only indirectly, from Scripture and proclamation" (lines 5524–5526).

Revelation is not relative or conditioned by anything beyond itself: "Revelation in fact does not differ from the person of Jesus Christ nor from the reconciliation accomplished in Him. To say revelation is to say 'The Word became flesh'" (lines 5455–5457). It has "an intertrinitarian basis in the will of the Father and the sending of the Son and the Holy Spirit" (lines 5463–5465) — "it can be established only as knowledge of God from God, of light in light" (line 5465–5466).

The distinction between the first two forms and revelation maps onto a temporal formula: Scripture and proclamation are marked by *ubi et quando visum est Deo* — "where and when it pleased God" (line 5351) — they become God's Word in the freedom of divine grace. Revelation, by contrast, is marked by *illic et tunc* — "there and then" — it is the accomplished divine act that *conditions* Scripture and proclamation rather than being conditioned by them (lines 5407–5412).

The biblical witnesses are *witnesses* pointing beyond themselves — they do not present their own personalities, experiences, or convictions but attest "that other." Barth cites Kierkegaard on "the difference between an apostle and a genius" (lines 5107–5108): what makes man a witness is "solely and exclusively that other, the thing attested, which constrains and limits the perfect or imperfect human organ from without" (lines 5104–5106). Grünewald's Crucifixion is the visual analogy: John the Baptist's "prodigious index finger" points entirely away from himself and toward Christ (lines 5113–5115).

## The Unity of the Three Forms

"We have been speaking of three different forms of the Word of God and not of three different Words of God" (lines 5510–5511). There is "no distinction of degree or value between the three forms" (line 5515). The three forms are mutually dependent:

- The **revealed** Word we know only from Scripture adopted by proclamation, or from the proclamation of the Church based on Scripture (lines 5530–5531).
- The **written** Word we know only through revelation that fulfils proclamation, or through proclamation fulfilled by revelation (lines 5533–5534).
- The **preached** Word we know only through the revelation attested in Scripture, or the Scripture that attests revelation (lines 5535–5536).

Barth explicitly invokes the doctrine of the Trinity as the only proper analogy: "we can substitute for revelation, Scripture and proclamation the names of the divine persons Father, Son and Holy Spirit and vice versa ... one may see specific support for the inner necessity and correctness of our present exposition" (lines 5537–5548). The *Deus dixit* (God said — the foundational event of revelation) is prior; its canonical attestation in Scripture and its promissory repetition in proclamation derive from it.

Barth traces a version of the threefold Word in Luther's *Dicta super Psalterium* (1513–16), where Luther distinguishes: (1) God speaking *per verbum externum* through the patriarchs and prophets; (2) the Word spoken through His Spirit in His Son; (3) the Word God speaks in Himself and to the saints in eternal glory (lines 5552–5570). Barth credits Luther with perceiving the three forms while noting that in this early text the dynamic of their mutual relations is still underdeveloped.

## Significance and Critique of Orthodoxy

Protestant orthodoxy maintained the unity of the Word but lost sight of the dynamic of the three forms. Proclamation effectively dropped out as a constitutive form — it was reduced to a *medium salutis* (means of salvation) for private individuals, while the "connecting point between revelation and Scripture in the present" became "the knowledge, faith, sanctification and blessedness of the individual" (lines 5675–5677). This is "the catastrophic crash of orthodoxy in the 18th century" — "the theology of the Church which had become too uncritical, which no longer understood itself at the centre" (lines 5710–5712). Barth's task in *Church Dogmatics* I.1 is to re-appropriate the threefold schema with its full dynamic: "re-adopting Luther's concepts and taking proclamation seriously again as the work of the Church in and through which God is to be served and not man, and God is to speak" (lines 5715–5717).
