# Rule of Faith (*Regula Fidei*)

## Definition

The *rule of faith* (Latin *regula fidei*; Greek *kanōn tēs pisteōs* or *kanōn tēs alētheias*) is the early Christian term for a concise, orally transmissible summary of apostolic teaching that functioned as the doctrinal standard against which heretical teachings were measured. It is attested across a wide range of second- and third-century writers and is remarkably consistent in content despite variation in wording and emphasis.

The rule of faith should be distinguished from later ecclesiastical creeds (e.g., Nicene, Chalcedonian) in two ways: (1) it was viewed not as a new church teaching but as a *summary of existing apostolic tradition* — the Church preserved and transmitted it, not created it; (2) it functioned *alongside* the emerging NT canon, not as a replacement for it. Irenaeus and others viewed the rule as "the order and connection of the Scriptures" (*Haer.* 1.8.1) — a summary of Scripture's own story line.

## Content of the Rule

Despite varying formulations, the rule consistently encompasses the following seven affirmations:

1. **One God, creator of heaven and earth** — against gnostic, Valentinian, and Marcionite polytheism/dualism
2. **This God spoke through the OT prophets concerning the coming Messiah** — against Marcion's rejection of the OT
3. **Jesus is the Son of God, born of the seed of David through the virgin Mary** — affirming human birth against Docetism
4. **Jesus is the pre-existent creator of all things, God come in the flesh** — high Christology against Ebionite adoptionism
5. **Jesus came for human salvation** — against gnostic notion that salvation is escape from the creator's world
6. **Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate, raised bodily from the dead, and exalted to God's right hand** — against Docetic denial of physical suffering and resurrection
7. **Jesus will return to judge the world** — eschatological anchor

The rule is fundamentally a narrative of redemption — creation, fall, incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, exaltation, return — not a collection of abstract doctrinal propositions. This explains why soteriological details (how exactly a person is saved) are not typically spelled out; the rule focuses on God's salvific actions in history.

## Primary Witnesses

The following authors provide instances of the rule (with dates and locations):

| Author | Date | Location | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ignatius of Antioch** | c.110 | Syria | "Incipient" rule focused on the person of Christ; anti-Docetic |
| **Aristides** | c.125 | Athens | Summary in *Apology* to Hadrian |
| **Justin Martyr** | c.150-160 | Rome | When asked by governor Rusticus about Christian beliefs |
| **Irenaeus of Lyons** | c.180 | Gaul | Most articulate and extensive formulations (*Haer.* 1.10.1; *Epid.* 6) |
| **Tertullian** | c.197-200 | North Africa | *Praescr.* 13 — used in polemical/legal argument against heretics |
| **Clement of Alexandria** | c.198 | Egypt | The rule as "concord and harmony of law and prophets with the covenant at the coming of the Lord" (*Strom.* 6.15.3) |
| **Origen** | c.220+ | Egypt | Used the rule to determine what was "in" and "out" in interpreting Scripture |

Additionally, 1 Clement (c.96) refers to the "venerable rule of our tradition" and the Epistle of the Apostles (*Epistula Apostolorum*) contains a creedal statement overlapping with the rule's content.

## Function in Second-Century Christianity

### Against Heresy
The rule was the Church's primary anti-heretical weapon. Irenaeus' strategy in *Against Heresies* was to show that gnostic, Valentinian, and Marcionite teachings directly contradict the rule. Tertullian in *Prescription against Heretics* employed Roman legal precedent to argue that heretics have no standing to appeal to Scripture since the Scriptures belong to the Church that preserved the apostolic rule.

The rule was particularly effective because of its brevity — it could be simply stated, easily memorized, and rapidly disseminated across geographical regions without a central authority.

### As "Field of Play" for Theology
Paul Hartog describes the rule as providing an "interpretive field of play" for theological development. The rule set outer boundaries; within those boundaries, there was still extensive room for doctrinal development and dispute. The rule did not stifle theological progress but provided the narrative framework within which it could happen.

### Relationship to the NT Canon
The rule and the emerging NT canon were mutually reinforcing. Orthodox writers viewed the rule as summarizing the NT's own story, and the NT books were used to validate the rule. Neither was independent of the other. As the NT canon crystallized in the second century (see [[nt-canon-formation|NT Canon Formation]]), the rule became increasingly anchored to specific textual sources — culminating in the patristic practice of using the rule to interpret difficult passages and the canon to expound the rule.

## The Rule and Apostolic Continuity

The rule was understood as the bridge from the apostles to the Church. Irenaeus: "We refer [the heretics] to that tradition from the apostles which is preserved through the succession of presbyters in the churches" (*Haer.* 3.2.2). This tradition was regarded as fixed — Irenaeus insisted the tradition "cannot change, grow, or develop into something else" (Behr).

This should not be confused with claims about doctrinal infallibility in later institutional senses. What Irenaeus meant was that the apostolic kerygma is a given — churches are receivers of tradition, not its creators. The bishops preserve it through succession.

## The Rule and Paul

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] argues (following Litfin, Pelikan, and Kelly) that the rule of faith "coheres with the earliest available creedal material to be found in (or discerned behind) the New Testament." Specifically, the seven affirmations of the rule match what is unambiguously present in Paul's seven undisputed letters — the earliest directly accessible layer of Christianity.

Paul:
- Was monotheistic and identified Jesus as Lord within the one God (*1 Cor 8:5-6*; cf. [[shema|Shema]])
- Viewed the OT as a reliable witness to the one God and to Christ (Romans passim)
- Affirmed the Davidic descent and human birth of Jesus (*Rom 1:3; Gal 4:4*)
- Held a pre-existence/incarnation Christology (*Phil 2:6-11*; *1 Cor 8:6*)
- Affirmed physical crucifixion (*1 Cor 1:23; 2:2*), bodily resurrection (*1 Cor 15:3-49*), and future return (*1 Thess 4:15-18; 1:10*)

The embedded creedal formulas in Paul (1 Cor 15:3-11 "received/delivered" formula; Phil 2:6-11 as pre-Pauline hymn; 1 Cor 8:6 as creedal statement) push the roots of this tradition back into the 30s-40s AD. See [[kyrios-christology-origins|Kyrios-Christology Origins]].

## Controversies

The rule of faith is implicitly the centrepiece of the debate over the [[bauer-thesis|Bauer thesis]]. Bauer argued that the rule was largely an invention of the "winners" (orthodox party) and that there was no meaningful way to distinguish it from the "traditions" of other groups. Kruger responds that: (1) *all* second-century groups — including heretical ones — appealed to the apostolic kerygma as their standard; (2) the apostolic kerygma is actually accessible in the earliest sources (Paul's letters); (3) when compared against those sources, the rule of faith shows much stronger continuity than gnostic, Valentinian, Marcionite, or Ebionite teaching.
