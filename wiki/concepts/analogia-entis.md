# Analogia Entis (Analogy of Being)

The *analogia entis* ("analogy of being") is the scholastic-Catholic principle that
creaturely being participates in divine being — and therefore that there is a structural
resemblance (*analogia*) between creatures and God that can serve as the basis for some
natural knowledge of God. It underlies Roman Catholic natural theology and grounds the
claim that human reason, without revelation, can arrive at some knowledge of divine
existence and attributes.

## Scholastic Background

The *analogia entis* builds on Aquinas's doctrine of participation (see [[analogy|Analogy and
the Divine Names]]): "That which is in God perfectly is found in other things according to a
kind of imperfect participation ... And in this way the creature has what is God's and for
this reason is rightly said to be like God" (*Summa contra gentiles* I.29, cited in Barth, line
1942–1944). It also informs the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): *inter creatorem et
creaturam non potest tanta similitudo notari, quin inter eos maior sit dissimilitudo notanda*
("between Creator and creature no similarity can be noted that would not imply a still greater
dissimilarity").

## Barth's Critique

[[karl-barth|Karl Barth]] makes the *analogia entis* the defining mark of Roman Catholic
dogmatics that he must reject as the representative Evangelical theologian. In
[[barth-church-dogmatics-i1|*Church Dogmatics* I.1]] §2.2 (lines 1898–1950), he characterises the
Roman Catholic prolegomena as beginning from the "self-originating and self-grounded reality
of divine revelation" given through Scripture, tradition, and the infallible magisterium, with
the *fides catholica* as the subjective principle. This system presupposes the *analogia entis*:
"the presence of a divine likeness of the creature even in the fallen world" (lines 1935–1937),
which functions as the ontological prior condition permitting the secular "There is" to be
applied to God.

Barth's objection: this transforms "grace into nature" and "the action of God" into a
"constantly available relationship" (lines 1928–1933). Where the Evangelical account holds
that grace is *actus purus* — pure free divine event — the *analogia entis* holds that grace
becomes a "transmitted material condition," an ecclesially mediated possession. The result is
that "the personal act of divine address becomes a constantly available relationship" (line
1931–1932), the Church's mediation closes off the radical divine freedom, and "being of the
Church" is given an institutional rather than Christological referent.

Barth famously called the *analogia entis* "the invention of Antichrist" — a phrase that
appears in later volumes and is presupposed here; in §2.2 the critique is conducted
methodologically rather than polemically, but the substance is already present.

Evangelical dogmatics must instead operate with the [[analogia-fidei|*analogia fidei*]] (analogy
of faith) — the analogy constituted solely by God's free revelation in Jesus Christ and
received only in faith, with no ontological continuity between creature and God that precedes
that revelatory act (lines 1950–1972).
