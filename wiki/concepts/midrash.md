**Midrash** (Hebrew: מִדְרָשׁ, from *dāraš*, "to seek/inquire/study") refers in later Judaism to both a method of biblical interpretation and the literary corpus produced by that method. The midrashic method interprets one passage of Scripture in light of another, reading the Bible as a unified, internally coherent whole in which any text can illuminate any other. In rabbinic literature (ca. 200–500 CE), midrash became the dominant form of exegesis, giving rise to extensive compilations (Midrash Rabbah, Mekhilta, Sifra, Pesiqta, etc.). But its roots are traceable to the Hebrew Bible itself.

## Etymology and Semantic Range

The root *dāraš* in the Hebrew Bible means "to seek," "to inquire," or "to investigate" — used of seeking an oracle (Amos 5:4), seeking YHWH himself (Deut 4:29; Ps 105:4), or seeking a legal ruling. In later Second Temple literature, *dāraš* takes on the specialized meaning of **studying and interpreting Torah** — seeking the Torah's meaning through inquiry.

The noun *midrash* appears only twice in the Hebrew Bible:
- 2 Chr 13:22: "the story [midrash] of the prophet Iddo"
- 2 Chr 24:27: "the commentary [midrash] on the Book of Kings"

Here *midrash* means simply "a commentary" or "a record" — not yet the technical sense it will acquire in rabbinic literature.

## Earliest Occurrence of the Interpretive Sense

[[hannah-harrington|Harrington]] identifies the earliest use of *dāraš* in the **technical sense of Torah study and interpretation** at **Ezra 7:10**: "For Ezra had set his heart to study (*lidrōš*) the law of the LORD, and to do it and to teach his statutes and rules in Israel."

The three-part formula — study it → do it → teach it — establishes Torah study as a structured practice with communal implications, not merely personal inquiry. This is the embryo of what will become the rabbinic *beit midrash* ("house of study") and the midrashic enterprise as a whole.

The root reappears at Neh 8:13 in a similar sense: "the heads of fathers' houses of all the people, with the priests and the Levites, came together to Ezra the scribe in order to study (*lěhaskil*) the words of the law."

## The Midrashic Method in Embryo

The *technique* of midrash — reading one biblical text in light of another, using verbal connections to harmonize or extend passages — appears in Ezra-Nehemiah before it is named.

**Example (Harrington)**: In Ezra 9:1-2, Ezra applies Deuteronomy's prohibition of intermarriage with seven Canaanite nations (Deut 7:1-6) to the entire current population of the land — including Ammonites, Moabites, and Egyptians who are otherwise treated more leniently in Deuteronomy 23. He does this by reading Deut 7 in light of Jeremiah's characterization of Israel's sacrilege as the desecration of a holy thing (*maʿal*, Jer 2:3). The result: a novel legal ruling derived from the cross-reading of two passages. J. Milgrom described this as Ezra "spinning a midrash" between Deuteronomy and Jeremiah.

**Example (Nehemiah 8:13-15)**: When the people gather to study the Torah during Sukkot, they "find written in the law that the LORD had commanded by Moses that the people of Israel should dwell in booths during the feast of the seventh month" (Neh 8:14). They then construct booths. Harrington notes: "The Scripture reference appears to be to Lev 23:40-42, which does not itself require the use of certain tree branches. However, by finding the connection between these verses and Lev 23:40 through the Hebrew word 'branch,' Nehemiah's community interprets the law creatively." This is midrash in practice.

## The Method in Second Temple Literature

[[hannah-harrington|Harrington]] traces the midrashic method through Second Temple texts:

- **Ben Sira (Sirach)** 3:20-22; 24:33: Refers to a method of "study" and "inquiry" into the Torah
- **Dead Sea Scrolls**: *midrash* occurs with three distinct meanings in the Scrolls:
  - General inquiry: 1QS vi 24 ("the *midrash* of the Torah")
  - Studying and interpreting Torah: 1QS viii 15, 26; 4Q270 7 ii 15
  - Interpreting one passage by another (the technical sense): 4Q174 (4QFlorilegium) i 14
- The *pesher* commentaries at Qumran (e.g., pesher Habakkuk) are a specific form of midrash applied eschatologically: the ancient text is read as encoding predictions about the community's own time

## Rabbinic Midrash

By the tannaitic period (ca. 70–200 CE), midrash had developed into a formal discipline with named hermeneutical rules — the Thirteen Middot of Rabbi Ishmael. See [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot of Rabbi Ishmael]]. These rules systematized the intertextual reading practice that Ezra-Nehemiah put in motion:

- **Qal wahomer** (light to heavy): if X is true in a lesser case, how much more in a greater
- **Gezerah shavah** (verbal analogy): if two texts share a key word, they illuminate each other
- **Binyan av** (building a general principle from specific cases)
- etc.

The literary corpus of midrash includes: Midrash Rabbah (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, and the five Megilloth), Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael (on Exodus), Sifra (on Leviticus), Sifre (on Numbers and Deuteronomy), and many others from the amoraic and later periods.

## Midrash vs. Peshat

Within Judaism, the tension between *peshat* (plain/contextual meaning) and *derash* (midrashic/interpretive meaning) is a fundamental hermeneutical debate. Rashi is the exemplar of the peshat tradition; the Talmud and the classic midrash collections tend toward derash. Medieval commentators (Nahmanides, Rashbam) attempted to hold both together. See [[halacha|Halacha]] and the [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot]] for the related legal tradition.

## Christian Analogues

The NT authors employ methods structurally analogous to midrash in their interpretation of the Hebrew Bible:
- **Matthew 1–2**: Reading OT prophecies as fulfilled in Jesus via verbal links and thematic connections (e.g., Hos 11:1 applied to Jesus in 2:15)
- **Romans 9–11**: Paul's sustained midrashic reading of the Pentateuch and prophets on the question of Israel
- **Hebrews**: Extended typological midrash on the Levitical priesthood and tabernacle

The early church's practice of reading "Christ in the OT" shares the midrashic assumption that Scripture is a unified whole in which earlier texts encode later meanings.
