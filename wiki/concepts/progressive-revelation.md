# Progressive Revelation

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Definition

Progressive revelation is the Bahá'í doctrine that God sends a series of [[manifestation-of-god|Manifestations of God]] in successive ages, each appropriate to the capacity of the time, each abrogating the previous holy law while affirming the underlying spiritual truths. Smith does not use the phrase "progressive revelation" as a technical term in the range under review, but describes the doctrine that underpins it throughout, particularly in the chapters on Babi and Bahá'í theology.

## How It Functions

In both Babi and Bahá'í theology, the series of Manifestations is understood as sequential and cumulative. Smith describes the Báb's formulation: there is "a succession of Manifestations, starting with Adam and continuing through Muhammad to the Báb and in the future to 'He whom God shall make manifest', and so on indefinitely. Each is the rising and setting of the same sun, but each gives a more comprehensive and developed expression of the divine teachings, encompassing all previous Manifestations" (lines 939–949).

The mechanism of succession: "In all three religions [Islam, Babism, Bahá'í] it is believed that God has addressed humanity through a series of divinely appointed messengers — including Abraham, Moses, Jesus and Muhammad — and that divine guidance for the present age is partly enshrined in a body of holy law based on scriptural revelations brought by the latest divine messenger" (lines 285–290). The key departure of Babism and Bahá'í from Islam is on the question of whether this series has ended: for Muslims, Muhammad is "the last" (line 293); for Babis and Bahá'ís, it continues.

**Abrogation of holy law:** Each new Manifestation abrogates the previous dispensation's holy law while the underlying spiritual realities remain constant. The Báb declared that the Bayán was "a new holy book superseding the Qur'an" (line 855) and that Islamic law was abrogated and replaced by his new holy law. Subsequently, Bahá'u'lláh in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas abrogated both Islamic and Babi laws (e.g., "various Islamic and Babi laws and practices are abrogated," lines 1804–1813).

**Age-appropriate teachings:** Each Manifestation "brings a renewal of God's grace and gives his followers the teachings appropriate for that particular age, and on that basis a new divine civilization develops" (lines 1611–1614). This means the *form* of religion changes with each dispensation even as the underlying relationship between humanity and God remains constant.

## Relationship to Islamic Naskh (Abrogation)

Islamic law already contains the principle of *naskh* (abrogation) — the doctrine that later Quranic verses can abrogate earlier ones, and that the Quran superseded earlier scriptures. The Babi and Bahá'í doctrine of progressive revelation extends and radicalizes this principle: not merely within a single scripture but across dispensations, so that the Bayán supersedes the Quran, and the Kitáb-i-Aqdas supersedes the Bayán. This is explicitly how Smith presents it — the Báb introduced "a new holy book superseding the Qur'an" (line 855); Bahá'u'lláh then abrogated Babi as well as Islamic laws (lines 1804–1809).

The Babi—Bahá'í use differs from classical Islamic naskh in one crucial respect: Islam deploys naskh only *within* the finality of Muhammad's revelation and does not apply it to permit future prophets. The Babi—Bahá'í extension of abrogation to inter-dispensational succession is precisely what Muslim authorities condemned as heresy.

## The Eschatological Claim: Bahá'u'lláh's Revelation as the Revelation for This Age

The doctrine culminates in the Bahá'í claim that Bahá'u'lláh's revelation is the Manifestation of God appropriate to the present age. Smith describes this as "the day of God, in which the whole human race is called upon to recognize God's messenger" (lines 1589–1590). Bahá'u'lláh is "the promised one of the religions of the past" (line 1592) and addresses Zoroastrians, Jews, Christians, and Muslims with this claim.

Bahá'u'lláh stated that "there would be no further Manifestation of God for at least a thousand years" (lines 1473–1474), so while the series of Manifestations is open-ended in principle, his own revelation is declared definitive for the foreseeable future.

## The Báb as Link

The doctrine in its Bahá'í form requires the Báb to be understood as both a full Manifestation in his own right and the specific precursor whose writings (the Bayán) prophesied the coming of Bahá'u'lláh. The Báb's theology already contained the essential elements: the succession of Manifestations, the abrogation of Islamic law, and the promise of a coming one whose revelation would supersede even the Bayán. In the Bahá'í interpretation, these elements constitute a deliberately structured preparation for Bahá'u'lláh's appearance.

## Key Text Connections

- The Báb's Bayán — foundational systematic statement of the doctrine of successive Manifestations.
- Bahá'u'lláh's Kitáb-i-Íqán (1862) — provided Babis with a clear account of "the concept of a succession of Manifestations of God and the fulfilment of prophecy" (lines 1311–1314).
- Bahá'u'lláh's tablets to rulers — applied the doctrine to his own proclamation as the Manifestation for this age.

Additional detail from Smith's *Concise Encyclopedia* (Oneworld, 2000), lines 25210–25268.

### Extended Definition

Smith defines progressive revelation as the Bahá'í doctrine relating the various recognized world religions as "different stages in the eternal history and constant evolution of one religion", itself divine and indivisible, and of which the Bahá'í revelation formed an integral part. Bahá'u'lláh's revelation occupied a unique role as it represented the culmination of those of the past (WOB 114–19, 166–7). No religion — including the Bahá'í Faith itself — could claim to be the final revelation of God to humanity (PDC 112) (lines 25210–25224).

Revelation was both recurrent and progressive. Each [[manifestation-of-god|Manifestation of God]] brought divine teachings appropriate to the spiritual capacity of the people of his day. As such, "religious truth" is relative to its recipients and not absolute. The prophets proclaimed "eternal verities" (moral and spiritual truths renewed by each Manifestation), but their message also changed to reflect the particular "spiritual evolution of human society" at their time of appearance — thus Bahá'u'lláh's message included both eternal moral truths and the call for world unity based on the proclamation of the oneness of humankind; and laws of marriage and criminal punishment varied from one religion to another. The cycle of successive divine messengers is like the annual coming of spring that brings new life to a cold, dead world which has come to neglect the teachings of the previous revelator (lines 25225–25252).

Over time, each religious system has declined as religionists blindly follow tradition rather than the pure teachings of the founder and base their belief on imitation rather than exercising independent investigation of truth. In the future God will continue to "send down" his messengers to humanity until "the end that hath no end", and they will "unfold" an "ever-increasing" measure of divine guidance (lines 25252–25263). However, Bahá'u'lláh was insistent that no further Manifestation would come for at least one thousand years after him (KA 32 k37) (lines 25264–25267).
