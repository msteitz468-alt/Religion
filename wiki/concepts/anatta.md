# Anatta (Non-Self)

**Anatta** (Pali; Sanskrit *anatman*), "non-self," is the Buddhist denial of a permanent, unchanging
self or soul. One of the **[[three-marks-of-existence|three marks of existence]]** (with impermanence,
*anicca*, and suffering, *dukkha*), it is among the most distinctive — and cross-traditionally radical —
Buddhist doctrines.

## The Teaching

What we call a "person" is a stream of five impermanent, interdependent
[[five-aggregates|aggregates]] (*skandhas*): form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and
consciousness. None of these is a self; nor is there a self behind them. Clinging to the illusion of a
permanent "I" is the engine of craving and so of [[four-noble-truths|suffering]]; seeing through it is
liberating.

[[walpola-rahula|Rahula]]'s much-cited chapter "The Doctrine of No-Soul: Anatta" presses the point
against the obvious objection — *if there is no self, who acts and who is reborn?* His answer: there
is action, [[karma|karma]], and continuity, but no unchanging agent behind them; the very *will to
have a self* is the craving the teaching aims to undo, and the [[samsara|rebirth]] of a craving-laden
continuum requires no soul. He treats anatta not as one doctrine among others but as the logical
entailment of [[dependent-origination|dependent origination]] and the linchpin without which the rest
of the Dhamma collapses.

**Does anatta deny *any* Self, or only identify what the Self is *not*?** Some readers (and the
[[buddha-nature|Buddha-nature]] literature) hold the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* merely shows that the
[[five-aggregates|aggregates]] are *not* the Self, leaving room for a transcendent True Self.
[[paul-williams|Williams]] rejects this: had the Buddha accepted a Self behind the aggregates he would
have said so plainly, as the [[upanishads|Upaniṣads]] openly do; the early texts give *only* denials,
and liberation comes simply from *letting go* of what is not-Self, with "no need to postulate a Self."
The contrary, near-*ātman* reading survives chiefly in the [[buddha-nature|tathāgatagarbha]] strand
(recorded, not adjudicated). For [[abhidharma|Abhidharma]] the
person is thus a **conceptual fiction** reducible to really-existing *dharmas* — "the person is
empty, but the dharmas are real."

[[mahayana|Mahayana]] [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] extends the analysis: **[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]**
argues that not only the self but **all phenomena, the dharmas included, are empty of intrinsic
nature** — see [[sunyata|emptiness]] and [[svabhava|svabhāva]]. He goes further still: in
[[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] 18.6–12 even the teaching of nonself is a **graded, skillful-means
teaching**, not the ultimate truth — the Buddha taught "self," "nonself," and "neither self nor
nonself" for differently situated hearers, since (Candrakīrti) "self" and "nonself" are counterposed
terms that give each other meaning, so neither states the ultimate alone.

## Cross-Tradition Significance

Anatta is the **direct antithesis** of the doctrines of an enduring soul elsewhere in the wiki:

- vs. the Hindu **atman/Brahman** (the [[upanishads|Upanishads]]' "that thou art") — the
  [[gautama-buddha|Buddha]] explicitly rejected a permanent *atman*; this is the central Buddhist–Hindu
  fault line (flag for a comparison page).
- vs. the Abrahamic **immortal soul** and bodily resurrection ([[resurrection-body|resurrection]]) —
  Buddhism replaces an enduring soul with *rebirth* driven by *kamma* without a transmigrating
  substance.
- vs. the Kabbalist **soul** ([[zoharic-afterlife|nefesh/ruach/neshamah]], [[gilgul|gilgul]]) — a
  structured, transmigrating soul, the opposite of anatta.
