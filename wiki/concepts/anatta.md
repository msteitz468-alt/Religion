# Anatta (Non-Self)

**Anatta** (Pali; Sanskrit *anatman*), "non-self," is the Buddhist denial of a permanent, unchanging
self or soul. One of the **three marks of existence** (with impermanence, *anicca*, and suffering,
*dukkha*), it is among the most distinctive — and cross-traditionally radical — Buddhist doctrines.

## The Teaching

What we call a "person" is a stream of five impermanent, interdependent aggregates (*skandhas*): form,
sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. None of these is a self; nor is there a
self behind them. Clinging to the illusion of a permanent "I" is the engine of craving and so of
[[four-noble-truths|suffering]]; seeing through it is liberating. [[mahayana|Mahayana]] extends the
analysis: not only the self but **all phenomena** are empty of inherent existence — see
[[sunyata|emptiness]].

## Cross-Tradition Significance

Anatta is the **direct antithesis** of the doctrines of an enduring soul elsewhere in the wiki:

- vs. the Hindu **atman/Brahman** (the [[upanishads|Upanishads]]' "that thou art") — the
  [[gautama-buddha|Buddha]] explicitly rejected a permanent *atman*; this is the central Buddhist–Hindu
  fault line (flag for a comparison page).
- vs. the Abrahamic **immortal soul** and bodily resurrection ([[resurrection-body|resurrection]]) —
  Buddhism replaces an enduring soul with *rebirth* driven by *kamma* without a transmigrating
  substance.
- vs. the Kabbalist **soul** ([[zoharic-afterlife|nefesh/ruach/neshamah]], [[gilgul|gilgul]]) — a
  structured, transmigrating soul, the opposite of anatta.
