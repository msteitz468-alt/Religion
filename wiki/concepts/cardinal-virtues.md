# Virtue and the Cardinal Virtues (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats habits and the natural (acquired) virtues at
[[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I-II, Q49–61, the foundation of his virtue ethics. (The
infused [[theological-virtues|theological virtues]] follow in Q62–70.)

## Habits (Q49–54)

A **habit** (*habitus*) is a stable quality disposing a power well or ill to its acts — "a
disposition whereby that which is disposed is disposed well or ill" (Q49). Habits arise three ways:
some are **natural** (the understanding of first principles), most are **acquired by repeated acts**
(Aristotle, *Ethics* ii), and some are **infused by God** (Q51, A4). They grow not by addition but by
a deeper participation of the form in the subject (Q52), and decay through contrary acts or disuse
(Q53).

## The definition of virtue (Q55)

Aquinas adopts the **Augustinian definition**: virtue is "a **good quality of the mind, by which we
live righteously, of which no one can make bad use, and which God works in us without us**" (Q55,
A4). He decomposes it by the four causes — formal (good quality/habit), material (of the mind),
final (by which we live righteously / no bad use), efficient ("which God works in us without us" —
this last clause applying only to **infused** virtue, and "not without our consent").

## Intellectual virtues and the primacy of prudence (Q57–58)

The five **intellectual virtues** are understanding, science, and wisdom (speculative) and art and
prudence (practical). **Art** concerns *making* (*facere* — an external product, needing no rectitude
of appetite); **prudence** concerns *doing* (*agere* — right action, which *does* require a rightly
ordered will). Decisively, **moral virtue requires prudence and prudence requires moral virtue**
(Q58) — the basis of the *connection of the virtues*.

## The four cardinal virtues (Q61)

**Prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance** are "cardinal" (from *cardo*, hinge) because all
other moral virtues hinge on them. Aquinas derives them two ways: by **formal principle** — right
reason itself (prudence), order in operations (justice), firmness against fear (fortitude), restraint
of desire (temperance); and by **subject** — reason, will, the irascible appetite, and the
concupiscible appetite respectively. Gregory: "The whole structure of good works is built on four
virtues." (Macrobius's grades — political, purifying, purified, exemplar — are also noted.)

## Vice as the contrary (Q71)

Opposed to virtue are three things distinguished by respect (Q71, A1): **sin** (an inordinate *act*,
opposed to virtue as productive of good work), **malice** (opposed to virtue as a kind of goodness),
and **vice** (opposed to virtue's very essence — "whatever is lacking for a thing's natural
perfection may be called a vice," Augustine). See [[sin]].

## The cardinal virtues in particular (II-II)

The **Secunda Secundae** treats each cardinal virtue at length, with its parts, gifts, opposed
vices, and precepts.

### Prudence (II-II Q47–56)

Prudence is **"right reason applied to action"** (*recta ratio agibilium*) — seated in the practical
reason, yet, uniquely, having "the nature of both an intellectual and a moral virtue" because it
**presupposes a rectified appetite** (Q47). Its three acts ascend counsel → judgement → **command**,
and command is its chief act (Q47, A8). Prudence is **about the means, not the ends** — the ends of
the moral virtues are fixed by *synderesis*/natural reason, and prudence finds the way to them. Its
eight **integral parts** are memory, understanding, docility, shrewdness (*eustochia*), reasoning,
**foresight** (the principal part, whence the name *pru-dentia*), circumspection, and caution
(Q49); its **subjective parts** are regnative, political, domestic, and military prudence (Q50); and
the **gift of counsel** (Q52) corresponds to it. The counterfeits — "**prudence of the flesh**"
(right means to a wrong end), craftiness, guile, fraud, and over-solicitude about temporal things —
spring chiefly from **covetousness** (Q55).

### Justice (II-II Q57–79)

- **Right and justice (Q57–58).** Justice has for its object the *ius*, "the right" — including
  **natural right** and **positive right**, and the *ius gentium*. Aquinas adopts the Roman
  definition: justice is "**the perpetual and constant will to render to each one his right**." It is
  always *toward another*, observes a **real mean** (the thing due itself, not a mean relative to us),
  and is the **chief of the moral virtues**. **General/legal justice** orders acts of all the virtues
  to the common good; **particular justice** governs dealings between individuals.
- **Its parts (Q61).** **Commutative justice** governs exchanges between individuals by an
  *arithmetic* mean (equality part-to-part); **distributive justice** apportions common goods by a
  *geometric* proportion (to each according to his standing). **Respect of persons** (Q63) is the sin
  against distributive justice.
- **Restitution (Q62)** is an act of commutative justice and is **necessary for salvation** —
  ill-gotten goods must be returned to their owner.
- **Murder and self-defence (Q64).** Public authority may lawfully kill malefactors for the common
  good; a private person may not. **Suicide is absolutely unlawful** (against self-love, the
  community, and God's lordship over life). The article on **self-defence** (Q64, A7) is the seedbed
  of the **principle of double effect**: one act may have two effects, the intended (saving one's
  life) and the unintended (the assailant's death), the killing being lawful only if proportionate.
- **Property and theft (Q66).** Private ownership is lawful (three reasons: care, order, peace), but
  the **use** of things must remain common in necessity; hence "**in cases of need all things are
  common**," and taking another's goods in **extreme need is not theft**. Robbery (with violence) is
  graver than theft.
- **Justice in words and exchange (Q67–78).** The injustices of judge, accuser, witness, and
  advocate (Q67–71); the verbal injuries distinguished by intention — **reviling** (from anger),
  **backbiting/detraction** (from envy; one must restore a good name as stolen property), tale-bearing,
  derision, and cursing (Q72–76). On the **just price** (Q77), selling a thing for more than it is
  worth is intrinsically unjust; hidden defects must be disclosed; trade for profit "has a certain
  debasement" yet is lawful when ordered to a necessary or virtuous end. See **[[usury-and-just-price]]**.

### The annexed (potential) parts of justice (Q80–122)

Where strict equality cannot be rendered, justice has **annexed virtues** that render what is *due*
as far as possible. The chief is **religion** (Q80–100), which renders God his due — see
**[[religion-virtue]]**. The rest descend by the dignity of the one owed:

- **Piety** (Q101) renders duty to **parents and country** (*patria*); its claims yield to religion,
  yet religion and piety never truly conflict (Q101, A4). **Observance** (Q102) honours **persons in
  dignity**; **dulia** (Q103) is the reverence owed a human lord — **specifically distinct from
  latria**, the worship owed God alone (with **hyperdulia** the highest dulia, owed the Virgin); see
  [[relic-veneration]] and [[religion-virtue]].
- **Obedience** (Q104) is a special virtue whose object is the **precept** as such; it is the most
  praiseworthy *moral* virtue, for "by obedience we slay our own will." But its limits are firm:
  **a lower authority's command yields to a higher's, and one must "obey God rather than men"**
  (Q104, A5), while the internal movement of the will is subject to God alone. Unjust or usurped
  commands need not be obeyed (Q104, A6).
- **Gratitude** (Q106) repays a benefactor a *moral* (freely-rendered) debt, always inclining to
  return "something more"; **vengeance** (Q108) is lawful only when its intention rests on a **good**
  (amendment, restraint, justice, God's honour), never on the evil of the person punished.
- **Truthfulness** (Q109) renders another the truth about oneself, observing a mean between
  **boasting** and false self-deprecation (irony); its opposed vice of **[[lying]]** (Q110) Aquinas
  judges never lawful, even to save a life. **Liberality** (Q117) is the mean in giving and keeping
  wealth, opposed to **covetousness** (Q118, a capital vice) and prodigality.
- **Epikeia / equity** (Q120) sets aside the letter of a law to follow the legislator's intention in
  a case the letter would make unjust — "a higher rule of human actions," directing legal justice
  itself. The **precepts of justice** (Q122) are the Decalogue, whose two tables order man to God
  (religion) and to neighbour and parents (justice and piety).

### Fortitude (II-II Q123–140)

**Fortitude** strengthens the soul against the fear that would deflect it from the good, **chiefly the
fear of death, and most of all death in a just war** (Q123). Its **chief act is endurance**
(*sustinere*) rather than attack — to stand firm is harder and more virtuous than to strike (Q123,
A6). **Martyrdom** (Q124) is its supreme act: "the greatest proof of the perfection of charity"
(Jn. 15:13), elicited by fortitude but commanded by charity, with death essential to its perfect
notion. Its parts include **magnanimity** (Q129) — the stretching of the soul toward great honours —
which Aquinas carefully **reconciles with [[humility-and-pride|humility]]** (the two regard different
things: God's gifts in us, and our own deficiency); its opposed capital vice is **vainglory** (Q132).
**Patience** (Q136) and **perseverance** (Q137) — bearing and persisting in good — **cannot be had
without grace**, since they flow from charity. See [[testimony-martyria]].

### Temperance (II-II Q141–170)

**Temperance** moderates the desire for the pleasures of touch — food, drink, and sex — by the rule
of reason. It is a true cardinal virtue but the **least** of the four, since it perfects a man in
himself rather than ordering him to the common good (Q141). Its subjective parts are **abstinence and
fasting** (Q146–147, fasting bridling lust, freeing contemplation, and making satisfaction for sin),
**sobriety** (Q149), and **chastity** with **virginity** (Q151–152). Aquinas defends **virginity as
more excellent than marriage** — the divine good above the human, the contemplative above the active —
though it is not the greatest virtue (martyrdom and religion surpass it) (Q152). The opposed capital
vices are **gluttony** (Q148) and **lust** (Q153–154), each with its "daughters"; he ranks the species
of lust, holding **simple fornication a mortal sin** (it wrongs the child's upbringing) and the
"unnatural vices" gravest as offences against the Author of nature. Among temperance's potential parts
are **continence** (Q155, resisting strong evil desire — short of full virtue, since the appetite is
not yet subdued), **meekness and clemency** moderating **anger** (Q157–159, where "zealous" anger
following reason is praiseworthy but anger is a capital vice), **studiousness** versus the vice of
**curiosity** (Q166–167), and **eutrapelia**, the virtue of due play and rest (Q168). Its central
potential part, **humility**, and its great opposite, **pride**, have their own page:
**[[humility-and-pride]]**.
