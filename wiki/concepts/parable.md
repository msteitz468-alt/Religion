# Parable

## Definition and genre

*Parabolē* is the term used in the [[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]] for the characteristic teaching form
of [[jesus|Jesus]], concentrated in the third discourse (Matt 13) but distributed across the gospel.
Matt 13:3 is Matthew's first use of the term, whereas Mark already uses it at 3:23 for the Beelzebul
response. See [[synoptic-problem|Synoptic problem]].

[[rt-france|R. T. France]] argues that *parabolē* in Matthew is wider than the English "parable" —
15:15 applies it to an aphorism with no comparative element, 24:32 to a simple comparison — and that
it is "closer to the Hebrew *māšāl* (which it regularly translates in LXX), which covers proverbs …
fables, prophetic utterances, and even riddles," along with Ezekiel-type allegorical parables.

France's own working definition, which he repeats at 13:13-15 and 13:36, is that "a *parabolē* is an
utterance which does not carry its meaning on the surface, and which thus demands thought and
perception" from the hearer. He treats comprehension as interactive rather than extractive: the hearer
must contribute "if true understanding is to result," so the same parable can enlighten one hearer and
repel another. Hence his flat denial that parables are a pedagogical simplification: "A parable is not
an easy option for understanding, but a challenge to which not everyone will be able to rise."

France's headline thesis for the discourse follows: modern readers wrongly assume parables are helpful
illustrations, whereas "far from giving explanations, parables themselves need to be explained." The
differential outcome lies in the hearer, not the parable — "the difference lies in the hearer's ability
to rise to the challenge" — and he concludes that "the medium (parables) is itself integral to the
message it conveys (the secrets of the [[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]])."

Two smaller observations reinforce the definition. France notes that *paratithēmi* ("set before,"
13:24, 31), typically used of serving a meal, might tentatively suggest that parables are "set before"
people to tackle themselves, "but that they are not spoon-fed." And he notes that the disciples' verb
at 13:36, *diasapheō*, "explain," fits his account exactly: "the story does not carry its meaning on
the surface; it needs to be 'made plain.'"

## Against the one-point dogma

France's most consequential methodological move is his rejection of the rule that a parable makes only
one point. He states it as a position he opposes: "over against the dogmatic assertion of earlier NT
scholarship, following Adolf Jülicher, that a parable has only a single point," with "all the rest …
mere narrative scenery, which must not be 'allegorized' to determine what each detail means."

His counter-argument is *literary*, not dogmatic. Of the sower he holds that the parable's four-part
construction demands that the detail be noticed, so that "to interpret those details individually is
not arbitrary 'allegorization' but a responsible recognition of the way Jesus constructed the story."
Applications follow throughout his reading of Matt 13:

- The title "parable of the sower" (from 13:18) accurately describes the scene but misleads
  interpretation, "whose four-part structure focuses not on the sower or even on the seed" but on the
  four soils; France prefers the traditional German title *Gleichnis vom viererlei Acker*, "Parable of
  the Four Types of Ground."
- He rejects the reading that the parable is only about assurance of an ultimate harvest despite
  disappointments, which does "scant justice" to the three failed soils and the differentiated yields;
  positively, "the parable also explains why it is not as large as it might have been."
- He reads a deliberate progression across the three failures: "the first seed never started; the
  second started well but did not survive; the third may even have survived, but produced nothing" —
  all equally useless to the farmer. In the third scene the problem is competition rather than soil
  quality, since "the luxuriant growth of the thorns shows that there is nothing wrong with the soil
  here; the problem is that it is already occupied" (cf. Jer 4:3).
- He corrects commentators who say only a quarter of the seed succeeded: "it is not legitimate to
  state, as some commentators do, that only one quarter of the seed was successful," since the parable
  gives no proportions and one should presume most seed fell on good ground.
- On the yields he flags a genuine crux — "it is not certain how the yield is being computed" — gives
  both options (bushels harvested per bushel sown; grains per germinating plant) and prefers the
  latter, on which thirty-fold is low-normal, "sixty-fold is an average crop, and a hundred-fold very
  good but not miraculous," against readings that make the harvest supernatural. Consistent with his
  method, "the inclusion of the three levels of yield seems likely to be intended to be noticed."
- Elsewhere he applies the opposite restraint where the literary form warrants it: of the weeds he
  judges the story "simply told at this point, with the wording little affected by the intended
  application," and flags the extent of farmer–slave dialogue as the surprising feature, suggesting an
  interest in the "leave them to grow together" policy that the explanation (13:37-43) does not take
  up directly.
- He blocks over-reading of the pearl: it "would … be too literalistic to read this parable therefore
  as a commendation of unpractical fanaticism," its point being the same issue of priorities as the
  treasure (compare the commendation of the "waste" of ointment at 26:6-13).
- He dismisses the smallest-seed objection — "only a pedant would worry" — holding that "the point of
  the parable does not depend on its botanical accuracy; parables often exaggerate for effect," and
  suggesting that "tree" may be a deliberate exaggeration designed to evoke Dan 4:12, 21.

France reports that the one-point assumption has weakened: "more recent interpreters have been less
convinced of the dogma of the one-point parable." The digests underlying this page record only
Jülicher, and Davies and Allison, as scholars France names in his treatment of Matt 13; no other
identification of "more recent interpreters" is available (see Reliability note).

## The authenticity of the explanations

While the one-point assumption held, France reports, the allegorical interpretation of 13:18-23 was
"inevitably dismissed as an early misinterpretation." He presses a sharp argument against that
thesis: the supposed misreading was early enough "to have found its way into all three Synoptic
versions, while the supposedly original intention has left no trace." His positive verdict is that the
four-scene interpretation is plausible not only as an early-church explanation "but also as reflecting
the sort of application Jesus himself must have had in mind when he told it." He argues that the
discourse's internal coherence — the explanation's "message of the kingdom" picking up "secrets of the
kingdom" at 13:11, and the shared emphasis on failure — shifts the burden of proof "on those who
continue to allege that he has himself failed to understand the parable," and he reports that this
position "is being abandoned" increasingly in modern interpretation.

He also rejects the underlying generic assertion that parables by nature allow no explanation and that
Jesus always left them uninterpreted: "But the gospels do not support that assertion. Nor does the
Jewish parable tradition" — instancing Nathan's "You are the man" (2 Sam 12:7). He notes that parables
in all strands of the Synoptic tradition carry application pointers, in their own wording, in their
context, or in editorial comment, and that "it is of course possible arbitrarily to declare all such
hints unauthentic, but the device wears thin after a time." His reframing of the whole issue: "The
point is not that they should not be explained, but that those explanations are not for everyone."

Even so, France holds the interpreted parable remains open. The soils are described in general
categories and not identified with particular groups, so "even as 'interpreted' the parable therefore
remains open-ended in terms of its pastoral application." He finds it remarkable that despite the
traditional title, no sower is identified at 13:18-23 — suggesting Jesus in the first instance (as at
13:37) but in principle any gospel preacher, with the focus falling entirely on the seed's fate.

On the explanation of the weeds he concedes more ground than at the sower: the item-by-item catalogue
of equivalences at 13:37-39 "has no parallel in the Jesus tradition," and the list is incomplete
(sleeping, the slaves, the fire, and the barn go unexplained, though the last two become clear at
13:42-43), its function being "to provide the interpretive basis for the judgment scene that follows."
His defence is that since the seed-and-harvest imagery was already established at 13:3-23 as a model
for kingdom-preaching, the identifications "occasion no surprise—indeed, they seem inevitable": "the
list is more a matter of pedantic explanation than of allegorical transference." He supplies an
operative definition of the objectionable sense of "allegorical" — "making a surprising or arbitrary
leap to a different area of discourse" — and denies that is happening here.

## The hardening problem (13:10-17)

France treats 13:10-17 as deliberately placed between parable and explanation, and notes that the
answer to the disciples' question is itself based on the parable's content: "the failure of some of the
soil to receive the seed is a comment on the human condition which Isaiah's prophecy sets out." He
flags the tension that "the parable itself has given a more nuanced account, with its four (not two)
different types of hearer, but here the first three groups are treated as one," none reaching
understanding. He suggests the disciples' question, given the range of *parabolē*, could carry the
sense "Why do you teach them so cryptically? Why not spell things out?" He reads "has been given" /
"has not been given" as divine passives, "since the passage's whole tenor focuses on God as revealer."

**France does not soften the difficulty.** He states that "the point of this paragraph is that this is
not just a fact of life but the purpose of God," and sets out the predestinarian problem in its
strongest form before answering: readers find it hard to avoid concluding that "God has chosen some
people to be enlightened and has deliberately left others in the dark," with parables reinforcing that
separation and Matthew endorsing Isaiah's viewpoint enthusiastically.

His response is explicitly partial. He offers considerations that "may modify the harshness of this
doctrine, even if they do not entirely neutralize it":

1. He endorses Davies and Allison (2:389-90) that focusing on the unenlightened misses the sayings'
   point, which is the positive blessing of God's gift of knowledge in a world characterized by
   ignorance of eschatological secrets.
2. The distinction between divine and human causation that moderns find necessary was less clear to
   biblical writers: "the same effect may thus be attributed both to human (or demonic) will and to
   the divine purpose."
3. Differential response to spiritual truth is empirically real; moderns explain it psychologically
   and environmentally, ancients in personal terms, so "a depiction of empirical fact easily shades
   into an attribution of design, whether human, demonic, or divine."
4. The paragraph depicts the situation and not the mechanism of entry, so "it is not appropriate to
   look for answers to our questions about predestination in this paragraph." His summary formula:
   "It tells us that there will always be some who do not respond, but it does not prescribe who they
   are to be."
5. Parables given without explanation are open-ended; "as imaginative challenge rather than simple
   proposition" they are the appropriate medium where hearers differ radically.
6. Teaching in parable form before impenetrable hearers "is a way of implementing the principle set
   out in 7:6" (do not throw pearls before pigs).

He then closes the section by conceding that these considerations "do not remove the robust stress on
the divine purpose in these verses." **France explicitly refuses to resolve the problem.**

He is equally explicit that the standard grammatical solution does not carry the weight put on it.
Conceding "there may be some truth" in the view that Matthew's *hoti* ("because") softens Mark's and
Luke's *hina* ("in order that"), he denies that "it is … the panacea for the problems of this passage
which it is sometimes supposed to be," on three grounds: Matthew "no less than Mark and Luke, has the
secrets given to some and not to others in v. 11," 13:12 "has compounded the inequality," and the full
Isaiah quotation at 13:14-15 makes explicit that failure to understand blocks repentance and healing.
His precise formulation: "'Because' does not in itself make the parables a means of curing the
people's blindness, but only a form of teaching appropriate to it," intentions and results being
blended. He states plainly that people respond to parables according to capacity, "some with
perception and some with dullness, and this is the way God intends it to be." His synthesis is that
Mark 4:12's "lest" expresses the purpose of the parabolic method while in Matthew the same clause
"expresses the inevitable result of the people's self-hardening" — but he declines to claim that
Matthew's theology of revelation is finally less stark: "The basic theme of 'to some and not to others'
remains." See [[messianic-secret|messianic secret]].

France does register counterweights within the chapter itself. He assigns the mustard seed and yeast a
structural role as "an important counterbalance to the 'pessimism' of vv. 10-17" regarding revelation
outside the disciple group. He insists that the "hidden things" of 13:35 are intended to be *uttered*
(cf. Mark 4:22): "God's ultimate purpose is not that people be kept in the dark." And on 13:34 he
resolves the apparent claim that Jesus taught the crowds *only* in parables not by a watertight formal
distinction but by observing that Jesus' public teaching remains "elusive, challenging, and
unsettling, leaving his audience in a dilemma as to what response they should make" — "and that is
what parables do, when given without explanation."

## Narrative setting and structure

France argues the discourse's narrative interruptions are not seams but enhance the message: "the
changes of audience and location serve to 'dramatize' the distinction between the disciples as
privileged recipients of revelation and the crowds." He reads the boat of 13:2 as both practical
pulpit and symbolic device that "serves symbolically to distance Jesus (and his disciples) from the
crowd." He identifies three explanatory models the parables offer for non-response: the varied nature
of the hearers (13:3-9), the unexpected nature of the message (13:31-33, 44-45), and division as an
empirical social reality (13:24-30, 47-50). He notes the second triad divides as the first did:
treasure and pearl form a natural pair, while the net finds its natural twin in the parable of the
weeds. He counts the householder saying (13:52) as the eighth "parable" of the discourse, noting it
uses the same "is like" formula but that "the object of the comparison here is not simply 'the kingdom
of heaven' but the scribe who has become its disciple."

On the [[formula-quotations|formula-quotation]] of Ps 78:2 at 13:35, France notes that the Hebrew uses
*māšāl* in the first line and *ḥîdâ*, "riddle," "dark saying," in the second — "a related idea," which
is why the quotation is apt.

## Realia and background

- **Darnel** (*zizania*, *Lolium temulentum*): a rye-grass relative resembling wheat early on but with
  narrower leaves and a smaller ear. Its grains are poisonous, "so that to have it mixed in with wheat
  renders the crop commercially useless" and potentially harmful. Infestation is not apparent until
  the ears form, "by that time it is too late to eradicate the darnel without damaging the wheat with
  which its roots are intertwined"; the only remedy is painstaking separation of the cut stalks. Roman
  law dealt specifically with the crime of sowing darnel in a wheatfield as an act of revenge, which
  France uses to argue the scenario was realistic and possibly familiar.
- **Mustard**: proverbially tiny in Jewish usage (m. Ṭehar. 8:8; m. Nid. 5:2). Black mustard
  (*Brassica nigra*) is normally under two meters, though some experts claim up to five and others
  three.
- **Leaven**: the ancient method was inserting a small amount of old fermented dough from the previous
  baking; "it is this 'leaven' (or sourdough) rather than 'yeast' proper which the woman is here
  using."
- **Treasure**: France reconstructs the legal logic — the treasure would legally belong to the field's
  owner, so the finder (presumably an employed worker) "hides the treasure again until he has legal
  ownership of the field." He distinguishes the pair: the treasure-finder could live off his treasure,
  whereas the pearl dealer "is apparently impoverishing himself to acquire something supremely
  beautiful and valuable" that he cannot live off unless he resells it. He suggests tentatively that
  the dealer's selling of lesser pearls "might … have led the hearers to reflect on the value of the
  kingdom of heaven in relation to other competing ideologies": once you have it, you need no other.
- **The dragnet**: France stresses that the type of net specified "is inevitably indiscriminate in
  what it catches"; the fish remain undifferentiated in lake and net alike until final scrutiny. He
  candidly flags a strain in the imagery: 13:49a repeats 13:40b and 13:50 exactly repeats 13:42, "even
  though the burning of unwanted fish seems less appropriate than the incineration of the darnel."

## Contested readings recorded

**The field is the world, not the church.** France judges it unlikely that the mixed church was the
main point of the weeds parable as Matthew understood it, because "the field is identified in v. 38
not as the church but as 'the world.'" He registers the history-of-interpretation position without
adopting it: many throughout the history of the church have assumed the parable is aimed at problems
within the church, and it "is often seen as Matthew's most graphic presentation of this theology of a
'mixed church.'" He further denies that *kosmos* here raises the geographical or Gentile question: in
this context "it does not raise the issue of the geographical extent of the proclamation to include
Gentiles as well as Jews, but simply refers to 'people.'"

**The furnace and the fate of the wicked.** France states that "the imagery of the incinerated weeds
here supports the view that the wicked are destroyed rather than that they are endlessly tormented,"
deferring fuller discussion to 25:46. He notes the "furnace of fire" "is not used elsewhere in the NT
as an image for hell," though the familiar fire imagery makes it unsurprising, and that the furnace
derives from the incineration of unwanted vegetation as Gehenna derives from Jerusalem's rubbish
dumps. See [[last-judgment|last judgment]] and [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]].

**13:52 is not Matthew's self-portrait.** France reports the option that "scribe" here is used in the
secular sense of a "writer" or civil servant, with some suggesting "this verse … is the author's own
self-identification as the paradigm 'discipled writer'" — Matthew having been such a writer in his
customs office — compared to Mark's supposed self-portrait in the young man of Mark 14:51-52. He judges
neither option fully satisfactory but prefers the first, on the ground that "insofar as this is
apparently intended to be a saying about disciples in general, not about Matthew in particular (note
'every scribe'), the first seems to make better sense." He does not read 13:52 as a self-portrait.

**The audience of the treasure and pearl.** France holds the pair applies to the crowd insofar as they
are potential disciples, obliging them to weigh single-minded commitment; "it is a message which, like
the parables as a whole, is designed to divide those who hear."

## Reliability note

The digital conversion used for this ingest dropped all footnote bodies throughout the commentary. In
France's treatment of Matt 13 the only scholars named in the running text are Adolf Jülicher and
Davies and Allison. His phrases "the dogma of the one-point parable" and "more recent interpreters"
are therefore unattributed here, because the identifying footnotes are not recoverable from the text
used. No position on this page should be assigned to any other named scholar on the basis of standard
parable-theory history.
