# The Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–10)

> **Not to be confused with** [[beatitude|Beatitude (The Last End of Man)]], the scholastic concept of *beatitudo* in Aquinas. This page concerns the macarisms opening the Sermon on the Mount.

## Definition

A beatitude, or macarism, is a statement of the form "Happy is/are ...". Such statements occur in
both pagan and Judeo-Christian literature (Pss 1:1; 32:1-2; 40:4; 119:1-2; 128:1), and the Greek
adjective *makarios* generated the verb *makarizō* and the noun *makarismos* (Rom 4:6, 9).

The set at Matt 5:3-10 opens the [[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]], with a shorter
parallel at Luke 6:20-26. [[rt-france|R. T. France]] reads the set as a manifesto reversing
conventional social values: "it commends those whom the world in general would dismiss as losers and
wimps." His summary formula for the genre governs his whole reading: "**Beatitudes are descriptions,
and commendations, of the good life**" — that is, not entrance requirements.

## Eight, not nine

France counts **eight** beatitudes (vv. 3-10), treating vv. 11-12 as a separate "so-called 'ninth
beatitude'" standing outside the tightly structured unit. His grounds are literary: vv. 11-12 lack
the epigrammatic conciseness and the "for it is they" formula, and the shift to second person "links
it directly with the verses that follow rather than with vv. 3-10."

He holds there is "no close parallel to Matthew's carefully structured set of eight beatitudes." Sir
25:7-11 resembles it in range but not regularity, and the Sirach lists are "strikingly conventional,"
lacking both the paradox and the reason-clauses.

### Structure

France states the pattern precisely: a present-tense quality or activity, then "a future verb,
except in vv. 3 and 10." He identifies a **kingdom-of-heaven inclusio**: vv. 3 and 10 share the same
second clause, "thus forming a framework which sets the tone for the promises which come between."
He notes that the first four qualities all begin with *p* in Greek — possibly deliberate alliteration
— but finds it "perhaps surprising" that no attempt carried the pattern through the remaining four,
and leaves the question inconclusive. He reads the structure functionally: it produces "an easily
memorable unit of teaching, a pocket guide to life in the kingdom of heaven."

He also notes that although vv. 4-9 do not name God as subject, "the implication of the passive
verbs is that it is God who will comfort, give the inheritance, satisfy, show mercy, and call them
his children."

A methodological point he draws from v. 4: it "illustrates the danger of treating the first half of
a beatitude in isolation from the second half." To call mourners happy without the promise "would
clearly be nonsense."

## *Makarios*: France's philology

France argues that "blessed" is too theological a rendering: "The Hebrew equivalent of *makarios* is
*'ašrê* rather than the more theologically loaded *bārûk*," and the Greek for "blessed by God" is
*eulogētos*, not *makarios*. On his reading macarisms are congratulations, not benedictions.

He also rejects a psychological reading. *Makarios* "does not state that a person feels happy" but
that a person is in a happy situation — "'Happy are those who mourn' is a particularly inappropriate
translation if the word is understood in that way."

France canvasses the alternatives and finds them all wanting:

| Rendering | France's objection |
|---|---|
| "Blessed" | too theological |
| "Happy" | too psychological |
| "Fortunate" | connotes luck |
| "Congratulations to..." | too colloquial |
| "Good on yer" (Australian) | untranslatable |
| Welsh *Gwyn eu byd*, "White is their world" | France's stated favourite |

## Against the "Matthew spiritualized / Luke radicalized" consensus

France lists five differences from Luke 6:20-26: Luke has four beatitudes against Matthew's eight,
roughly corresponding to Matthew's first, fourth, second and eighth; Luke's four woes have no
Matthean parallel; Luke is second-person where Matthew is third; the tone differs, Matthew's being
spiritual and ethical, Luke's situational; and the cumulative effect casts doubt on a single
original set.

He holds that "there is nothing to suggest that 'poor,' 'hungry,' 'weeping,' and 'hated' in Luke are
to be understood as anything other than literal," reinforced by the matching woes.

His key position follows. France casts "serious doubt on the common assumption that there was a
single original set of beatitudes which either Matthew has 'spiritualized' or Luke has
'radicalized.'" He proposes instead that Jesus used the form on various occasions and that Matthew
and Luke drew on different traditions. He does hold that Matthew was aware of the second-person form
and of one of Luke's beatitudes — evident from 5:11-12 — but "has chosen not to reproduce the
distinctively socioeconomic manifesto" of Luke. See [[synoptic-problem|the Synoptic problem]].

## Now and not yet

On whether the promises are earthly or heavenly, France declines the dichotomy. The present tense of
vv. 3b and 10b "warns against a purely futuristic interpretation," and "the tension between 'now' and
'not yet' ... may appropriately be seen as running also through the promises." He states that "the
simple dichotomy between 'now' and 'then' may miss the breadth of Matthew's conception." See
[[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]].

## Background: the *'anāwîm*

France grounds the content in the OT *'anāwîm*/*'anîyîm* — the righteous "meek" or "poor" (he notes
"the two terms are used interchangeably") whom God will vindicate against the proud and wicked.
Verse 5 reproduces LXX Ps 37:11, v. 8 reflects Ps 24:3-6, and vv. 3-4 echo Isa 61:1-3. He also notes
the phrase *'anîyî-rûaḥ*, "the poor in spirit," occurs in a similar sense at 1QM 14:7 (cf. *rûaḥ
'anāwâ* at 1QS 4:3).

## The eight, one by one

**Poor in spirit (v. 3).** France defines this as a relationship with God, not weakness of
character: "'Poverty in spirit' is not speaking of weakness of character ('meanspiritedness')." It
is "a positive spiritual orientation, the converse of the arrogant self-confidence" that treats God
as irrelevant. He commends the NEB's "those who know their need of God" while calling it perhaps too
specific. He glosses "theirs is the kingdom" as meaning not that they hold royal authority "but that
they are the ones who gladly accept God's rule and who therefore enjoy the benefits which come to
his subjects," and treats poverty of spirit as the product of the repentance demanded at 4:17.

**Those who mourn (v. 4).** France argues the mourning is "not primarily, as modern use of the verb
might suggest, that of personal bereavement" but the condition of those "whose situation is
wretched," on the strength of the Isa 61:2-3 echo and the restoration of oppressed Israel.

**The meek (v. 5).** On "inherit the earth/land" France notes the psalm envisages an earthly
reversal but declines a literal territorial reading, citing "a general tendency in the NT to treat OT
promises about 'the land' as finding fulfillment in nonterritorial ways." His conclusion: "The focus
is on the principle of reversal of fortunes rather than on a specific 'inheritance.'"

**Hunger and thirst for righteousness (v. 6).** France reports that many interpreters take
*dikaiosynē* here as God's action — his justice or intervention, or Pauline justification — and
**rejects both**, holding that in Matthew *dikaiosynē* "is overwhelmingly concerned with right
conduct, with living the way God requires." He notes *chortazomai* is "a graphic word used also for
fattening animals," implying being well filled, "colloquially being 'stuffed.'" See
[[righteousness|righteousness]].

**The merciful (v. 7).** France reads this within Matthew's reciprocity principle (6:14-15; 7:2;
7:12; 18:21-35), noting that mercy is broader than forgiveness — a generous attitude — and that it
"sets aside society's assumption that it is honorable to demand revenge." The passive, he holds,
speaks of God's dealing rather than others'.

**The pure in heart (v. 8).** France allows that in a first-century Jewish context "pure in heart"
might imply contrast with outward ritual [[purity|purity]] (cf. 23:25-28) "but no such connotation
is likely in Ps. 24, on which this beatitude is based" — a qualified, not asserted, reading. On
seeing God he holds this is "a privilege reserved for the new Jerusalem" (Rev 22:4); on earth it
remains "only a foretaste," and it is the [[angels|angels]] of God's people, not the people
themselves, who see his face (18:10).

**The peacemakers (v. 9).** France reads this as going "beyond a merely peaceful disposition to an
active attempt to 'make' peace," illustrated by the costly demands of 5:39-42 which overturn the
*lex talionis*; but he notes 10:34 shows not all conflict can be avoided. He endorses H. D. Betz
(*Sermon*, 140) that the discourse "recognizes war, persecution and injustice as part of the evil
world" and that peacemaking means "risking peacemaking out of a situation of powerlessness." On
"called God's children" he explains the Semitic "sons of ..." idiom for shared character (8:12;
13:38; 23:31), while allowing a relational sense too, and notes that Matthew generally reserves "son
of God" for [[jesus|Jesus]] and does not use Pauline adoption language.

**The persecuted (v. 10).** France reads the wording as implying persecution is not merely possible
but expected: "the uncompromising wording of this beatitude suggests that it will be — persecution."
He draws a portrait of the disciple as "not a hermit engaged in the solitary pursuit of holiness but
as one engaged in society."

## The relation to 5:11–12

Although France excludes vv. 11-12 from the set, he flags "because of me" in v. 11 as a significant
advance on v. 10's "righteousness": it "makes it clear that this discourse is not just a call to
moral conduct but is grounded in the unique authority and radical demands of Jesus himself." On
reward he notes that "Unlike many modern Christians, Matthew is not coy about the 'reward' that
awaits those who are faithful," while the parable of 20:1-15 warns against a crude quid-pro-quo
concept. He reads "reward in heaven" as "probably best understood not of a location so much as of a
relationship with God." He suggests "the prophets who came before you" "perhaps suggests that Jesus'
disciples are now the prophetic voice on earth" (cf. 10:41; 23:34), so that persecution becomes a
badge of honour. See [[discipleship|discipleship]] and [[grace|grace]].

## The form elsewhere in Matthew

France notes that the beatitude of 11:6 is uniquely singular, unlike 5:3-10, and so "must have
reference to John's question": the evidence should have sufficed for [[john-the-baptist|John the
Baptist]]. He reads 13:16-17 as a beatitude with a double contrast — first with the crowds described
by Isa 6:9-10, then with "people in the past who were in no way hardened against the truth ... but
who lived too soon" — and places it within a wider NT motif of "incredulous wonder" at the privilege
of living at the fulfilment (Heb 11:13, 40; John 8:56; 1 Pet 1:10-12), contrasting Pss. Sol. 17:44
and 18:6, whose beatitudes concern a still-future blessing. At 16:17 he reads the beatitude as
congratulating [[peter-apostle|Peter]] on insight "revealed to him by God himself" rather than
puzzled out, while cautioning that a similar corporate beatitude was already addressed to all the
disciples at 13:16-17. He also reads the "little children" of 11:25-26 as Jesus' true disciples
forming an alternative community, embodying "the same paradoxical value-scale as the beatitudes."

## Key text–concept connections

| Locus | France's reading |
|---|---|
| Matt 5:3, 10 | Present-tense kingdom clause forms an inclusio; guards against a purely futuristic reading |
| Matt 5:5 | Reproduces LXX Ps 37:11; "the principle of reversal of fortunes rather than ... a specific 'inheritance'" |
| Matt 5:6 | *Dikaiosynē* as right conduct, not divine action |
| Matt 5:8 | Reflects Ps 24:3-6; ritual-purity contrast allowed but not asserted |
| Matt 5:11–12 | Outside the eight; introduces "because of me" |
| Luke 6:20–26 | Literal, socioeconomic; not a "radicalization" of a spiritualized Matthean original |

## What France leaves unresolved

- Whether the *p*-alliteration of the first four is deliberate.
- Whether a ritual-purity contrast lies behind "pure in heart" — allowed for the first-century
  context, denied for Ps 24.
- Whether the promises are earthly or heavenly — the dichotomy itself is declined.
