# Kavanah (Intention / Direction of the Heart)

**Kavanah** (Hebrew, from a root "to straighten, to direct") denotes the inward intention,
attention, and devotion brought to a religious act — "to direct the heart to the Father in
heaven." In [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]] (Part III),
[[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]] makes it the inner pole of the [[mitzvah]] and the
counterpart of *keva* (fixed regularity).

## Three Layers

Heschel layers the term: (1) **attentiveness** — "kavanah is attentiveness to God"; (2)
**appreciation** — "an act of valuation … of being commanded, of living in a covenant"; (3)
**integration** — "the act of bringing together the scattered forces of the self." It is "an
attitude of the whole person," not merely a guiding thought. A mitzvah is therefore "like a
musical score, and its performance … an artistic act": "It is not enough to do the mitsvah;
one must live what he does."

## Why Kavanah, and Its Limit

Without devotion a deed "will leave the life of the doer unaffected" — "the true goal for man
is to be what he does." Yet the deed finally *exceeds* intention: "a mitsvah is an act in
which we go beyond the scope of our thought and intention." Heschel notes the halakhic debate
over whether kavanah is strictly required (Mishnah Zevahim) and sides with the deed's
validity while insisting on devotion as the goal. This balance defines the polarity of *keva*
and *kavanah*; see [[halakhah]] and the related [[the-ineffable|sense of the ineffable]] that
observance is meant to keep alive.
