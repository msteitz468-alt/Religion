# Miracles

In [[matthew|Matthew]] the miracle narratives are concentrated above all in the anthology of
chs. 8–9, nine stories comprising ten individual miracles. This page records the treatment of
these narratives in R. T. France, *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the wiki's sole ingested
source on the theme. France's compositional argument is the centre of his account, and every
interpretive claim below is attributed to [[rt-france|him]].

## The Compositional Argument

France frames chs. 8–9 as a deliberately constructed *parallel collection* to the
[[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]] — a collection of deeds matching the collection of
teaching, and "almost as long as the discourse just concluded." He holds the two chapters were
"designed to play a foundational role in the building up of Matthew's account of Jesus as the
Messiah," supplying the narrative basis for [[jesus|Jesus]]' claim at 11:2-6 and echoing forward
into the disciples' mission in ch. 10.

### Structure

France describes the anthology as "nine separate miracle stories comprising ten individual
miracles" — 9:18-26 holding two intertwined healings — "arranged in three groups of three (8:1-17;
8:23–9:8; 9:18-34)." Between the triads stand two narrative interludes (8:18-22; 9:9-17), each of
which, on his reading, "focuses on the call to discipleship" and the varied individual responses
to it; he treats them as "case studies" enabling readers to weigh their own response to 7:13-27
(see [[discipleship|Discipleship]]).

- **First triad (8:1-17)** — France's thematic reading is that all three patients were
  disadvantaged from a Jewish standpoint: "the leper was by virtue of his illness an outcast ...
  the centurion ... was a Gentile, and the third patient was a woman." He argues the "weaknesses"
  of 8:17 accordingly "involve social as well as physical dimensions," the Gentile and the woman
  gaining an acceptance with Israel's Messiah they could not have assumed.
- **Second triad (8:23–9:8)** — linked geographically: "Geographically they are grouped around the
  crossing of the lake, which was signaled in v. 18," with miracle one during the crossing, two on
  arrival, three on return, the explicit mention of the boat at 8:23 and 9:1 reinforcing the
  grouping. France finds three new domains of authority here: Jesus "has authority to quell wind
  and water, to expel demonic spirits, and to forgive sins."
- **Third triad (9:18-34)** — "A third group of miracles further extends the range of afflictions
  which must bow to the authority of Jesus": death first and foremost, then a long-standing impure
  condition, then blindness and dumbness.

### Abbreviation of the Marcan narratives

France's evidence for authorial design is Matthew's systematic compression of his Marcan
material, which he quantifies. He calls the ratios spectacular: "the seven verses of 8:28-34
compared with the twenty verses of Mark 5:1-20," and nine verses at 9:18-26 against Mark's
twenty-three (Mark 5:21-43). In word counts he gives 135 Matthean words to Mark's 330 in the
Gadarene story — Mark's "Legion" description reduced to two "almost banal words" — and for the
third triad, "Matthew needs a mere 48 words for the woman with the hemorrhage, compared to Mark's
154," with 90 to Mark's 192 for the dead girl. On the sources of the anthology he notes that six
of the nine stories are paralleled in both Mark and Luke, one (8:5-13) "in Luke but not in Mark,"
and two (9:27-31; 9:32-34) resemble stories Matthew tells elsewhere (12:22-24; 20:29-34).

France's conclusion from this compositional data is that chs. 8–9 evidence "a careful and original
arrangement of traditional material by Matthew to serve his editorial purpose" — authorial design,
not mere compilation. For the redactional detail see [[synoptic-problem|the Synoptic Problem]].

### France's minority positions

Two positions in this material France defends at length against the prevailing reading. Both are
recorded here as his positions.

- **8:7 as a question.** France argues that Matthew 8:7 must be read as a question ("Am I to come
  and heal him?") rather than as the traditional statement, holding that "commentators generally
  recognize that both the wording and the flow of the dialogue require that it be construed as a
  question" while noting that English versions "have been slow to catch up," citing E. V. Rieu,
  the NEB margin, and TNIV. He holds that reading v. 7 as a question "places the racial issue
  firmly in the forefront of the reader's understanding of the story."
- **9:27-31 as independent tradition.** Against the usual dismissal of the passage as "a colorless
  doublet" of 20:29-34, France argues that "it seems to me more likely that this is an independent
  tradition preserved only by Matthew," given how much is not parallel — the following, the house,
  the faith question, the fierce warning, the disobedience. He reverses the usual judgment by
  locating the real doublet elsewhere: it is 9:32-34, "more appropriately than in relation to the
  preceding pericope," that deserves to be called a Matthean doublet (with 12:22-24), since little
  there is distinctive.

On the wider question of why Matthew repeatedly narrates pairs where his sources have
individuals, France declines to decide: "The reason for Matthew's 'seeing double' remains a
matter of speculation."

## Authority as the Unifying Theme

France treats authority (*exousia*) as the theme unifying deeds and teaching across chs. 5–9. He
notes that the term itself appears only at 8:9 and 9:8 within the section, but that "the theme is
seen both in the expectation of miraculous deliverance" and in the reactions to it. The interludes
display authority no less than the miracles: Jesus issues all-embracing calls expecting instant
obedience and "regards his presence among his disciples as sufficient authority for them to be
exempt from the pious duty of fasting."

France applies the philosophers' category to the healing word of 8:13: Jesus' third-person
imperative "so let it be done for you" is "a 'performative utterance,' not stating that something
will happen ... but making it happen."

Despite the emphasis on authority, France's closing evaluation of the anthology is that "the
overriding note is not one of hard power but of deliverance and joy, as people are set free from
danger, disease, demonic powers, and death."

## Miracle and Faith

France holds that *pistis* in Matthew (apart from 23:23) always denotes practical faith expecting
a miracle. The centurion's faith is therefore "to be understood not in the Pauline sense of a
soteriological commitment, but as the practical conviction that Jesus has the authority to heal."
He notes that 9:22 contains the only occurrence in Matthew of the alliterative formula *hē pistis
sou sesōken se*, which the other Synoptics use also of Bartimaeus, the sinful woman, and the
Samaritan leper, and suggests it may reflect Matthew's view that this "unclean" woman's
restoration was best expressed in salvation language. He cites Luz (2:42-43) to the effect that
"the saving is more than the healing."

On the ruler's approach at 9:18 France notes that Matthew raises the request "on a different
level" from Mark and Luke — not medical help to prevent death but the assumption that death itself
is subject to Jesus' authority — and finds it surprising that the word "faith" is *not* used of
him, though "like the 'faith' of the centurion in 8:8-10 it is entering uncharted waters."

## Purity, Touch, and the Social Dimension

Several of France's readings turn on ritual [[purity|purity]]. He records that biblical *lepra*
denotes a disfiguring skin condition believed contagious, which "rendered the affected person
ritually unclean and thus excluded them from normal life and worship" under Lev 13–14 (developed
in m. Nega'im), and that unlike other, temporary and remediable uncleannesses, "No other disease
carried this stigma, hence the horror with which the 'leper' was regarded." He stresses the NT's
consistent lexical distinction — lepers are "made clean," other diseases "cured" — concluding that
"The terminology suggests that the physical suffering was not regarded as the most serious aspect
of a 'leper's' problem." Since the OT treats leprosy as medically incurable (2 Kgs 5:7 puts curing
it on a par with raising the dead), "for lepers to be made clean is a mark of the Messiah's
coming (11:5)."

France leaves the question of Jesus' own defilement by touch open: "The narrative does not explain
whether Jesus simply ignored the ritual consequences ... or whether the instant healing ... made
the issue irrelevant." On the paired stories of 9:18-26 he holds that both raise ritual purity — a
blood discharge and a corpse both defiled by touch — and that although "The issue of impurity is
not directly raised in either narrative ... a Jewish reader could hardly have been unaware of it";
Jesus' disregard of the taboo "would be noticed." He raises but does not settle whether Jesus'
life-giving touch "cancels out" the impurity of death, or whether the father's grief simply had
not considered the problem.

On the command to show oneself to the priest, France argues against reading *eis martyrion* as
witness to the gospel — "But that is a lot to build on just three occurrences of the phrase *eis
martyrion*" — and rejects the view that the visit witnesses *to the priest* that Jesus keeps the
purity law, since "the plural 'to them' tells decisively against that interpretation, since only
one priest is mentioned." He takes the phrase to mean priestly certification proving the cure to
the people.

## Particular Cruxes

**Sin and illness (9:1-8).** France is careful that "it is not stated that the paralysis was
caused by sin"; the link is only that curing the one proves authority over the other. He notes
that the only other gospel healing linking sin and illness (John 9:1-3) does so in order to
dismiss the idea. He also rejects the view of "several commentators" that the plural of 9:8
indicates the church shares Jesus' authority to forgive, holding that it "introduces an element
which is extraneous to this pericope and which would detract from its emphasis on Jesus' unique
authority."

**The storm (8:23-27).** France holds the story echoes the recurrent OT theme of God's control
over wind and waves (Job 38:8-11; Pss 65:5-8; 89:8-9), with a specially clear echo of Ps
107:23-30.

**The raising of the ruler's daughter (9:18-26).** France notes that Matthew states the girl was
dead before her father approached; where Mark and Luke leave room to read Jesus' "sleep" as
correcting a false diagnosis, "Matthew will have none of that explanation." He works through the
"not dead but asleep" crux at length: "sleep" cannot be the standard death metaphor (which would
yield "not dead but dead"), and the verb is *katheudō*, not the LXX and NT metaphorical
*koimaomai*; his solution is that Jesus draws a thought-provoking parallel with literal sleep —
"if death is 'sleep,' then it allows the possibility of waking up." He flags his own surprise at
the narrative's restraint: compared with John 11 the story is "surprisingly low-key, hardly
differentiating the resuscitation of the (recently) dead from other kinds of physical healing,"
and adds that "Even more surprising is the almost casual inclusion of 'raise the dead' among the
disciples' roles in 10:8," asking parenthetically: "(Did it happen in Matthew's church? one
wonders.)" He observes that Matthew's omission of the parents and three disciples (Mark 5:40)
produces "a closer parallel to the private miracles of Elijah and Elisha (1 Kgs 17:19-23; 2 Kgs
4:33)."
