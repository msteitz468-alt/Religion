# Sin, the Capital Vices, and Original Sin (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s Treatise on Vice and Sin ([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I-II,
Q71–89) is the systematic Catholic hamartiology, completing the *Prima Secundae*'s account of human
acts and habits.

## What sin is (Q71)

Sin is fundamentally a **disordered human act** — "nothing else than a bad human act," an act
discordant with the rule of reason and the eternal law. Aquinas adopts Augustine's definition (Q71,
A6): "**Sin is a word, deed, or desire against the eternal law**" (*Contra Faustum* xxii). He
distinguishes three contraries of virtue (Q71, A1): **sin** (the inordinate act), **malice** (the
contrary goodness), and **vice** (the bad disposition itself); see [[cardinal-virtues]].

## The seven capital vices (Q72, A2)

Sins are distinguished chiefly by their **objects** (Q72). The **seven capital vices** (so called
because others arise from them), from Gregory the Great (*Moralia* xxxi): **vainglory, envy, anger,
sloth (acedia), covetousness, gluttony, and lust** — five spiritual, two carnal. **Pride** is not
counted among the seven but is "the queen of them all," the root of all sin.

## The causes of sin (Q75–80)

Sin's causes include **ignorance** (Q76), **passion** in the sensitive appetite (Q77), and
**malice/choice** (Q78); externally, sin can be occasioned by the **devil** as persuader, but God is
never the cause of sin (Q79–80). No one acts intending evil precisely as evil (Pseudo-Dionysius).

## Original sin (Q81–83)

A central locus for Christian anthropology and the later Reformation debates:
- **Transmission** (Q81): Adam's first sin is passed to all his descendants **by propagation (origin),
  not by imitation** — explicitly against the **Pelagian** view. All humanity is "one body" whose
  first mover was Adam's will; only Adam's *first* sin is transmitted (his later personal sins, and
  other ancestors' sins, are not).
- **Essence** (Q82): original sin is, **formally, the privation of original justice** (the lost
  rectitude by which reason was subject to God) and, **materially, concupiscence**. It is equal in all,
  since the privation of original justice admits no degrees; its subject is the **soul**, not the flesh.

## The wounds of nature (Q85)

Sin diminishes but **cannot wholly destroy** the good of nature. Original sin inflicts **four wounds**
(from Bede): **ignorance** (reason deprived of order to truth), **malice** (will deprived of order to
good), **weakness** (the irascible deprived of order to the arduous), and **concupiscence** (the
concupiscible deprived of rational moderation). The natural inclination to virtue is lessened but not
abolished — the ground on which [[grace|grace]] heals (*gratia sanans*).

## Mortal and venial sin (Q88)

**Mortal sin** destroys the soul's order to its last end (turning away from God / against charity);
it is irreparable by any intrinsic principle and merits eternal punishment. **Venial sin** disorders
the *means* while the order to the end remains; it is reparable and merits only temporal punishment.
Venial sin is "sin" only **analogically**. A circumstance cannot make a venial sin mortal unless it
adds a new species.
