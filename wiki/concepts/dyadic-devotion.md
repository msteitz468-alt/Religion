# Dyadic Devotion

A term coined by [[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]] to describe the distinctive devotional pattern of earliest Christianity: the *joint* veneration of both God (the Father) and Jesus as co-recipients of corporate religious devotion. The "dyadic" quality refers to a structural pairing — God and Jesus are not collapsed into one but are consistently held together as two distinguishable yet uniquely linked objects of worship.

## Definition and Evidence

In Hurtado's analysis, early Christian devotional practice was characterized not merely by *beliefs* about Jesus but by a constellation of *ritual actions* in which Jesus was included alongside God:

1. **Baptism**: Initiation performed in (or invoking) Jesus' name — "calling upon" him over the candidate.
2. **Corporate invocation**: The assembly's communal acclamation and invocation of Jesus as Lord, evidenced in *Maranatha* ("Come, Lord") in 1 Corinthians 16:22 (Aramaic, indicating origin in early Jewish-Christian circles).
3. **Hymns and odes**: Chanted texts addressed to or celebrating Jesus (cf. Pliny: Christians sing "a hymn to Christ as to a god").
4. **The Lord's Supper**: The common meal understood as the Kyrios Jesus presiding and spiritually present — explicitly compared by Paul to meals in honor of pagan deities (1 Cor 10:21: "the table of the Lord" vs. "the table of demons").
5. **Prayer to/through Jesus**: Direct prayer to Jesus (2 Cor 12:8; the Maranatha appeal); prayer "in Jesus' name"; Jesus as heavenly intercessor (Rom 8:34; 1 John 2:1).
6. **Confession**: The central oral ritual act of declaring "Jesus is Lord" (*kyrios Iēsous*) as the defining profession of faith (Rom 10:9-13).

## The Shema Reformulation

The most concentrated textual evidence of dyadic devotion is **1 Corinthians 8:4-6**, which Hurtado (and others) read as a reformulation of the [[shema|Shema]] (Deut 6:4):

> *"For us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist."*

The emphatic *"one"* is applied to each figure; both are given universal scope. God is the Father/source/goal of all things; Jesus is the Lord/agent through whom all things exist and are redeemed. The Shema's affirmation of the one God has been widened — Hurtado says "adapted" — to accommodate the unique place of Jesus, without displacing God. This is not bitheism but a novel dyadic structure within a fundamentally monotheistic framework.

## Why This Is Significant: The Novelty Claim

Hurtado's central argument is that this dyadic devotional pattern has **no adequate precedent or parallel** in the ancient Jewish tradition from which it emerged. He surveys the relevant Jewish literature at length:

- **"Chief agent" figures** (e.g., the "Chosen One" in 1 Enoch 37–71; Wisdom; Michael): These figures can hold an exalted place in visions of the end or in cosmological speculation. But they are never the *objects of corporate cult devotion* in actual Jewish practice. They appear in dreams and literary imagination — not in the worship of any known Jewish community. Even the "Chosen One" of 1 Enoch is pictured as himself joining the worship of the one God (1 En 61:10-11), not receiving it.
- **Jewish monotheism as functionally "inclusive"**: Second Temple Jewish texts exalt various divine agents without treating this as violation of monotheism — but none of these agents received the systematic, multi-form cultic attention Jesus received from the earliest weeks and months of the Jesus-movement.

The implication: the Jesus-movement represents a genuine **mutation** in Jewish religious practice, not merely a development or extension of existing patterns. Given that the earliest Jesus-followers were themselves mostly Jews, this mutation is historically remarkable. Hurtado attributes it primarily to the explosive conviction that God had raised Jesus from death and enthroned him at God's right hand.

## Dyadic Devotion and Trinitarian Development

The dyadic pattern in early Christianity — rigorously monotheistic yet including Jesus as a second distinguishable figure in the divine identity — laid the structural groundwork for later Trinitarian theology. The Councils of Nicaea and Constantinople would formalize and philosophically articulate what was already present as a devotional reality from the earliest years. Hurtado argues the devotional practice came first; the theological formulation came later to give it coherent expression.

## Controversy

The major scholarly controversy this concept engages is the question of *where* and *how* the worship of Jesus as divine Lord originated:

- **Wilhelm Bousset** (*Kyrios Christos*, 1913): Jesus-worship originated among Gentile converts in cities like Antioch and Damascus, under the influence of pagan hero and deity cults. Jewish monotheism would have prevented Jewish circles from venerating Jesus as Lord.
- **Hurtado** (following and refining work by Martin Hengel and others): The dyadic devotional pattern is demonstrably present in Paul's earliest letters, and Paul was converted within the first couple of years of the movement — before any substantial Gentile Christianity could have influenced it. Furthermore, the earliest evidence of the practice (*Maranatha* in Aramaic) points to Aramaic-speaking Jewish circles, not Greek-speaking Gentile ones.
