# Shaykhism

*Populated from Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (Oneworld, 2000). All claims from that source unless marked.*

## Definition

Shaykhism is a school of Shí'í thought which centres on the teachings of Shaykh [[ahmad-al-ahsai|Ahmad Al-Ahsá'í]] and his successors. Shaykh Ahmad attracted a large and influential following, but his ideas were controversial and his immediate successor, Sayyid [[kazim-rashti|Kázim Rashtí]], faced considerable opposition from some groups within the clerical establishment (lines 28417–28431).

## Relationship to the Babi Movement

After Kázim Rashtí's death, there was a period of division in which various prominent disciples sought leadership. A significant number of Shaykhis accepted the Báb, forming the core of the new Babi movement. Eventually most of the non-Babi Shaykhis turned to Hájí Muhammad [[karim-khan-kirmani|Karím Khán Kirmání]] and his successor-descendants, and the movement lost much of its earlier heterodoxy — in part, it would seem, to distance itself from Babism. Babi-Bahá'í accounts accord Shaykh Ahmad and Sayyid Kázim the status of precursors and heralds of their religion, and there are some doctrinal continuities with early Shaykhism. Karím Khán and other later Shaykhi leaders were prominent opponents of Babism (lines 28432–28456).

## Key Doctrines

Early Shaykhi doctrine is complex and under-researched. Some aspects seem deliberately obscure, presumably to conceal heterodox elements, and others are subject to varying interpretations (lines 28457–28461). Key elements include:

**1. Unknowability and transcendence of God**: Human beings must therefore approach the divine through the prophets and [[imams|Imáms]]. These personages were regarded as eternal spiritual realities and manifestations of God's primal will. This viewpoint led the Shaykhis' opponents to accuse them of attributing God's attributes to another — the heresy of *tafwíd*. It also led the Shaykhis to extreme veneration of the Imáms (lines 28462–28474).

**2. The intermediate world (Húrqalyá)**: Another crucial doctrine was the existence of an interworld of archetypal images (*'álamu'l-mithál*) — the Húrqalyá — which contains counterparts of all that exists in the physical world. This is the world in which:
- The Shí'í eschatological figure of the hidden Twelfth Imám resides
- The believer can encounter the Imáms
- Muhammad made his miraculous night ascent to heaven
- The resurrection of the individual occurs

(Lines 28475–28488.)

**3. The "Fourth Support" — the Perfect Shí'í**: The early Shaykhis believed that there would always be a "Perfect Shí'í" alive in the world who would act as an intermediary between the Imám and the faithful. Together with belief in divine unity, prophethood and imamate, this constituted the fundamental elements of religion. By implication Shaykh Ahmad and Sayyid Kázim occupied this role; Shaykh Ahmad stressed that his own authority derived from intuitive knowledge derived directly from inspiration from the Imáms (lines 28488–28502).

**4. Messianic expectation**: Babi and Bahá'í accounts identify a major emphasis on messianic expectation in early Shaykhism (lines 28502–28504).

## Relationship to Bahá'í Origins

The Shaykhis provided the key intellectual and social context from which the Babi movement emerged. Many of the Báb's early followers, including [[mulla-husayn|Mullá Husayn]], [[quddus|Quddús]], and [[tahirih|Táhirih]], came from Shaykhi backgrounds. The Bahá'í tradition regards Shaykh Ahmad and Sayyid Kázim as divinely guided precursors who prepared the way for the Báb's declaration.
