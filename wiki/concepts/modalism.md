# Modalism

**Modalism** is the doctrine that the one God "exists in different **modes** of being …
as the Father, and as the Son, and as the Spirit. All three are God, but there is only one
God, because the three are not distinct from one another but are all the same thing, in
different modes of existence." Driven by the monotheistic concern to avoid "two gods," it
denies the **distinction** of the divine persons — and so, in [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]'s
schema ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]), it became a "dead end" on
the road to [[trinity|Trinitarian]] orthodoxy.

Ehrman stresses that modalism was apparently the **majority view** at the start of the
third century, held even by the bishops of Rome. Hippolytus complains it had "introduced
the greatest confusion among all the faithful"; [[tertullian|Tertullian]] grants that "the
majority of believers" preferred it. Its leading exponent was **Callistus, bishop of Rome
(217–222)**: "the Father is not one person and the Son another, but … one and the same."

## Names and Opponents

- **Patripassianism** — opponents' label, "those who maintain that it was the Father
  (*pater*) who suffered (*passus*)."
- **Sabellianism** — after the later modalist Sabellius; the term used by Dionysius of
  Alexandria (~260 CE).

Modalists argued from Isaiah 44:6 ("beside me there is no other") and Romans 9:5 (if Christ
is God and there is one God, they are the same). The chief opponents, **Hippolytus** and
**[[tertullian|Tertullian]]**, replied that John 10:30 says "I and the Father *are* one"
(plural verb), not "I am the Father," and that one cannot be father and son to oneself. The
**anti-modalist** polemic produced the concept of the divine **economy** (three persons
unified in will), Hippolytus's term **triad**, and Tertullian's coinage of the word
**[[trinity|Trinity]]**.
