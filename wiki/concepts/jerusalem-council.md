# Jerusalem Council (the Consultation of Acts 15)

The **Jerusalem Council** is the consultation of the Jerusalem church described in [[acts|Acts]]
15:1–35, convened to settle whether Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses
to be full members of the community. As read by [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]] (BECNT *Acts*, 2007),
it is the structural and theological center of Acts and the source of the only formal apostolic
decree in the book.

## "Consultation," not a later technical "council" (Bock)

Bock stresses that the meeting is best called a **"consultation"** rather than a "council" in the
later technical ecclesiastical sense (following Jervell). His reasons: it "includes more than the
apostles and engages the Jerusalem congregation in a major way," and it lacks the juridical
apparatus of the later ecumenical councils. Bock also observes its **structural centrality** —
citing Fitzmyer, Acts 1–14 runs roughly 12,385 English words and Acts 15–28 roughly 12,502, so
chapter 15 "sits right in the middle of the book," a central chapter "in terms of both its location
in Acts and the theological issue." It is also **Peter's last appearance in Acts**; afterward the
Jerusalem church recedes until Acts 21 and the narrative follows the Gentile mission "into all the
world."

## The Dispute: Circumcision and the Basis of Inclusion

The trigger is the demand of certain Judean believers — and of "believers who belonged to the party
of the [[pharisees|Pharisees]]" (15:5) — that Gentiles be circumcised and keep the whole law. Bock
reconstructs the (unrecorded) pro-circumcision logic: [[circumcision|circumcision]] is the Abrahamic
covenant sign predating Moses (Gen. 17:9–10), so "to be among the people of God one has to partake
of the sign of the Abrahamic covenant." Bock frames the real question as one of **basis, not
admission**: "the debate now is not whether Gentiles should be included but on what basis they
should enter the community" (citing J. J. Scott). Table fellowship and purity are central (cf. Gal.
2:11–14; Acts 15:20).

## The Three Speeches and James's Ruling

The decision unfolds through three speakers (15:7–21):

- **[[peter-apostle|Peter]] (15:7–11).** Recounting Cornelius (Acts 10), Peter argues that God gave
  the Spirit to Gentiles "without any circumcision being done"; to require it is "testing God"
  (15:10). The demand is a "yoke" (*zygos*) "no one, not even Jews," could bear. Bock reads Peter's
  v. 11 as a confession of faith: **"Salvation is through grace by the work of Jesus ... not through
  works of the law,"** with "grace" fronted for emphasis.
- **Barnabas and Paul (15:12).** They recount the signs and wonders God worked among the Gentiles —
  divine approval of the mission. (Bock notes [[barnabas|Barnabas]] is named *before* Paul at 15:12,
  25, a possible Antioch-source marker.)
- **[[james-the-just|James]] (15:13–21).** James renders the decisive ruling, appealing to
  **Amos 9:11–12** in its **Septuagint (LXX)** form (with material from Isa. 45:21 and possibly
  Jer. 12:15). Bock notes the key MT/LXX difference: where the Hebrew has Israel *possess* the
  remnant of Edom, the LXX has *all peoples seek* the rebuilt Davidic kingdom — so "Edom now refers
  to all humanity." Bock reads James as asserting **fulfillment now, not mere analogy**: the
  rebuilt "booth of David" is realized in Jesus's messianic arrival and present Gentile inclusion.
  Bock takes James's deliberate use of the Greek version as a mark of "deference and sensitivity"
  to the Gentiles who would recognize it.

## The Four-Item Apostolic Decree (the "Jakobsklauseln")

James's ruling issues in the four abstentions (15:19–20, restated 15:29 and 21:25), which German
scholarship (Wikenhauser) labels the ***Jakobsklauseln*** ("James clauses"). As Bock lists them:

1. **pollution of idols** (*alisgēmata tōn eidōlōn*) — idol-food / desecration associated with idol
   rituals (in 15:29 restated as *eidōlothyta*, "things sacrificed to idols");
2. **sexual immorality** (*porneia*) — probably with a cultic dimension (temple prostitution);
3. **things strangled** (*pnikton*) — meat from an animal killed so the blood is not drained;
4. **blood** (*haima*) — drawn from Lev. 17:10–14 (the life is in the blood).

Bock's key interpretation: the list **"seems to reflect an ethos instead of being the invocation of
a specific text"** — a request for faithfulness to the one true God, moral worship, and sensitivity
to Jewish scruples over unclean/strangled food — and it is **not about salvation but community
peace** (a "cross-cultural concern that leads to harmony," with a parallel spirit in Paul, Rom.
14–15). Bock notes this is, as Johnson observes, **the only official formal decree in Acts**.

The decision is then circulated as an **encyclical letter** (15:22–29) carried by Judas Barsabbas
and Silas to Antioch, Syria, and Cilicia — one long periodic Greek sentence (one of only two in
Luke-Acts, the other being Luke 1:1–4), marked by the official formula *edoxe* ("it seemed good,"
vv. 22, 25, 28), climaxing in "**it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us**" (15:28).

### Manuscript variation

Bock notes text-critical variation in the decree. **Codex D (the Western text) drops "things
strangled"** and adds a negative form of the Golden Rule, shifting the decree from the ritual toward
the ethical; **𝔓45 lacks the reference to immorality**, making the decree "almost exclusively
ritualistic." Bock (with Metzger) treats the four-item form as original.

## Relation to the Acts 15 / Galatians 2 Chronology

Bock connects the consultation to the disputed relationship between Acts 15 and Galatians 2, but
declines to adjudicate it here. He lays out the scholarly options (Acts 15 = Gal. 2:1–10; Acts
11:30 = Gal. 2; the scene as unhistorical; historical roots with unclear timing) and states his own
slight preference — that **Acts 11:30 corresponds to Gal. 2:1–10 and that Acts 15 and Gal. 2 are
therefore distinct events** (which would explain why Galatians never mentions the decree: it had
not yet occurred). This bears on the broader question of the consistency of Acts with the Pauline
letters; those positions are recorded, without resolution, on
[[acts-paul-tensions|Acts and the Paul of the Epistles]].
