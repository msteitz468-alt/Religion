# Mysticism

**Mysticism**, in the strict sense developed in the Christian tradition, is the claim to a
**direct, experimental (i.e. experiential) knowledge of God** — an immediate cognition of the
divine that is not reached by reasoning from creatures but is supernaturally bestowed. This
page presents the general theory of mysticism; for the specific apophatic method see
[[apophatic-theology]] and [[negative-theology]], for the doctrine of God's self-disclosure
[[theophany]], and for the eschatological consummation [[beatific-vision]]. For the perennialist
claim that all mystical experience is one, see [[perennialism]] — a position the main source on
this page exists to refute.

The principal source is **A. B. Sharpe's *[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True Nature and
Value]]*** (1910), a Thomistic apologia. Sharpe's framework structures the page; positions he
reports only to reject (pantheist, psychological-reductionist) are marked as such.

## The Word "Mystical"

Sharpe derives the word from the Greek mystery-rites: *mystai* / *memnēmenoi*, the initiated,
combining two ideas — special knowledge obtained by instruction (*myeō*) and the obligation of
secrecy (*myō*). "The one idea common to all uses is that of special knowledge confined to a
*corps d'élite* of persons with a peculiar aptitude for its acquisition." The early Church
conceived itself as a body of mystics: initiated by baptism, admitted to the *mysteria*
(sacraments), with "secret" liturgical prayers said *mystikōs*, in silence. The term was first
applied in the technical theological sense by [[pseudo-dionysius|Dionysius]] (see
[[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]]).

Sharpe sharply distinguishes the popular and the strict senses:

- **The "mystical tendency"** — a latent, primitive, emotional-symbolic disposition present in
  everyone (the vague apprehension of nature, art, or sudden events as pointing beyond
  themselves). This is *not* mysticism, "just as the rudimentary elements of science contained
  in primitive conceptions of nature were not yet actually scientific." Magic, on Sharpe's
  account, is the primitive undifferentiated matrix — "rudimentary science, theology and
  mysticism all in one" — from which all three were progressively distinguished as thought moved
  "from confusion to distinction." spiritualism and "nature-mysticism" fall short of true
  mysticism: their object is the transcendental *inferred*, not *actualised*.
- **Mysticism proper** — "the direct intellectual communication of God with the soul … due not
  to any natural endowment or effort of the mystic, but to the favour of God alone."

## Natural vs. Supernatural Mysticism (Sharpe's Governing Distinction)

Sharpe's whole argument turns on a division of "two ideas of mysticism," grounded in an
epistemological premise: knowledge is either **theoretical** (built up from sense-impressions)
or **experimental** (direct contact with the object). Natural theoretical knowledge of God
reaches only the *concept* of a First Cause; "direct experimental knowledge of Him we have and
can have none, in the ordinary course of things."

- **Natural mysticism** — the attempt to find God in or through nature and created things.
  Sharpe takes W. R. Inge's definition as representative: "the attempt to
  realise the presence of the living God in the soul and in nature … the immanence of the
  temporal in the eternal, and of the eternal in the temporal." Sharpe denies this is mysticism
  at all: it is a mode of *inference*, identical in method with the **Via Remotionis** of
  speculative theology (abstracting all finite attributes to arrive at a concept of God). It
  yields ideas, not an experienced presence.
- **Supernatural mysticism** — the position of [[pseudo-dionysius|Dionysius]] and the Catholic
  tradition. God is *epekeina pantōn*, "beyond all," incommensurable with creatures; any direct
  knowledge of Him as He is in Himself must be supernaturally given. "Mysticism is … either
  supernatural or nothing." Its practical parallel to the Via Remotionis is "the withdrawal of
  the intelligence, under divine guidance, from the contemplation of any sensible image whatever,
  and its illumination, not by an abstract idea, but by an actual presence."

Sharpe endorses Royce's phrase that "the mystic is the only thorough-going
empiricist" — meaning the experience is genuinely empirical, like sensation: "Mystical cognition
is to the soul precisely what sensation is to the body." Because in God essence and existence are
one, experimental knowledge of His existence precludes discursive reasoning about His essence —
hence mystics' simultaneous **certitude** and **inability to describe** what they know. Mystical
knowledge therefore presents itself to the natural intellect as "Ignorance," "Darkness," and
"Silence."

## The Nature of Mystical Experience

Sharpe orders the soul's relation to God in **three modes**: (1) mere unconscious subjection
(all creatures); (2) conscious realisation of dependence — natural religion and "natural or
acquired contemplation" (meditation on revealed truths by natural faculties under grace) — which
"is not in the true sense mystical"; (3) the direct intercourse of the soul with God, mysticism
proper.

The mark of (3) is **passivity**: "God is not discovered by the mystic … He makes Himself known
'experimentally.'" Ordinary cognition requires *apperception* (active mental direction); in
mystical states "God takes possession of the mental powers and focusses them upon Himself," the
discursive reason in abeyance. This is **rapture / ecstasy** — the soul *taken out* of its normal
relations, not self-emptied.

**Preparation** is ordinarily necessary though never sufficient: the gift is free, but the soul
must be purified (Gerson: mystical theology "is acquired through the school of the affections and
the vehement exercise of the moral virtues"). Yet "it is at least ideally possible to attain the
highest sanctity without any mystical experience whatever, in the true or Dionysian sense" —
mysticism belongs to the *bene esse*, not the *esse*, of the Church.

The **stages** (Sharpe's formulation, drawing on the classic purgative–illuminative–unitive
scheme): devout preparation → the immediate presence of God as an experienced reality →
progressive union. He maps these onto **St Teresa's** sub-stages — prayer of
quiet/recollection, prayer of union, and spiritual marriage — and on **St John of the Cross**,
in whom the soul "seems to be God rather than itself, and indeed is God by
participation, though in reality preserving its own natural substance as distinct from God."

**Visions and locutions** occur only within states of union and are, all authorities agree, "the
least important part," to be practically disregarded; their value lies not in the image but in
what is conveyed through it.

## The Object of Mystical Knowledge

Mystical contemplation is "the sight of God" — not bodily sight, nor the ordinary perception of
an idea, but the direct perception of God Himself. Sharpe assimilates it to
**[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas's]]** account of the **[[beatific-vision|Beatific Vision]]**: normally a *species
intelligibilis* is the "form" to the soul's "matter"; in the vision of God, **God Himself becomes
the form to the soul's matter**, known directly as the soul knows its own ideas. This requires the
**lumen gloriae**, a created elevating grace. Earthly mystical vision differs from the beatific
only in being transient and restricted to the soul (which must be temporarily abstracted from the
body) — Sharpe reads **[[paul-apostle|St Paul's]]** "third heaven" (2 Cor. 12, "in the body or
out of the body I know not") as a transient visitation of the *lumen gloriae*.

He adopts **[[augustine|Augustine's]]** threefold classification of visions (*De Genesi ad
Litteram* xii): **corporal** (e.g. Belshazzar) — not mystical; **spiritual/imaginary** (e.g.
Peter at Joppa) — supernatural but not strictly mystical, often a *gratia gratis data* for others;
**intellectual** (Paul's third heaven) — the genuinely mystical. Sharpe's rule, after John of the
Cross: certainty of an experience's divine character "varies inversely with the degree of
sensibility or quasi-sensibility which belongs to it."

## The Psychology of Mysticism

Sharpe argues the supernatural element lies in the *object*, not in the *manner* of the faculties'
operation — so the psychology is open to natural investigation. He weighs three views of the mode
of mystical perception:

1. **A special mystical faculty** — a power distinct from intellect and will (the trichotomist
   *nous/psychē*/body form was condemned as **[[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinarian]]**; a
   moderate form attributed to Ockham). Sharpe rejects it as **gratuitous**: it adds nothing the
   intellect does not already do.
2. **Automatism / the subliminal self** (attributed to William James and
   Delacroix) — mystical states are subjective products of the subconscious, no supernatural
   source needed (*entia non sunt multiplicanda*). Sharpe concedes the *resemblance* to states
   induced by drugs, pathology, or nervous excitement, but answers that for genuine Christian
   mystics no such cause exists, and that the great mystics are conspicuously sane and competent;
   where no natural cause can be found, divine agency is the probable one (reinforced by a "moral
   probability" argument).
3. **The conciliation theory** (Maine de Biran, Görres, Delacroix) — the experience is a *real*
   divine communication, but the soul becomes *conscious* of it by the same natural psychological
   mechanism as automatism (the subconscious as "le véhicule de la grâce divine"). Sharpe
   **endorses** this and notes it coincides with the practical view of Church authority.

The Church's working **criterion** for distinguishing genuine from spurious is the external,
"**pragmatic**" test of orthodoxy and morality — necessary but, Sharpe insists, *not sufficient*:
heretical or immoral mysticism is necessarily spurious, but orthodox and moral mysticism need not
be genuine (subjective certitude does not prove a state mystical).

## Against the Pantheist and Reductionist Theories

Two rival theories are reported only to be refuted:

- **The pantheist / absolutist (perennialist) theory** holds that beneath all the doctrinal
  trappings the mystics of every religion contemplate one and the same impersonal Absolute — "for
  Catholic, Protestant, Buddhist and Mohammedan precisely one and the same." Sharpe answers (a)
  **logically**: we have only the mystics' own reports, and cannot credit part while discarding
  the rest — Teresa's explicit intuition *of the Holy Trinity* cannot be filtered out; the
  recorded experiences (Paul, Schopenhauer, Julian of Norwich, the
  Hesychast, the Buddhist, Mohammed, Plotinus) "are so various as to be mutually destructive"; and
  (b) **morally**: if the Christian mystics were deceived about contemplating the triune God, their
  expenditure of life is hard to justify. See [[perennialism]].
- **The naturalist / psychological-reductionist theory** — chiefly James's
  *Varieties of Religious Experience*, that a natural "cosmic consciousness" is aroused by natural
  stimuli (nitrous oxide, fasting, optical strain). Sharpe's Appendix I marshals the new
  psychology of **"imageless thought"** (Bühler, Aveling) to argue that thinking proceeds by
  *concepts*, not images, so the embodied soul's direct intuition of an immaterial object is
  unproblematic — and that James's own instances can each be traced to an ordinary sense-image
  plus its concept. There is therefore "no specifically cosmic form of consciousness, but only an
  aptitude, common to mankind, for contemplating ideas apart from images." Genuine mystical states
  alone lack any traceable natural cause — hence their attribution to "direct divine illumination."

## Immanence, Transcendence, and Evil

- **Immanence and transcendence** (Sharpe's Ch. VII): God is immanent as the efficient cause from
  which everything continuously derives being (*per essentiam, praesentiam, potentiam*) and
  transcendent as absolutely incommensurable with creatures. Sharpe rejects the **Spinozist**
  (God = nature/substance) and **Hegelian/Eckhartian** (God = nature + "more," intensively)
  identifications, under which mysticism would be impossible (its object being non-existent). The
  terms are "two ways in which God may be apprehended by us, not … two modes of His existence":
  "the transcendence of God is immanent, and His immanence is transcendent." The German mystics'
  doctrine of the **"ground" (Grund)** — the created ground (substance of the soul) meeting the
  uncreated ground (God immanent) — he defends in tauler and Ruysbroeck but flags **Eckhart**
  for at times collapsing the two into ontological identity (incipient pantheism).
- **Evil** (Ch. VI): with Aquinas (and the Stoic Cleanthes, Plotinus, Augustine), evil is
  **privation / negation**, having "no substantive existence"; there can be no *Summum Malum*. Evil
  is the work of created free-will, reconciled with divine goodness by God's independence and
  supremacy ("an irregularity of detail which subserves the regularity of the whole"). Sharpe
  rejects Manichaean dualism and the pessimism he ascribes to Buddhism, Schopenhauer, Hartmann,
  Spinoza, and Bradley. Mysticism's contribution is *practical*, not theoretical: the experienced
  "familiar friendship of God" as a real counterpoise to suffering — hence the mystics' paradox
  that "it would be better to be united to God in hell, than to be separated from Him in heaven."

## True vs. Spurious ("Heretical") Mystics

Sharpe distinguishes genuine mystical *experience* (ineffable, neither true nor false) from
*doctrines* deduced from it (which can be false). "Mystical experience … cannot be either true or
false, in the sense in which a doctrine must be"; a divine vision confers no immunity from later
intellectual or moral error (neither Moses nor Paul claimed it). He distinguishes mysticism from
**theosophy** ("the degenerate offspring of a false theory of mysticism") and classes as *not*
truly mystical: the **Beghards/Fraticelli** (condemned at Vienne; held the beatific vision
attainable by nature without the *lumen gloriae*), **Jacob Boehme** (a
pseudo-philosophical system reached by auto-suggestion), and **Swedenborg**
(allegorised Protestant theology presented as revelation). On **Quietism** (Molinos, Madame
Guyon) he is nuanced: the underlying experience may have been genuine and supernatural, but the
doctrine of total annihilation of the self drawn from it is "contradictory … of the elementary
facts of human nature." Orthodox mystics whose claims were rightly scrutinised before acceptance:
Teresa, John of the Cross, **Margaret Mary Alacoque**.

## Mysticism, Philosophy, and Religion

Mysticism cannot be investigated directly (only through contemplatives' reports) and lies beyond
philosophy's *science of causes*, which cannot treat the First Cause inductively. Its one point of
contact with philosophy is as **experimental evidence for natural theology** — a verification of
the existence of God and the substantial soul. Against the supposed antagonism of mysticism and
"institutional" religion, Sharpe holds the two are *continuous*: "mysticism represents, not a
short cut, but an advanced stage of the journey"; all religion is an approach to God, and the
mystical and the ordinary interpenetrate — his exemplar is the ***Imitation of Christ***,
mystical in tone throughout while insisting on elementary duties. He leaves open
that transient mystical experience may occasionally be granted even to non-mystics.
