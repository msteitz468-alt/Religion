# Logos Christology

The identification of Jesus Christ with the pre-existent divine *Logos* (Word) of John 1:1–18. One of the most discussed Christological frameworks in the history of Christian theology, the Logos title appears only in the Johannine prologue (John 1:1, 14) and in the related text of Revelation 19:13 ("his name is the Word of God"). Despite its brevity in the text, it has had an outsized influence on patristic Christology, trinitarian doctrine, and the history of Christian engagement with Greek philosophy.

## The Text

**John 1:1**: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
**John 1:14**: "And the Word came [egeneto] in human flesh and dwelt among us, and we saw his glory, glory as of an only-begotten from a father, full of grace and truth."

Three claims in 1:1:
1. The Word was pre-existent ("in the beginning *was*")
2. The Word was in relationship with God ("the Word was *with* God" — *pros ton theon*)
3. The Word was God ("the Word *was* God" — *theos ēn ho logos*)

For the grammatical and Christological controversy about clause 3, see [[word-was-god-1-1|John 1:1 — "The Word Was God"]].

## "Logos" as a Title: Its Limited Scope

The word *logos* (Word) as a Christological title appears exactly **four times** in the Gospel — three times in 1:1 and once in 1:14 — and is never used again in the rest of John. [[j-ramsey-michaels|Michaels]] (NICNT, 2010) argues that this limits the significance of the Logos title as an interpretive key to John's Christology. He reads the preamble (1:1–5) as moving from "the Word" toward "the light" as its governing image, with "the light is shining in the darkness" (1:5) as the preamble's triumphant conclusion. "Light," not "Logos," is the dominant image through the first half of the Gospel; accordingly, Michaels reads John primarily as a Gospel of revelation rather than a Gospel of the Word.

This does not diminish the theological weight of the Logos title in vv. 1–14 — but it cautions against making "Logos Christology" the organizing framework for interpreting John as a whole.

## Background and Tradition

The background of the Logos concept in John is debated. The major proposals:

**Jewish Wisdom tradition** (most favored). The pre-existent Logos of John 1 resonates strongly with personified Wisdom in Proverbs 8 ("I was there when he set the heavens in place"; "I was the craftsman at his side"), Sirach 24, and Wisdom of Solomon 7–9. Jewish wisdom was with God "from the beginning," was present at creation, "came" to Israel, and "dwelt" among God's people. The connection to [[woman-wisdom|Woman Wisdom (Personified Wisdom)]] is very close. Some scholars (Witherington) prefer "Wisdom Christology" to "Logos Christology" as the dominant framework.

**Philo of Alexandria.** The Jewish-Hellenistic philosopher Philo (c. 20 BCE–50 CE) used *logos* extensively as a mediating divine principle between the transcendent God and the material world — a hypostasis that is God's "image" and "firstborn." The parallels with John 1 are striking but the relationship is debated: Philo's *logos* is never incarnate and never personal in the way John's Word is.

**Stoic philosophy.** Hellenistic philosophy used *logos* for the rational principle pervading the cosmos. Less likely as a direct background for John, but part of the general intellectual environment.

**Hebrew *dabar* (word).** The creative and revelatory word of God in the Hebrew Bible — "God said, and it was so" (Gen 1); the word of the LORD came to the prophets — provides the OT grounding for John's use. The Targums sometimes translate YHWH with *Memra* (Aramaic "word"), though this parallel is contested.

## The Incarnation

John 1:14 uses the verb *egeneto* (came to be, came into existence) — the same verb used for creation (1:3, "all things came into being through him") — for the Word's entry into flesh. The Word who was eternal and through whom all things were created "came to be" in human flesh. This is the most direct statement of incarnation in the NT.

*Eskēnōsen* ("dwelt among us") literally means "pitched his tent" or "tabernacled." This alludes to the [[tabernacle|Tabernacle (Mishkan)]] and the [[divine-glory-kabod|Divine Glory (*Kābôd*)]] of the wilderness — God dwelling among his people in the *mishkan*. The connection is reinforced by "we saw his glory (*doxa*)," echoing the visible *kābôd* that filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35). The incarnation is John's answer to the question of where God's glory now dwells.

## Patristic and Later Use

The Logos title became enormously productive in early Christian theology:

- **Ptolemy** (Valentinian Gnostic, mid-2nd c.): the earliest known commentator on John, used 1:1–5 to construct an aeon-hierarchy. Creation becomes the work of three entities (God, Beginning, Logos). Irenaeus refutes this reading but agrees with Ptolemy on John's authorship.
- **Justin Martyr** (2nd c.): used *logos* to claim Jesus as the divine reason/word present in all philosophers who "lived according to reason" — a missiological application.
- **Origen** (early 3rd c.): most extensive early commentary on John; Logos Christology integrated into his subordinationist metaphysics (see [[origen|Origen]] figure page).
- **Arianism**: used Logos Christology (especially "the Father is greater than I," 14:28) to argue the Son is a created, subordinate being.
- **Nicene orthodoxy**: insisted the Son is *homoousios* (same substance) with the Father; "the Word was God" (1:1c) as the proof-text.

**Christopher Stead's analysis** (*Philosophy in Christian Antiquity*, 1994, esp. Ch. 6 on Middle Platonists and Philo, Ch. 13 on Logos and Spirit): The Middle Platonic revival (Antiochus of Ascalon onward) supplied a theistic, hierarchical framework in which the transcendent God relates to the world through intermediary principles. Philo of Alexandria was the key Jewish exponent: his Logos functions as God's "image," "firstborn," or "second God" — a mediating hypostasis that allows the simple, unchanging God to create and govern without direct contact with matter. Christians inherited this conceptual toolkit via Alexandria and adapted it decisively: the Logos is identified with the pre-existent Christ who *becomes flesh* (John 1:14). Stead notes the philosophical pressure this created — how can the immutable, simple God "come to be" in human nature? — which drove the later precision around *ousia*, *hypostasis*, and the two natures. He treats the patristic achievement as real philosophical and theological work, while cautioning that most of it remains theology using philosophical tools rather than autonomous philosophy (see his Ch. 8 on the debate about Christian philosophy). Stead's semantic and logical scrutiny of the development from Philo through the Nicene and Chalcedonian settlements provides the wiki's primary modern scholarly account of how Logos Christology was philosophically elaborated.

**Harry Austryn Wolfson's analysis** (*The Philosophy of the Church Fathers*, 1956, esp. Part Two on the Trinity, the Logos, and Platonic Ideas, and Part Three on the Incarnation): Wolfson reconstructs the "philosophy of the Church Fathers" as a systematic Christian version of Greek philosophy (parallel to Philo’s Jewish version), using his hypothetico-deductive method of piecing scattered terms, formulas, and analogies into a unified system. NT background: tripartite confessions (baptismal, 2 Cor salutation, Paul’s hortatory) arise from Paul’s identification of the Holy Spirit with the preexistent Christ/wisdom (taking over the Law’s function); John identifies the Logos with this preexistent Christ (plus Wisdom of Solomon influence), with vagueness on "became flesh" and the Spirit’s distinctness. Early Apostolic Fathers and Apologists often identify preexistent Christ as both Logos and Holy Spirit (no clear pre-birth Trinity). Differentiation of Logos and Holy Spirit appears with the Apologists’ "twofold stage theory" of the Logos (becomes distinct hypostasis/person at a point, e.g., for creation or incarnation; interpretations of John 1:1). Single stage theory in Irenaeus and Origen (debate on Clement; "eternal generation" as both continuous process and from eternity; generation "by will" [Philo/Apologists/Origen/Athanasius debates] or "by nature"; Holy Spirit’s origination parallel, with lingering identifications of Spirit with preexistent Christ—application of "God," "wisdom," and functions to both—plus verbal/causal distinctions). 

Wolfson traces the "mystery of the generation" (God as begetter vs. artisan; physical analogies like uttered word or sun beam/light for Logos and Spirit; aptness questioned; mystery remains) and the "mystery of the Trinity" (reconciling one God with three each God via relative unity: unity of rule or unity of substratum/genus/species—ousia/hypostasis, homoousios; detailed in Origen [hypostases for individuals, ousia for common genus, homoousios + unity of rule], Tertullian [personae and substantia; contrasting pairs], Basil fixing vocabulary amid Nicene vagueness and heresies [ousia/hypostasis for common substratum in Creed, Son begotten of Father’s ousia and homoousios; expression open to multiple interpretations generating suspicions like homoiousios]). 

For the Incarnation ("two and one"), Wolfson details the use of physical union analogies (Aristotelian composition/mixture/"predominance" [soul-body or form-matter, loosely called mixture/composition]; Stoic mixture/confusion; five kinds total) to explain duality of natures with unity of person. Orthodox preference for "predominance" (Tertullian as electrum [popular sense], Origen, Gregories, Augustine, Cyril of Alexandria, Leontius, John of Damascus, Fifth Ecumenical Council). Perichoresis ("total penetration"/"thorough penetration," Stoic term for mixture) for interchange of properties (Gregories, pseudo-Cyril, Maximus, John Damascene). Mystery remains due to differences from human union. Unorthodox uses differ (Apollinaris/Monophysites apply "predominance" to deny full human nature; Nestorius uses "composition"/synapheia for two persons). Connected to wills/operations controversies (Monenergist/Monothelete; Sophronius, Sergius, Honorius, Maximus vs. Pyrrhus; prohibitions of "one will"/"two wills").

Wolfson places heavy emphasis on Philo as the indispensable foundation and prolegomenon (allegory classifications traceable to him; scriptural presuppositions; Logos/ideas; subordination of philosophy). He also devotes Part Four to "the Anathematized" (Gnosticism as pagan syncretism "Christianized" into tiered systems with prolation—not true philosophy, though Fathers trace elements to Plato etc.; heresies like Modalism [Sabellius] and Creationalism [Arius] for Trinity, neo-Ebionism [Theodotus, Nestorius] and neo-Docetism [Apollinaris] for Christology, arising logically from difficulties in catholic beliefs). His reconstruction presents the Fathers as creators of a coherent philosophic system solving the "three and one" and "two and one" problems by adapting solutions to the one-and-many problem. (Complements Stead’s more concise treatment; Wolfson is more exhaustive on NT-to-Fathers development, allegory, single/double faith theories, and heresies.)

## Relation to John's Christology

Michaels's framing: the Gospel's Christology is a **parabola** — the Son descends from the Father into the world to reveal the Father, and then returns to the Father. The Logos title names the pre-existent origin point; the narrative traces the descent and return. The Logos is both divine (1:1c) and obedient/subordinate (14:28; 5:19, 30) — the tension Michaels identifies as the Gospel's central Christological contribution: Jesus as God's unique Envoy who simultaneously claims deity and submission.
