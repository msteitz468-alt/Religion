"Woman Wisdom" (*ḥokmâ* as a personified female figure) is one of the most theologically generative images in the Hebrew Bible. She appears primarily in Proverbs 1-9 as a figure who:

- Calls out to the gullible at the city gate with a warning and invitation (1:20-33)
- Addresses her audience with authority in the public square (8:1-36)
- Recounts her presence with YHWH at the moment of creation (8:22-31)
- Sets a banquet and invites the young to eat and gain life (9:1-6)

Her rival and literary counterpart, **Woman Folly** (also called "the unchaste wife" in the father's lectures, chs. 5-7), mirrors her invitations but leads to death (9:13-18).

The interpretation of Woman Wisdom is one of the most contested questions in OT theology and has had decisive consequences for both the Arian controversy (4th century CE) and for contemporary feminist theology.

**The ch. 7 / ch. 8 diptych (Waltke).** Waltke reads Proverbs 7 (the seductive strange woman) and Proverbs 8 (Woman Wisdom's self-praise) as a deliberately "hinged" pair operating in contrasting registers (citing Newsom): "The unchaste wife moves covertly at dusk and speaks falsely; Wisdom moves publicly and speaks direct and authoritative truth... The foreign wife inhabits the earthly and mundane; Wisdom soars in heaven above space and time." This diptych is completed in the epilogue (ch. 9), where the two rival banquets — Wisdom's built house of seven pillars (9:1–6) versus Woman Folly's slovenly, foodless imposture (9:13–18) — bring the prologue's antithesis to its apex, and where Wisdom's "built house / prepared banquet" doubles as a janus to Collection II (the finished house = the prologue; the banquet about to begin = the proverbs of Solomon).

## The Key Text: Proverbs 8:22-31

The most theologically significant passage in Proverbs:

> "The LORD *possessed/created* (*qānânî*) me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old. Ages ago I was *set up* (*nissaktî*), at the first, before the beginning of the earth. . . . When he established the heavens I was there; when he drew a circle on the face of the deep. . . . Then I was beside him like a *master workman* or *little child* (*'āmôn*); I was daily his delight, rejoicing before him always, rejoicing in his inhabited world and delighting in the children of man." (8:22-23, 27, 30-31)

The four major interpretive cruxes:

**1. *qānânî* (8:22) — "created" or "possessed"?**  
The Arians read *qānânî* as "created" — God created Wisdom as the first of his creatures; the Son (identified with Wisdom) is therefore a creature, not co-eternal with God. Athanasius responded with two strategies: the "creation" refers to the incarnation; or the "creation" refers to Wisdom's image being imprinted on creatures. Waltke: both the Arian use and Athanasius's responses are exegetically driven by theological agendas, not by the text itself.

Waltke's own philological preference (following Irwin) is a **third** option, "**to beget / bring forth**," over both "acquire/possess" (Aquila, Symmachus, Vulgate; the normal Proverbs sense) and "create" (LXX, Targum, Syriac). His grounds: the parallel verbs "I was formed" (8:23) and "I was brought forth" (8:24–25, *ḥôlaltî*, from *ḥîl* "to writhe in childbirth") unambiguously carry a birth metaphor; and Ugaritic *qnyt 'lm* ("procreatress of the gods") and Deut 32:6 (the begetting Father) support it. Crucially, Waltke insists the metaphor is guarded against polytheism ("a literal, polytheistic interpretation involving the LORD with a sexual partner... is unthinkable in this book"): "brought me forth" signifies that "Solomon's inspired wisdom comes from God's essential being... a revelation that has an organic connection with God's very nature and being, unlike the rest of creation that came into existence outside of him."

**2. *'āmôn* (8:30) — "master workman" or "little child"?**  
If "master workman" (craftsworker/artisan): Wisdom actively participated in creation. If "little child" (nursling, darling): Wisdom was present with God as a companion-observer, not an agent. The two readings yield different degrees of Wisdom's role.

Waltke rejects *both* the majority "artisan/craftsman" reading (no lexical support without circular argument; a poor parallel to "delighting/playing"; and it would be "unique... for Woman Wisdom to describe herself by a male image") and the "nursling/ward" reading (which would require a feminine form and would make Wisdom's claim to grave authority "not very credible" if she were merely a rejoicing little child). His own choice is a fourth option: a Qal infinitive absolute of the root I *'mn* ("to be firm, faithful") → "**constantly / faithfully**" (with Symmachus, Theodotion, Targum), which best matches the adverbs "daily" (*yôm yôm*) and "always" in the immediate context. Waltke further argues (against Fox and the LXX, who make God the one delighting) that in 8:30b–31 **Wisdom herself is the actor** — the one "delighting" and "celebrating/playing" before the LORD and rejoicing in his inhabited world, a cultic act of joyful vitality (cf. 2 Sam 6; Job 38:7).

**3. The identity question: Is she a hypostasis?**  
A hypostasis is a concrete heavenly being who represents or stands for God, semi-independent from him. Classical Christian reading: yes. Waltke's reading: no — she is a literary personification of Solomon's revealed wisdom.

**4. What exactly is she personifying?**  
The major proposals: (a) God's attribute of wisdom (*hypostatic* reading — a divine being); (b) creation's inherent order (*ma'at* thesis — von Rad); (c) Solomon's revealed wisdom as encoded in the prologue's teaching; (d) a heavenly figure who later becomes the divine Logos.

## Interpretive Positions

### Position 1: Hypostasis / Divine Being (Patristic Reading)

**Claim**: Woman Wisdom is a heavenly being who exists independently of, yet in intimate relationship with, God. She is the pre-existent agent of creation. Beginning with Justin Martyr (ca. 125 CE), she was identified with Jesus Christ.

**In favor**:
- Her self-description (before the earth, beside YHWH at creation) sounds like a being, not a metaphor
- Early Christian tradition near-universally made this identification
- NT Logos Christology (John 1) and Pauline Christology (Col 1:15-17) seem to draw on wisdom categories

**Problems** (Waltke):
- The grammatico-historical meaning of Prov 8:22-31 does not support treating her as a hypostasis
- Von Rad's thesis that she represents a divine cosmic order (*ma'at*) fails because no text in Proverbs teaches that creation reveals wisdom — Agur's confession (30:1-6) explicitly denies that humans can gain wisdom from creation
- LXX and early Jewish interpreters (Sirach, Wisdom of Solomon) significantly modify Prov 8 in different and contradictory directions — there is no straight trajectory from Prov 8 to NT high Christology
- John never uses *sophia* in connection with the Logos; his Christology is sui generis

### Position 2: Literary Personification of Solomon's Teaching (Waltke's Position)

**Claim**: Woman Wisdom is the literary personification of the father's revealed wisdom as encoded in the prologue. She is not a heavenly being. The prologue equation: Wisdom's call in the public square (1:20-33) = the father's lectures in the home — she is the "public voice" corresponding to his "private voice." Her self-description in 8:22-31 establishes the *authority* of this wisdom (it was present with God from before creation) and its *reliability* (it paid attention when the cosmos was formed).

**In favor**:
- Consistent with the prologue's literary structure (Wisdom = father's teaching; Folly = unchaste wife)
- Agur's proof (30:1-6): wisdom cannot be obtained from creation; only YHWH's special revelation provides it — which is exactly what the book of Proverbs claims to be
- Explains why Solomon names himself (1:1) as the source: he received wisdom by divine gift (1 Kgs 4:29-34) and the prologue is his personification of that gift
- No hypostasis is needed; the personification is a literary device serving a pedagogical purpose

**Problems**:
- Does not fully account for the cosmic claims of 8:22-31 — "before the beginning of the earth" and "beside him like a master workman" seem to exceed a metaphor for human teaching
- The NT's use of wisdom categories for Christology becomes harder to explain

### Position 3: Literary Personification as Type of Christ (Waltke's Canonical Christology)

This is Waltke's canonical-theological resolution. The grammatico-historical reading yields: Woman Wisdom is a literary personification. The canonical-theological reading adds: she functions as a *type* of Jesus Christ — similar in structure but inferior in degree.

**Similarities between Woman Wisdom and Christ**:
- Both existed with God before all things
- Both played a role in creation
- Both descended from heaven to dwell among humanity and were rejected by the masses
- Both teach heavenly wisdom and call those who listen "children"
- Both offer life and immortality; both threaten death to those who refuse

**Christ's superiority (the asymmetry required by typology)**:
- Wisdom was *begotten/created* by God; Christ *is* God
- Wisdom *witnessed* creation; Christ *is* the Creator
- Wisdom will *laugh* at the time of judgment; Christ *is* the Judge
- Wisdom offers food and drink; Christ *is* the food and drink (John 6:53)
- Wisdom calls on Spirit to write God's word on hearts; Christ *sends* the Spirit to do so

This typological reading preserves the legitimate Christian instinct to connect Wisdom and Christ while rejecting the exegetical overreaching of direct identification.

### Position 4: Creation's Inherent Order / Egyptian *Ma'at* (von Rad)

**Claim**: Woman Wisdom represents the order God implanted in creation itself — analogous to the Egyptian concept of *ma'at*, the principle of justice and cosmic order woven into reality. Wisdom's call is creation itself inviting humanity to align with its inherent moral structure.

**In favor**:
- Explains the international character of wisdom (same truths discoverable cross-culturally)
- The creation context of 8:22-31 fits

**Problems** (Waltke, decisive):
- No text in Proverbs teaches that creation reveals wisdom
- Agur's confession directly refutes natural theology: apart from YHWH's special revelation, humans cannot attain wisdom (30:1-6)
- The "fear of the LORD" as Proverbs' epistemological foundation explicitly requires special revelation, not natural theology
- Nel's argument: "The fear of the Lord does not allow one to interpret wisdom as natural theology"

### Position 5: Jewish-Christian Sophia Theology (Feminist Reading)

**Claim**: Woman Wisdom (*Sophia* in Greek) represents a feminine divine principle, recoverable as a theological resource for feminist theology. She is the face of the divine feminine in Scripture, suppressed by androcentric tradents.

**Associated thinkers**: Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza, Elizabeth Johnson (*She Who Is*)

**Problems** (Waltke, Jobes):
- John never uses *sophia* for Christ in the Gospel or Epistles — "if the development of Wisdom-Sophia in Hellenistic Jewish writings provided such a fine fit with the apostolic understanding of Jesus that culminates in the high Christology of John's gospel, it is certainly strange beyond comprehension that the Greek word *sophia* does not occur once in John's gospel"
- The Logos of John's prologue is *superior* to Woman Wisdom: Wisdom was created/begotten; the Logos is God; Wisdom was a reflection of light; the Logos is the Light itself
- Pushing Woman Wisdom toward a goddess figure misreads both her ANE context and the literary structure of Proverbs

## The Arian Controversy

The most consequential doctrinal dispute using this text: Arius (~318 CE) used Prov 8:22 ("The LORD *created* me") to argue that the Son of God was God's most exalted creature, not co-eternal with the Father. The formula "there was a time when he was not" was Arius's slogan.

Athanasius (~325 CE) responded with two strategies:
1. The "creation" of Wisdom in 8:22 refers to Christ's becoming incarnate — the human nature, not the eternal Son, was "created"
2. The "creation of Wisdom" was actually the creation of Wisdom's image in creatures as they participated in creation

The Nicene Council (325 CE) rejected Arianism by declaring the Son *homoousios* (of the same substance) with the Father.

Waltke's assessment: both sides argued from a text that doesn't support their claims in its grammatico-historical sense. "Theological principles largely explain exegetical results in the patristic period." A correct reading of Prov 8 would make it neither a proof text for the Son's co-eternity (which requires eisegesis) nor a proof text for his creatureliness (which misidentifies Woman Wisdom with Christ).

## Jewish Uses of Wisdom Personification

- **Sirach 24**: Wisdom identifies herself explicitly with the Torah of Moses — completing an integration implied but undeveloped in canonical Proverbs
- **Wisdom of Solomon 7:22-8:1**: Wisdom described with 21 philosophical attributes; "fashioner of all things"; the "world soul" mediating between Creator and creation
- **Jerusalem Targum on Gen 1:1**: "The LORD created the heavens and the earth by wisdom" — Prov 8:22 + Gen 1:1 combined
- **Philo**: Wisdom/Logos as the agent of creation — most developed pre-Christian synthesis

None of these provide a direct trajectory to NT Christology; each modifies Prov 8 substantially in different directions.

## NT Connections

- **John 1:1-14**: The Logos draws on wisdom language but is explicitly *superior* to Woman Wisdom at every point; the word *sophia* never appears in John
- **1 Cor 1:24, 30**: "Christ the Wisdom of God" — relates wisdom to the cross, not to creation; atonement is not Woman Wisdom's domain in Proverbs
- **Col 1:15-17**: "Firstborn of all creation" — Christ as primogeniture over creation, not as a creature
- **Heb 1:3**: "The radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of his nature" — alludes to Wis 7:26 but in a way that makes Christ superior to Wisdom, not equivalent to her
- **Matt 11:19; 12:42**: Jesus identifies himself with Wisdom and asserts his superiority to Solomon

## The Book's Feminine Frame: Woman Wisdom and the Valiant Wife

Waltke's second volume reads Woman Wisdom (Prologue) and the [[valiant-woman|valiant wife]]
(epilogue, 31:10–31) as the intentional **frame** of Proverbs — "no coincidence but … the book's
frame." The load-bearing distinction is that they are *different kinds* of figure: Woman Wisdom is
"purely symbolic," a personification of Solomon's revealed wisdom, whereas Waltke reads the valiant
wife as a **historical** exemplar who *incarnates* wisdom's ideals "without removing her from the
historical realm." McCreesh and (with leanings) Whybray read the valiant wife too as an allegory of
Wisdom; Waltke resists this, noting *ʾēšet ḥayil* "denotes a real woman in its other occurrence
(12:4)" and in Ruth 3:11, and that Woman Wisdom (unlike the wife) "is never clearly pictured as a
wife or a mother." The two pages are reciprocal: see [[valiant-woman|the Valiant Woman]] for the
full allegory-vs-real controversy.
