# The Antitheses (Matthew 5:21–47)

## Definition

"The antitheses" is the conventional name for the six paired sayings at Matt 5:21-47, each
contrasting what "was said" with what Jesus says. [[rt-france|R. T. France]] notes the label
descriptively: each example is "presented in the form of a contrast (hence the frequent description
of this section as 'the antitheses')" between what was said and Jesus' more demanding ethic. The six
concern murder (vv. 21-26), adultery (vv. 27-30), divorce (vv. 31-32), oaths (vv. 33-37),
retribution (vv. 38-42) and love of neighbour (vv. 43-47), and are summed up at v. 48.

France judges the variation in the introductory formula — "You have heard that it was said to people
long ago" / "You have heard that it was said" / "It was said" — to carry "no discernible difference
in intention." He also resists dividing the six into two sets of three: the repeated full formula
with "again" at v. 33 is "often taken to indicate" such a division, but "it is not easy to see any
difference in principle between the first three and the last three," and the third and fourth are
closely similar.

France concedes at the outset that the demands are widely judged unworkable: "So demanding are
Jesus' alternative rulings that those who fully grasp his intention often declare them to be
unworkable in the real world," posing "formidable problems for those who wish to treat it as a
straightforward code of conduct."

## The dialogue partner: France's two philological arguments

The decisive question for France is whether Jesus is setting his teaching against the divine law
itself. He answers with two philological observations:

1. ***errethē* is divine speech.** France argues the passive is used in the NT "specifically for
   quotations of Scripture or divine pronouncements," and is the same form as in the
   [[formula-quotations|quotation formula]] of 1:22. "This is thus not a reference to human teaching
   but to divine declaration."
2. **"To people long ago."** The declaration was made to "the ancients," so "the reference cannot
   then be to any contemporary or recent tradition." The first half of each contrast must therefore
   be Mosaic law as heard in synagogue, not [[oral-torah|oral tradition]].

Given both, France's resolution is that "the dialogue partner is not the OT law as such but the OT
law as currently (and sometimes misleadingly) understood and applied."

His evidence for this is the uneven fidelity of the six "quotations": the first and second are
straight Decalogue citations; the third differs significantly from Deut 24:1 and is "angled in a
different direction"; the fourth merely summarizes; the fifth is exact but quoted for a purpose
other than the original's; and the sixth adds a non-pentateuchal clause, "spinning" Lev 19:18. He
characterizes the effect as "significantly developing and indeed distorting their intention."

France adopts **David Daube's** comparison with a rabbinic convention contrasting what is "heard" —
a literal but inadequate understanding — with what one must "say": a more enlightened, possibly more
creative, superior interpretation.

On the force of the "But I tell you" formula, France is emphatic: "His sovereign pronouncement, 'I
tell you,' is not a contribution to exegetical debate, but stands alongside the law on which it is
based as a definitive declaration of the divine purpose. As such it provokes the question 'Who is
this?'"

## France's three-mode taxonomy

France classifies how Jesus goes beyond the law across the six examples into three modes:

| Mode | Antitheses | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Interiorization | 1st–2nd (murder, adultery) | The literal ruling is *not* set aside but pressed inward to thought and attitude |
| Pre-emption | 3rd–4th (divorce, oaths) | The actions the law regulates "should never have occurred in the first place" |
| Judicial-to-personal | 5th (retaliation) | A judicial ruling is declared inapplicable to personal ethics |
| Extension | 6th (love) | Love is extended far beyond the law's purview, against a contemporary corollary |

## "Bypass, not abolish"

France's decisive formulation addresses what becomes of the "jots and tittles" of 5:18 when some
regulations are left aside — divorce regulations when divorce does not happen, oath rules when
swearing is ruled out, retribution limits when even resistance is renounced. He asks whether this is
"after all, to 'abolish' them," and answers: "**No, it is rather to bypass them, to leave them behind
as no longer needed.**"

He allows a residual practical role: "In the real world it may be that there will still be divorce,
untruthfulness, and judicial retribution, and for these purposes the regulations of the OT law may
retain a practical role. But insofar as they are needed, that is a mark of the failure of the more
radical ethic which Jesus here sets out."

At the fifth antithesis he restates it in the sharpest form: "The 'fulfillment' of the law (v. 17)
here consists in leaving it behind in favor of something of a different order altogether, the
righteousness of the kingdom of heaven." See [[fulfillment|fulfilment]], [[old-law|old law]],
[[new-law|new law]] and [[righteousness|righteousness]].

France's governing hermeneutical rule for applying the six is a warning against replacing one code
with another: "Jesus' illustrations of the 'greater righteousness' are not to be treated as if they
were a new set of literal regulations to replace those of the scribes and Pharisees."

## The six examples

### 1. Murder (5:21–26)

France reads v. 22 as leaving the act–penalty correlation intact while going behind it: "the actual
committing of murder is only the outward manifestation of an inward attitude which is itself
culpable, whether or not it actually issues in the act of murder." He explicitly excludes a modern
application: "our modern questions concerning the appropriateness of capital punishment for murder
are not raised."

He finds an ascending scale in the *punishments* (judgment, trial, hellfire) but "no such clear
escalation in the offenses cited": "stupid" and "fool" are "not readily distinguishable in either
meaning or severity," and Jesus himself uses the latter at 23:17. He therefore reads the saying
rhetorically rather than as a graded penal code: the hearer "might well have chuckled over the
incongruous image of a person being tried for anger" before being "pulled up short by the saying's
conclusion." He contrasts 1QS 6:25–7:9, where abusive language draws "a graded range of periods of
exclusion from the assembly" — a real graded penal code, against which Jesus' hellfire is
deliberately disproportionate rhetoric.

France takes *adelphos* in vv. 22-24 as "probably ... a fellow disciple rather than a literal family
member," but adds "It would, however, be pedantic to suggest that Jesus' ruling applies only to
relations with fellow disciples." He reads vv. 23-24 and 25-26 as "in effect two little parables
about reconciliation," and emphasizes the deliberate improbability of the altar scenario — a
worshipper who has travelled some eighty miles to the only altar leaves the animal and makes a
week-long round trip to reconcile: "The improbability of the scenario emphasizes Jesus' point."

He distinguishes *geënna* — the place of final destruction of the wicked, well attested in Jewish
apocalyptic, named from the Valley of Hinnom, once a site of child sacrifice to Molech — from Hades,
the place of the dead: "It is not the same as Hades." He flags the burning-rubbish-dump explanation
of Gehenna as "a later tradition" and conditions it: "if true, this would provide a vivid image."
See [[last-judgment|last judgment]].

### 2. Adultery (5:27–30)

France reads vv. 27-28 as structurally identical to vv. 21-22. He adds an important qualification:
the commandment concerns "specifically a man who has sexual relations with another man's wife," so
"the 'woman' in Jesus' declaration is thus to be understood also as another man's wife." He
explicitly rejects the scrupulous reading: "The focus is thus not (as some tender adolescent
consciences have read it) on sexual attraction as such, but on the desire for (and perhaps the
planning of) an illicit sexual liaison," comparing the coveting of Exod 20:17.

He reads vv. 29-30 as exaggeration using "a shocking but well-recognized metaphor of
self-mutilation," and insists the theme of *skandalizō* is "impediments to ultimate salvation, and
the importance of eliminating them at all costs, a theme which could have many different
applications ... certainly not only to sexual temptation." He warns against doctrinal over-reading:
the "whole body" thrown into hell "belongs to the pictorial imagery ... it is not the basis for a
doctrinal debate over either the nature of human existence after death or the physicality of hell.
Nor should this passage be used to suggest that amputees will be raised in an imperfect body."

### 3. Divorce (5:31–32)

France makes an editorial decision to treat divorce **here** rather than at 19:3-12, asking the
reader of the later passage to refer back: "it seems better to deal with the issue more generally
here." The full treatment therefore belongs to this section of his commentary. See
[[divorce|divorce]] for the detail.

In outline: he stresses that Deut 24:1-4, the only pentateuchal passage directly on divorce, "was
not concerned with the rightness or wrongness of divorce in itself, nor with permissible causes of
divorce, but only with the aftermath of a divorce which is assumed to have taken place," and that
the "quotation" at v. 31 "is not in fact a direct quotation of the Deuteronomy text but an inference
from it." He argues Jesus raises the issue "not to discuss the grounds ... but in order to query the
assumption that any divorce could be acceptable in the first place." On the *porneia* exception he
concludes that "Matthew's Jesus thus appears to hold a Shammaite position — though a particularly
strict one," and offers as an argument ("it can be argued") that adultery had already destroyed the
one-flesh union, so that "Matthew is merely making explicit what was assumed by Mark and Luke to be
already obvious." He firmly rejects the modern view permitting divorce after adultery but forbidding
remarriage, on the ground that "Divorce and the right to remarry are thus inseparable" in the Jewish
context. His verdict on the OT text is that "In opposing current divorce legislation Jesus is
rescuing Deut 24:1-4 from misuse for a purpose for which it was never intended."

### 4. Oaths (5:33–37)

France states the structural parallel with divorce: in both, "A law which aims to control human
failure ... is set aside in favor of a bold reassertion of the way God intended things to be" —
lifelong marriage, and simple truthfulness in speech without oaths to undergird it. Such laws are
"not so much abrogated as declared unnecessary where the greater righteousness of the kingdom of
heaven obtains."

He makes an important concession here. Because the OT law "not only provided for but in some cases
demanded such elaborating oaths (e.g., Num 5:19-22), there is a prima facie case to be made that
Jesus is here opposing the intention of one aspect of the law." He distinguishes carefully: with
*vows* Jesus is "not so much opposing OT legislation as telling his disciples not to take up an
option which the law offered but did not require," but some oaths *were* legally required, "and
these, too, are swept aside by Jesus' blanket prohibition if it is taken as a literal regulation."

He notes v. 33's words are "again not an exact citation" but the gist of several passages, and
raises without settling whether the false-swearing prohibition echoes the ninth commandment or the
third. He explains the listed surrogate oaths as evasive substitutes for God's name — a habit "which
underlies much of our 'social swearing' today" — dismissed because heaven, earth and Jerusalem are
inseparably linked with God: "All such surrogate oaths display not reverence but theological
superficiality." He records with amusement that "some early patristic interpreters took this verse
as a ruling against the use of hair dye!"

On "from evil" / "from the Evil One" at v. 37 he finds the ambiguity immaterial: "The context here
gives us no obvious reason for preferring the personal to the abstract sense." He raises the
self-imposed objection — is God's own covenant oath then "from evil"? — and answers that in one
sense yes, "if people were prepared to trust God's simple word there would be no need for an oath,"
but God's oath is a statement of his own dependability, unlike human oaths enlisting God for less
dependable words.

In application France holds that Christians need not refuse legal or political oaths: as with
divorce regulations, "there is still a place for legal oaths ... to cope with the actual
untruthfulness of people, even sadly sometimes of disciples," and "Refusal to take a required oath
can in such circumstances convey quite the wrong impression."

### 5. Retribution (5:38–42)

France judges this the most radical of the six: "Here Jesus' teaching moves even further away from
the spirit of the OT law quoted than in any of the previous examples."

He insists "eye for eye" is sentencing guidance: "They provide guidance in sentencing for those
responsible for trying a case of physical assault," and Jesus does not comment on its judicial
appropriateness — "His concern is only with the inappropriateness of such a formula to personal
ethics." Applied to personal ethics the formula becomes "a justification for 'getting your own
back,' and thus ultimately for the relentless perpetuation of the traditional blood-feud."

He corrects a common apologetic: while talionic rules "may have been intended originally to limit
the extravagant vengeance associated with an oriental blood-feud ... the OT texts do not express
this intention," and Deut 19:21 is preceded by "Show no pity" to stop judges mitigating penalties.
He holds the pentateuchal rulings were "clearly intended to be applied literally," but that by
Jesus' time financial compensation had generally replaced mutilation, "so that it is probably not
physical brutality as such which Jesus is here opposing, but rather the essential principle of even
legitimate retribution." He notes the same eye/tooth examples appear in the much earlier code of
Hammurabi (§§196-201).

France states the radicalism plainly: "Jesus' position is shockingly radical: not only no
retaliation, but even no resistance to one who is admittedly 'bad.'" He stresses the breadth: "no
distinction is made between active and passive resistance, violent and nonviolent, legal and
illegal." He rules out both "the Evil One" (the examples are human) and the abstract "Do not resist
evil" (it is improbable that Jesus or Matthew would countenance "such an amoral attitude"); the
person is genuinely bad, "but even their admitted badness does not justify the disciple in resisting
them."

He delimits the issue precisely: "the issue, then, is not whether one should stand up for good in
principle (or as it affects other people), but whether one should stand up for oneself when under
threat" — and adds the important qualification that "a willingness to forgo one's own rights and
even to allow oneself to be insulted and imposed on is not incompatible with a firm stand for
justice in principle and for the rights of others."

On the illustrations: the right cheek requires a right-handed assailant to use a backhanded slap,
"far more insulting" and entailing double damages, so "This is more a matter of honor than of
physical injury" and the disciple is to forgo compensation he is legally entitled to; France links
the response to Isa 50:6, God's servant who "gave my back to those who struck me." The *himation*
could not legally be forfeited, so "What the opponent could not have dared to claim, the disciple is
to offer freely." *Angareuō* he explains as the Roman soldier's right to conscript a subject for
forced labour, its only other NT use being Simon of Cyrene (27:32); volunteering a double stint for
the occupier "was unheard of," and "suggests that Jesus advocated a response to the Roman occupation
which not only full-blown Zealots but even the ordinarily patriotic populace would have found
incomprehensible." He calls v. 42 "the most obviously impractical of all these illustrations, just
because it does not speak of an exceptional situation," and notes Jesus' wording "does not allow us
the luxury of distinguishing" neighbour from beggar, while the discrimination principle of 7:6
"seems to offer a welcome refuge."

France's summary formula: "In place of the principle of retribution he sets nonresistance; in place
of the defense of legal rights he sets uncalculating generosity; in place of concern for oneself he
sets concern for the other." He concedes the standard objection — that this "would be to encourage
the unscrupulous and the feckless" — and answers by reframing rather than softening: "instead of
therefore dismissing Jesus' teaching as starry-eyed utopianism, a proper response ... is to ask in
what practical ways Jesus' radical principles can be set to work in our very different world." He
adds a rule about the whole set: "none of these illustrations sets out a prudential maxim; all of
them (like the Beatitudes) challenge us to unnatural behavior, and all must seem crazy to a secular
world." Application he leaves open: "It is for each disciple to work out for themselves how this
principle can most responsibly be applied."

### 6. Love of neighbour and enemy (5:43–47)

France notes that here uniquely "there is no question of Jesus contradicting what 'was said' in the
Pentateuch; indeed, he might be thought rather to be defending it," since "hate your enemy" is not
part of Lev 19:18. He locates the crux in the term "neighbour," which for most contemporary
interpreters was restrictive, "leaving non-neighbors outside the command to love; hence the popular
addition 'and hate your enemy.'"

He concedes the OT sense frankly: "The general use of 'neighbor' in the OT suggests that Lev 19:18
has this restrictive sense, applying specifically to fellow members of the Israelite community." See
[[israelites|Israelites]]. He goes further, in a striking concession, holding that the added clause
"was not so far wide of the mark," citing Deut 23:3-6; 7:1-6; 20:16-18, Joshua, and Pss 137:7-9 and
139:21-22 — such hatred "would be felt by many to be a patriotic duty which appropriately
complemented the communal loyalty expressed by Lev 19:18." He balances this with Exod 23:4-5; Prov
24:17; 25:21; Lev 19:34; Deut 10:19.

France states that "We cannot now know whether the extended version of Lev 19:18 quoted by Jesus
came from a recognizable source," and that "The nearest approximation to it in surviving literature
is probably the Qumran rule 'to love all the sons of light ... and to hate all the sons of
darkness'" — though "sons of light" is far narrower than Lev 19:18's neighbour. He rejects
J. Jeremias's rendering "You shall love your compatriot (but) you need not love your adversary,"
saying he "is stretching this linguistic elastic to its limit," even granting that biblical "hate"
can mean "love less."

He flags the apparent tension that Lev 19:18, criticized here, later appears "with no hint of
criticism" as half of Jesus' summary of the law (22:37-40) and on a par with the Decalogue (19:19);
his resolution is that here "he is quoting it in its popularly understood form."

France calls "Love your enemies" "virtually an oxymoron; an enemy is by definition not loved," and
the climax of the kingdom's paradoxical values, attracting "the incredulity, and often the scorn, of
many interpreters." He argues the command is grounded not in pragmatic wisdom "but as a reflection
of the character of God himself (v. 45)" — "Matthew's Jesus plays his strongest ethical card." He
notes that Jesus "does not specify whether he is talking about personal hostility or about political
enemies," and adds that "even to raise the question is probably to engage in the sort of casuistry
Jesus' simple demand was intended to sweep aside."

He defines *agapaō* as "not only a matter of emotion but also of an attitude which determines our
behavior, acting for the good of the other," suitably expanded by "pray for those who persecute
you." An important qualification follows from ch. 23's diatribe: "His concept of love is apparently
not at the level of simply being nice to people and of allowing error to go unchallenged. Love is
not incompatible with controversy and rebuke."

France denies that loving enemies makes one God's child — "that status is already implied in the
term 'your Father who is in heaven'" — holding rather that such love "will be the proper outworking
of that relationship and demonstrate its legitimacy." From the sun and rain falling on bad and good
he draws a pointed contemporary application: it "should give pause to some contemporary patterns of
prayer for God's discriminatory benevolence to his own people whether in matters of weather and
natural resources or with regard to health, prosperity." He reads "tax collectors" and
"[[gentiles|Gentiles]]" as traditional terms for those at the bottom of the moral scale, used to
underline "how basic a human instinct this is: everyone looks after their own."

France notes historically that "This teaching of Jesus on the love of enemies formed one of the most
distinctive traits of the early Christian movement, and has been widely influential ever since."

## Origin and reception

The label "antitheses" is, on France's account, a scholarly description rather than a Matthean
title. His own account of the section's origin is that it belongs to Matthew's expansion of the
Lukan sermon outline with material on fulfilling the law (5:17-48). He treats it as the section
which raises "the issue of Jesus' messianic authority in relation to the existing authority of the
Torah and of its authorized interpreters" more extensively than anywhere else in the gospel
tradition.

France records that his fulfilment-based reading "has gained a considerable degree of assent in
recent decades, over against the older view of a legally conservative Matthew."

## What France leaves unresolved

- Whether the six divide into two sets of three — he resists the division without excluding it.
- Whether the false-swearing prohibition echoes the ninth or third commandment.
- Whether "from evil" at v. 37 is abstract or personal — he declines to prefer either.
- Whether the enemies in view are personal or political — he holds the question itself to be
  casuistry.
- The source of the "hate your enemy" clause — "We cannot now know."
- How the radical demands translate into practice — deliberately left "for each disciple to work
  out for themselves."

Elsewhere in his commentary France leaves a related tension open: at 15:11 he states that it is
"not easy to see either in Jesus' own time or in that of the gospel's first readers how this might
have been squared with his assurance that he had not come to abolish the law (5:17)," treating it as
a live tension running through NT Christianity rather than something he resolves. See
[[purity|purity]].
