# The Two Cities (*Civitas Dei* / *Civitas Terrena*)

The organizing concept of [[augustine|Augustine]]'s [[city-of-god|*City of God*]]: human history is
the intertwined pilgrimage of two communities — the **city of God** (*civitas Dei*) and the **earthly
city** (*civitas terrena*) — distinguished not by geography or institution but by two contrary loves.

## Definition

Augustine takes *civitas Dei* (Greek *polis theou*) from Scripture, where it names Jerusalem / the
church under both the Old and New Covenants (Ps. 48, 87; Heb. 11–12; Rev. 21–22). He extends it to the
**whole community of God's people** — angelic and human, militant and triumphant — and contrasts it
with the perishing kingdoms of this world. The two cities are defined by love: the heavenly city by
love of God to the contempt of self, the earthly city by love of self to the contempt of God.

## Structure in *City of God*

- The city of God "lives by faith" as a **stranger and pilgrim** in this fleeting course of time,
  awaiting the "fixed stability of its eternal seat."
- The earthly city, "though it be mistress of the nations, is itself ruled by its lust of rule"
  (*libido dominandi*).
- Augustine traces the antagonism of the two communities back to its first divergence in the **fall of
  the angels** and forward to the **last judgment** and the eternal destiny of good and evil — making
  the concept a framework for a total philosophy of history.

The two cities are mingled in this age and will be separated only at the end; the earthly Roman empire
is an ambiguous instrument, not simply identical with the *civitas terrena*.

## Origin: the Two Angelic Societies (Book XI)

The two cities do not begin with humanity but with the **angels**. Reading the first "day" of Genesis,
Augustine takes the created "light" to be the angels, and "God divided the light from the darkness" to
figure the original separation of the holy angels (who cleave to God) from the apostate angels (who
"swelled with pride"). The distinction is **not of nature but of will** — both societies were "by
nature good," but one "by will upright," the other "by will depraved." The defining contrast is already
the two loves: "the one blazing with the holy love of God, the other reeking with the unclean lust of
self-advancement." The human cities continue these angelic communities, each man being finally
associated with the angels of his party.

## The Two Loves (Book XIV)

The cities are constituted not by birth, nation, or institution but by the **object and order of
love**. The book of the fall culminates in the formula that gives the work its key:

> "Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of
> God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self. The former, in a word, glories in
> itself, the latter in the Lord."

This love-self / love-God axis (*amor sui* / *amor Dei*) is the single criterion. The earthly city
seeks glory from men and is ruled by the *libido dominandi* (love of ruling); in the heavenly city
"the princes and the subjects serve one another in love." Living "after the flesh" means living
**according to oneself**, not according to the body — so the cities are spiritual orientations, not
social classes (see [[original-sin]] for the fall that founds them).

## Historical Course (Books XV–XVIII)

From the fall onward the two cities run an **intertwined history**, which Augustine narrates as a
parallel:

- **Cain and Abel** ([[cain|Cain]] / [[abel|Abel]]) are the first citizens. Cain, the firstborn who
  "built a city," founds the earthly city; Abel, "a sojourner" who built none, is the pilgrim citizen
  of the city of God and a type of the slain Christ — "the natural before the spiritual."
- **Two genealogical lines** carry the cities to the flood: the line of **Cain** (the earthly city) and
  the line of **[[seth|Seth]]** (the heavenly), the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 being Seth's line, not
  angels.
- The earthly city's paradigm becomes **[[babylon|Babylon]]** ("Confusion"), founded by
  [[nimrod|Nimrod]] at Babel; the heavenly city's line narrows through [[noah|Noah]], Heber, and the
  patriarchs to **[[abraham|Abraham]]**, in whom the twofold promise (the land to carnal Israel; "all
  nations blessed" to the seed of faith) is given.
- In the empires, Augustine reads **two Babylons**: "Babylon, like a first Rome … Rome herself is like
  a second Babylon" — Assyria/Babylon in the east and [[rome|Rome]] in the west are the successive
  heads of the earthly city, running their course alongside the city of God down to the present.

The two cities remain mingled — "from the beginning down to the end" — sharing temporal goods and ills
"but with diverse faith, diverse hope, and diverse love, until they are separated by the last judgment."

## The Ends of the Two Cities (Books XIX–XXII)

The cities part forever at the end. In this age the pilgrim heavenly city *uses* the earthly
[[peace|peace]] of "Babylon," obeying its laws and adopting its diverse customs "so long only as no
hindrance to the worship of the one… God is thus introduced"; but each city moves toward its own end —
the [[supreme-good|supreme good]] or the supreme evil. At the **[[last-judgment|last judgment]]** the two
are separated: the earthly city reaches its end in **[[eternal-punishment|eternal punishment]]** (the
"second death"), the heavenly city in the **[[resurrection|resurrection of the body]]**, the
**[[beatific-vision|beatific vision]]**, and the eternal Sabbath. The "thousand years" of Revelation 20
is read as the present commingled age of the Church ([[millennialism]]), at whose close Satan is loosed
and "the whole city of the devil" assails "the whole city of Christ" before the final judgment divides
them.
