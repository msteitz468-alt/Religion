# Mahavrata (The Five Great Vows)

## Definition

*Mahāvrata*, "Great Vow" (plural *mahāvratāni*), designates the five — later six — solemn, permanent commitments at the heart of Jain ascetic life. The word *vrata* in its earliest Sanskrit use conveyed not merely a "temporary vow" but a "calling" — a solemn, lifelong dedication of oneself to a single purpose. Taken at the ceremony of ascetic initiation ([[jain-asceticism]]), the five Great Vows and their integration into a totally realigned way of life constitute "the central defining characteristic of the monk and nun, governing their external, observable behaviour and providing a system of internal, spiritual control" (Dundas, *The Jains*, ch. 6).

The Great Vows are accepted by [[svetambara|Śvetāmbaras]] and [[digambara|Digambaras]] alike as lying at the heart of Mahāvīra's ethical teachings. Lay Jains take analogous, lesser vows called [[anuvrata|aṇuvratas]] (minor vows), but do not undertake the mahāvratas.

## The Five Great Vows

The classic account is in the second book of the *Ācārāṅga* (AS 2.15), linked directly to Mahāvīra's description of life-forms. Each vow is followed by five "realisations" (*bhāvanā*) outlining more detailed implications whose observance ensures the vow is fully and correctly executed (TS 7.3).

### 1. Non-violence (*ahiṃsā*)

The ascetic rejects the act of killing any life-form whatsoever and undertakes that for the rest of his life he will confess, repent of and avoid violence, "whether performing it himself, compelling another to perform it or approving another carrying it out, mentally, vocally and physically." The five realisations specify: care in walking (institutionalised in the four-month monsoon rain retreat); control of mind; control of speech; care in setting down the alms bowl; and inspection of all food and drink for life-forms (Dundas, ch. 6, citing AS 2.15 and TS 7.3).

### 2. Truthfulness (*satya*)

The ascetic abstains from lying. The realisation that underlies this vow enjoins deliberateness in speech and avoidance of anger, greed, fear or mirth. Significantly, the vow's real significance "draws not only from a positive commitment to truth but from an assessment of whether an enunciated statement will lead to violence." A classic narrative theme has the monk refusing to reveal an animal's location to a hunter and enduring torture rather than speak a truth that would harm another (YS 2.61).

### 3. Non-stealing (*asteya*)

The ascetic should not take what has not been given, interpreted as rejection of theft (TS 7.10) but also — through the realisations — as forbidding the excessively prolonged occupation of any space of ground or the consumption of alms without the teacher's permission. On a profounder level, Śīlāṅka notes that this vow also signifies not taking the lives of other creatures (Śīlāṅka on AS 1.3).

### 4. Celibacy (*brahmacarya*)

The ascetic renounces sexual activity. The realisations advise against any contact — mental or physical — with women and against eating or drinking anything likely to stimulate the sexual drive. The prohibition extends to the claim that "innumerable life-forms are destroyed in each ejaculation of semen" (YS 2.79), connecting celibacy directly to the first vow of non-violence.

### 5. Non-possession (*aparigraha*)

The ascetic renounces any attachment, interpreted through the realisations as relating to the objects of the senses and later tradition understood as referring to possessions in general. Attachment to possessions and sense objects "engenders the passions, the prime cause of violence" (YS 2.110).

## The Sixth Vow: No Eating After Dark

A further sixth vow forbidding eating after dark came to be added to the basic five. Its first occurrence (SKS 1.2.3.3) describes it as supplementing the Great Vows; it may in origin have been little more than a subdivision of the first Great Vow. The ostensible reason is that alms-seeking at night would inevitably involve trampling on small life-forms (Aparājita on BhA 1179) and that cooking at night attracts insects to flames (NBh 3399–400). Hemacandra claims consuming insects that have fallen into food causes loss of intelligence and that nocturnal ghosts pollute food. The injunction has binding force upon all ascetics and technically applies to lay people as well, though few follow it except on holy days.

## Derivation from the First Vow

Though the five vows relate on the surface to different activities, they are understood in Jainism as developing from the first — the renunciation of violence. "Without the abandonment of violent activity, all religious behaviour, no matter how correct, is worthless" (YS 2.31). This derivational structure is foundational to Jain ethics: truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy and non-possession all draw their deepest significance from their relationship to non-violence.

## The Nature of Non-violence (*Ahiṃsā*) in the Vows

The first Great Vow technically enjoins the ascetic to abstain from destruction of living creatures. Jainism came in time to see action as truly violent only when accompanied by lack of care (*pramāda*): "Carelessness is stated in an early scriptural text to be action itself" (SKS 1.8.3). Umasvāti provided the standard definition: violence (*hiṃsā*) is "the removal of life through a careless action of mind, body or speech" (TS 7.8). Jinabhadra (sixth/seventh century) stated the decisive principle: "It is the intention that ultimately matters… Even if a person does not actually kill, he becomes a killer if he has the intention to kill; while a doctor has to cause pain but is still non-injuring and innocent because his intention is pure" (Dundas, ch. 6, quoting Jinabhadra).

On this basis Jain writers allowed — in rare medieval cases — that monks could justify certain defensive acts of violence, and Jinadatta Sūri (twelfth century) argued that a person engaged in religious activity who is forced to fight and kill would not lose spiritual merit (UR 26).

## Brahmanical and Buddhist Parallels

The ancient Jain ascetic community was shaped within a common north Indian renunciatory world and the Great Vows are in many respects similar to those of early Buddhist and brahman renouncers, with the brahmanical renouncers probably providing an original source of borrowing for the first four vows (Dundas, ch. 6). Irrespective of this history of origin, the Great Vows were very quickly integrated into Jain ascetic practice.

## Initiation and the Great Vows

At the Digambara initiation (*dīkṣā*) ceremony, "male Digambara initiates, as well as taking the five Great Vows in the presence of their teacher, are painted with symbols by a caste priest within a temple in the same manner as an image being consecrated." At the paradigmatic performance of [[sallekhana]], the dying ascetic takes the Great Vows again as a form of re-initiation into the final rite of passage.
