# Adhyāsa (Superimposition)

**Adhyāsa** — "superimposition" — is the foundational concept of [[shankara|Śaṅkara's]]
[[advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedanta]], laid out in the freestanding preamble (the **Adhyāsa-bhāṣya**) to
his commentary on the [[brahma-sutras|Brahma Sutras]]. It is Śaṅkara's diagnosis of **what is wrong with
ordinary experience** and therefore of what knowledge must cure.

## The Definition (Verbatim)

Śaṅkara's definition (Thibaut's translation, *Bhāṣya* on I.1.1):

> "The apparent presentation, **in the form of remembrance**, to consciousness of something previously
> observed, **in some other thing**."

The "in the form of remembrance" clause is technically important: it distinguishes *adhyāsa* from
*recognition* (seeing a cow of the same species = seeing the universal again in a new instance) and from
*renewed perception* (seeing Devadatta again in a new town). In superimposition, the absent thing is
*remembered* and imputed as present — hence the silver is not actually before one when one sees
mother-of-pearl; it is erroneously *remembered* onto it.

Three competing philosophical definitions are surveyed and harmonized:
1. **Anyathākhyātivādins** ("other-attribution"): superimposition = attributing the attributes of one
   existing thing to a different, co-existing thing
2. **Akhyātivādins** ("non-cognition"): superimposition = error arising from failure to apprehend the
   difference between what is superimposed and what it is superimposed upon
3. **Third view**: superimposition = fictitious assumption of attributes contrary to the nature of the
   substrate

Śaṅkara's consensus formula: all three agree that superimposition is the "apparent presentation of the
attributes of one thing in another thing" — differing only in technical explanation of the mechanism.
The canonical examples: "Mother-of-pearl appears like silver"; "The moon although one only appears as if
she were double."

## Śaṅkara's Radical Application: The Mutual Superimposition of Self and Not-Self

Śaṅkara's radical claim is that the most basic, universal case of this error is the
**mutual superimposition of the Self and the not-Self**:

- We superimpose the **not-Self (the "this," the object — body, senses, mind)** onto the **Self (the
  "I," pure witness-consciousness)** — saying "I am fat," "I am blind," "I act," "I suffer."
- And conversely we superimpose the Self's reality and consciousness onto the inert not-Self.

Specific examples from the Bhāṣya:
- *Extra-personal attributes* superimposed on Self: "I am well because my family is well/ill"
- *Bodily attributes* on Self: "I am stout, lean, fair, standing, walking, jumping"
- *Sense-organ attributes* on Self: "I am mute, deaf, one-eyed, blind"
- *Internal-organ attributes* on Self: "I am subject to desire, intention, doubt, determination"
- *Self* superimposed on internal organ: the *ahaṃkāra* (the producer of the "I"-notion) is mistaken
  for the Self

This mutual confusion *is* **avidyā** (ignorance), and on it "rests the entire empirical world"
(*vyavahāra*) — all ordinary knowing, all action, all the [[karma|karma]]-driven round of
[[samsara|saṃsāra]].

The objection and Śaṅkara's reply: the Self, being self-luminous and never an object of the senses,
cannot be the locus of superimposition. Śaṅkara replies that the Self is not an absolute non-object —
it *is* the object of the "I"-notion (*asmat-pratyaya*), known through immediate intuitive
self-presentation (*aparokṣatva*). Nor must the locus of superimposition be in sensory contact:
"non-discerning men superimpose dark-blue colour on the ether, which is not the object of sensuous
perception."

The presupposition structure: This superimposition is not just an occasional error but the **structural
precondition** of all knowing activity. "The means of right knowledge cannot operate unless there be a
knowing personality" — and the "knowing personality" (the individual knower with body and senses)
exists only because the Self is superimposed on the body, and the body's properties are superimposed
on the Self. Even Vedic injunctions and prohibitions — including the entire system of meritorious and
non-meritorious action — operate *within* and *by means of* this superimposition.

## Why It Matters

Adhyāsa makes the human problem **epistemic, not moral**: bondage is a *mistake*, a case of mistaken
identity, not a sin or a debt. Therefore liberation ([[moksha|mokṣa]]) is **knowledge** (*vidyā*) that
removes the error — not action, ritual, or devotion, which operate *within* the superimposed world and
cannot undo it. This is the root of Advaita's signature thesis that **knowledge alone liberates** (see
[[jnana-karma-samuccaya|jñāna-karma-samuccaya]]), and the conceptual basis for reading
[[atman|ātman]] = [[brahman|Brahman]] once the superimposed not-Self is withdrawn.

## Contested

Adhyāsa is exactly what [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]] attacks at the opening of his *Śrī-Bhāṣya* (the
*Mahāpūrvapakṣa*): he argues the notion is incoherent — ignorance cannot have Brahman (pure
consciousness) as either its locus or its object without contradiction. Critics also note its formal
resemblance to the Buddhist analysis of illusion, feeding the "crypto-Buddhist" charge against Śaṅkara.
