The **ḥerem** (Hebrew: חֵרֶם, "devoted thing," "the ban"; verb: *ḥāram*, "to devote/destroy") is an OT category designating something irrevocably given over to YHWH — either by consecration (withdrawn from ordinary use) or by destruction (turned over to divine judgment). In its most theologically and ethically significant application, it refers to the commanded total destruction of people and property in certain military contexts, particularly the Israelite conquest of Canaan (Joshua) and the wars against Amalek (1 Samuel 15).

## Semantic Range

The root *ḥrm* covers a wide spectrum:

- **Consecrated property**: Objects or persons permanently given to God, not redeemable (Lev 27:28-29). An Israelite's own property could be devoted in this sense.
- **Judged/destroyed populations**: Enemy populations designated for total destruction — no survivors, no spoils taken. This is the military *ḥerem* of Joshua and Samuel.
- **Forbidden association**: Persons or things placed off-limits (e.g., Deut 7:26 — bringing an idol into one's house makes the house *ḥerem*).

The connecting thread is **absolute divine ownership**: what is *ḥerem* belongs entirely to YHWH and cannot be appropriated for human benefit.

[[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] (on Josh 6:17–21; [[woudstra-joshua-nicot]]) holds that **both** connotations are intended at once: absolute, irrevocable consecration that cannot be redeemed (Lev 27:28–29) **and** sentence to utter destruction (Deut 13:16). The *ḥerem* is applied **in degrees** across the conquest narratives (Deut 13:16; 20:10–18; 1 Sam 15:3).

## The Military ḥerem in Joshua

In the conquest narratives, the *ḥerem* is commanded against specific Canaanite cities. The clearest statement is Deuteronomy 20:16-18:

> "In the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes, but you shall devote them to complete destruction — the Hittites and the Amorites, the Canaanites and the Perizzites, the Hivites and the Jebusites — as the LORD your God has commanded."

### Degrees of the ban (Woudstra on Joshua)

Attributed to [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] ([[woudstra-joshua-nicot]]; digests C–D):

| Application | Form of *ḥerem* |
|---|---|
| **Jericho** (6:17–21) | Most rigorous form, “by way of example”: all living things put to the sword; city burned; only silver, gold, bronze, iron go into the Lord’s treasury as *qōḏeš* |
| **Ai** (8:1–2, 26–27) | Population treated like Jericho’s, but cattle and spoil may be taken (// Deut 2:34–35; 3:6–7) — term *ḥerem* not always used, but the idea is present |
| **Southern cities** (10:28–40) | Stereotype of complete devotion “in a punitive sense”; “all that breathed” as the Lord commanded Moses |
| **Hazor** (11:10–15) | Made an example: inhabitants under *ḥerem*; city burned (exception among mound-cities Israel did not burn, v. 13); no rebuild-curse as on Jericho |
| **Other northern cities** | Less severe than Hazor; booty and cattle kept (Deut 6:10 motif: live in cities not built) |
| **Gibeon** (ch. 9) | Anomaly: treaty instead of ban; spared from death yet cursed to temple serfhood as a living “monument” of required separateness |

On Jericho, Woudstra stresses that metals kept for the treasury explain the Achan story (ch. 7): Israel must keep clean of accursed things or put the curse on the camp.

### The Logic of the ḥerem

[[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] identifies the theological rationale drawn from the OT itself (Introduction and commentary on Josh 6–11):

1. **Accumulated guilt**: Genesis 15:16 — "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full" — tells Abraham that the Canaanite dispossession will wait four generations. By Joshua's time, that patience has been exhausted. The *ḥerem* is judicial punishment for centuries of wickedness, not arbitrary genocide. On Josh 11:19–20 he ties hardening of Canaanite hearts to that same Gen 15:16 horizon.

2. **Divine instrument / divine “giving”**: Israel does not act on its own initiative but as YHWH's judicial instrument. Conquest summaries stress that the Lord “gives” cities into Israel’s power; ambush and planning (Ai) go hand in hand with divine generalship (cf. 5:13–15). Southern and northern campaigns link the ban to the Moses → Joshua command chain (10:40; 11:12, 15).

3. **Contamination prevention**: Deuteronomy 7:3-4; 20:18 — the Canaanites are to be destroyed specifically to prevent Israel from adopting their religious practices. The *ḥerem* is the extreme edge of the holiness-separation principle. See [[holiness|Holiness]].

4. **YHWH's territorial claim**: The land is YHWH's inheritance (Lev 25:23). The Canaanites forfeited their right to dwell in it by their practices. The *ḥerem* is the enforcement of that forfeiture.

5. **Eschatological foreshadow (Woudstra)**: Temporal destruction under the ban is “a prelude and a foreshadowing of a more final judgment that God will mete out to those whose unrighteousness will be full (cf. Gen. 15:16) in the end of days” (on Josh 6:17–21). The defeated-kings list (ch. 12) is read as prelude to a purified world given as inheritance (Ps 37:9; Matt 5:5).

### Hardening and responsibility (Josh 11:19–20)

Woudstra on 11:19–20: only Gibeon made peace; the rest went out with “stubborn heart” because of the Lord’s hardening (Piel of *ḥāzaq*), so that Israel could strike them with the curse without mercy as Moses commanded. Ultimate purpose: Canaanite destruction amid Gen 15:16 iniquity. **This does not exonerate the Canaanites** — “the other way was open, as is shown by what the Gibeonites did.” He refuses to eliminate or modify this presentation of God; “a merely human way of measuring God’s actions in history proves inadequate.” Related monism note: evil ultimately functions under God’s dominion, yet God is not the author of sin (nn. on 11:20).

### Achan: *maʿal* against the ḥerem

The Achan episode (Josh 7) demonstrates the *ḥerem*'s seriousness from the opposite direction. Woudstra’s framing (Josh 7:1 commentary):

- Offense term **māʿal** (“trespass”): lit. “to act under cover,” hence treacherously — a **breach of trust** against the Lord by purloining what was sanctified (// Lev 5:15).
- **Corporate solidarity and individual responsibility** go hand in hand: one man offends, the entire people are viewed as trespassing; Achan robbed the nation of purity/holiness before God; “folly in Israel” (7:15) is corporate language.
- By partaking of the accursed they themselves became accursed (7:12); the Lord will not be with them until the accursed thing is destroyed from among them.
- Sin is named as **breach of covenant** (7:11, 15); root process is coveting (Exod 20:17) of the Shinar robe, silver, and gold.
- Execution (valley of Achor): not vindictiveness but **covenant retribution**; family fate may reflect common knowledge (goods in the tent); conclusions about eternal destiny “had better not be made rashly.”

Woudstra also notes a **structural book-level frame**: the same *maʿal* vocabulary appears as actual corporate trespass after the first city (ch. 7) and as **potential** danger after the conquest cycle in the eastern-altar controversy (22:16, 20) — teaching that the promised land can be truly possessed only if the people adhere to God’s commandments. See [[covenant|Covenant]] (Woudstra / Shechem and *maʿal*).

This episode establishes the *ḥerem* as a two-edged category: Israel can violate it from within just as the Canaanites violated it from without.

### Gibeon: spared from ḥerem, marked as accursed

Woudstra on Josh 9: Israel failed to seek the Lord’s counsel and allowed a Canaanite enclave contrary to Deut 7; yet the oath by the Lord must stand. Those spared from the *ḥerem* live as **accursed ones** among Israel (woodcutters and water carriers; // Gen 9:25 servitude of Canaan) — a living monument of required separateness from Canaan, serving only at legitimate Israelite cult centers.

## Theological Evaluation Within the OT

The OT itself does not present the *ḥerem* as a universal or perpetual practice. Key features of its historical limitation:

- It is **geographically bounded**: primarily Canaan (Deut 20:16-18), as distinct from the rules for cities "very far from you" (Deut 20:10-15, where survivors are taken).
- It is **historically bounded**: associated with the conquest period; later OT warfare does not routinely invoke it.
- **Saul's failure** to apply the *ḥerem* to Amalek (1 Sam 15) becomes the occasion of his rejection — suggesting the practice was still operative in certain contexts in the early monarchy.
- **No post-exilic renewal**: The prophets and the Second Temple period do not re-invoke the *ḥerem* as a military category.
- Within Joshua, **wipe-out language coexists with admitted survivors** and unfinished subjugation (10:20; 13:1; 15:63; etc.) — Woudstra takes this realism as a mark of historiography, not mere epic.

## Normativity Question

[[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] makes a key methodological distinction (Introduction VI.1; [[woudstra-joshua-nicot]]):

> "While certain practices, such as the wholesale extermination of enemy populations by the ḥerem, have ceased to be normative, the revelation contained in the written record regarding such practices does not cease to be normative."

This separates two questions:
1. **Should Christians practice the *ḥerem* today?** No — it was a historically particular divine command, not a timeless moral principle.
2. **Is the biblical account of the *ḥerem* still authoritative Scripture?** Yes — it reveals something true about divine judgment, the seriousness of sin, the divine ownership of the land, and the holiness of God.

Against labels such as Bright’s “pre-Christian” / “sub-Christian” for Joshua’s conquest material, Woudstra argues that because *ḥerem* originates from the Lord’s order, one cannot correctly call it an expression of a “sub-Christian” sentiment without involving God in a gradual evolution from cruel to benign; the *ḥerem* teaches that God the Creator may also destroy his creatures (Josh 6 commentary; Introduction VI).

This is the most careful position available within a high-biblical-authority framework. It is not universally accepted. See [[herem-canaanite-conquest|Controversy: The ḥerem and Canaanite Conquest]].

## NT Connections

The military *ḥerem* has no direct NT equivalent — the command to love enemies and the eschatological transfer of judgment to God's final act make it theologically discontinued.

However, several NT concepts draw on *ḥerem* language and logic:
- **Anathema** (Greek: *anathema*) is the NT word corresponding to *ḥerem*, used for things devoted to destruction (Rom 9:3; 1 Cor 12:3; 16:22; Gal 1:8-9). Paul's "let him be *anathema*" echoes the *ḥerem* of someone who violates the covenant.
- **1 Cor 5:1-11** (the expulsion of the incestuous man from the Corinthian community): Woudstra notes the parallel with Achan (ch. 7), while cautioning against collapsing the difference between old and new dispensation.
- **Revelation's destruction of Babylon**: The eschatological judgment of Rome/Babylon has *ḥerem* resonances — YHWH’s final judicial act against accumulated wickedness (Woudstra’s “prelude” language on Josh 6).

## The Controversy

The *ḥerem* is the OT's sharpest ethical challenge: is divinely commanded genocide morally defensible? This question cannot be contained within the concept page. See [[herem-canaanite-conquest|Controversy: The ḥerem and Canaanite Conquest]] for the full range of positions.

## Arnold on Deuteronomy 7 (chs. 1–11 volume)

[[bill-arnold|Arnold]] ([[arnold-deuteronomy-1-11-nicot]], on Deut 7) frames *ḥerem* within **election by YHWH’s love, not Israel’s merit** (7:7–11) and the demand for exclusive loyalty among the seven Canaanite nations (7:1–6). He treats warfare and cult destruction with ethical seriousness, engaging process/gradual-dispossession and metaphorical readings of *ḥerem* language where the commentary argues them (digest F), while still locating the ban as covenant-protection against idolatrous assimilation. Complements Craigie (covenant-protection framing on Deut 7) and Woudstra (degrees of ban in Joshua) without collapsing the three.
