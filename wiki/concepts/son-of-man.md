# Son of Man

The **Son of Man** is, in its developed apocalyptic form, a heavenly figure — a cosmic
judge enthroned beside God at the end of the age. [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]
([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) treats it both as a Jewish
divine-mediator figure (part of the [[two-powers-in-heaven|gradation of divinity in
Judaism]]) and as central to the self-understanding of the historical
[[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]].

## The Jewish Figure

- **Daniel 7** — amid four beasts rising from the sea (world empires), "one like a son of
  man" comes "on the clouds of heaven" from God's realm and is given everlasting dominion.
  Debated referent: the restored kingdom of Israel, or an angelic being at Israel's head.
- **1 Enoch, the Similitudes (chs. 37–71)** — the figure becomes a fully **divine being**:
  named "before the creation of the sun and the moon," concealed in God's presence,
  worshiped by all the earth, seated on God's throne of glory to judge, never to "pass
  away or perish"; called "the Elect One" and "the messiah" — "a divine being who has
  always existed … as exalted as one can possibly be without actually being the Lord God
  Almighty himself." A later addition (1 En. 70–71) identifies him with **[[enoch|Enoch]]**:
  "a man, a mere mortal … exalted to this supreme position next to God."

## Jesus and the Son of Man (Ehrman's reading)

Ehrman argues that in the **earliest Synoptic sayings** Jesus speaks of the Son of Man as a
figure *other than himself* — a coming cosmic judge: "Whoever is ashamed of me … of that one
will the Son of Man be ashamed when he comes" (Mark 8:38). "A reader who thinks Jesus is
talking about himself … has brought that understanding to the text, not taken it from the
text." Had early Christians invented the sayings, they would have written "I, the Son of
Man." The Sheep-and-Goats judgment (Matt 25:31–46) and the "twelve thrones" saying (Q,
including Judas) pass the criterion of dissimilarity and likely go back to Jesus. Ehrman's
conclusion: Jesus expected the Son of Man imminently and saw himself as the future
**[[messianic-secret|messiah]]**/king of the coming kingdom — but did *not* identify himself
as the Son of Man, and so "did not consider himself to be the heavenly angelic being who
would be the judge of the earth."

After the [[resurrection|resurrection]], "Son of Man" became one of the exalted titles
applied *to* Jesus (see [[exaltation-christology|exaltation Christology]]). By
[[tertullian|Tertullian]]'s time the phrase had been re-read as denoting Christ's
**humanity** ("son of man and Son of God") rather than the apocalyptic judge.

## Whether Jesus identified himself as the Son of Man

This is itself a live scholarly dispute: many scholars assume the identification; Ehrman
argues the wording of the key sayings is against it. (Recorded as a position, not adjudicated.)

## France's Daniel 7 Reading (NICNT Matthew, 2007)

*Added 2026-07-18. All interpretive claims here are [[rt-france|France]]'s own. Recorded as a
position; not adjudicated against the Ehrman reading above.*

France holds that the title's **primary OT source is Dan 7:13-14**, where "the 'one like a son
of man' (who represents Israel) is a victorious figure" enthroned by God in heaven to rule all
nations. He lists seven Matthean passages drawing on Dan 7:13: 10:23; 16:27-28; 19:28; 24:30;
25:31; 26:64; 28:18.

### The load-bearing move: the coming is *to God*, not to earth

France's distinctive claim is directional. In Dan 7:13-14 the son of man comes **to God** to be
enthroned: "There is nothing in the imagery of Daniel to suggest a coming to earth, as Christian
interpretation has traditionally found in these passages." From this he draws an exegetical
default:

> "despite centuries of later Christian interpretive tradition, when the gospels speak of 'the
> Son of Man coming' the presumption must be that they are speaking not of an eschatological
> parousia but of a heavenly enthronement."

This is the same commitment France carries in his NIGTC *Mark* on Mark 13:24-27 (see
[[rt-france|R. T. France]]), and it is his most contested exegetical position in both volumes.

### The 10:23 crux

Applying this to "you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man
comes," France argues first that "the narrative setting shows that 'Israel' here means in
effect [[galilee|Galilee]]" — the saying concerns completing the Twelve's Galilean mission. He
then reads the coming of the Son of Man as marking **the end of a mission specifically to
Israel**, "when the universal kingship of the Son of Man is established after his resurrection"
(cf. 28:18, followed at once by "all nations"), whereupon the mission widens beyond the bounds
of 10:5-6.

He names and rejects the two consequences of reading 10:23 as parousia language: "either that
Jesus mistakenly expected an immediate parousia or that his words here had no bearing on the
situation of the Twelve" — and would have had no meaning for a first-time reader who has not
yet heard of a parousia.

**France holds this tentatively and says so.** "Perhaps this is to press the evocative imagery
of this verse too far, to seek for too specific a point of reference." He claims only that "some
such scenario makes better sense of the Danielic imagery."

### Extrapolation, not exposition

On the forgiveness saying (9:6), France notes that "on earth" distinguishes present from future
heavenly sovereignty, and that forgiveness was no part of Daniel's vision: "Jesus is not
expounding Dan 7, but boldly extrapolating from that vision to make a claim for his present
status." Elsewhere he reads the Son of Man's sending of angels against Dan 7:13-14 (with its
judgment context, Dan 7:10, 22) so that he stands "in the place of the divine judge himself"
(13:41; 19:28; 25:31-46).

### The title as an open question

France argues the designation lacks ready-made conceptual content, which is precisely why it can
function as the basis of an inquiry at 16:13: "clearly the title in itself leaves the question
unanswered … as a title it invites the question 'Who?'" He notes Matthew has "the Son of Man" at
16:13 where Mark has "I," and the reverse at v. 21, indicating interchangeability in the
tradition, and suggests Matthew's choice here reflects awareness of the designation's open-ended,
puzzling character.

This enigmatic quality also supplies France's answer to why blasphemy against the Son of Man is
forgivable while blasphemy against the Spirit is not: because the truth about Jesus is not yet
openly revealed, "it would be possible to 'speak against' him … without being aware that one was
opposing the saving purpose of God" (cf. Acts 3:17; the unwitting/deliberate distinction in
Num 15:27-31). His test case: "Even [[peter-apostle|Peter]], in the heat of the moment, would
'speak against the Son of Man' (26:69-75) and still be forgiven."

## France in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the fidelity reingest of [[france-mark-nigtc|France's NIGTC Mark]]. All
interpretive claims are [[rt-france|France]]'s; recorded as a position alongside the Ehrman
reading above, not adjudicated.*

### Four working assumptions

At the first Son of Man saying in [[mark|Mark]] (2:10), France states his working assumptions:
(1) "the Son of Man" is Jesus' own self-designation; (2) it derives at least partly from Dan
7:13-14; (3) it was not a current messianic title in Judaism; (4) Jesus chose it as "a term with
clear messianic overtones but without a ready-made nationalistic content such as was carried by
'Messiah' or 'Son of David'." He rejects the generic ("humanity in general") reading of 2:10 and
2:28: given the pericopes' stress on unique ἐξουσία, "the title must be understood not primarily
as identifying Jesus with the rest of humanity, but precisely as setting him apart" — noting Mark
uses the plural οἱ υἱοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων (3:28) when a generic sense is intended. The ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς of
2:10 underlines the claim's boldness: forgiveness, "hitherto thought to be an exclusively
heavenly function, can now be exercised ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς" because of the Danielic Son of Man's
presence.

### Title of choice after Caesarea Philippi

France observes that after 8:29 ὁ Χριστός disappears until 14:61-62, deliberately replaced by
the "less-known and more enigmatic" ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου (see [[messianic-secret|messianic
secret]]). The δεῖ of the Son of Man's suffering (8:31) rests, for France, on Scripture rather
than political inevitability — and since Dan 7's figure is victorious, the suffering element must
come from elsewhere: he reaffirms his long-held position that Isaiah 53 is "by far the most
probable source" of Jesus' conviction that he must suffer and die (see
[[servant-songs|Servant Songs]]), with verbal echoes surfacing at 10:45 and 14:24. He finds deep
irony in the second prediction's παραδίδοται εἰς χεῖρας ἀνθρώπων (9:31) — the one destined for
sovereignty over humanity (Dn. 7:14) delivered into human hands — and reads James and John's
request (10:35-40) as seizing the royal connotations of the Daniel 7 figure while filtering out
the passion. At 10:45 the Son of Man who should have been *served* gives his life as λύτρον ἀντὶ
πολλῶν — Mark's compact soteriology.

### Enthronement, not parousia — France's signature reading

France's most distinctive claim (consistent with his Matthew commentary, above): the "coming of
the Son of Man" sayings (8:38; 13:26; 14:62) use the *enthronement* language of Dan 7:13-14 —
a coming *to God* to receive authority — for the imminent vindication of the rejected Son of
Man, not a parousia descent ending history. On 13:24-27 he writes that what is described is "a
change of government: the temple and all that it stood for is out, and the Son of Man is in";
this, he argues, dissolves the embarrassment of 13:30 ("this generation") — "They did!" The
"seeing" of 13:26 is the visible earthly consequences within the generation: the temple's
destruction and the powerful growth of the international church, with 13:27's ingathering of the
elect "from the four winds" an ironic reversal of return-from-exile texts — inclusion of all
nations. He does retain a parousia in Mark, locating it in the "surprisingly brief, allusive and
low key" appendix 13:32-37, where the subject changes at περὶ δέ.

At 14:62 Jesus discards the offered ὁ Χριστός for his chosen title, weaving Ps 110:1 and Dan
7:13 into "an enthronement declaration whose total effect is greater than the sum of the parts";
the alleged sitting/coming "mixed metaphor" dissolves once both texts are read as OT metaphors
of sovereign authority. France claims the non-parousia reading of 14:62 is "now firmly (though
by no means universally) established," citing his *Jesus and the Old Testament*: within the
synoptic tradition Dan 7:13-14 is applied to periods from post-resurrection authority (Mt 28:18)
to last judgment, "but nowhere was the 'coming' understood as a coming to earth at the
parousia" — Rev 1:7 being probably the earliest such use. He notes with D. L. Bock that the
blasphemy verdict (14:64) rests above all on the self-claim to sit at God's right hand, and that
Qumran texts (4Q174; 4Q246) show the coupling of "Messiah" and "Son of God" was not foreign to
first-century Judaism, "though not in the metaphysical sense of later Christian theology."
