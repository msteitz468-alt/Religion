# The Necessary Existent (*wājib al-wujūd*)

The **Necessary Existent** is the being whose existence has no cause — necessary through itself,
as against the **possible existent** (*mumkin al-wujūd*), whose existence is contingent and
requires a cause. The distinction is the cornerstone of [[avicenna|Avicennan]] metaphysics and the
hinge of Discussions 4–10 of the *[[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]* /
*[[incoherence-of-the-incoherence|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]]* exchange, where it carries the proofs of
God's **unity**, **simplicity**, and **incorporeality**.

This page records the concept as analyzed in [[averroes|Averroes]]'s reply; the parallel
[[divine-simplicity]] page treats the cognate doctrine in [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]].

---

## The possible / necessary division and its origin

Averroes traces the division of being into "possible" and "necessary" not to the ancient
philosophers but to the **[[mutazilism|Muʿtazila]]**, "before the Ashʿarites," from whom
**Avicenna** took it: "This method of proving the unity of God is peculiar to Avicenna, and is not
found in any of the ancient philosophers." Avicenna redefined the terms so that **possible = "that
which has a cause"** and **necessary = "that which has no cause."** Averroes regards this redefinition
as a source of confusion, and holds that the division "is not primarily a logical division" but is
"only known through the nature of existing things" — making Avicenna's proofs epistemically circular.

**Ghazālī's deflationary objection** exploits the redefinition: "the genuine meaning of this word is
the negation of a cause for its existence, and this is an absolute negation" — a pure negation, he
argues, constitutes no real essence, so the whole Avicennan apparatus is a verbal trick. Averroes
replies that "necessary existent" has a **positive** sense — "a nature which has absolutely no cause,
no exterior agent, and no agent which is part of it" — and that whether one says "necessary existent"
or "causeless existent," the inference that a causeless being cannot be composite still follows.

## The proof of divine unity (Disc. 5)

The Avicennan proof: two necessary existents would have to share "necessary existence" either
through each one's own essence (making the species exclusive to one) or through a common cause
(making both effects) — both impossible; hence the Necessary Existent is unique. Averroes accepts
the conclusion but **reformulates** the proof as a clean tripartite disjunction: two necessary
existents could differ only (a) **numerically** (→ a shared species → composition), (b)
**specifically** (→ a shared genus → composition), or (c) **in rank** (→ one is the cause of the
other → only one ultimate First). Only (c) is coherent, and it yields uniqueness. He also argues
from extremity: "that which is of the extreme perfection of existence must be unique," as a line
cannot have two extreme points on the same side.

---

## Simplicity and the essence–existence question (Disc. 6, 8)

Because the Necessary Existent has no cause, it can have no composition of any kind (quantity,
matter and form, genus and species, subject and attribute, or **quiddity and existence**) — any
compound "is only one because of a oneness existing in it," which oneness must come from something
one through itself, i.e. an external cause. Averroes grounds this in pure act: "since in everything
composed of attribute and subject there is potency and act, it was a necessary implication… that
the First could not be composed."

The sharpest intra-philosophical dispute is over **essence and existence**:

- **Avicenna**: in composite things existence is an attribute *additional to the essence*, acquired
  from an agent; the First (having no agent) therefore has existence identical with essence.
- **Averroes**: rejects Avicenna's premise as "a most erroneous theory" (it would make "existence"
  an accident outside the soul common to the ten categories, generating an infinite regress) and
  rejects the auxiliary "necessary-through-another / necessary-through-itself" as "superfluous and
  erroneous." Against Ghazālī, he insists the philosophers "do not deny the quiddity of the First,
  but only deny that it has the kind of quiddity which is in the effects." Following **al-Fārābī**
  (the *Book of the Letters*), he treats "existent" as predicated **analogically** (*per prius et
  posterius*), the genus of substance rather than an accident.

## Incorporeality (Disc. 9–10)

Ghazālī argues the philosophers cannot prove the First is incorporeal unless they also hold all
bodies temporal (which they do not). Averroes' preferred demonstration runs through bodies: the
series of substances that are both active and passive must terminate in a substance of **pure act**;
the celestial body, though necessary in its substance, is possible in its local motion, and so
requires an **absolutely** necessary mover "without any potency in a body" — "such an entity is
absolutely without body." All bodies are finite in power and "only acquire their power of infinite
movement through an incorporeal being." On the charge that an eternal world implies materialism
(Disc. 10), he answers that every body, "temporal or eternal," requires an external eternal cause.

---

## Hermeneutical note

The whole cluster turns on Averroes' insistence that the predicates at stake — "existent,"
"necessary," "intellect," "substance" — are said of God and of creatures **analogically, not
univocally**. Ghazālī's objections in Disc. 7 (that the First and the first separate intellect
"share" intellectuality, hence form a genus, hence are composite) were, Averroes concedes, **valid
against Avicenna**, who "was not aware of this nature which stands midway between the univocal and
the equivocal" — but they do not touch the older Aristotelian–Fārābian position. See
[[divine-attributes-in-islam]].
