# Charity (Caritas) in Aquinas

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] gives **charity** the longest single treatise in the
[[summa-theologica|*Summa*]]'s moral theology ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] II-II Q23–46), because
charity is "the mother and the root of all the virtues" and the form by which every other virtue is
ordered to God. This page covers the Thomist account; for [[augustine|Augustine]]'s *caritas/cupiditas*
distinction and its hermeneutical use see [[charity-and-cupidity]].

## What charity is (Q23)

- **Charity is friendship (*amicitia*) with God** (Q23, A1). Following Aristotle's *Ethics* VIII,
  friendship requires mutual benevolence founded on a shared *communicatio*; God shares his own
  beatitude with us (1 Cor. 1:9), so "charity is the friendship of man for God." This is the
  defining Thomist move — love of God is not merely desire (*amor concupiscentiae*) but the
  reciprocal love of friends.
- **It is a created habit infused in the will** (Q23, A2) — **against [[peter-lombard|Peter
  Lombard]]**, who identified charity with the Holy Spirit moving the will directly. Aquinas insists
  the act must be voluntary, so it requires "some habitual form superadded to the natural power."
- **It is the most excellent virtue** (Q23, A6), greater than faith and hope, because faith and
  hope attain God as the source of truth or good *for us*, while "charity attains God Himself that
  it may rest in Him." No true virtue is possible without it (Q23, A7), and it is the **form, mother,
  and root** of the virtues, directing all their acts to the last end (Q23, A8).

## Its growth and loss (Q24)

Charity is infused **by the Holy Spirit alone**, not according to natural capacity (Q24, A2–3). It
**increases** only by intensification in the subject, not by addition, and can grow **indefinitely**
in this life — "our heart is enlarged" (Q24, A4–7). Aquinas marks **three degrees**: beginners
(avoiding sin), the proficient (growing in good), and the perfect (longing to "be dissolved and to
be with Christ") (Q24, A9). Charity cannot decrease directly, but **one mortal sin destroys it
entirely and at once** — unlike faith and hope, which remain "lifeless" after mortal sin, because
charity *is* the union with God that mortal sin severs (Q24, A11–12).

## The order of charity — *ordo caritatis* (Q25–26)

The objects of charity are fourfold (Augustine): **God, oneself, one's neighbour, one's own body**
(Q25, A12). We love **God above all**, then **ourselves** (the soul) more than our neighbour, then
our **neighbour's soul** more than our own body (Q26). Two distinct axes govern the order:

- **By nearness to God** (the *species* of love) — we wish a **greater good** to the better person;
- **By nearness to the lover** (the *intensity* of love) — we love more intensely those more closely
  united to us.

Hence a man must love his **father more in reverence** (the father is a principle, like God) but his
**children more intensely** (they are part of himself); his **wife more intensely** but his **parents
more reverently** (Q26, A9–11). **Enemies** must be loved as to the general disposition (readiness,
and general signs of love); special acts toward an individual enemy belong to the perfection of
charity or to cases of necessity (Q25, A8–9). The order of charity **endures in heaven** (Q26, A13).

## The act and effects of charity (Q27–30)

- **To love** is more proper to charity than to be loved (Q27, A1); God is loved **for his own
  sake** (Q27, A3), and there is **no measure** in loving him — "the measure of loving God is to
  love him without measure" (Bernard, Q27, A6).
- **Joy** (Q28) and **peace** (Q29) are not distinct virtues but **acts/effects of charity** —
  numbered among the Fruits of the Spirit (Gal. 5:22). Peace is "the tranquillity of order"
  (Augustine), the union both of a man's own appetites and of his will with his neighbour's, and is
  the **direct** effect of charity, where justice produces peace only indirectly.
- **Mercy** (Q30) is "heartfelt sympathy for another's distress, impelling us to succour him." It is
  a virtue when ruled by reason, and **the greatest virtue toward our neighbour**; yet charity, which
  unites us to God, surpasses it.

## Beneficence, almsgiving, fraternal correction (Q31–33)

The exterior acts of charity. **Almsgiving** (Q32) is an act of charity through the medium of mercy,
and is a matter of **precept** when the giver has surplus and the neighbour is in urgent need —
"the temporal goods which God grants us are ours as to the ownership, but as to the use of them they
belong… also to such others as we are able to succour" (Q32, A5). In **extreme** need, all things
are common. **Fraternal correction** (Q33) is an act of charity (aimed at the sinner's amendment),
distinct from punitive correction (which is justice for the common good); secret admonition must
precede public denunciation unless the sin harms others.

## The vices opposed to charity (Q34–43)

- **Hatred** (Q34) — of God is the **greatest of sins** (a direct, voluntary aversion); hatred of
  neighbour arises from envy.
- **Sloth / acedia** (Q35) — "an oppressive sorrow" specifically at the **Divine good** in which
  charity rejoices; a **capital vice** (per Gregory) with six daughters (despair, faint-heartedness,
  sluggishness, spite, malice, wandering of mind).
- **Envy** (Q36) — sorrow at a neighbour's good as diminishing one's own excellence; a capital vice;
  envy of another's **spiritual** good is a sin against the Holy Ghost.
- **Discord, contention, schism, war, strife, sedition, scandal** (Q37–43) — disorders of the peace
  and unity charity creates. **[[schism]]** (Q39) is a special sin distinct from heresy — opposed to
  the *unity* of the Church (refusal to submit to the Sovereign Pontiff) rather than to the faith,
  though it is "the road to heresy." **sedition** is always mortal, **but resisting a tyrant is
  not sedition** — the tyrant is the true seditionist (Q42). On **[[just-war|war]]** (Q40) see the
  dedicated page.

## Precepts, and the gift of wisdom (Q44–46)

The two great commandments (Q44) command charity directly, since "the end of the commandment is
charity" (1 Tim. 1:5); to love God "with the whole heart" means, in this life, **habitually**
consenting to nothing contrary to his love. The **gift of wisdom** (Q45) — right judgement about
Divine things "by a certain **connaturality**" with them, caused by charity in the will though seated
in the intellect — and its opposite, **folly** (Q46), conclude the treatise.
