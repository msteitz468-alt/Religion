# Bauer Thesis

## Definition

The "Bauer thesis" refers to the historical argument advanced by Walter Bauer (1877–1960) in his 1934 work *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (*Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei im ältesten Christentum*). It fundamentally reframes the relationship between heresy and orthodoxy in early Christianity.

**Core claim**: In a number of major geographical centres of early Christianity (Asia Minor, Antioch, Egypt, Edessa), what later came to be called "heresy" was actually the *first* or *dominant* form of Christianity present. "Orthodoxy" — the form that eventually won — was a later arrival and achieved its dominance largely through the political influence of the Roman church, not through historical precedence or theological superiority. Therefore, the distinction between "orthodoxy" and "heresy" in the early centuries is anachronistic and ideological: these are labels applied after the fact by the victorious party.

**Standard paradigm overturned**: The standard paradigm (represented by [[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] and Eusebius) held that the apostles preached a unified, orthodox faith, and that heresies arose later as corruptions of that original purity. Bauer inverted this: heresy first, orthodoxy second.

## Key Claims

1. **Geographic priority of heresy**: In Edessa, Egypt (especially), Antioch, and Asia Minor, the earliest forms of Christianity were heterodox. Orthodoxy arrived later and displaced them.
2. **Rome as power broker**: The Roman church used its wealth, prestige, and organizational capacity to impose its version of Christianity on other regions, standardizing "orthodoxy" across diverse local traditions.
3. **Terminological implications**: Terms like "heresy" and "orthodoxy" should be placed in quotation marks and used only descriptively — they tell us which group won, not which group was right or historically prior.
4. **The Eusebius problem**: Eusebius retroactively portrayed early Christianity as pristinely orthodox with heresy as a late intrusion; Bauer argued this was historical reconstruction in service of ideology.

## Scholarly Impact

Bauer's thesis generated enormous influence in 20th-century scholarship, shaping:
- Helmet Koester and James Robinson's "trajectories" model of early Christianity
- Elaine Pagels' *The Gnostic Gospels* (1979) and *Beyond Belief* (2003)
- Bart Ehrman's *Lost Christianities* (2003) and *How Jesus Became God* (2014)
- Terminology shifts: scholars began using quotation marks around "orthodox" and "heretical"
- Methodological consensus: the existence of diversity was broadly accepted as correct

## Validity Assessment (Kruger/Scholarship)

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] (*Christianity at the Crossroads*, ch. 4-5), building on earlier refutations by Turner (1954), Robinson (1989), and Köstenberger/Kruger (*The Heresy of Orthodoxy*, 2010), offers a structured evaluation:

### What Bauer Got Right

**Diversity**: Early Christianity was genuinely diverse — more so than Eusebius' narrative suggests. Multiple groups claiming apostolic authority coexisted, held radically different Christologies, and disagreed about the status of the OT, the nature of creation, and the means of salvation. Bauer was right to challenge a simplistic "pristine orthodoxy" model.

### What Bauer Got Wrong

**A. Numerical dominance of heresy**: Bauer claimed heretical groups were numerically dominant in most regions. The evidence refutes this.

- **Leadership evidence**: Every major city had identifiable orthodox bishops. Not a single named heretical bishop can be found in 2nd-c. sources. Valentinus "almost" became bishop of Rome — but that claim is historically dubious and remains an exception even if true.
- **Literary evidence**: NT papyri outnumber apocryphal texts approximately 6:1 in the 2nd c. (Hurtado's manuscript catalogue). Patristic citation frequency is dramatically biased toward canonical books.
- **Gnostic self-understanding**: Theodotus and Heracleon (Valentinian writers) explicitly described themselves as "the few" compared to "the many" (psychics = orthodox). Even Elaine Pagels acknowledges "Bauer's suggestion that those later called 'heretics' formed the majority goes beyond even the gnostics' own claims."
- **Celsus' testimony**: The pagan critic Celsus — writing c.177 and the most hostile source toward Christianity — explicitly identified "the Great Church" as the mainstream form of Christianity he was attacking. Celsus described the fracturing of Christianity as a *later* development from an originally unified movement.
- **Ignatius evidence**: Bauer argued Ignatius' push for obedience to bishops was evidence bishops were struggling for power in heretic-dominated settings. But Ignatius also called for submission to the presbytery — which makes no sense if the presbytery was heretic-dominated.

**B. Geographic claims**: Trebilco's analysis of Asia Minor shows Bauer consistently overlooks earlier orthodox evidence (e.g., letters in Revelation to Smyrna, Pergamum, Philadelphia; Papias as orthodox bishop of Hierapolis at the century's beginning). Bock challenges Bauer's Egypt, Edessa, and Rome claims. Hultgren finds Bauer regularly appeals to the argument from silence. "Edessa is the one place where Bauer's claim might hold" but the evidence is too thin either way (Bock).

**C. The impossibility of distinguishing heresy from orthodoxy**: Bauer and his followers (Koester, Ehrman) argue that since all groups claimed apostolic authority, there is no principled way to distinguish between them. But this argument ignores the fact that **first-century sources exist** — specifically Paul's seven undisputed letters — against which second-century claims can actually be assessed. Kruger/Litfin show the [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]] closely coheres with the Pauline kerygma, while gnostic, Marcionite, Valentinian, and Ebionite teachings consistently deviate from it on precisely the points the rule addresses (monotheism, OT authority, Christ's humanity/divinity, physical resurrection).

**D. The argument from silence**: Bauer repeatedly uses the absence of evidence of orthodoxy in a region as positive evidence for heretical dominance. This is methodologically problematic; absence of evidence in limited sources is not evidence of absence.

## The Functional vs. Descriptive Use of "Orthodoxy"

Bauer's followers insist on a merely *descriptive* usage: "orthodoxy" = the form of Christianity that happened to win. But Kruger argues this leads to a reducio ad absurdum: if the label can attach to *any* self-designated Christian group regardless of any other criterion, "Christianity" becomes meaningless.

The alternative is to use the apostolic kerygma — accessible through Paul's undisputed letters — as the historical baseline. This does not require theological partisanship; it simply uses the earliest available attestation of what Christianity claimed to be. On this standard, the rule of faith has a much stronger claim to "originality" than gnostic or Valentinian systems.

## Legacy of the Debate

The Bauer debate has permanently reshaped scholarship:
- **Accepted**: Diversity in early Christianity is real and should not be minimized
- **Rejected or heavily qualified**: Heretical geographical dominance; the impossibility of historical differentiation between groups; Rome as the dominant force imposing orthodoxy in the 2nd c.
- **Ongoing**: The precise extent of diversity; the dating and provenance of specific heterodox texts; the degree to which patristic heresiological accounts accurately represent the groups they attack
