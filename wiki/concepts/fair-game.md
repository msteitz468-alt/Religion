# Fair Game

**Fair Game** is a [[church-of-scientology|Church of Scientology]] policy designating certain individuals — labeled **"Suppressive Persons" (SPs)** — as legitimate targets for harassment, litigation, deception, and destruction. It is one of the most documented controversies surrounding the Church and a central exhibit in debates over whether Scientology is a [[scientology-religion-or-commercial-enterprise|religion or a harmful cult]].

## The Policy

The original Fair Game policy letter, authored by [[l-ron-hubbard|L. Ron Hubbard]] (HCO Policy Letter, December 23, 1965) states that Suppressive Persons:

> "May be deprived of property or injured by any means by any Scientologist without any discipline of the Scientologist. May be tricked, sued or lied to or destroyed without any discipline from the organization."

A follow-up (October 18, 1967) specified the "penalties for lower conditions" including "enemy" status. Hubbard's *HCO Manual of Justice* (1959) had already established the ethos: Dianetics and Scientology are "self-protecting sciences. If one attacks them one attacks all the know-how of the mind."

## Official Cancellation (1968)

On October 21, 1968, Hubbard issued a policy letter titled "Cancellation of Fair Game," which stated that the phrase "Fair Game" would no longer be used. The document has been central to Church defenses in litigation. However, critics, ex-members, and numerous court decisions hold that this cancellation applied only to the *label*, not the *practice* — the declaration explicitly noted it was being cancelled because it was "not meant to cause… a bad PR situation" and did not change underlying ethics policies.

## Documented Uses

The most thoroughly documented application of Fair Game practices:

- **Operation Freakout** (1973–74): A Guardian's Office operation against journalist **Paulette Cooper** (*The Scandal of Scientology*, 1971). Plan included framing Cooper for bomb threats against the Church and against Secretary of State Henry Kissinger. Cooper was federally indicted before the Church's involvement was exposed in the 1977 FBI raids.
- **Guardian's Office covert operations** generally (1966–1977): "private CIA" with up to 5,000 operatives; surveilling critics, planting moles, stealing government documents (**Operation Snow White**).
- **CAN takedown**: 50+ lawsuits against the Cult Awareness Network; CAN driven to bankruptcy; bought by Scientologist Stephen Hayes for $20,000; reborn as "New CAN" under Scientology control.
- **Litigation strategy**: 2,700 simultaneous lawsuits against the IRS; individual critics sued repeatedly (Larry Wollersheim's case ran 1980–2002 before a final $8.6M settlement).
- **Post-GO**: After the Guardian's Office was officially dismantled following the FBI raids (1979), the **Office of Special Affairs (OSA)** took over similar functions, now using private investigators as cut-outs to maintain plausible deniability (per LA Times investigations).

## Significance in the "Religion" Debate

**Gerald Armstrong** (Hubbard's former archivist, now critic) argued in court testimony: "Freedom of religion without freedom to discuss religion and religious experience is impossible." An organization cannot claim both First Amendment religious protection *and* the right to hold exclusive secrets about which no one else may speak. He called this being a "secret-selling company" masquerading as a religion.

Courts have split: some have treated Fair Game as evidence against religious status; others have found it insufficient to disqualify the Church.

## Atack's Account (*A Piece of Blue Sky*, 1990)

Atack provides the most detailed primary-document record of Fair Game's formal policy history:

- **"Penalties for Lower Conditions" (October 18, 1967)**: the HCOPL that specified "enemy" status and its consequences — the formal statement of what was later called Fair Game in its most explicit form.
- **The 1968 "cancellation"**: Atack quotes the cancellation letter explicitly and shows it states the *phrase* is cancelled because it causes "a bad PR situation" — the accompanying note makes clear the underlying *practices* are unchanged.
- The Armstrong case record (especially Judge Breckenridge's June 1984 ruling) and the English Latey ruling (July 1984) both independently confirm that Fair Game continued in practice after the nominal 1968 cancellation. See [[armstrong-case|Armstrong Case]] and [[latey-ruling|Latey Ruling]].
- The GO's **"acceptable truth"** framework ([[acceptable-truth|Acceptable Truth]]) is the PR complement to Fair Game: externally deny the policy while internally continuing to apply it.
- Atack draws on 6 years of document review and court records, including the Armstrong case transcripts (28 volumes), to document specific operations carried out under Fair Game principles through the 1970s and 1980s.
