*Aggadah* (also *Haggadah*; Hebrew: אַגָּדָה, "telling, narrative") designates the non-legal material in the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] and related rabbinic literature: narrative, theological reflection, ethical teaching, parable, folklore, cosmology, biblical interpretation, and wisdom sayings. It is one of the two primary content categories of the Talmud, paired with [[halacha|Halacha]] (legal material).

## Scope and Character

Aggadah encompasses an enormous range of material:

- **Biblical narrative expansion**: Elaborations, gaps filled, and motivations supplied for events only sketched in Scripture (e.g., the inner life of Abraham, the character of the serpent in Eden)
- **Theological reflection**: Discussions of God's nature, divine justice, reward and punishment, the afterlife, the Messiah
- **Parables** (*meshalim*): Extended analogies illuminating halakhic or theological principles
- **Ethical maxims and wisdom sayings**: Especially concentrated in tractate *Avot* (Sayings of the Fathers)
- **Cosmological and apocalyptic speculation**: The nature of creation, the heavenly world, angelic beings
- **Historical narrative**: Stories about the sages (many hagiographic; some critical)
- **Homiletics** (*derashot*): Interpretive expansions of biblical texts, often highly imaginative

## Relationship to Midrash

Aggadah and **Midrash** are overlapping but distinct categories:
- Aggadah refers to non-legal content wherever it appears — in the Talmud, in the Midrashim, in liturgical poetry
- Midrash refers specifically to the literary genre of biblical commentary and the collections of such commentary (*Midrash Rabbah*, *Midrash Tanchuma*, etc.)
- All Midrashic aggadah is Aggadah; not all Aggadah is Midrash (e.g., Talmudic stories about sages are Aggadah but not technically Midrash)

## Authority and Legal Weight

The classical position: Aggadah is **not legally binding** (*ein madinim me-haggadah*). One may not derive binding halakhic rulings from homiletical or narrative material. This principle reflects the sense that Aggadah belongs to a different epistemic mode than law: it speaks to the spirit, not the letter.

In practice, this boundary is porous. Aggadic interpretations sometimes shape halakhic decisions; halakhic discussions regularly end with ethical Aggadah. The relationship between the two is best described as mutually constitutive rather than cleanly separated.

## The Aggadah as Jewish Spirit

[[isaac-unterman|Unterman]] characterizes Aggadah as "an expression of the Jewish spirit" — the imaginative, emotional, and theological register of rabbinic Judaism that the dry Halachic apparatus alone could not capture. The Aggadah preserves a record of how the rabbis imagined the biblical world, felt the weight of suffering (especially under Roman persecution), and maintained hope through narrative.

## Relationship to Hellenistic and Later Literature

Rabbinic Aggadah developed in a world of Hellenistic narrative, folklore, and philosophical reflection. The distinction between Aggadah and Hellenistic *midrash* (allegorical interpretation, as in Philo of Alexandria) is significant: Philo allegorizies Scripture to map it onto Greek philosophy; rabbinic Aggadah expands Scripture into a narrative world that is deeply Jewish in character, even when using Hellenistic literary devices.
