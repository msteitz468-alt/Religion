# Formula-Quotations

## Definition

"Formula-quotations" is the conventional designation for a set of Old Testament citations in the
[[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]] introduced by a recurring editorial formula built on the phrase "to
fulfill what had been declared through the prophet." They are the most visible literary vehicle of
the theme of [[fulfillment|fulfillment]].

[[rt-france|R. T. France]] counts the formula occurring ten times (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 4:14; 8:17;
12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9), and notes that most commentators add an eleventh at 2:5, where the
quotation is inserted into the direct speech of the chief priests and scribes. The typical wording
is that of 1:22: "All this happened to fulfill what had been declared by the Lord through the
prophet, who said…"

France observes that the prophet is named only where it is Isaiah or Jeremiah; those in the Book of
the Twelve and unidentifiable sources remain anonymous, with 1:22 the one unexplained exception ("no
obvious reason for not identifying [[isaiah|Isaiah]]"). The formula at 2:23 varies conspicuously,
using the plural "the prophets" and *hoti* in place of *legontos*.

France also notes that other quotations convey the same fulfillment message and are excluded from
the traditional list only because of a different introductory formula (3:3; 11:10; 13:14-15; 15:7-9;
21:42), plus statements about scriptures being fulfilled with no text specified (26:54, 56); these
are, in his words, "an equally important part of his project."

## France's position: editorial gloss, not testimony book

France reports the suggestion that the formula-quotations reached Matthew as an already collected
group, "perhaps from some sort of book of 'testimonies,' OT proof-texts for Christian apologetic."
He rejects it: texts such as Rachel's weeping and the Nazorean motif (2:18, 23) could hardly have
belonged to any such collection, owing their presence instead to Matthew's "imaginative perception
of OT 'pre-echoes' of details in the stories of Jesus."

His conclusion is that the formula-quotations are not part of Matthew's received tradition but his
own editorial gloss on the story, testimony to "the ingenuity of his pervasive midrashic agenda,"
of which the eleven are the most prominent outcrops. Commenting on 8:17 he generalizes the
function: the formula-quotations provide "an editorial comment on the narrative to which they are
attached."

## Quotation technique

France stresses that many of the texts cited "would not have been on anyone's list of obvious
messianic proof-texts": Isa 7:14 (eighth-century events), Hos 11:1 (an exodus reminiscence), Jer
31:15 (the trauma of Babylonian exile), Ps 78:2 (not from a prophet at all), Zech 11:13 (reworked
and attributed to Jeremiah), and the elusive 2:23. "There is obviously something more subtle going
on here than the simple claim that messianic predictions have been fulfilled."

**Text form.** France notes the quotations often do not follow the LXX, sometimes look like
independent renderings of the Hebrew, and often match no surviving version; he infers that Matthew
"was sometimes willing to modify the wording of the text in order to draw out more clearly" the
fulfillment he perceived. His summary formulation of the practice, at 2:6: "Rather than add a
footnote, Matthew has incorporated the fulfillment into the wording of the text" — a procedure he
likens to the targums and to a modern preacher's adapted quotation.

**Conflation.** France identifies the combination of two or more related Old Testament texts into a
single "quotation" as one particular modification technique (2:6; 21:5, and most elaborately the
Zechariah/Jeremiah quotation at 27:9-10).

**Direction of dependence.** France gives three reasons the narratives were not manufactured out of
the texts: (a) texts such as Hos 11:1 and Jer 31:15 would not have come to mind without a prior
narrative; (b) the quotations are adapted, which would be unnecessary if they were the source; and
(c) it is "an odd sort of apologetics which could create a story out of a text and then parade this
(fictional) story as proof" of fulfillment. His own position is "a dialectic between narrative
tradition and text … but the narrative tradition is necessarily prior," with a mutual interaction in
which the text is chosen for its relevance to the narrated event while the story is told in terms
that draw attention to the correspondence (notably Hos 11:1, Jer 31:15, and 27:3-10).

**Readership.** France advances a programmatic hermeneutical thesis: Matthew wrote for different
classes of readers and "deliberately provided 'bonus' meanings accessible only to the more
'sharp-eyed' or better instructed among his readers." He insists that Matthew's method, though not
ours and sometimes puzzling because of cultural distance, "is very far from haphazard or
unprincipled," presupposing God's purposeful control of both the words and the events of the Old
Testament.

## The five infancy quotations (1:18–2:23)

France notes that five of the eleven recognized formula-quotations fall within 1:18–2:23, forming
together with the [[genealogy-of-jesus|genealogy]] "a concentrated 'manifesto' setting out how Jesus
the Messiah fulfills the hopes of OT Israel." He argues that these quotations are not incidental
comment but "one of the key structural principles of the whole section": each scene is narrated in
order to demonstrate a fulfillment. His striking corollary is that Matthew includes these particular
infancy stories not mainly because he had different traditions available but "because he was
mounting a carefully constructed argument from Scripture, and it is these particular incidents which
enable him to present it." Beyond the quotations, France notes an allusive scriptural texture in
which readers are invited to see Jesus as new [[moses|Moses]], new Israel, new Solomon, and the
"star out of Jacob."

**1. Isa 7:14 at 1:22-23.** France notes the formula here carries two expansions — "by the Lord"
(also at 2:15) and "All this happened" — and reads the latter as widening the fulfillment beyond the
conception to "the whole complex of events": conception, birth, and especially naming. He identifies
three features drawing Matthew to the text: a child born to a virgin mother, the naming of the
child, and the christological title "God with us." Matthew's single deviation from the LXX is a
generalizing "they will call" in place of the Hebrew "she" and LXX "you," which France says "leaves
the way open for Jesus to be given his name not by Mary but by [[joseph|Joseph]]"; he states there
is "no indication that Matthew's plural verb came from any source other than his own creative
interpretation of the text," and may look ahead to what people will learn to say about Jesus. France
notes Matthew is not embarrassed by the mismatch between "Jesus" and "Immanuel," drawing Immanuel
into his theology by translating it, "though he has not made any such linking of the meanings of the
two names explicit." On the original setting see [[fulfillment|fulfillment]] (Ahaz, c. 735 BC).

**2. Micah 5:2 at 2:5-6.** France explains that the formula differs here ("because that is what has
been written") because the quotation stands inside direct speech, but holds that "most interpreters
agree that the following quotation is of the same character as the other 'formula-quotations.'" This
is where he formulates the "rather than add a footnote" principle (above).

**3. Hos 11:1 at 2:15.** France notes Matthew's form fairly translates the Hebrew and diverges from
the LXX, which rendered "my son" as "his [Israel's] children" — it is the Hebrew wording that gives
Matthew his point of entry. See [[hosea|Hosea]] and, for France's defence of the move,
[[fulfillment|fulfillment]] and [[typology|typology]].

**4. Jer 31:15 at 2:17-18 (unresolved).** France notes the formula changes from purposive "in order
to fulfill" to "Then was fulfilled" (as also at 27:9), suggesting this "may be designed to avoid
directly attributing evil actions (infanticide and betrayal) to God's declared intention," while
adding that the difference "is not very great." He states the situations are very different:
Jeremiah depicts Rachel weeping in her grave over descendants lost by deportation, not death. He
canvasses two possible bridges — that "Ramah" may trigger recall of Jer 40:1, where the exiles were
gathered for the march to Babylon and Jeremiah himself was released (hinting at a future "Jeremiah,"
cf. 16:14, who escapes the children's fate and also went to Egypt); and that ambiguity over Rachel's
burial place, Benjaminite Ramah (1 Sam 10:2) versus the traditional site outside Bethlehem (Gen
35:16-20; 48:7), "may have contributed to Matthew's feeling that this text about Ramah was in fact
also relevant." He then concedes this is among Matthew's most elusive quotations and that "few claim
with any confidence to have fathomed just what he intended." **France leaves this crux open.** See
[[jeremiah|Jeremiah]].

**5. "He shall be called a Nazorean" at 2:23 (unresolved).** France flags an open scholarly dispute:
the text behind 2:23 "is so elusive that scholars are still debating what text (if any) Matthew is
referring to." He stresses two formal peculiarities of the formula — plural "the prophets" and *hoti*
for *legontos* — which together suggest Matthew is citing "not a quotation of a specific passage but
rather a theme of prophecy" (cf. 26:56). He reports the popular *nēṣer* ("branch," Isa 11:1)
wordplay and the alternative *nṣr* ("watch, preserve") proposals but objects that they explain only
*Nazōraios* and not "he shall be called," and that "such a wordplay is totally invisible in Greek,
the language in which Matthew is writing." He rejects the *naziraios*/Judg 13:5 proposal on four
grounds. His own "most promising approach" takes its cue from Nazareth's very absence from
Scripture: to be "called a Nazorean" was to invite ridicule, the verb pointing to derogatory
name-calling (John 1:46; Matt 26:71). His conclusion is that the words represent the prophetic
expectation of a Messiah "who came from the wrong place, who did not conform to the expectations of
Jewish tradition," the embarrassment of [[nazareth|Nazareth]] being turned to apologetic advantage —
but he ends with an explicit admission of irresolution: "No solution to the exegetical problem posed
by 2:23 is straightforward," and if Matthew's readers shared a clearer understanding of *Nazōraios*,
"it is not now available to us." **France leaves this crux open.**

## Later formula-quotations in the ranges read

**Isa 8:23–9:1 at 4:15-16.** One of Matthew's longest formula-quotations. France judges the citation
abbreviated and "in a form closer to the Hebrew than to the LXX, but not fully corresponding to the
sense of either." Dropping the verbs produces a string of geographical terms filling out the identity
of "the people who sat in darkness" and throwing the focus onto terms appropriate to
[[capernaum|Capernaum]] rather than [[nazareth|Nazareth]]. France judges that Matthew understood "way
of the sea" of the Lake of [[galilee|Galilee]], while "it is more likely that Isaiah was referring to
the major route from Damascus to the Mediterranean." On "beyond the Jordan," which normally means the
east side (as at 4:25) though here Matthew clearly means the west, France raises but does not answer
the question: "Did he then understand Isaiah to be speaking not from his own Palestinian standpoint
but from that of the Assyrian invader?" **Unresolved.**

**Isa 53:4 at 8:17.** France's first compositional observation on Matt 8–9 is that "the only element
in this whole complex of material which is peculiar to Matthew is the formula-quotation which
concludes the first set of stories." He notes that 8:17 quotes a verse of Isa 53 that "is not the
normal focus of christological interest," and that elsewhere in the New Testament (1 Pet 2:24; Rom
4:25) it is applied to sin rather than healing. The LXX and the targum rendered the terms
metaphorically; Matthew "either knows a different Greek version or has produced his own more literal
rendering of the Hebrew," seeing in the literal senses a pointer to Jesus the healer.

**Isa 42:1-4 at 12:18-21.** France identifies this as Matthew's longest formula-quotation, "a
slightly abbreviated rendering of Isa 42:1-4 in a version markedly different from that of the LXX."
He catalogues the divergences ("I have chosen" for "I uphold"; "beloved" for "chosen"; "be a
messenger of" for "bring out"; "quarrel" for "shout"; "to victory" for "truly/faithfully"), while
noting agreement with the LXX at 42:4c ("in his name the nations") against the MT ("in his law the
islands"). He leaves the version's origin open — "whether his own creation or drawn from a Greek
version now unknown to us" — judging it a reasonable independent rendering of the Hebrew's general
sense. He argues that Matthew's choice of *erizō* ("quarrel, wrangle") for the Hebrew's "shout" makes
the application to Jesus explicit, since Jesus "has just withdrawn to avoid a further 'shouting
match' with the [[pharisees|Pharisees]]." On why the quotation is so long, France answers that "the
length of the quotation makes it clear that Matthew wants his readers to see it all, not just the
secrecy element, as a blueprint" for the ministry; by continuing into Isa 42:4 Matthew "points
forward to the future triumph of this gentle Messiah." He notes that the heavenly voice at 3:17 echoes
Isa 42:1 with *agapētos*, "beloved," reflecting the non-LXX version quoted here, and that the
rewording of 42:3's last clause to "to victory" compensates for the omission of Isa 42:4a-b. See
[[messianic-secret|messianic secret]].

**Ps 78:2 at 13:35.** France notes vv. 34-35 is presented entirely as editorial comment rather than as
the words of Jesus — the reverse of 13:14, where Jesus was the speaker — and that here "the normal
quotation formula returns." He flags the attribution crux: "The standard quotation formula is used even
though in this case the OT passage quoted is not from a prophet but from Ps 78:2." His solution is that
the psalmist (traditionally Asaph) is understood as making a prophetic utterance, the quoted words being
themselves a description of the prophet's role; he cross-references 11:13 (and 5:17) for "the 'prophetic'
role of nonprophetic scriptures" in Matthew. On text form, Matthew quotes the first line in LXX form
while the second line "is entirely independent of the LXX" — consistent with the mixed text-form
typical of the formula-quotations. See [[psalms|Psalms]] and [[parable|parable]].

## What is *not* a formula-quotation

France repeatedly polices the boundary of the category:

- **Isa 40:3 at 3:3** is not usually reckoned among them: it lacks "fulfill" and uses a masculine
  participle that "identifies John himself in terms of a prophetic model" rather than tracing
  fulfillment in events. See [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]].
- **Mal 3:1 at 11:10** likewise "does not have the regular formula of Matthew's fulfillment-citations
  …, but its effect is similar." France argues the quotation conflates Mal 3:1 with Exod 23:20, the
  first half being closer to LXX Exod 23:20 and the second reflecting the Hebrew of Mal 3:1, though
  Malachi is primarily in mind; he reports evidence that the two passages had already been connected in
  Jewish interpretation, so that the conflated wording was the standard form in which Mal 3:1 was
  remembered. He identifies the load-bearing christological move as the shift of Mal 3:1's first person
  ("before me") to a second person ("before you") in all three Synoptics, permitting the forerunner to
  precede someone other than God himself.
- **Isa 6:9-10 at 13:14-15** France argues is *not* a Matthean formula-quotation, on three grounds: it
  is spoken by Jesus rather than given as editorial comment, its introductory formula differs, and it
  uses unaltered LXX rather than the mixed creative Greek typical of the formula-quotations. He cautions
  that Matthew uses the LXX form here as the standard Greek text available to him "and not necessarily
  because it suits his purpose better than the Hebrew," though it does cohere with his *hoti*.
- **Isa 29:13 at 15:8-9** France describes as a slight adaptation of the LXX, which restructures the
  Hebrew's last two lines and adds "in vain," possibly reflecting a variant text (*wetōhû*, "and in
  vain," for the similar-looking *watehî*, "and it is"), making explicit what the received Hebrew
  implies.
