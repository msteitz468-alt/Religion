# Theopaschism ("One of the Trinity Suffered")

The post-Chalcedonian formula that **"one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh"** (*unus ex Trinitate passus est*) — affirming, via the [[communicatio-idiomatum|communication of properties]], that the subject of Christ's suffering is the divine Word himself, not merely an assumed man. Drawn from [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Jaroslav Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 5).

## The Controversy

The formula tested whether Chalcedon's "one hypostasis" really meant the Word is the single subject of the incarnate life. It surfaced in the **Trisagion controversy**: at Antioch the hymn was revised to "Holy God, holy and mighty, holy and immortal, **thou who wast crucified for us**, have mercy on us" — raising the question, "Did one of the Trinity suffer in the flesh?" Many rejected the addition as an innovation against "the ancient tradition of the catholic church" (an appeal to [[lex-orandi-lex-credendi|liturgical norm]]).

The **theopaschite formula** was, however, defended as orthodox by [[justinian|Justinian]] and Proclus ("God the Logos, one of the Trinity, was incarnate; therefore he himself both works miracles and suffers"), and was effectively ratified at the Second Council of Constantinople (553). It cut against both the [[nestorianism|Nestorian]] insistence on the Logos's strict [[divine-impassibility|impassibility]] (the East Syrians argued that to say "one of the Trinity suffered" meant either the Father and Spirit suffered too, or the Logos was severed from them) and the [[oriental-orthodoxy|aphthartodocetist]] denial that Christ's flesh was genuinely passible.
