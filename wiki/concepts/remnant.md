The remnant (*šĕʾār / šĕʾērît*) — the survivors of judgment who carry the covenant people's
future. Documented here primarily from the book of [[isaiah|Isaiah]] via Oswalt (NICOT,
chs. 1–39).

## Definition and Origin (as documented)

In Isaiah the theme is bound up with the prophet's son **Shear-jashub**, "a remnant will
return" (7:3). Oswalt reads the name's emphatic word order as "only a remnant" — "the
essential message of the name is negative, but it is not unremittingly so": to Ahaz it also
promises that "there will be a remnant of Judah (and the house of David) preserved from
destruction." Oswalt endorses (with qualification) Kissane's judgment that the remnant is
close to "Isaiah's most characteristic idea," and remarks that although the term disappears
after 17:3, "it is still perhaps the most apt summary of the entire book."

## Key Loci in Isaiah 1–39 (attributed to Oswalt)

- **1:9** — "a little remnant" spared, else Sodom and Gomorrah. Oswalt rejects the
  form-critical claim that early Isaiah allowed no restoration hope: "Isaiah is not
  commissioned to bring his people to repentance, but nowhere is he forbidden to preach
  repentance and promise restoration."
- **4:2–3** — the survivors in Zion "called holy, every one written for life"; the
  book-of-life registration is responsive to human choice, not bare predestination.
- **6:13** — the burned stump whose "holy seed" (a phrase absent from the LXX; Oswalt
  retains the MT) is the hope-note of the hardening commission, linked forward to 10:33–11:1
  and the Abrahamic promise: "The Lord does not destroy the forest of human pride to leave
  us a field of stumps."
- **10:20–23** — the remnant "will rest upon the LORD, the Holy One of Israel, in truth";
  yet the Abrahamic numbers "cannot be used as a hedge to protect oneself from judgment."
- **28:5–6; 30:17; 37:30–32** — "In every age there is a remnant which is a part of that
  great final one"; the surviving-flagpole image; the agricultural sign-figures after 701
  echoing chs. 7–12.

## Comparative Note (attributed)

Oswalt cites Hasel: remnant language in Assyrian conquest rhetoric is "wholly negative"
(what is left after devastation); Isaiah inverts the conquerors' term — "even a remnant, in
God's hand, is more enduring than all Assyria's might."
