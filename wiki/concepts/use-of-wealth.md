# Use of Wealth (Christian Wealth Ethics)

A perennial question of Christian ethics: whether the Gospel demands literal renunciation of property
or the right *use* of it. The classic early formulation is [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of
Alexandria]]'s homily [[quis-dives-salvetur|Quis Dives Salvetur]] ("Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be
Saved?"), one of the earliest sustained treatments of wealth in Christian literature.

## Clement's Position: Disposition, Not Dispossession

Reading Mark 10:17–31 ([[allegorical-reading|allegorically]], not literally), Clement argues that
Christ's "sell whatsoever thou hast" commands the renunciation of the *passions* about wealth, not of
wealth itself: "let a man do away, not with his possessions, but rather with the passions of his soul."
Wealth is a morally neutral **instrument** — "its nature is to minister, not to rule"; responsibility
lies in "the mind of man, which has in itself both free judgment and full liberty." Mere poverty is no
virtue (else beggars would be saved by destitution alone), and pagan philosophers who renounced
property sometimes only intensified their pride. The genuinely rich are "rich in virtues"; the
disposition to hold goods "as gifts of God," to minister freely from them, and to lose them "with a
cheerful mind" is what makes one "poor in spirit." Almsgiving, a spiritual director, and good use of
the "mammon of unrighteousness" form the program.

## The Enduring Tension

Clement's "right use" tradition coexists, often uneasily, with a **literal-renunciation** tradition —
the radical dispossession of [[quis-dives-salvetur|the Rich Young Ruler]] read at face value, monastic
poverty, the apostolic community of goods (Acts 2–4), and later movements (the Franciscans, various
reformers) that read the Gospel as a call to actual poverty. The two readings of the same pericope
constitute a standing interpretive divide in Christian social teaching. Clement's own [[paedagogus|
Paedagogus]] already carries a communitarian note ("All things therefore are common, and not for the
rich to appropriate an undue share").
