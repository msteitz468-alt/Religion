# Analogy and the Divine Names (Aquinas)

The doctrine of **analogy** is [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s account of how human language can
signify God truly though imperfectly. It is developed at [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]]
I, Q13 ("The Names of God"), and it resolves a problem set up by [[divine-simplicity|divine
simplicity]]: if God is utterly simple and exceeds all our concepts, how can perfection-terms
("good," "wise," "living") be applied to Him at all?

## The two rejected extremes

Aquinas frames analogy between two errors (Q13, A5):

- **Univocal predication** (the same sense for God and creatures) — impossible. A creature
  receives the perfections of its cause "not in its full degree, but in a measure that falls
  short." "Wise" said of a man signifies a perfection distinct from his essence; said of God it
  signifies nothing distinct from His essence — and "leaves the thing signified as incomprehended,
  and as exceeding the signification of the name."
- **Purely equivocal predication** (wholly unrelated senses) — also impossible, for then "from
  creatures nothing could be known or demonstrated about God at all… against what the Apostle says:
  'The invisible things of God are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made' (Rom.
  1:20)."

## Analogical predication

"Therefore it must be said that these names are said of God and creatures in an analogous sense,
i.e. according to proportion" (Q13, A5). The mode of community is "a mean between pure equivocation
and simple univocation": the term signifies "various proportions to some one thing." The divine
names follow the analogy of *one thing to another* (as "healthy" is said of medicine and of an
animal because medicine *causes* health in the animal): "whatever is said of God and creatures, is
said according to the relation of a creature to God as its principle and cause, wherein all
perfections of things pre-exist excellently."

Two refinements:
- **Names signify the divine substance, not merely negation or relation** (Q13, A2). Against the
  view of [[maimonides|Maimonides]] ("Rabbi Moses") that affirmative names express only *remotion*
  ("God lives" = "God is not inanimate"), and against the view that they express only God's relation
  to creatures ("God is good" = "God causes goodness"), Aquinas holds: "God is good" means "whatever
  good we attribute to creatures, pre-exists in God, and in a more excellent and higher way." Names
  "signify the divine substance… although they fall short of a full representation of Him."
- **Perfection vs. mode of signifying** (Q13, A3). What a perfection-term *signifies* (goodness,
  being, life) belongs to God most properly and primarily; its *mode of signification* (drawn from
  creatures, which we know first) does not. So "good," "wise," "being" apply to God *literally*,
  while "lion," "rock" apply only *metaphorically*.

The divine names are therefore **not synonymous** (Q13, A4), though they name one simple reality:
the intellect forms many conceptions "proportional to the perfections flowing from God to
creatures," signifying the one God "under many aspects."

## "He Who Is" and the Tetragrammaton (Q13, A9, A11)

The most proper name of God, considered as to its source, is **"HE WHO IS"** (*Qui est*, Exod.
3:14), for three reasons: it signifies existence itself (and in God essence *is* existence); it is
maximally indeterminate, denominating "the infinite ocean of substance" (Damascene); and it
consignifies present existence, "whose existence knows not past or future." Aquinas ranks the most
proper names: **"HE WHO IS"** (as to source), **"God"** (as to the object intended, the divine
nature), and the **Tetragrammaton** (as signifying the incommunicable, singular divine *suppositum*).

## Comparison

The treatment converges with [[maimonides|Maimonides]] on the uniqueness of the *Ehyeh asher
Ehyeh* / Tetragrammaton name as signifying pure, necessary existence (see [[divine-attributes]],
[[divine-name]]), and both deny univocity between God and creatures. The decisive divergence is
that Maimonides admits no positive *essential* attributes (only negations and attributes of
action — see [[negative-theology]]), whereas Aquinas's analogy lets perfection-terms be predicated
*substantially* of God, truly if imperfectly.
