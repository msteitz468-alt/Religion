# Kingdom of Heaven

## Definition

"The kingdom of heaven" (Greek *hē basileia tōn ouranōn*, literally "the kingdom of the heavens") is
the characteristic phrase of the [[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]] for what the other Synoptics usually
call "the kingdom of God." It is the content of the proclamation of both [[john-the-baptist|John the
Baptist]] (3:2) and [[jesus|Jesus]] (4:17), and the announced subject of the parable discourse, where
[[rt-france|R. T. France]] notes the phrase is linked explicitly to each parable (13:11, 24, 31, 33,
44, 45, 47, 52).

## "Heaven" rather than "God"

France holds that "kingdom of the heavens" is functionally identical to "kingdom of God," and that the
form Jesus himself used was "kingdom of God." He rejects the conventional explanation that Matthew
substitutes "heaven" as a reverential circumlocution, on the ground that Matthew names God freely
elsewhere, and concludes: "'The kingdom of heaven' may be simply a stylistic preference which, as
such, requires no explanation."

Where Matthew departs from his usual phrase, France declines to make the departure carry weight. At
6:33, one of only five Matthean occurrences of "kingdom of God," he explains the change by the
context's need for a more personal reference to God himself and adds that "nothing in the context
requires any more subtle reason for the change of terminology." At 12:28 he explains the switch as
required both to balance "Spirit of God" and by the preceding reference to Satan's kingdom, so that
"Jesus' saying thus vividly contrasts the personal kings of the two kingdoms." See [[satan|Satan]].

France does treat "heaven" in Matthew's usage as relational rather than locative. He reads "reward in
heaven" (5:12) as "probably best understood not of a location so much as of a relationship with God,"
citing Matthew's preference for "kingdom of heaven." He insists that "enter the kingdom of heaven,"
though "the most strongly spatial metaphor" in Matthew's kingdom vocabulary, must be treated as a
metaphor: to enter it "does not mean to go to a place called heaven … but to come under God's rule."
On 16:19 he states that the traditional portrayal of [[peter-apostle|Peter]] as porter at the pearly
gates "depends on misunderstanding 'the kingdom of heaven' here as a designation of the afterlife"
rather than of God's rule among his people on earth. Not every use is relational: at 6:20 France weighs
whether "heaven" is a surrogate for God ("treasures with God") but concludes that the direct contrast
with "on earth" and the sense of provision implied in "store up for yourselves" "strongly suggest an
otherworldly focus."

## "Kingdom" as reign, not realm

France's central lexical claim is that *basileia* is a verbal noun describing God ruling, not a thing
called a kingdom. The phrase "represents, in other words, a sentence of which the subject is not
'kingdom' but 'God'" — better rendered "kingship" or "sovereignty." He argues that the English
"kingdom," rather than "reign," fosters a common misunderstanding, and criticizes the modern habit of
abbreviating to "the kingdom" as "conspicuously absent from the Synoptic tradition"; he shows that
Matthew omits "of heaven/God" only where *basileia* depends on another noun ("gospel of the
*basileia*," "sons of the *basileia*," "word of the *basileia*") and "never uses *hē basileia* alone in
the manner of Acts 20:25 and modern usage." He objects even to the adjectival use of "kingdom" in
recent writing, citing Keener's "kingdom works" with an exclamation mark.

His compact definition, at 6:33: "God's kingship means God's people living under God's rule." At 6:10
he formulates the petition as asking not that God *become* king but "that his actual kingship *de jure*
may be fully implemented *de facto* as people submit to his sovereignty," and holds that the third
clause builds on the second, since "the essence of the coming of God's kingship is that he is duly
obeyed and his purpose fulfilled."

France notes that the concept of God's rule is fundamental to the Old Testament and later Judaism even
though the phrase itself is infrequent, reaching definitive expression in Daniel, and cites the Kaddish
prayer that concludes the synagogue liturgy: "May God let his kingship rule in your lifetime and in
your days." He adopts G. R. Beasley-Murray's phrase "the coming of God" for the prophetic hope
underlying the kingdom declaration.

## Already and not yet

France reads the tense of the proclamation as decisive. On *ēngiken* at 3:2 and 4:17 he argues the
perfect means the kingdom has completed the process of coming near and is already present: "That which
has completed the process of 'coming near' is already present, not simply still on the way" — hence his
rendering "has arrived," supported by the parallel usage at 26:45-46, by Mark 1:15, and by the "already"
of 3:10. At 12:28 he argues the aorist *phthanō* ("has caught up with") carries the same implication as
the perfect of *engizō* at 3:2; 4:17; 10:7: "God's kingship is already a reality." He adds that the
kingdom's arrival is joy to those who embrace it but threat to opponents — it has caught up with the
[[pharisees|Pharisees]], "breaking uncomfortably into their cozily controlled world of tradition and
turning everything upside down."

The future pole is equally firm. France calls "your kingdom come" (6:10) "perhaps the most clearly
futuristic reference to God's kingdom in Matthew," read in light of the mustard seed and yeast, where a
hidden present kingdom prepares a future public demonstration; and he notes the irony that the
*later-added* doxology makes disciples who have just prayed for the kingdom's coming immediately
declare it already a reality, "vividly illustrat[ing]" the already–not yet tension. See
[[lords-prayer|Lord's Prayer]]. Because the kingdom has already been announced and is working its way
in through Jesus' ministry, France holds that "every clause of this prayer has an immediate relevance
to the present situation and concerns of those who are praying."

France explicitly rejects reading "seek first the kingdom" (6:33) as "trying to bring in God's kingdom"
as an eschatological event, arguing from the present imperative *zēteite* and the verb's normal sense:
"One must pray for the coming of God's reign (6:10) because it is God who will bring it into being."

From the parables of treasure and pearl he infers a present dimension of benefit: "it is unlikely that
the blessings of the kingdom of heaven are envisaged as purely future and eschatological."

## The kingdom in the Sermon on the Mount

France identifies a kingdom-of-heaven inclusio in the [[beatitudes|Beatitudes]]: 5:3 and 5:10 share the
same second clause, "thus forming a framework which sets the tone for the promises which come between."
He glosses "theirs is the kingdom of heaven" as meaning not that the poor in spirit hold royal
authority "but that they are the ones who gladly accept God's rule and who therefore enjoy the benefits
which come to his subjects," poverty of spirit being the product of the repentance demanded at 4:17.

On 5:19 France notes that the careless disciple is at least "within the kingdom of heaven," unlike the
scribes and Pharisees of 5:20, but scarcely worthy of it; he rejects reading this as first- and
second-class citizenship, taking "great" and "small" to mean high or low in God's esteem, and warns
that "it is not helpful to press it into supporting a view of the kingdom of heaven as a social
structure." On 5:16 he insists the aim of visible good deeds is not "the improvement and enlightenment
of society as such" but the glorifying of God — "not so much the betterment of life on earth as the
implementation of the reign of God." On 6:19-24 he concludes the warning is "not specifically against
ill-gotten wealth but about possessions as such," which however neutral can become a rival loyalty:
"The principle of materialism is in inevitable conflict with the kingship of God." See
[[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]] and [[righteousness|righteousness]].

## The kingdom and the constitution of a people

France holds that Matthew's conviction is that the future of the kingdom of heaven lies not in the
institutions of Judaism but in "a newly constituted people of God focused not on national origin but on
allegiance to Jesus the Messiah." He calls 8:11-12 "one of the clearest statements of Matthew's
understanding of the relation between Israel and the new community," combining continuity (the Hebrew
patriarchs remain at the head of the table) with discontinuity (newcomers admitted, "sons of the
kingdom" thrown out); the saying's paradoxical force "depends on these 'sons of the kingdom' being
those whom everyone would expect to be included," the same term denoting the saved at 13:38. He defends
the messianic-banquet reference: "reclining" suggests a formal meal, Jewish tradition (b. Pesaḥ. 119b;
Exod. Rab. 25:8) gave the patriarchs a leading role, and the imagery derives from Isa 25:6 — a feast
"for all peoples" which "Jewish tradition soon made … a blessing specifically for Israel." He links the
meal with tax collectors at 9:10 back to that saying: "here, as there, the guest list is not at all
what most Jews would have expected." See [[gentiles|Gentiles]], [[israelites|Israelites]].

France draws a paradox from the calling of the first disciples: "four local fishermen do not sound like
a world-changing task force," anticipating the mustard-seed parable's account of the kingdom's
insignificant beginnings. He states bluntly that "the kingdom of heaven apparently involves a degree of
fanaticism which is willing to disrupt the normal rhythms of social life," and that Jesus can hardly
have been surprised that [[discipleship|discipleship]] remained a minority movement. He notes that the
missionary discourse gives the disciples the same message as John and Jesus without the explicit call
to repentance supplied by Mark 6:12, since "Matthew takes that for granted as the corollary of the
coming of God's kingship." See [[twelve-apostles|Twelve Apostles]].

## The kingdom in the parable discourse (Matt 13)

France reads the discourse as answering the question raised by chs. 11–12: "why is it not being
welcomed and acted on by all those who hear?" Its key term is "the secrets of the kingdom of heaven"
(13:11). He derives *mystērion* from Dan 2 (LXX and Theodotion) rendering the Aramaic *rāz*, where God
gives Daniel privileged access to a divine secret other wise men cannot penetrate, and argues that
"mystery" is a poor English rendering, since English "mystery" suggests something intrinsically
unfathomable whereas the revealed divine message is not obscure; he prefers "secret." He suggests that
Matthew's and Luke's plural (against Mark's singular) may indicate "not a single item, but the whole new
world of realities which opens up" on entering the kingdom. He insists "secret" does not mean jealously
guarded — citing Mark 4:22 — but that until people become insiders they cannot grasp it, the secret being
communicated as disciples share the message.

On the mustard seed and yeast France reads a barely perceptible beginning issuing in a spectacular
transformation: the kingdom may now be disdained but will become impossible to ignore. He stresses that
both images work through *natural process*, so that those impatient (such as John the Baptist at 11:3)
must be prepared to wait, "for the outcome may take time, but it is certain." He reads the roosting
birds as recalling Nebuchadnezzar's tree-vision (Dan 4:12, 21), where the birds likely represent subject
nations, inviting comparison "between the great but short-lived earthly empire of Babylon and the far
greater and more permanent kingdom of heaven" — but restrains the Gentile-inclusion reading, which
"might be a bonus point for the sharp-eyed reader … but it is not emphasized." He makes much of the verb
"hid" at 13:33, linking it to the kingdom's hiddenness (11:25; 13:11, 35, 44) and to the
[[messianic-secret|messianic secret]] of 16:20; 17:9: "The truth about the kingdom of heaven is not only
inconspicuous; it is also deliberately kept hidden for the time being." He distinguishes the pair:
the leaven's remarkable feature "is not its own growth … but the expansion which it causes in the new
dough." His summary: "The kingdom of heaven may be initially insignificant, but it is pervasive."

France notes a recurring grammatical awkwardness: "the kingdom of heaven is not like the man himself,
but like the situation which results from his action." At 13:43 he reads the eschatological reversal of
the hiddenness motif — in contrast with the present hiddenness of the kingdom and its adherents, "one
day they will shine in heavenly glory for all to see" — and notes the shift from "the kingdom of the Son
of Man" (13:41) to "the kingdom of their Father" (13:43), which he calls "the goal to which the hard
road of discipleship must eventually lead." See [[parable|parable]], [[son-of-man|Son of Man]],
[[last-judgment|last judgment]].

## Contested and unresolved

**The kingdom under violent attack (11:12).** France labels this "one of the most controversial sayings
in the gospel" and notes that the logical sequence becomes obscure at this point. His own reading takes
*biazomai*, *biastēs*, and *harpazō* in their normal negative senses: "This is, in Matthew, a declaration
that the kingdom of heaven has been and remains subject to violent opposition," grounded concretely in
John's imprisonment and coming execution and in the hostility to Jesus. He canvasses further options in
their own strength — "ravage" (less common for the verb, but fitting the violence of the first clause and
the noun *biastai*, and requiring no specific identification of the violence) and "seize" (a takeover bid
on the Jesus movement by people with an agenda of violence, against Rome) — and then declines to settle
the second clause: "The phrase remains obscure, and such interpretations do not yet fit easily into what
we know of the time of Jesus' Galilean ministry," though they would suit Matthew's own time better. On
11:11 he reads the contrast as one between two eras rather than a verdict on John's personal salvation:
"The issue here is not John's personal salvation, but his place in the scheme of salvation history."

**Political reception.** France suggests [[herod-antipas|Herod Antipas]] "may well have been concerned
about the possible implications of Jesus' announcement of the coming of a 'kingdom of heaven.'"

**A gap to note.** In the ranges of the commentary read for this ingest, France's fullest treatment of
why Matthew prefers "heaven" to "God" is the discussion at 3:2 recorded above; elsewhere (e.g. in the
Sermon on the Mount material) he refers the reader back to that passage rather than restating the case.
No further or subtler rationale should be attributed to him.

## The Kingdom of God in Mark (France, NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the fidelity reingest of [[france-mark-nigtc|France's NIGTC Mark]]. All
interpretive claims are [[rt-france|France]]'s.*

France notes that [[mark|Mark]] uses ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ only 14 times (five in 10:13-27) yet
centres the ch. 4 discourse on it; the phrase is "probably best represented in English by 'God's
kingship'" and "encapsulates the paradox which we have already seen to lie at the heart of
Mark's story." Summarising his own 1990 monograph *Divine Government*, he argues *basileia* is
an abstract noun for God's kingship with no single time/place/event referent, so "it is
therefore not appropriate to ask whether 'the kingdom of God' is past, present, or future, as if
it had a specific time-reference like 'the day of Yahweh'."

**Present fulfilment.** On 1:15 — the reference point, France argues, for all later unspecified
mentions of proclamation in Mark — the perfects πεπλήρωται and ἤγγικεν announce present
fulfilment, not mere imminence: "Jesus is not predicting some future event, but announcing the
time of fulfilment." The twin imperatives μετανοεῖτε καὶ πιστεύετε recall the prophetic call to
Israel to return (*šûb*) to covenant obedience. The kingdom is a μυστήριον (4:11) — "privileged
information rather than a puzzle," Daniel's *rāz* — not because obscure but because "it
challenges and opposes every natural human valuation": dynamically present (1:15) yet its full
experience future (14:25; 9:1). At 9:1 the perfect ἐληλυθυῖαν sees the kingdom "come in power"
within the generation (cross, resurrection, Pentecost, church growth, and/or A.D. 70) — to
classify it as a failed parousia prediction is "surely perverse" — with the transfiguration "at
least a partial and proleptic fulfilment."

**The paradox of insignificant beginnings.** France (quoting Myers) stresses that the kingdom
announcement issues not in cosmic events but the gathering of "socially insignificant people in
an unnoticed corner of provincial Galilee." The seed growing secretly (4:26-29) concerns the
kingdom's inner dynamic during apparent inaction — harvest "in God's time and in God's way, not
by human effort"; the mustard seed (4:30-32), with its Ezekiel 17/31 and Daniel 4 echoes,
points to the kingdom's future multi-national scope and "imperial" pretensions "which will take
the place of the human empires of OT times."

**The kingdom's alternative value scale.** France notes 10:1-31 has the densest concentration
of kingdom language in the gospel (vv. 14, 15, 23, 24, 25), pressing "the first are last" into
marriage, children, and possessions: "of such is the kingdom" (10:14-15) concerns those who
share the child's *status*; the camel/needle saying states impossibility, not difficulty — "the
salvation of the rich is always a miracle, but miracles are God's speciality" (10:27); and
10:42-44 is the most powerful statement of the alternative value scale: "The 'natural'
assumptions and valuations by which people operate no longer apply in the kingdom of God. It is
a genuinely alternative society." Act Two as a whole he reads as the disciples' "re-education
... to the revolutionary viewpoint of the kingdom of God" (see [[discipleship|discipleship]]).

**Political misreading and the cross.** France notes the crowd's cry about "the coming kingdom
of our father David" (11:10) has "an altogether more political and nationalistic ring" than the
kingdom of God Jesus announced. At 14:25 the "cup of death" and "cup of future glory" are
linked: through the death ὑπὲρ πολλῶν comes the consummation of God's kingship. From 15:2 the
kingship of Jesus becomes "the focal theme for the rest of Mark's account of the passion": Jesus
"does enter into his true kingship, paradoxically enthroned on the cross." Joseph of Arimathaea,
προσδεχόμενος τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ (15:43), is for France "a sympathetic fellow traveller"
comparable to the scribe of 12:34 who was "not far from the kingdom of God" — the one
open-minded scribe symbolising, France says, "what might have been."
