# Biblical and Historical Criticism in Antiquity

The application, by ancient writers, of **philological, chronological, and historical-critical methods** to sacred texts — anticipating, centuries early, the techniques of modern historical-critical biblical scholarship. The concept is developed here chiefly from Robert Louis Wilken, [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|*The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*]], whose central exhibit is [[porphyry|Porphyry of Tyre]].

## Porphyry on the Book of Daniel

Porphyry's treatment of [[daniel|Daniel]] is the paradigm case. Using skills in chronography (learned in part from Callinicus's history of the Ptolemies) and the literary criticism he had absorbed from Longinus, he argued that Daniel was **not** sixth-century prophecy but a *vaticinium ex eventu* ("prophecy after the fact") composed in Judaea in the second century BCE, under Antiochus IV Epiphanes:

> "whatever he spoke of up till the time of Antiochus contained authentic history, whereas anything he may have conjectured beyond that point was false, inasmuch as he would not have foreknown the future." (Jerome, *Comm. in Danielem*, prologue)

P. M. Casey's verdict (quoted by Wilken): "Centuries before the advent of modern biblical criticism, Porphyry already knew that the book of Daniel was a Maccabean pseudepigraph." Wilken judges that on the central question — historical narrative vs. genuine prediction — "Porphyry had the stronger case."

## The Methods Involved

1. **Chronological/historical analysis** — testing a text's internal events against independently known history to date its composition.
2. **Authorship criticism (pseudepigraphy)** — arguing a work was written later than, and by someone other than, its ascribed author.
3. **The distinction between *vaticinium ex eventu* and genuine prediction**.
4. **Literary-source and harmonization criticism** — Porphyry also catalogued the evangelists' discrepancies (divergent genealogies and infancy narratives; OT citations misattributed — Mark 1:2 to Isaiah; "Jeremiah" for Zechariah at Matt 27:9) and the Peter–Paul conflict of Galatians 2.

## Relation to Allegory

This critical method stands in direct opposition to the **allegorical** hermeneutic ([[allegorical-reading]]). Porphyry knew allegory well (his *De Antro Nympharum* allegorizes Homer) but **refused to let it "explain away"** the literal and historical difficulties of Scripture — precisely the move [[origen|Origen]] used to defend the Bible. Robert Grant's formulation: Porphyry accepted Origen's negative observations and "rejected the deeper spiritual meanings which Origen sought to find." The opposition of *critical-literal* and *allegorical-spiritual* readings of the same texts is thus already fully drawn in the third century.

Wilken's broader point: "The question of faith and history, so much a part of modern theological discourse since the Enlightenment, was also a significant part of the debate between pagans and Christians in the ancient world."
