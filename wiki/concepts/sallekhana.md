# Sallekhana (The Religious Death)

## Definition

*Sallekhānā* (also called *samthārā* or *saṃthāra*, "death-bed", in contemporary Śvetāmbara usage) is the Jain practice of a controlled, voluntary death through fasting — a gradual withdrawal from food and drink undertaken with a sacred formula on one's lips, directed entirely toward the soul, and aimed at eliminating [[karma|karma]] as death approaches. The name derives from the idea that the body is "scoured out" (*sallīkhita*) of its negative factors, so that the mind can focus solely upon spiritual matters. It represents the logical culmination of the central Jain austerity of fasting.

## Theological Basis

Two Jain teachings converge to explain sallekhānā: the general Indian belief that the last moment of life has decisive bearing on the state of an individual's next birth, and the specifically Jain teaching about the possibility of karma's destruction through gradual withdrawal from mental and physical activity (Dundas, *The Jains*, ch. 6).

## Not Suicide

Jain writers have always been adamant that sallekhānā is not equivalent to suicide. They contrast it with the "fool's death" — forms of self-destruction carried out through despair or inadequacy which involve violence to one's body. Akalanka argued that suicide would have to involve the passions, as well as the probable use of poisons or weapons (TSRV p. 550), neither of which is present in the slow, controlled practice of sallekhānā. The act "is exclusively directed towards the soul and must be performed with a sacred formula on one's lips" (MA 94).

## The Paradigmatic Account

The earliest statement about the religious death emphasises its solitude and difficulty (AS 1.7.8). The paradigmatic Śvetāmbara canonical description is the account of the death of the brahman convert Skandaka Kātyāyana in the *Exposition of Explanations* (Bh 2.1). Skandaka, a pupil of Mahāvīra, initially engages in severe bodily mortification but maintains full energy and mental lucidity. Receiving Mahāvīra's permission, he:

1. Takes the five Great Vows again
2. Finds an appropriate spot of ground, surrounded by other monks
3. Recites appropriate ritual formulae
4. Takes the five Great Vows once more (the double-taking represents an initiation into the final human rite of passage)
5. Abandons food, drink and his body — "without actively waiting for death"
6. Omits sixty meals
7. Confesses and repents, dying in meditation, "controlling his life until the very last minute"

The ceremonial nature of this sallekhānā is confirmed by the fact that Skandaka spreads *darbha* grass — a standard constituent of Vedic ritual — on the ground where he fasts to death.

## Digambara–Svetambara Differences

The older Śvetāmbara texts are primarily concerned with the force which sallekhānā derives from being performed by heroic monks at the height of their physical and mental powers. Later canonical texts shift emphasis: sallekhānā is presented as requiring the controlling involvement of a teacher as presiding guide throughout the whole process and, furthermore, as a form of death accessible to lay people (Dundas, ch. 6).

Early medieval writers suggest that when bodily illness or infirmity prevent anybody from performing the Obligatory Actions, sallekhānā can be an appropriate end to life (TSRV p. 551). One Digambara story describes a Jain soldier who, wounded in battle, resolved to fast to death and so went to heaven (BKK 124).

The extension to the laity is confirmed by inscriptional evidence from south India attesting to the voluntary religious death of prominent lay people, but sallekhānā seems to have ceased to play even an ideal role in lay spirituality by about the twelfth century.

## Historical Practice and Decline

Sallekhānā in the sense of fasting over a long period along with ascetic reinitiation has not been a general or practical goal among the Śvetāmbaras for considerable time. It is perhaps only the revived Digambara ascetic body — taking its lead from the great Ācārya Śāntisāgara who performed sallekhānā in 1955 — which might regard fasting to death as a wished-for conclusion to a monastic career. Pious lay people, when advanced in years and encouraged by ascetics, will still occasionally decrease their food intake with a view to bringing about a religious death. The interest and excitement elicited by a Sthānakvāsī nun who died at eighty-seven after a fifty-one-day fast is testimony to the relative rarity of the religious death (Dundas, ch. 6).
