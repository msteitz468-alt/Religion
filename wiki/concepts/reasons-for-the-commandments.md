*Hebrew*: **ta'amei ha-mitzvot** — the reasons/purposes of the commandments.

The question of whether the divine commandments have discoverable rational purposes, and what those purposes are, is one of the central problems of Jewish religious philosophy. [[maimonides|Maimonides]] devotes Part III, Chapters XXVI–XLIX of the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|Guide for the Perplexed]] to its systematic treatment; Chapters XLI–XLIX constitute the detailed examination of all fourteen classes of commandments in turn.

## The Governing Principle: Every Commandment Has a Cause

Maimonides argues that **every commandment has a rational purpose**, and that obedience is not nobler when the commandment is purposeless. This position is stated programmatically and carried through the entire treatment. Regarding those laws whose reason he cannot state, he writes that if the Sabean religious practices were better known to us, "we would clearly see the reason and wisdom of every detail in the sacrificial service, in the laws concerning things that are unclean, and in other laws, the object of which I am unable to state" (Ch. XLIX). He applies a general therapeutic hermeneutic: "Every positive or negative precept, the reason of which is unknown to thee, take as a remedy against some of those diseases with which we are unacquainted at present, thank God" (Ch. XLIX).

The dominant historical-contextual explanation is that most commandments function as **a fence against the idolatry of the Sabians** — the ancient pagan nation whose practices pervaded the religious environment in which the Torah was given. "Most of the precepts serve, as has been stated by us, as a mere fence against those doctrines [of idolatry], and relieve man from the great and heavy burdens, from the pains and inflictions which formed part of the worship of idols" (Ch. XLIX). See [[sabians]] for full treatment of who the Sabians are as Maimonides understands them.

## The Fourteen Classes

Maimonides organizes the commandments into fourteen classes. Part III Chapters XLI–XLIX treat classes six through fourteen in detail (earlier chapters covered the first five).

**Class Six: Punishments** (Ch. XLI). Punishments are calibrated by four factors: (1) the gravity of the sin; (2) the frequency of the crime; (3) the strength of temptation; (4) the ease of committing the act in secret. The governing principle is proportionality: "all these things which God has commanded as a punishment are not excessive nor inadequate, but, as is distinctly stated, according to the fault" (Deut. 25:2, cited Ch. XLI). Capital punishment is reserved for crimes that undermine faith, family, or the social order. Death by the court is decreed for idolatry, incest, murder, and breaking the Sabbath — the last because "the keeping of Sabbath is a confirmation of our belief in the Creation" (Ch. XLI). A rebellious elder is put to death because allowing any individual scholar to override the Great Synhedrion would render the law's stability purposeless (Ch. XLI).

**Class Seven: Civil Laws** (Ch. XLII). These govern commercial equity: no overcharge, rules for four types of bailees, wages paid promptly, inheritance laws preserving family bonds. The Edomites and Egyptians are treated mercifully in the inheritance and kinship statutes: "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite, for he is thy brother... Thou shalt not abhor an Egyptian, because thou wast a stranger in his land" (Deut. 23:7, cited Ch. XLII).

**Class Eight: Festival Laws** (Ch. XLIII). The Sabbath confirms belief in Creation and provides rest. The Day of Atonement instills repentance. The Feast of Weeks ("anniversary of the Revelation on Mount Sinai") is kept one day because the revelation was one day; the counting of the Omer between Passover and Shavuot is compared to "one who expects his most intimate friend on a certain day [and] counts the days and even the hours" (Ch. XLIII). The four species of Sukkot symbolize Israel's exchange of the barren wilderness for a fertile land.

**Class Nine: Prayer and Devotional Acts** (Ch. XLIV). Prayer, Shema, tefillin, mezuzah, tzitzit, Torah reading — all "serve to remind us continually of God, and of our duty to fear and to love Him" (Ch. XLIV).

**Class Ten: Temple Laws** (Ch. XLV). The Temple's site, vessels, and ministries are designed to instill reverence. The Temple was placed in the West specifically in opposition to universal sun-worship: "I believe that he [Abraham] did so because it was then a general rite to worship the sun as a deity. Undoubtedly all people turned then to the East. Abraham turned therefore on Mount Moriah to the West... and turned his back toward the sun" (Ch. XLV). The two cherubim over the ark teach belief in angels (against Dualism: one figure would have been mistaken for God's image; two with the declaration "the Lord is One" establishes a plurality of angels under one God) (Ch. XLV). The incense was burned twice daily to counteract the smell of the slaughterhouse: "There is a well-known saying of our Sages, 'In Jericho they could smell the incense'" (Ch. XLV).

**Class Eleven: Sacrificial Laws** (Ch. XLVI). This class is centrally explained by the **anti-Sabian logic**. Scripture reports that the Egyptians worshipped Aries and held shepherds in contempt; therefore "the very act which is considered by the heathen as the greatest crime, is the means of approaching God" (Ch. XLVI). Sacrifices use sheep, goats, and cattle — species the idolaters venerated and refused to slaughter — in order to "eradicate these false principles." The Passover lamb was killed and its blood sprinkled publicly "in order to free ourselves of evil doctrines and to proclaim the opposite" (Ch. XLVI). Leaven and honey were forbidden from offerings because idolaters used them; salt was required because the Sabians never used it in sacrifices (Ch. XLVI). The prohibition against eating blood opposes the Sabian practice of gathering around blood to commune with spirits: "The Law, which is perfect in the eyes of those who know it, and seeks to cure mankind of these lasting diseases, forbade the eating of blood, and emphasized the prohibition exactly in the same terms as it emphasizes idolatry" (Ch. XLVI). The goat sent to Azazel on the Day of Atonement is "a symbolic character, and serves to impress men with a certain idea, and to induce them to repent; as if to say, we have freed ourselves of our previous deeds, have cast them behind our backs, and removed them from us as far as possible" (Ch. XLVI). The sin-offering for the New Moon is uniquely called "a sin-offering *unto the Lord*" to prevent confusion with Egyptian lunar sacrifice (Ch. XLVI).

**Class Twelve: Purity Laws** (Ch. XLVII). The laws of cleanness and uncleanness apply *only* with respect to the Sanctuary and holy objects — not to ordinary life. This is a deliberate lightening of the Sabian burden: "the practice of the Sabeans, even at present general in the East, among the few still left of the Magi, was to keep a menstruous woman in a house by herself, to burn that upon which she treads, and to consider as unclean every one that speaks with her" (Ch. XLVII). Leprosy is interpreted as the traditional punishment for slander (Ch. XLVII). Maimonides acknowledges he cannot find reasons for cedar-wood, hyssop, and scarlet in the purification rites (Ch. XLVII).

**Class Thirteen: Dietary Laws** (Ch. XLVIII). Forbidden foods are primarily unwholesome: "I maintain that the food which is forbidden by the Law is unwholesome" (Ch. XLVIII). Pork is forbidden because of excessive moisture and the dirt of the animal: "if it were allowed to eat swine's flesh, the streets and houses would be more dirty than any cesspool, as may be seen at present in the country of the Franks" (Ch. XLVIII). Meat boiled in milk is "undoubtedly gross food... but I think that most probably it is also prohibited because it is somehow connected with idolatry" (Ch. XLVIII) — though Maimonides notes he has not found this custom specifically described in the Sabian books. Slaughter (*shehitah*) must be easy and swift: "the Law enjoins that the death of the animal should be the easiest" (Ch. XLVIII). The prohibition against killing a mother and her young on the same day, and sending away the mother bird, are grounded in animal psychology: "there is no difference in this case between the pain of man and the pain of other living beings, since the love and tenderness of the mother for her young ones is not produced by reasoning, but by imagination" (Ch. XLVIII).

**Class Fourteen: Sexual Laws, Circumcision** (Ch. XLIX). These commandments seek to limit and restrain sexual desire: "The law about forbidden sexual intercourse seeks in all its parts to inculcate the lesson that we ought to limit sexual intercourse altogether, hold it in contempt, and only desire it very rarely" (Ch. XLIX). Prostitution is banned to preserve family lineage and prevent strife. Marriage requires public ceremony and written divorce documents. **Circumcision** has two objects: (1) to weaken the organ of generation and moderate lust — "there is no doubt that circumcision weakens the power of sexual excitement" (Ch. XLIX); (2) to mark membership in the covenant of Abraham's monotheism: "every one that is circumcised enters the covenant of Abraham to believe in the unity of God" (Ch. XLIX).

## Sacrifices as Concession and Educational Strategy

Maimonides presents the institution of sacrifice not as God's intrinsic desire but as a divine educational concession to human habit. This is stated most clearly in Ch. XLVII, where he contrasts the burning of a pigeon or a handful of flour in Israelite worship with the burning of children practiced by heathen nations: "Let him consider the difference between a man burning his own son in serving his god, and our burning a pigeon to the service of our God" (Ch. XLVII). The Law met Israel where it was and gradually redirected existing religious instincts toward the service of the one God. (The fuller statement of this position appears in Ch. XXXII, referenced in Ch. XLVI.)

## The Limits of Maimonides' Own Knowledge

Maimonides is candid about the limits of his historical knowledge. He admits he cannot explain the table of showbread ("up to this day I have not been able to assign any reason to this commandment," Ch. XLV), the cedar-wood/hyssop/scarlet in purification rites (Ch. XLVII), or why wine is included in sacrificial offerings despite idolaters also using wine (Ch. XLVI). He attributes these gaps to the extinction of Sabian practice: "my knowledge of the Sabean doctrines, which I derived from books, is not as complete as the knowledge of those who have witnessed the public practice of those idolatrous customs, especially as they have been out of practice and entirely extinct since two thousand years" (Ch. XLIX).

## Hermeneutical Method

The method is explicitly **historical-contextual rationalism**: each commandment is explained by reconstructing the religious environment (primarily Sabian idolatry) against which it was directed. This is combined with a **therapeutic** hermeneutic — the commandments as medicine for "the diseases of the human soul" caused by false doctrine. Maimonides cites two main source-texts for Sabian practice: a book called *Tomtom* (also *Tintom*) and the *Nabatean Agriculture* (mentioned in Ch. XLI, XLVI).

This approach stands in tension with the view (also recorded as existing) that commandments are nobler when their reasons are unknown. Maimonides explicitly opposes this view without naming its proponents; the controversy is one of the major disputed questions in medieval Jewish philosophy.
