# Network Conversion (Lofland-Stark Model)

The **Lofland-Stark model of conversion** is an empirically grounded theory of religious conversion developed by John Lofland and Rodney Stark from fieldwork on the first American Unification Church ("Moonies") converts in the early 1960s. The model was first published in *American Sociological Review* (1965) as "Becoming a World-Saver: A Theory of Conversion to a Deviant Perspective." It is one of the most widely cited conversion theories in the sociology of religion.

Stark applies the model directly to early Christian growth in *The Rise of Christianity*, Ch. 1.

## The Core Claim

**Social network ties, not ideological persuasion, drive religious conversion.**

People convert to new religions primarily because:
1. They have pre-existing personal relationships (friendship, kinship, romance, business ties) with members of the new religion
2. Those ties become stronger than their ties to non-members
3. The ideological content of the new religion becomes plausible within the context of those relationships

Conversion is **not** primarily:
- A response to compelling preaching
- The result of intellectual inquiry into doctrinal claims
- A rational calculation that the new religion's beliefs are more correct

These factors may be present, but they are secondary. What predicts conversion is the social network, not the sermon.

## The Fieldwork Origins

Lofland and Stark studied the first Americans to join what became the Unification Church (Sun Myung Moon's movement) as it established itself in the San Francisco Bay Area in the early 1960s. The converts were not obviously moved by the ideology — Moon's theology was, to outsiders, bizarre and implausible. What distinguished those who converted from those who investigated and left was the degree to which their social ties shifted toward the community:

- Those who converted formed close friendships with existing members before, during, or shortly after their doctrinal investigation
- Those who did not convert typically maintained stronger social ties outside the group, making defection socially costless
- Ideology served to *rationalize* and *interpret* a conversion that was primarily driven by social attachment

**The "world-saver" pattern**: Converts became actively committed to spreading the movement because they experienced the community as so valuable that they wanted others to share it. The social goods of belonging, meaning, and affective bonds were what they were actually proselytizing, even if their verbal content focused on doctrinal claims.

## The Seven Steps of the Model

The original Lofland-Stark model was a sequential stages model (though Stark later simplified it). The steps:

1. **Tension**: Pre-existing felt discrepancy between the seeker's ideal and actual life conditions — not necessarily crisis, but a persistent low-level dissatisfaction or searching
2. **Religious seekership**: General openness to religious solutions to personal problems
3. **Turning point**: A significant life disruption — job loss, move, relationship break — that makes the person particularly open to new attachments
4. **Affective bonds**: Formation of warm personal relationships with existing members
5. **Weakening of outside attachments**: Existing ties to family, friends, and institutions outside the group weaken, reducing the social cost of conversion
6. **Intensive interaction**: Deep involvement with the community through shared meals, study, worship, and communal life
7. **Deployment as missionary**: Full conversion completed when the convert begins actively recruiting others

**The network criterion is decisive at step 4–5**: People who go through steps 1–3 but do not form strong affective bonds with members rarely convert, regardless of ideological exposure.

## Application to Early Christianity

Stark (*Rise of Christianity*, Ch. 1) draws four implications from the Lofland-Stark model for understanding early Christian growth:

### 1. Christianity Must Have Maintained Open Social Networks

If conversion follows social ties, then Christianity grew by **remaining socially connected to non-Christians**. A sectarian community that sealed itself off from the surrounding society would have no conversion potential, however internally committed. The fact that Christianity grew at a sustained 40%/decade means it must have had constant social porosity — members who remained embedded in pagan households, workplaces, and neighborhoods.

This is confirmed by the epistolary evidence: Paul's letters address questions about maintaining relationships with pagan spouses (1 Cor 7), eating meat offered to idols in contexts of social obligation (1 Cor 8–10), and participating in civic social life. These are not abstract ethical questions — they are questions about maintaining the social ties through which conversion occurred.

### 2. The Role of Preaching Is Overrated

Traditional accounts of Christian growth privilege preaching, revival, and dramatic conversion experiences. The Lofland-Stark model suggests these are usually rationalizations of socially-driven changes rather than primary causes. Paul's success in Corinth, Thessalonica, and Philippi is better understood as movement along pre-existing social networks (Jewish diaspora communities, household connections, artisan networks) than as the result of his oratorical skill.

### 3. Household Conversion as the Structural Norm

Acts repeatedly records **household conversions**: Cornelius's household (Acts 10), Lydia's household (Acts 16:14-15), the jailer's household (Acts 16:31-34), Cornelius's relatives and friends (Acts 10:24). This pattern — an anchor person converts and their entire social network follows — is exactly what the Lofland-Stark model predicts. The household was the primary social network unit in the ancient world; conversion at the household level was structurally inevitable once the anchor person's ties to Christians exceeded their ties to outsiders.

### 4. Slow Growth Among the Socially Isolated

The Lofland-Stark model predicts that conversion is hardest to achieve among people with strong, dense social networks entirely outside the movement. In the Roman world, the most socially isolated individuals — newly arrived migrants, freed slaves, the homeless poor — were paradoxically both the most "available" and the least likely targets for network conversion, because they lacked the existing ties that would form the bridges. Urban Christianity's success among the socially marginal was partly a function of providing a substitute social network for people who had none.

## Later Developments and Revisions

**Stark's self-revision**: In later work, Stark acknowledged that the original 7-step sequential model was too rigid. Conversion does not always follow the steps in order. The core insight — that social ties are the primary driver — survived subsequent research; the specific stage sequence did not prove universal.

**Criticisms and alternatives**:
- **Cognitive models**: Some researchers argue that belief change and meaning-making are more autonomous than the network model allows; people can convert intellectually before social ties shift
- **Cultural context**: The model was developed on American new religious movements; how well it transfers to pre-modern societies where social ties were far denser and more constrictive is disputed
- **Coercive conversion**: The model is designed for voluntary conversion in pluralistic societies; it does not address forced conversion, conversion under social pressure, or conversion as ethnic/political identity performance

**Later Stark applications**: In *Cities of God* (2006), Stark extends the quantitative analysis from *Rise of Christianity* Ch. 6 using Paul's letters, showing that Paul consistently moved through cities with existing Jewish diaspora communities (pre-existing social networks) rather than moving into cities without Jewish populations. This is the Lofland-Stark model at the macro-level: even the missionary strategy of early Christianity followed network lines.

## Significance for Understanding Early Christianity

The network-conversion model is one of several tools Stark uses to argue that early Christian growth requires no special explanation beyond normal social processes. If:
- Conversion follows social ties
- Christianity maintained open networks
- Christianity provided unusually valuable social goods (community, nursing, charity, belonging)

Then a 40%/decade growth rate over 300 years is unsurprising. The model does not explain *why* people valued Christian community so highly (that is Stark's argument in Ch. 7 and Ch. 10 — urban chaos and the love ethic), but it explains *how* that value translated into documented growth.
