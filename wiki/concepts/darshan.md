# Darshan

**Darshan** (Sanskrit *darśana*, "seeing, sight, audience"; from *dṛś*, "to see") is the central
act of Hindu temple worship — the mutual **exchange of sight** between the devotee and the deity.
It is not passive viewing; it is a charged, bidirectional act:

> "To 'have darshan' of the deity or the king or a great person is not merely to see but to be
> in the very presence of the divine, and not merely to be present but to be seen by the divine —
> the exchange of glances is the ritual act itself."

Doniger uses the temple encounter to illustrate that "this is also a form of bhakti." [[bhakti|Bhakti]]
is not only emotional devotion expressed verbally or through music; it is enacted corporeally
through the look.

## Structure of the Temple Encounter

The Hindu temple is **architecturally modeled on a royal palace** — not on a congregational assembly
space (as in a mosque, church, or synagogue). The deity is a **king** receiving courtiers; the worshipper
is a supplicant granted audience.

**Sequence of temple worship (*pūjā*):**
1. Approaching the temple precinct (leaving profane space)
2. Entering through the gateway tower (*gopura*) — a liminal threshold
3. Circling the temple (*pradakṣiṇā*) clockwise, keeping the shrine on one's right
4. Entering the dark inner chamber (*garbhagṛha*, "womb-house")
5. **Darshan of the deity** — standing before the image in the darkness; the deity's eyes (in
   South Indian images, typically large and emphasized) look back at the worshipper; the exchange
   is the ritual's culmination
6. Receiving *prasāda* — food or water that has been "seen by" the deity (leftover from the
   exchange, now consecrated)

**The asymmetry of power in the gaze:** The deity's eyes are permanent, omnipresent, and sculpted
to maximize the exchange. In the installation ceremony (*pratiṣṭhā*), the image's eyes are the
*last thing* ceremonially "opened" (in some traditions by a goldsmith touching them with a gold
needle while the priest averts his gaze — the deity's first look is too powerful for the priest).
The devotee is "seen" by a power larger than themselves.

## Darshan and Bhakti

In South Indian temple bhakti (c. 6th–9th c. CE Nayanmars and Alvars; see [[bhakti|Bhakti]]),
darshan is integrated into the bhakti framework:

- The **Alvars** (Vaishnava poet-saints) compose devotional poetry that is often structured as a
  description of seeing the deity — a verbal darshan. The poem itself becomes an act of seeing
  and being seen.
- The **Nayanmars** (Shaiva poet-saints) similarly orient toward the image in the *sthala* (place)
  — each of the 276 Shaiva pilgrimage sites is a specific locale where Shiva's specific form
  is "seen"
- **Kannappar** (the Pariah hunter-saint; see [[bhakti|Bhakti]]) offers his own *eyes* to Shiva —
  taking the logic of darshan to its extreme: if eyes are the medium of exchange, the gift of
  the eyes themselves is the ultimate offering

## Darshan and Kingship

The **royal analogy** for darshan is not incidental; it is structural. In the Gupta period and
South Indian medieval polity:

- The **king performs darshan** for his subjects — public audience (*darbar*) is an act of grace,
  like a deity's grace
- The **deity performs darshan** for the king first — the king is the deity's chief devotee, and
  the deity's temple is the source of royal legitimacy
- **Temple donations** are an extension of this logic: by donating to the deity's palace
  (the temple), the king participates in the exchange of glance-and-gift

The convergence of royal and devotional darshan is why Doniger calls the bhakti temple system a
"theology of divine monarchy."

## Darshan in Non-Temple Contexts

Darshan extends beyond the temple:

- **Darshan of a guru or holy person**: going to be in the presence of, and seen by, a saint is
  itself meritorious — the same logic as temple darshan
- **Pilgrimage** (*tīrtha*): going to a holy site is framed as "having darshan" of the deity
  who resides there; the site is the deity's body
- **Darshan of a corpse** (less common): in some bhakti traditions, seeing a saint's body after
  death retains the grace-transferring quality of the living person's gaze
