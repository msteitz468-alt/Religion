## Overview

"God's knowledge of particulars" names the disputed question of whether the divine
intellect knows individual, temporally changing things — this specific eclipse, this
specific person, this specific act of obedience or disobedience — as opposed to knowing
only universal or abstract forms. In Islamic philosophical theology the question became
a flash-point between [[falsafa|the philosophers]] (especially [[avicenna|Avicenna]])
and [[kalam|kalām]] theologians. [[al-ghazali|Al-Ghazālī]] devotes Discussions 11–16
of the [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]] to dismantling the
philosophical account and is so serious about its implications that it constitutes the
**second of his three charges of unbelief (takfīr)** against the philosophers — a
judgment reserved for doctrines he regards as incompatible with Islam at the level of
*kufr*.

---

## The Avicennan Thesis: Universal Knowledge Only

Avicenna and the *falāsifa* generally held that God knows temporal particulars "only
in a universal way" (*Discussion 13*, §1):

> "He knows things through a universal knowledge which does not enter time and which
> does not change in terms of the past, the future, and the present." (Disc. 13, §1)

The argument runs as follows. Temporal particulars — a solar eclipse as it is
*happening now*, or the individual Zayd as *this* person — require a knowing act that
tracks temporal change: before the eclipse one knows "it will be," during it "it is,"
after it "it was." But these three cognitions are different and successive; for them to
succeed one another in the knower implies change in the knower's essence. Change in
God is impossible. Therefore God cannot have the kind of knowledge that tracks temporal
particulars as particulars.

What God *does* know, on this view, is the universal causal structure of the world —
the paths of the heavens, the intersection of lunar nodes, the rules governing all
eclipses — such that "nothing escapes His knowledge," but His knowledge is atemporal
and unchanging. Applied to persons: God knows "man in the absolute sense" and all of
man's necessary attributes, but does not know the individual Zayd as *this* person,
because individuation is established through sense-reference ("this," "here," "that
direction"), which requires spatial and temporal indexicality impossible for God (Disc.
13, §6).

## Ghazālī's Objections

### 1. The doctrine empties divine providence and contradicts the Qurʾān

Ghazālī's primary objection is practical-theological: the Avicennan thesis "uprooted
religious laws in their entirety" (Disc. 13, §7). If God knows only universal man and
not the individual Zayd:

> "God would not know what of his states has newly come about, because He does not know
> Zayd in his particularity…it follows necessarily that one would have to say that when
> Muḥammad, God's prayers and peace be upon him, challenged [the heathen] with his
> prophethood, [God] did not know then that he made the challenge." (Disc. 13, §7)

The Qurʾān's language of reward and punishment, prophecy, revelation, and divine
oversight of human acts — all presupposes that God knows particular individuals and
particular events. The philosophical doctrine makes this incoherent.

### 2. The philosophers' logic is internally inconsistent

Ghazālī turns the philosophers' own premise against them (Disc. 13, §16–17). If
the relation of knowledge to its object is so essential that knowing different temporal
states of *one* thing would force multiplicity in God's essence, then knowing multiple
*universal* forms — animal, man, inanimate substance, whiteness, blackness — would also
force multiplicity, since these are generically different and no knowledge of one
substitutes for knowledge of another. Avicenna's solution (unified universal knowledge
does not multiply the divine essence) proves too much: if it works for universals, it
can work for particulars too; if it fails for particulars, it should fail for universals.

> "By my word, how does the rational person allow himself to deem impossible the
> unifying of knowledge of one thing whose states divide into the past, present, and
> future but not allow as impossible the unification of knowledge connected with all
> the different genera and species, when the differences and remoteness between the
> various remote genera and species are far greater than the differences that occur
> between the states of the one thing that divide in terms of time?" (Disc. 13, §17)

### 3. The alternative: eternal knowledge as a pure relation

Ghazālī proposes (without dogmatically asserting) that God could possess *one*
permanent knowledge whose relation to the object changes as the object changes — just
as an observer's spatial relation to a moving person changes without the observer
undergoing intrinsic change (Disc. 13, §14). The succession is in the *relation*, not
in the divine essence. He illustrates this with a thought-experiment: a knowledge of
Zayd's arrival created once and perpetuated unchanged in us would serve as knowledge
that "he will arrive," then "he is arriving," then "he has arrived" — without any
change in the knowledge-act itself (Disc. 13, §14).

### 4. Challenging the impossibility of divine change

Ghazālī also presses a second-order critique (Disc. 13, §18–29): the philosophers
cannot coherently rule out the possibility that God undergoes temporal change in
His cognitions, since on their own principles (the world is eternal; temporal events
proceed from an eternal celestial soul) an eternal entity *already* generates temporal
events. If the eternal can be the source of the temporal without being changed, the same
logic might allow temporal cognitions in God without destroying His eternal character —
or alternatively, if the philosophers admit that even God is "compelled" by His nature
to emanate, a compulsion to receive knowledge from temporal events is no worse.

## The Celestial Souls and Particulars (Discussions 14–16)

Discussions 14–16 present the philosophers' *alternative* locus for knowledge of
particulars: the **souls of the heavens**. This is the cosmological machinery that
makes the denial of God's direct knowledge of particulars less alarming within the
philosophical system.

### Heaven as an animate being (Discussion 14)

The philosophers held that the heavens are animate — each sphere has a soul related to
its body as human souls are to human bodies — and that the heavenly motion is
*voluntary*, not merely mechanical:

> "Heaven is an animal, and it has a soul whose relation to the body of heaven is
> similar to the relation of our souls to our bodies." (Disc. 14, §2)

Ghazālī's response is twofold: (a) he grants the *possibility* — "God is capable of
creating life in every body" — but denies that the philosophers have demonstrated it
by rational proof; knowledge of such things belongs to prophets by revelation, not to
philosophers by syllogism (Disc. 14, §3–4). He then offers three alternative
hypotheses (compulsion from an external body; compulsion directly from God's will;
an unconscious intrinsic natural quality) to show that the "voluntary motion"
conclusion is not rationally forced (Disc. 14, §9–12).

### The purpose moving the heavens (Discussion 15)

The philosophers claim the heavens move voluntarily in order to *imitate* God and draw
near to Him by successively occupying every possible spatial position — a perfection
achievable "as a species" since no one position can encompass all positions at once
(Disc. 15, §3). Ghazālī objects:

1. This reduces to absurdity: a human who wanders around town to "be in every possible
   place" and calls this worship is rightly deemed foolish (Disc. 15, §5).
2. The directional arbitrariness is unexplained: if the purpose is attainable by
   east-to-west motion, why does the outer sphere move east-to-west while inner spheres
   differ? The philosophers cannot explain the specific direction, only the general
   principle of motion (Disc. 15, §6–11).
3. The "secrets of the heavenly kingdom are not known with the likes of these imaginings.
   God makes them known only to his prophets and saints by way of inspiration, not by
   way of inferential proof." (Disc. 15, §7–8)

### Celestial souls knowing particulars and "the preserved tablet" (Discussion 16)

The climactic philosophical claim: the celestial souls know *all* terrestrial
particulars because (a) each particular motion of a sphere requires a particular
volitional representation; (b) knowing particular motions entails knowing all their
causal consequences down the chain to terrestrial events; (c) the entire causal chain
from the First Cause to the last terrestrial event is thus laid open to celestial
cognition. This is what the philosophers identify with the Qurʾānic "preserved tablet"
(lawḥ maḥfūẓ, Q. 85:22) — not a physical inscribed slab but the celestial souls
that retain all particular forms (Disc. 16, §1–7).

The philosophers then extend this to account for prophecy and dream-divination: both
involve the human soul making contact with the preserved tablet of celestial cognition.
In sleep, preoccupation with sensory appetite diminishes, allowing partial contact; for
prophets, the imaginative and rational faculties are strong enough to achieve contact
while awake (Disc. 16, §6–7).

Ghazālī's refutation targets three premises of this argument (Disc. 16, §11–17):

1. **The voluntariness of heavenly motion** — already refuted in Discussion 14.
2. **The need for particular representations** — a universal will directed at a
   single continuous circular motion suffices; no series of renewed particular
   representations is necessary, unlike a pilgrim choosing many successive particular
   directions to Mecca (Disc. 16, §13–15).
3. **From particular motions to all causal consequences** — this is "pure insanity":
   a human who walks does not thereby know every consequence of his shadow, the
   pressure on the ground under his foot, the change in his internal humors (Disc.
   16, §16).

He then poses a decisive dilemma: either the celestial soul's knowledge of particulars
is restricted to *present* ones (destroying prophecy and dream knowledge, which concern
the future) or it extends to *infinite* future particulars — and it is impossible that
infinite discrete particular cognitions should combine in one created soul simultaneously
without succession (Disc. 16, §17). This impossibility, he grants, does not apply to
God's knowledge — "the attachment of God's knowledge to His objects of knowledge through
correspondence is not in the same manner as the attachment of the cognitions belonging
to creatures" — but it exposes the philosophers' appeal to the celestial soul as an
inadequate substitute.

Against the philosophers' Qurʾānic reading of "the preserved tablet," Ghazālī asserts
that the jurists and believers who know religious law "do not understand by 'the tablet'
and 'the pen' the meaning [you have given to these terms] at all" (Disc. 16, §10). The
alternative is simpler: the prophet knows the hidden through God's direct apprising,
without any celestial intermediary.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ghazālī operates here by *burhān* (demonstrative proof) and dialectical reductio, not
by scripture-citation. His primary move in Discussions 11–13 is to accept the
philosophers' premises and demonstrate that those premises generate internal
contradictions (especially the parallel between temporal and generic multiplicity in
knowledge). He then supplements with Qurʾānic arguments about divine omniscience and
prophetic knowledge only as secondary, ad hominem pressure on the philosophers'
implicit Qurʾānic allegiances.

The **Ẓāhir/Bāṭin** dimension appears in Discussion 16 (§10): the philosophers treat
"the preserved tablet" and "the pen" (Q. 96:4; 85:22) as esoteric (*bāṭin*) symbols
for the celestial souls and the pure intelligences respectively. Ghazālī refuses this
reinterpretation: the *ẓāhir* (plain sense) received by the community of scholars is a
real inscribed tablet and a real pen, and the philosophers have no rational proof that
forces the allegorical reading. This is consonant with his broader insistence (against
the Bāṭiniyya as well as the philosophers) that esoteric re-readings of Qurʾānic
language require prophetic warrant, not philosophical inference.

## Relation to the Three Charges of Unbelief (Takfīr)

Ghazālī's three charges of *takfīr* in the *Tahāfut* are directed at:
1. The eternity of the world ([[eternity-of-the-world]])
2. **God's knowledge of particulars** (this concept — Disc. 13)
3. The denial of bodily resurrection ([[bodily-resurrection]])

The knowledge-of-particulars charge is the most philosophically intricate of the three.
Ghazālī regards it as unbelief because denying God's knowledge of Muḥammad's individual
prophetic act, or of individual human obedience and disobedience, directly undermines
the scriptural foundations of revelation, prophecy, and divine judgment.

## Averroes's Reply (Tahāfut al-Tahāfut, Disc. 11–13)

This is the **second of the three *[[takfir]]* charges**. In Discussions 11–13,
**[[averroes|Averroes]]** rejects both Ghazālī's objection and Avicenna's formulation:

- **Knowing universals ≠ knowing particulars — the categories are equivocal.** Knowledge of
  individuals is sensation/imagination (passive, multiplied and changed by its objects); knowledge of
  universals is intellect (invariable). Ghazālī's *tu quoque* — that one simple divine knowledge
  already spans many distinct genera, so why not temporal states? — **equivocates on "knowledge."**
- **Divine knowledge is neither universal nor individual.** "The most competent philosophers do not
  call God's knowledge of existents either universal or individual," because both belong to the
  passive human intellect. God's knowledge is **pure act and the cause** of existents — "it is His
  knowledge which produces the existents, and it is not the existents which produce His knowledge" —
  so the eclipse-style "change in the knower" objection simply does not apply.
- **Against making it a public dogma.** The deepest formulation — that "God is the totality of
  existents," a truth Averroes says the steadfast philosophers and the Sufi masters know — "must not
  be written down and it must not be made an obligation of faith." See
  [[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy]].

Averroes thus salvages divine omniscience without Avicenna's "universal way" (which Ghazālī rightly
saw as denying God knowledge of Muḥammad's individual act) — by denying that human "universal vs.
particular" applies to the First at all. He also charges Ghazālī with presenting philosophers'
conclusions as if they were self-evident first principles, "confusing the sciences in a most terrible
way."
