# Creation Ex Nihilo

The doctrine that God brought the world into being **out of nothing** (*ex nihilo*) — from no
pre-existing material substrate — as opposed both to fashioning it from co-eternal matter (Plato)
and to its being a necessary, beginningless emanation (Aristotle). The three Abrahamic traditions
each defend it; this page gathers their treatments.

## Genesis 1:1 — Hamilton’s absolute-clause reading

Attributed to [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]], *The Book of Genesis: Chapters 1–17* (NICOT); source page [[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot]]. Densified from reingest digests 2026-07-18.

Hamilton’s own translation of 1:1–2 opens: “In the beginning God created the universe. And the earth—it was a desert and a wasteland…” (raw L2273–2277). He treats Gen 1:1 as among Scripture’s best-known and most contested verses: absolute independent clause vs dependent temporal clause, and the relation of v. 1 to v. 2 and the rest of the chapter (raw L2285–2287 / Gen 1:1).

### Four options; stakes of the choice

Four translation options for *berēʾšîṯ*: (1) absolute, independent clause; (2) indeterminate relative temporal “Initially”; (3) construct subordinated to v. 2; (4) construct subordinated to v. 3 with v. 2 parenthetical. Options (1) and (4) have widest scholarly support; “A knowledge of the Hebrew language will not be sufficient in itself to settle the matter” (raw L2289–2291 / Gen 1:1).

The absolute vs temporal issue is not “esoteric quibbling”:

> “Does Gen. 1:1 teach an absolute beginning of creation as a direct act of God? Or does it affirm the existence of matter before creation of the heavens and the earth? … in the beginning there was one—God; or … two—God and preexistent chaos? … the concept of the creation of chaos would be a contradiction in terms.” (raw L2327 / Gen 1:1)

Hamilton therefore prefers the traditional absolute reading as teaching an **absolute beginning** as a direct act of God — one God, not God + co-eternal chaos/matter. That is the load-bearing exegetical move from this commentary for *creatio ex nihilo* discourse (without claiming that the later technical Latin doctrine is itself stated in Genesis).

### Arguments he advances for the absolute reading

- Against pure-theology shortcuts: Westermann and others choose the traditional translation from wider-chapter theology (repudiation of Enuma elish–type cosmogony); Childs: grammar allows both but “theology of P excludes” preexistent-chaos. Hamilton: theology alone is insufficient — lexical, grammatical, syntactical, comparative, and stylistic arguments also support “In the beginning” (raw L2329–2333 / Gen 1:1).
- Lexical: Isa 46:9–10 uses *rēʾšîṯ* absolutely for God’s disposition over beginning and end as historical time as such; only Gen 1:1 has the noun followed by a finite perfect verb — unlike 2:4b’s infinitive-construct relative “when” (raw L2335–2337 / Gen 1:1; 2:4b).
- Article absence not fatal; all ancient versions translate absolute independent clause; Masoretes accented with disjunctive *ṭiphâ* (absolute), not conjunctive (construct) (raw L2339 / Gen 1:1).
- Comparative: Enuma elish 1–9 is more like Gen 2:4bff. than Gen 1:1–3; if any parallel, 1:2 // EE 1–8 and 1:3 // EE 9 — **Gen 1:1 is unique**. Gunkel: other cosmogonies have “no word which would come close to the first word of the Bible” (raw L2341 / Gen 1:1–3).
- Structural: v. 1 = opening superscription and summary (functional analogue of the *tôlĕdôt* introductory sentence for later Genesis divisions); v. 2 = situation prior to detailed creation in vv. 3ff.; generalization → particularization (raw L2415–2419 / Gen 1:1–3; cf. [[toledot|toledot]]).

Hamilton thus anchors an absolute beginning in Gen 1:1 as direct divine act, rejecting readings that leave preexistent matter/chaos beside God. Later philosophical formulations (Aquinas, Maimonides, kalām below) are recorded as their own traditions’ articulations, not back-read into the NICOT exegesis.

## Christianity — Aquinas

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats creation at [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q44–46.
God is the **first cause** of all beings by the participation argument: every being has its being
by participation, while God alone is self-subsisting existence (*ipsum esse* — see
[[divine-simplicity]]), so all else must derive its being from Him (Q44, A1); even **prime matter
is created**, not a co-principle (Q44, A2). God is also the exemplar cause (the ideas in the divine
mind, not subsisting Platonic forms) and the final cause, creating to communicate His goodness
(Q44, A3–4).

**Creation** is "the emanation of all being from the universal cause" (Q45, A1); the *ex nihilo*
means "not *from* anything" — the "from" signifies order, not a material out of which. Creation is
**not properly a change** (a change needs a common subject); it is a real **relation** in the
creature to God as the principle of its being, while on God's side it is only a relation of reason
(Q45, A3). To create belongs to **God alone**, since producing being absolutely (not merely *this*
or *that* being) requires infinite power; Aquinas therefore rejects [[avicenna|Avicenna]]'s
cascade of mediated creation through the Intelligences and Peter Lombard's "ministerial" creation
(Q45, A5). Creation is common to the whole Trinity but **appropriated** (power→Father, wisdom→Son,
goodness→Spirit), and every creature bears a "trace" of the Trinity (Q45, A6–7; see [[trinity]]).

On **whether the world is eternal**, Aquinas takes a position distinct from both his Islamic and
some of his Christian contemporaries: that the world *began in time* is held by **faith** and
**cannot be demonstrated by reason** — see [[eternity-of-the-world]].

### The work of the six days — Aquinas's Genesis hermeneutic (Q65–74)

In the Treatise on the Work of the Six Days (Q65–74), Aquinas's most striking feature is his
**hermeneutical restraint**. On nearly every question he sets two patristic readings side by side
without forcing a choice: **Augustine** (*De Genesi ad litteram*), for whom creation was
**simultaneous** and the "days" denote orders of nature or of angelic knowledge (not time); and
**Basil, Ambrose, and Chrysostom**, for whom the days were **successive in time** (Q66, A1; Q74, A2).
He states a **two-rule method** (Q68, A1): hold the truth of Scripture firmly, but do not bind it so
tightly to one contested scientific gloss that the gloss's falsification would discredit Scripture.
He notes that Moses used **language accommodated to the unlearned** (Q67, A4; Q68, A3; Q70, A1), reads
"light" and "firmament" flexibly (literal vs. metaphorical; starry heaven vs. atmosphere), and
accommodates the Ptolemaic cosmology of his day — a model of the Thomist subordination of the spiritual
senses to a literal sense that is itself held with humility (cf. [[fourfold-sense]]).

## Islam — Creation in Time vs. an Eternal World

In Islamic [[kalam|theology]], creation *ex nihilo* and the world's temporal origination
(*huduth*) are defended against the [[falsafa|philosophers']] doctrine of an eternal,
necessarily-emanated cosmos. This is the subject of the First Discussion of
[[al-ghazali|al-Ghazali]]'s [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|*Tahafut al-Falasifa*]] — see
[[eternity-of-the-world|the eternity of the world]] and the controversy page.

For **[[averroes|Averroes]]** (in the *[[incoherence-of-the-incoherence|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]]*, Disc.
2–3), strict creation from absolute non-being is incoherent: "an opposite cannot be changed into its
opposite," and "non-existence itself cannot become existence." The agent instead converts **potency
into actuality** in an eternal substrate; "everything that comes into being comes into being from
something else," so prime matter (*hyle*) must be eternal. What scripture calls God's "making what
He wants out of nothing" he reads as the First Principle bestowing on each existent the
characteristic by which it becomes existent — not literal production from absolute non-being. See
[[necessary-existent]], [[emanation]].

## Judaism — Maimonides

[[maimonides|Maimonides]] calls *creatio ex nihilo* "a fundamental principle of the Law of our
teacher Moses … next in importance to the principle of God's unity" ([[guide-for-the-perplexed|
*Guide*]] II:13), attributing its first philosophical teaching to Abraham. His distinctive
argument is that **time itself is created** — "time depends on motion … and the things upon whose
motion time depends are themselves created beings" (II:13) — so one cannot posit a "before" the
world without smuggling in an eternal substrate. He holds creation to be undemonstrated but more
defensible than [[eternity-of-the-world|Aristotle's eternity]], and accepts it on prophetic
authority (II:16). See [[maimonides]].
