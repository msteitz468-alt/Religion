# Halakhah (and its Polarity with Agadah)

**Halakhah** is the body of Jewish law and practice — "the science of deeds." In
[[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]] (Part III),
[[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]] reads it not in isolation but as one pole of a structural
**polarity** with [[aggadah|agadah]] ("the art of being"): "Halacha without agada is dead,
agada without halacha is wild … Halacha is the string, agada is the bow." Halacha deals with
quantity, regularity (*keva*), the outer act; agadah with quality, spontaneity
([[kavanah]]), the inner life. The two are "like body and soul, mutually dependent."

## Polarity as Structural Principle

For Heschel, tension is constitutive, not a defect to be synthesized away: "this world is
called *alma deperuda*, 'the world of separation'" (Zohar), and "all tension ends in God."
The central practical problem is "how not to let the principle of regularity (keva) impair
the power of spontaneity (kavanah)." Strikingly, halakhah's own authority rests on agadah:
"the mystery of revelation belongs to the sphere of agada. Thus … its authority is derived
from agada."

## Two Symmetrical Errors

Heschel rejects two reductions in parallel:

- **Religious behaviorism / pan-halachism** — reducing Judaism to external compliance, "a
  sort of sacred physics," with no inner devotion or doctrine. He traces this to Spinoza
  (Torah as mere political legislation) and Mendelssohn ("freedom in doctrine and conformity
  in action"), and to the Septuagint's "fatal" rendering of *Torah* as *nomos* ("law"),
  whereas Aramaic *oraita* means "teaching." "One may be a scoundrel within the limits of
  the law"; Jerusalem fell because its people did "not act beyond the requirements of the
  law."
- **Spiritualism / antinomianism** — reducing Judaism to inwardness or faith alone
  ("spiritualism is a way for angels, not for man"); see the [[mitzvah]] page on the
  faith-vs-works debate.

Heschel also reframes the ground of obligation: against Kant's "I ought, therefore I can,"
Judaism says "Thou art commanded, therefore thou canst."
