# Sabbath

The sabbath is the seventh-day rest commanded in the Decalogue. In [[matthew|Matthew]] it is
the presenting issue of two linked conflict stories — the disciples plucking grain (12:1-8)
and the healing of the withered hand (12:9-14) — which stand at the head of the Galilean
controversies. This page records the treatment in [[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of
Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the wiki's only ingested source on the passage.

## The Sabbath as Boundary Marker

France states that the sabbath and circumcision were the most obvious distinguishing marks of
the Jews as the people of God — "the sabbath and circumcision being the most obvious
distinguishing marks of the Jews as the people of God" — so that sabbath observance was a
matter of national pride as well as of legal debate.

France reports that two lengthy Mishnah tractates, *Šabbat* and *ʿErubin*, govern sabbath
rulings, and that although they did not reach final form until the end of the second century
they are "agreed to represent in principle the sort of discussions and rulings in which
Pharisaic scribes were engaged in Jesus' day." The elaboration is organized around a list of
thirty-nine forbidden classes of work. See [[oral-torah]].

## France's Framing: Not Whether, but What

France's governing position is emphatic and needs stating before anything else: **the
controversy is not about whether the sabbath is to be kept.** He writes, "The controversy
centers not on whether the sabbath should be observed (there is no suggestion that Jesus
questioned that) but on what that observance entailed." On France's reading Jesus does not
break sabbath law and does not question sabbath observance.

France connects the conflicts backward to 11:28-30: Jesus' arguments "attempt to restore the
'rest' to what was in danger of becoming ... more a burden than a blessing," and 12:1-14 is
"a striking example" of "two contrasting ways of understanding the 'rest' which the sabbath
was meant to provide." (On 11:28-30 France holds the rest is eschatological but also present —
"it is for the present as well as for the future" — comparing Heb 4:1-11.)

France structures the disagreement along **two axes**:

1. **Authority** — who may declare what is forbidden (vv. 3-6, 8);
2. **Priorities** — getting behind the regulations to the law's original spirit and intention,
   "as in 5:21-48" (vv. 7, 11-12). See [[antitheses]].

## The Grainfields (12:1-8)

France notes that the Old Testament permitted the poor to hand-pick grain in another's field
(Deut 23:25; cf. Lev 23:22), "but that is not the issue here; it is specifically that of
sabbath law." The alleged violation, he explains, is that picking could count as "reaping" and
rubbing out the husks as "threshing," both on the list of thirty-nine; harvesting on the
sabbath is forbidden in the OT law itself (Exod 34:21).

France argues the disciples' **hunger cannot itself justify** overriding sabbath law — casual
pickings would not alleviate real starvation — and is mentioned only to set up the link to
[[david|David]]; Matthew, unlike Mark, omits "need" at v. 3. "Jesus will justify their action
not on the basis of need ... but of his own authority." The Pharisees address Jesus rather than
the disciples, France explains, because "it is understood that a teacher is responsible for his
disciples' behavior."

### The David analogy: authority, not precedent

This is France's most load-bearing exegetical move, and he makes it against a common reading.
What David infringed was the showbread regulation (Lev 24:9), not sabbath law: "It is David's
authority to override a legal prescription, not his attitude to the sabbath as such, which is
at issue." France then **rejects the precedent reading outright**: "There would be little force
in an argument which simply asserted that if the law has been broken once it can be broken
again." The argument is about *who* was exceptionally allowed to override, not *that* one may.

France further observes that 1 Sam 21:1-6 does not say David entered the house of God; by
having David himself enter, Jesus' account "makes his action even bolder than the OT original."
He infers an unstated fourth "something greater" — that Jesus places his own authority
alongside David's, so "something greater than David is here" is implied at v. 6 as well.

### The priests and the temple (12:5-6)

France describes the move from the David narrative to the priestly principle in rabbinic terms:
"in rabbinic terms, we move now from haggadah to halakhah." The ground of priestly exemption he
identifies as *office plus institution* — who they are (priests appointed for divine service)
and what requires it (the temple) — "the authority of the office and the necessity of the
service overriding the sabbath rules which for other people ... remain inviolable."

France stresses the shock value of "something greater than the temple is here": "It is hard to
overestimate the shock value of this pronouncement." See [[jerusalem]].

### Mercy, guiltlessness, and lordship (12:7-8)

France reads the Hos 6:6 citation as a prophetic priority principle already used at 9:13: "a
positive concern for the good of others ('mercy') takes precedence over formal compliance with
ritual regulations."

He notes that calling the disciples "guiltless" goes beyond what Jesus has argued and implies
the Pharisaic interpretation of sabbath law was itself wrong: "They had found guilt where God
saw none." The echo of "guiltless" from v. 5 "implicitly claims for Jesus' verdict the same
divine sanction as the law had provided for the priests."

On 12:8 France rejects the generic reading of "son of man" ("human beings are in control of the
sabbath"): after vv. 3-6 it "would be a strange non sequitur to claim a general human freedom
to dispense with sabbath law." He rates the saying as christologically even more daring than
vv. 3-6 — the sabbath is traced to God's direct command, enshrined in the Decalogue, and called
"my sabbath" by God, so to call an individual its "lord" is "the most extraordinary claim to an
authority on a par with that of God himself." See [[son-of-man]].

## The Withered Hand (12:9-14)

France notes that healing "was not in itself included among the thirty-nine forbidden acts" and
was hard to associate with them, especially as Jesus healed by word — but that Mishnaic
discussion assumes healing is disallowed and debates only the exceptions. He reports that
exceptions were normally allowed only where there was imminent danger of death (m. Yoma 8:6),
plus assistance in childbirth (m. Šabb. 18:3).

France observes that none of Jesus' sabbath healings is urgent, and that this test case is
deliberately clear: "a paralyzed arm, though very inconvenient, is not a threat to life," so
"the healing can safely be left until tomorrow."

On the animal-in-the-pit argument France stresses that "Jesus appeals not to a legal authority
but to the common sense which normal people would follow." He sets the Qumran ruling of CD
11:13-14 — "No one may help an animal to give birth on the sabbath; and if it falls into a well
or into a pit he may not lift it out on the sabbath" — against later rabbinic discussion (b.
Šabb. 128b), which canvasses throwing in articles or feeding the animal but concludes that
relief of animal suffering should override the regulation. France infers that the instinct
behind the later ruling, together with "the less altruistic desire to protect a valuable
asset!", "was evidently already prevalent in Jesus' time in common practice," so Jesus could
assume no objection.

France is candid about the double edge of "it is permissible to do good on the sabbath": its
lack of specificity contrasts with rabbinic thoroughness, "could result in widely divergent
practice," and "lends itself to use as a convenient self-justification for any chosen course of
action." What distinguishes it from rabbinic rulings and most OT laws is that "it is positive
rather than prohibitive," putting the onus on the individual — like Jesus' version of the
Golden Rule (7:12). See [[new-law]].

France classifies 12:9-13 form-critically as "a pronouncement story rather than a miracle
story"; the cure is brief and purely verbal, "so that no visible 'work' is involved."

## Consequences

France states that the combined effect of "The Son of Man is the Lord of the sabbath" (v. 8)
and "It is permissible to do good on the sabbath" (v. 12b) is "to call in question the whole
scribal industry of sabbath regulation" — no wonder, he adds, that they wanted to get rid of
him.

He nevertheless cautions against overreading 12:14: a group of local Galilean
[[pharisees|Pharisees]] is a very different constituency from the Jerusalem chief priests and elders;
though sabbath-breaking was in theory a capital matter (Exod 31:14-15; m. Sanh. 7:4), "it was
hardly realistic for these Pharisees to think of having Jesus officially executed in Galilee,"
and "get rid of" need not carry that sense. France reads Matthew's "their synagogue" (12:9) —
probably [[capernaum|Capernaum]]'s — as beginning to hint at the growing rift with the
synagogue establishment.

## Coverage and Gaps

The wiki's coverage of the sabbath rests on a single Christian commentary reading a Christian
gospel. Jewish halakhic treatment of the sabbath in its own voice, patristic and later
Christian sabbath/Lord's-Day theology, and Seventh-day traditions are all unrepresented.
