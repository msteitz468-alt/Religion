# The Death of Satan / Secularization of Evil

The **secularization of evil** is the modern cultural process — named "the death of Satan"
by [[andrew-delbanco|Andrew Delbanco]] (after Wallace Stevens) — by which Western, and
especially American, culture **lost a shared symbolic and moral language for evil** even as
evil itself remained an inescapable experience. The concept names not a decline in
wrongdoing but the **erosion of the category**: the personified Devil ceased to be credible,
"sin" and "evil" shrank into specialized or ironic usage, and moderns were left, in Susan
Sontag's words, "with a sense of evil but no longer the religious or philosophical language
to talk intelligently about evil."

This concept is distinct from, but adjacent to, classical [[theodicy|theodicy]]. Theodicy
asks: *given* that evil is real, how is God just? The secularization of evil asks the prior,
modern question: *do we still possess a concept of evil at all*, and what follows when we do
not?

## The Thesis (Delbanco)

In *The Death of Satan* (1995), Delbanco offers a "national spiritual biography" of America:

- **From a named enemy to inarticulate dread.** "For the 'infant' Americans three hundred
  and fifty years ago, evil had a name, a face, and an explanation. It was called the Fall,
  it was personified in the devil, and it was attributed to an original sin." That apparatus
  decayed; when "you discard the old words and symbols, you arrive at an unprecedented
  condition of inarticulate dread."
- **The "unnaming" of evil.** Successive vocabularies for the malign — the Christian *Satan*,
  Marxism's "exploitative classes," psychoanalysis's "repression" and "neurosis" — "have
  been discredited to one degree or another," and "nothing has come to take their place."
- **A declension in stages.** The Puritan "Age of Belief" (the Devil as a vivid daily
  presence) gives way through the Enlightenment "Age of Reason," the Romantic "birth of the
  self," the modern "Age of Blame," and finally the **"Culture of Irony,"** in which the
  very idea of evil comes to seem naïve.

## Key Components

- **The banality of evil.** Hannah Arendt's Eichmann — the orderly, "ordinary" bureaucrat —
  crystallized the modern fear that "the concept of evil might actually be incompatible with
  the very nature of modern life." Bureaucratic and corporate diffusion of responsibility
  ("following orders," "doing my job") makes the perpetrator vanish; evil threatens to become
  "an epistemological problem."
- **The age of blame.** When sin is no longer owned, evil is redistributed as grievance and
  victimhood — a "morally fraudulent" competition over "whose history is the richest in pain
  and injustice."
- **The culture of irony.** The ironic sensibility — first heard, in Delbanco's image, among
  the colonists who "snickered in the back pews" when no judgment followed a comet — dissolves
  the earnestness that the language of evil requires. The decisive modern cleavage is between
  **belief** and **irony.**
- **The persistence of metaphor.** Against the rationalist hope (and Sontag's call) to live
  "purified of metaphoric thinking," the concept insists on an ineradicable "deep
  metaphysical need" (Berlin): even secular, scientific description still divides the world
  into norms and violations, health and sickness, value and waste.

## Relation to Other Wiki Concepts

- **[[theodicy|Theodicy]]** — the classical problem this concept *succeeds*: from "is God
  just given evil?" to "have we kept the idea of evil?" The modern, post-Holocaust phase of
  the theodicy literature, Delbanco notes, now opens by *proving evil is real* — a symptom of
  the loss this concept names.
- **[[satan|Satan / the Devil]]** — the figure whose "death" gives the concept its name; the
  long premodern development is on the figure page.
- **[[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]]**, **[[demiurge|the Demiurge]]**, **[[principalities-powers|
  principalities and powers]]** — premodern symbolic languages of evil that the secularizing
  process renders "dead metaphors."
- **[[apocalyptic-literature|Apocalyptic literature]]** — the genre that most fully named
  cosmic evil; its eclipse is part of the same arc.

## Caveat (Contradiction Protocol)

The "death of Satan" is a **secularization thesis**, and like all such theses it is
contested: Delbanco himself stresses that secularization "has always been resisted," and
that "many Americans — and many millions throughout the world — have refused to give up"
belief in a providential, evil-naming order (traditional Christians, Jews, Muslims, and
others). The concept describes the trajectory of *secular* Western elite culture; it is not a
claim that belief in the Devil or in evil has actually disappeared. Recorded here without
adjudication.
