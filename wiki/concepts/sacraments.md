# The Sacraments

A **sacrament** (Latin *sacramentum*, rendering Greek *mystērion*) is, in
[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s definition ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Tertia Pars Q60–Q65), "the sign
of a holy thing **insofar as it makes men holy**" — not any sacred sign, but specifically a
sensible sign that **effects the sanctification it signifies**. The Tertia Pars treatise on the
sacraments in general (Q60–65) is the foundational text of Latin scholastic sacramentology and the
frame for the seven individual sacraments ([[baptism]], confirmation, the
[[transubstantiation|Eucharist]], [[sacrament-of-penance|penance]], extreme unction, holy orders,
matrimony).

## What a sacrament is (Q60)

- A sacrament is a **sensible sign** of a sacred reality, because human knowledge moves from the
  sensible to the spiritual.
- It is composed of **"words and things" (*res et verba*)** as **form and matter**: the sensible
  element (water, oil, bread) is the *matter*; the words are the *form* that determine the otherwise
  ambiguous significance of the element. Determinate words are required, but the *sense* (not the
  sound or language) is what matters, so any language suffices.
- A sacrament signifies three things at once: **Christ's Passion** (the past cause), **grace** (the
  present effect), and **eternal glory** (the future end).

## Necessity, causality, and grace (Q61–Q62)

Sacraments are **necessary** for three reasons: human nature needs sensible signs; the remedy for
sin must reach man where sin subjected him (to corporeal things); and bodily exercise wards off
superstition. They were not necessary before sin; the Old-Law sacraments were signs of the *future*
Christ and did **not cause grace** (they could not draw power from a Passion not yet accomplished),
whereas the New-Law sacraments signify grace as *present and conferred*.

Aquinas's central thesis (Q62): the sacraments are **instrumental efficient causes of grace**, not
mere occasions or signs — "they effect what they signify." **God is the principal cause**, Christ's
humanity is the *united* instrument, and the sacraments are *separate* instruments whose power flows
specifically from **Christ's Passion**. (This is the metaphysical content later summarized in the
phrase *ex opere operato* — the sacrament works "by the work worked," not by the worthiness of
minister or recipient — though Aquinas develops the doctrine without leaning on the slogan.)
**Sacramental grace** adds, beyond grace generically, a "divine assistance" proper to the specific
end of each sacrament.

## The sacramental character (Q63)

Three sacraments — **Baptism, Confirmation, and Holy Orders** — imprint an indelible **character**
(*character sacramentalis*): a spiritual power, seated in the soul's cognitive part, that is a
**participation in Christ's eternal priesthood**. It is indelible because Christ's priesthood is
eternal; this is why these three sacraments **cannot be repeated**. The Eucharist (the *end*, not a
deputation to further action), penance, and matrimony imprint no character and may be repeated.

## The minister and the number of the sacraments (Q64–Q65)

- **God alone institutes** sacraments; Christ as man had a "power of excellence" over them (He
  instituted them, the Passion's merit works in them, they are sanctified by His name, and He could
  confer their effects without the rite) — a power He chose **not** to share with ministers, to
  prevent factions ("I am of Paul…").
- **Validity does not depend on the minister's worthiness** (the **anti-Donatist** position): the
  minister is an instrument — "the pipe's material, silver or lead, is accidental to the flow of
  water." A wicked minister confers a valid sacrament (though he sins in doing so). What is required
  is the **intention to do what the Church does**.
- **Seven sacraments**, justified by analogy to the stages of bodily and social life (generation =
  Baptism; growth = Confirmation; nourishment = Eucharist; healing = Penance; restoration = Extreme
  Unction; plus the two "social" sacraments, Orders and Matrimony) and as remedies for the defects of
  sin. Holy water and the like are **sacramentals**, not sacraments (they confer no grace). The
  **Eucharist is the greatest** absolutely (it contains Christ himself substantially, and all others
  are ordered to it); **Baptism** is greatest by necessity; three are absolutely necessary —
  Baptism, Penance (for post-baptismal mortal sin), and Orders (for the Church).

## Reception and divergence

This sacramental realism — seven grace-causing sacraments working *ex opere operato*, three imprinting
an indelible character — is definitive for [[catholicism|Catholicism]] (codified at Trent against the
Reformers) and largely shared with [[eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern Orthodoxy]]. The **Protestant**
Reformers reduced the sacraments (most to two, Baptism and the Lord's Supper), rejected *ex opere
operato* in favor of sacraments as signs that require and strengthen faith, and denied the sacrificial
and character categories — divergences to be recorded on the respective sect pages and in
`canon_scope`/doctrine notes.
