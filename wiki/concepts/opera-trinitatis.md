# *Opera Trinitatis Ad Extra Indivisa Sunt*

"**The outward works of the Trinity are undivided**" (*opera trinitatis ad extra sunt indivisa*) is a classical axiom of Trinitarian theology asserting that all of God's actions toward creatures — creation, providence, reconciliation, redemption, sanctification — are the acts of the one undivided Triune God, and cannot be attributed exclusively to one divine person (or, in [[karl-barth|Barth]]'s terminology, one [[modes-of-being|mode of being]]) to the exclusion of the others.

## Classical Sources

The principle is materially from Augustine: "Just as they are inseparable in fact, so are they inseparable in their work (*inseparabiliter operantur*)" (*De Trinitate* I, 4; cited in [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1-s8-12|*CD* I.1]], ll. 3910–11). Augustine continues: "With respect to creation the Father and Son and Holy Spirit are one source, just as they are one Creator and one Lord" (l. 3915). An act cannot be divided "where the nature is not only equal, but even indivisible (*indiscreta natura*)" (l. 3916). The formula received dogmatic force at the **Council of Florence** (1441): "The Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are not three sources of creation, but one source" (Denz. No. 704; ll. 3918–19).

## Doctrinal Function

The axiom guards against two errors:

1. **Tritheism or tripartitism** — treating the three persons as three independent agents who divide divine work among themselves, as though creation belonged to the Father alone, redemption to the Son alone, and sanctification to the Spirit alone.

2. **Mythological drama** — presenting "the work of God in the form of a dramatic entry and exit of now one and now another of the divine persons, of the surging up and down of half or totally individualised powers or forms or ideas, of a shifting co-existence and competition of the three hypostases" (ll. 3881–85). This Barth calls "pagan mythology."

## Appropriation (*Appropriatio*)

The axiom is complemented by the doctrine of **appropriations** (*appropriationes*): while all works *ad extra* are undivided, it is legitimate (and commanded) to *appropriate* certain works or attributes especially to one mode of being because of affinity between that mode and that work. Thomas Aquinas defines appropriation as: "to apply that which is common to one in particular … not … because it belongs more to one person than to another … but because that which is common has a greater likeness to that which is proper to one person than to what is proper to another" (*De Veritate* q. 7, art. 3; ll. 3839–42).

Classical appropriations (ll. 3821–37):
- Augustine: unity to the Father, equality to the Son, connection to the Spirit
- Thomas: power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, goodness to the Spirit
- Bonaventura: eternity to the Father, appearance to the Son, event (enjoyment) to the Spirit; or principle/execution/goal
- Luther's Catechism: Father/creation, Son/redemption, Spirit/sanctification
- Calvin: source (*principium*), wisdom (*sapientia*), power (*virtus*)

Barth's three rules for valid appropriation (ll. 3846–73): (1) must be *intelligible* — a manifest analogy between the appropriated attribute and the mode's distinctive relation of origin; (2) must be *non-exclusive* — the appropriated attribute belongs in fact to all modes; (3) must be *scriptural* — derived from Scripture, not freely invented.

## Application in §10

In [[barth-church-dogmatics-i1-s8-12|*CD* I.1]] §10, Barth applies *opera indivisa* to the doctrine of God the Father as Creator. It follows both that (a) "not only God the Father is to be claimed as the Creator" — Son and Spirit are equally the subject of creation (l. 4763) — and that (b) the appropriation of creation especially to the Father is nonetheless "not just permitted; it is also commanded" (ll. 4882–83), because there is an affinity between the Father's intradivine relation (as the origin from whom the Son proceeds) and the Creator's relation to the creature (as the origin from which everything distinct from God exists). The axiom also rules out Patripassianism: the statement "God the Father suffered and died" is forbidden, not because the Father was absent from the Son's passion, but because such a statement would violate the particularity of the mode-of-being distinctions that the axiom protects even within their unity (ll. 4890–4921).
