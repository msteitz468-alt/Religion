# NT Canon Formation

## Defining the Problem

The question of how the New Testament canon was formed encompasses several sub-questions:
- *When* did Christians recognize these books as Scripture?
- *Who* made the decisions?
- *Why* these 27 books and not others?
- Does the word "canon" mean a fixed, closed list, or the actual functional use of books as Scripture?

The answer depends substantially on *which definition of canon* one uses — and the definitional question is itself contested.

## The Definitional Debate

### Sundberg's "Exclusive Definition" (Dominant 20th-c. Consensus)

A.C. Sundberg (1968) and Harry Gamble argued that "canon" properly means a *final, fixed, closed list* — the definitive decision that these books and only these belong. On this view, the NT canon was not formed until the 4th century (Athanasius' Festal Letter of 367 lists all 27 books; councils of Hippo 393 and Carthage 397/419 confirm the list). This produces the common claim that the NT canon was a late 4th-century invention.

### Kruger's "Functional Definition"

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] and others (Craig Allert, John Barton, Lee McDonald) argue this definition is too narrow. A "canon" functionally exists when a community treats a book *as Scripture* — reads it in public worship, appeals to it as authoritative, cites it with scriptural formulae ("as it is written"). On the functional definition, the NT canon was largely in place by the *middle of the second century*.

The functional definition does not deny the importance of formal lists; it simply distinguishes (a) the *emergence* of the canon from (b) the *ratification* of the canon. Formal 4th-century lists did not create the canon; they reflected a recognition already operative in church practice.

## The Core Canon by Mid-Second Century

Kruger argues that a "core canon" of approximately 22-23 of the eventual 27 books functioned as Scripture by c.150-180 CE. Evidence:

### The Muratorian Fragment (c.180 CE)

The Muratorian Fragment (named for Lodovico Muratori, who published it in 1740) is the earliest extant canonical list. Probably composed at Rome c.180 CE. It:
- Explicitly lists books that are "accepted" (*recipitur*) in church: four Gospels, Acts, 13 Pauline letters, Jude, 1-2 John, Revelation, Wisdom of Solomon, Apocalypse of Peter (with caveat)
- Explicitly lists books that are *not* accepted: Shepherd of Hermas (good to read privately, not in church), Letter to Laodiceans, Letter to Alexandrians (Marcionite), other heretical texts
- Total: approximately 22/27 NT books, with explicit "in/out" language

The Fragment demonstrates that by c.180, the concept of a defined Christian scripture with hard boundaries was operational — not merely theoretical.

### Irenaeus of Lyons (c.180 CE)

[[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] in *Against Heresies* explicitly names and quotes from: four Gospels (defending the fourfold Gospels against Marcion's single gospel and gnostic gospels), Paul's letters (13, treating them as Scripture), Acts, Revelation, 1 John, 1 Peter. He operates with approximately 23/27 NT books. His famous argument for *four* Gospels on theological grounds ("four regions of the world, four winds," *Haer.* 3.11.8) actually presupposes that the fourfold Gospel was *already* established — he is defending a received tradition, not creating one.

### Theophilus of Antioch (c.170-180 CE)

Theophilus of Antioch cites the Gospels and Pauline letters together as "the divine word" (*Logos Theios*), placing them on the level of OT Scripture. A fourfold Gospel collection and a Pauline corpus are already treated as a scriptural unit.

### Justin Martyr (c.150-160 CE)

[[justin-martyr|Justin]] uses "memoirs of the apostles" (*apomnēmoneumata tōn apostolōn*) as the designation for the Gospels, which are read alongside OT prophecy in weekly worship (*First Apology* 67). He cites Matt/Mark/Luke. He never uses a citation formula equivalent to "as it is written" for the Gospels, but their liturgical status (alongside OT) implies scriptural treatment. Justin also cites Paul's letters (Romans, 1-2 Corinthians, Galatians), though less explicitly.

### Marcion (c.144 CE) — *Terminus Post Quem*

Marcion of Sinope arrived in Rome c.144 and formed his own canon: an edited Gospel of Luke + 10 Pauline letters (excluding pastorals). His *reduction* of the existing Christian literary corpus is crucial evidence:
- Marcion did not *create* a canon as a new idea — he *trimmed* a pre-existing corpus
- The orthodox reaction to Marcion was not "we must now assemble our canon" but "you have mutilated our already existing scriptures"
- Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 4.1-2) explicitly says Marcion cut down the orthodox corpus; Irenaeus makes the same point

This refutes the "Marcionite catalyst" hypothesis (Harnack's view that orthodoxy created its canon as a *response* to Marcion). The canon was *prior* to and *larger* than Marcion's reduction.

### The Apostolic Fathers (c.90-150 CE)

Even before formal canonical lists, the Apostolic Fathers demonstrate functional scriptural use of what become NT books. The Holmes edition of the Apostolic Fathers (*The Apostolic Fathers*, 3rd ed., 2007) provides the primary evidence for the following:

- **1 Clement** (ca. 96): knows Paul's letters as inspired ("in the Spirit"); quotes 1 Cor at length; the letter itself was treated as nearly canonical — included in Codex Alexandrinus (5th c.) immediately after the NT, and cited as authoritative by Clement of Alexandria and Origen
- **Ignatius** (ca. 107–117): refers to a Pauline letter collection (*Ign. Eph.* 12.2: "Paul makes mention of you [Ephesians] in every letter"); knows at least 5–6 Pauline letters; neither cites the Gospels by name nor quotes from them with scriptural formulae, but presupposes Gospel traditions throughout
- **Polycarp** (*Phil.* ca. 107–110): cites Ephesians as "Scripture" — "as it is written in these Scriptures" (*Phil.* 12.1) — explicitly scriptural formula on a Pauline letter; the earliest surviving instance of a Pauline letter cited with the OT citation formula
- **Epistle of Barnabas** (ca. 70–135): cites Matt 22:14 with "as it is written" — the standard OT formula now applied to a Gospel text; in Codex Sinaiticus (4th c.) following the NT; cited as scripture by Clement of Alexandria
- **Didache** (late 1st c.): three uses of "the gospel" (*euangelion*) as a source of instructions without citing a written text by name; the Lord's Prayer (8.2–3) with the distinctive doxology ("for yours is the power and glory forever") not found in Matthew's oldest manuscripts; known to Clement of Alexandria, Origen, and Didymus the Blind, but excluded by Athanasius and the Muratorian Fragment
- **Shepherd of Hermas** (ca. 90–155): no OT-style citation formula on any text; Christological content minimal; yet present in Codex Sinaiticus after the NT; cited as scripture by Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen; ultimately excluded by Athanasius (*Festal Letter* 39, 367 CE) and the Muratorian Fragment
- **2 Clement** (ca. 140): in *2 Clem.* 2.4, cites Matt 9:13 ("I have come not to call the righteous but sinners") with the formula "another scripture (*graphē*) says" — **the earliest surviving instance** of a NT text explicitly called "scripture" (*graphē*) with the same term used for OT texts; present in Codex Alexandrinus after 1 Clement; treated as scripture by some but excluded by Eusebius
- **Papias** (ca. 100–125): Fragment 3.15 names Mark as Peter's interpreter; Fragment 3.16 names Matthew as composer of "oracles (*logia*) in the Hebrew language" — the primary patristic evidence for the apostolic connection of both gospels; Papias explicitly values "the living voice" of oral tradition from the elders over written books (*graphai*), representing a stage when oral tradition still had epistemological priority over written texts
- **Fragment of Quadratus** (ca. 117–138): the earliest Christian apology (addressed to Hadrian), claims that some healed or raised by Jesus were still alive when he wrote — placing the ministry of Jesus within living memory ca. 120–130 CE

### Aristides (c.125 CE)

In his *Apology* to the Emperor Hadrian, Aristides invites the emperor to "read the Gospel" as the definitive expression of Christian belief — implying an already identifiable, authoritative text.

## Books on the Boundary

Not all 27 NT books achieved universal recognition simultaneously. The "disputed" books (*antilegomena* in Eusebius' terminology) include:
- Hebrews (attributed to Paul by some; uncertain authorship)
- James, 2 Peter, 2-3 John, Jude (the "Catholic Epistles" of uncertain or disputed provenance)
- Revelation (accepted in West; disputed in East due to millennialism concerns)

Eusebius (*H.E.* 3.25) categorized NT books into *homologoumena* (universally acknowledged), *antilegomena* (disputed), and *notha* (spurious). His four disputed books that eventually made it in were Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, and Jude.

Books that circulated widely but did not enter the canon include: Shepherd of Hermas, 1 Clement, Epistle of Barnabas, Didache, Apocalypse of Peter. Their absence from the eventual canon is not due to late suppression but to early-and-ongoing evaluation against the apostolic criterion.

## The Apostolicity Criterion

The primary question guiding canon formation: was the book *apostolic*? This meant either:
1. Directly authored by an apostle, or
2. Closely connected to apostolic testimony (Mark = Peter; Luke = Paul)

Apostolicity was the *necessary but not sufficient* condition. The Shepherd of Hermas, though highly valued, was excluded in part because the Muratorian Fragment explicitly notes it was written "in our own times" — not during the apostolic period. The criterion was consistency with the apostolic rule of faith ([[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]]) *plus* traceable connection to apostolic authorship or testimony.

## Apocryphal Literature and the Canon Boundary

The explosion of apocryphal literature — gospels, acts, epistles, apocalypses — in the second century (Kruger, Ch. 6) was contemporary with canon formation and contributed to the urgency of boundary-marking. Typology of apocryphal literature:
- **Apocryphal Gospels**: Gospel of Thomas, Gospel of Peter, Gospel of Mary, Gospel of Philip, Gospel of Truth (Valentinus), Protevangelium of James (infancy narrative)
- **Apocryphal Acts**: Acts of Paul (with Thecla narrative), Acts of Peter (Quo Vadis?), Acts of John, Acts of Andrew, Acts of Thomas
- **Apocryphal Epistles**: 3 Corinthians, Epistle to the Laodiceans, Epistle of the Apostles (*Epistula Apostolorum*)
- **Apocryphal Apocalypses**: Apocalypse of Peter, Apocalypse of Paul, *Apocryphon of John*

These texts were not uniformly condemned immediately — some (Shepherd of Hermas, Epistle of Barnabas) circulated in canons for some time. What eventually produced clear boundaries was: (1) formal canonical lists (Muratorian Fragment, Irenaeus); (2) the apostolicity criterion applied consistently; (3) patristic citation patterns (NT books cited vastly more frequently than apocryphal ones).

## The Canon and High Christology

Kruger notes an important structural point: the NT canon was not merely a response to the *controversy over heresy* — it was the *precondition* that made those controversies adjudicable. The canon debate (which books count as apostolic?) and the doctrinal debate (which teaching is apostolic?) were parallel, not sequential. The canon enabled the [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]] to be anchored in specific, apostolically traceable texts.

High Christology in the canonical books (Pauline pre-existence texts: Phil 2:6-11; 1 Cor 8:5-6; Col 1:15-20; John's prologue) correlates with the functional definition of Scripture operating in the church from an early date. See [[kyrios-christology-origins|Kyrios-Christology Origins]].

## Codex Technology and the Canon

The Christian adoption of the codex (book format, not roll) from the very beginning of textual production may be connected to canon consciousness. J.K. Elliott and Harry Gamble suggest the codex format — which could hold larger collections — both reflected and encouraged the gathering of texts into collections: Gospel codices, Pauline letter codices, eventually full NT codices. See [[nomina-sacra|Nomina Sacra]] for the scribal markers that distinguished Christian texts.
