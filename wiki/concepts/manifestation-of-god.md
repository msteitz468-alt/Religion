# Manifestation of God

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Definition

The Manifestation of God (*mazhar-i iláhí* in Persian) is the central theological concept of both the [[babi|Babi Religion]] and the [[bahai|Bahá'í Faith]]. Smith identifies it as "central to the theology of the Báb's system, as also in Bahá'í belief" (line 932). The term is deliberately preferred over "prophet": Smith notes that Bahá'ís "generally use the term 'Manifestation of God' rather than 'prophet'" (lines 297–298).

**Core definition:** The doctrine holds that God is in essence unknowable and inaccessible. The purpose of human creation is the knowledge and love of God, but this is only possible through the Manifestations of God (lines 935–937). These beings "originate from the Primal Will or Logos. They combine the station of revelatory divinity with human servitude" (lines 937–939). Each Manifestation "brings a renewal of God's grace and gives his followers the teachings appropriate for that particular age, and on that basis a new divine civilization develops" (lines 1611–1614).

## The List of Manifestations

Smith identifies different lists at different stages. According to the Báb's theology, there is a succession of Manifestations "starting with Adam and continuing through Muhammad to the Báb and in the future to 'He whom God shall make manifest', and so on indefinitely" (lines 939–941).

In the Bahá'í elaboration:
- Bahá'u'lláh's own writings recognize the prophets listed in the Quran (notably Adam, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad) and additionally Zoroaster (lines 1614–1616).
- **'Abdu'l-Bahá later added Krishna and the Buddha** (lines 1617–1618).

Thus the full list of Manifestations as given in Smith includes: Abraham, Moses, Krishna, Zoroaster, Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, the Báb, and Bahá'u'lláh (see lines 286–300, 1614–1618).

## Theological Structure

**God's transcendence:** God is "in essence unknowable and can only be approached through his attributes" (lines 1607–1609). The Manifestations "most perfectly express" these attributes (line 1610).

**Dual station:** The Manifestations "combine divine and human qualities" (line 1611). The Báb's formulation: they combine "the station of revelatory divinity with human servitude" (lines 938–939).

**Cyclical pattern:** Each appearance of a Manifestation of God "represents a re-enactment or return (*raja'a*). The Manifestation appears in obscurity and suffers persecution until eventually his dispensation develops and triumphs. Around him circle his chief disciples, and opposing him are the forces of negation, headed by the Dajjal or antichrist" (lines 951–956). Each is "the rising and setting of the same sun" (lines 942–943).

**Spiritual eschatology:** Prophetic fulfilment is a spiritual reality. "Those who believed in the Manifestation of God for the present age had attained paradise, and those who had met him had met God. Those who denied him were already in hell fire" (lines 971–973). Heaven and hell are thus "spiritual states: there is no bodily resurrection; and creation is eternal" (lines 1623–1624).

## Break from Islamic "Seal of the Prophets" Doctrine

This concept directly challenges the Islamic doctrine of the finality of prophethood. For Muslims, "Muhammad is the 'seal of the prophets' (understood to mean the last) and the Islamic holy law (the sharia) provides the standard for human behaviour until the resurrection" (lines 292–295). For Babis and Bahá'ís, the resurrection has already come; the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh are divine messengers whose revelations have abrogated and superseded Islamic law. The series of Manifestations is explicitly open-ended: it continues "so on indefinitely" (line 941), and Bahá'u'lláh stated only that there would be no further Manifestation "for at least a thousand years" (lines 1473–1474).

## How the Báb Used This Concept

Smith notes that the Babi theology of the Manifestation was developed especially in the Báb's later writings from Maku and Chihriq. The Báb presented himself as "a new point of revelation" (line 857) — not simply the Mahdi restoring Islam but the Manifestation for the present age whose new holy law (the Bayán) superseded the Quran. The Báb's theology also introduced a specific future Manifestation: "He whom God shall make manifest" (*Man-yuzhiruhu'lláh*), described as "the origin of all divine names and attributes" (lines 924–925), for whom "a thousand perusals of the Bayán would not be equal to reading one of his verses" (lines 927–928). The Bahá'í tradition identifies this figure as Bahá'u'lláh.

## Key Text Connections

- [[the-bab|The Báb's]] Bayán and later writings — systematic elaboration of the doctrine.
- Bahá'u'lláh's [[bahaullah|Kitáb-i-Íqán]] — provided Babis with "a clear account of their religious doctrines, particularly the concept of a succession of Manifestations of God and the fulfilment of prophecy" (lines 1311–1314).
- Bahá'u'lláh's Kitáb-i-Aqdas and tablets to rulers — applied the concept to proclaim his own station.

## Relationship to Progressive Revelation

The Manifestation of God concept is the theological foundation for [[progressive-revelation|Progressive Revelation]]: each Manifestation is appropriate to the capacity of the age, abrogates the previous holy law, and gives teachings suited to that particular time.

## Controversies

The concept breaks fundamentally with the Islamic understanding of the finality of prophethood and with Christian claims for the unique and final saving work of Christ. Within the Babi movement, the question of whether the Báb's claims were those of a gate/intermediary or a full Manifestation was itself contested, with later Bahá'í interpretation favoring the latter. See [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith 1995]] for the ambiguity of the early claims (lines 466–492).
