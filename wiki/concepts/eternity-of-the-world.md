# Eternity of the World

The question of whether the world is **pre-eternal** (*qadīm*, having no temporal beginning) or **temporally originated** (*ḥādith*, brought into being at a specific moment by a creating God) is the first and most foundational controversy between the Islamic philosophical tradition (*[[falsafa]]*) and the *[[kalam]]* theologians, most prominently represented here by [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] in the [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]].

---

## The Philosophers' Thesis

The dominant view among the philosophers — identified by al-Ghazālī as the position of "their multitudes, both ancient and modern" — is that the world is **pre-eternal**: "it has never ceased to exist with God, exalted be He, to be an effect of His, to exist along with Him, not being posterior to Him in time, in the way the effect coexists along with the cause and light along with the sun" (§1). The Creator's priority over the world is "a priority in essence and rank, not in time" (§1).

The analogy is explicit: just as light co-exists with the sun the instant the sun exists, so the world co-exists with God the instant God exists. God is the necessary, eternal cause; the world is the necessary, eternal effect. Any temporal gap between Creator and creation would require a change in the Eternal — which is deemed impossible.

Plato's language of the world being "generated and originated in time" was reinterpreted by later philosophers as metaphor (§2). Galen alone took a non-committal position in his *What Galen Believes as Sound Judgment*, stating the matter "is unknowable to him, not because of any shortcoming on his part, but because of the inherent difficulty of this to [human] minds" — though al-Ghazālī notes this "appears to be most unusual in their doctrine" (§3).

## The Four Proofs and Ghazālī's Rebuttals

Al-Ghazālī's method throughout is to argue **via the philosophers' own demonstrative logic** (*burhān*): he does not appeal to revelation or tradition to refute them, but instead shows their proofs fail on their own terms. His stated aim is purely destructive: "to muddy their doctrine and throw dust in the face of their proofs with that which would reveal their incoherence. We have not undertaken to defend a specific doctrine" (§134). A positive defense of creation-in-time was reserved for a planned sequel, *The Principles of Belief* (§134).

### Proof 1: The Impossibility of a Temporal Proceeding from an Eternal (§6–10)

**The argument**: Suppose the Eternal exists without the world. For the world to then come into existence, something would have to give preponderance to existence over non-existence. If no new "giver of preponderance" arises, the world remains a "pure possibility" and cannot exist. If a new giver of preponderance does arise, the question merely regresses: "Who originated this giver of preponderance and why did it originate now and not earlier?" (§6). Since the states of the Eternal are similar (unchanging), either nothing ever proceeds from Him or everything proceeds from Him perpetually. "A [divine] state of refraining [to act] standing differentiated from [another divine] state of commencing [to act] is impossible" (§7). Therefore, the world must be co-eternal with God.

The crux is also stated as: if God did not will the world's existence before its creation, then His will must have been created anew — but a created will either arises in God (impossible, since He is not a receptacle for temporal things) or outside God (which would not make Him a wilier). And if some cause of the new will is posited, the same question recurs of that cause (§8–9).

**Ghazālī's first objection** (§13–33): Al-Ghazālī challenges the philosophers to show, by demonstrative proof rather than mere analogy to human will, why an eternal will cannot be related to a temporal act. He argues the impossibility claim is assumed, not demonstrated: "In all of what you have stated, there is nothing but [an expression of] unlikelihood and the drawing of an analogy with our resolve and will, this being false, since the eternal will does not resemble temporal [human] intentions" (§22). He turns this back on the philosophers by noting they themselves hold positions (e.g., that one divine essence knows all universals without multiplicity) that appear impossibly counter-intuitive to opponents — yet they deflect such objections by saying divine attributes are not analogous to creaturely ones. Al-Ghazālī presses: why, then, can the divine will not similarly differ from human will in being determinately connected to a temporal act without a new state?

He also traps the philosophers on their own doctrine of separated souls (§30): Avicenna holds that human souls are temporally created but are non-material substances; their number after death becomes an infinite, non-embodied set. This set is "neither even nor odd" — which the philosophers' opponents claim is as impossible as al-Ghazālī's eternal will connected to temporal creation. The philosophers cannot escape this problem on their own terms (§33).

**Ghazālī's second objection** (§65–76): Temporal events must have a causal terminus. If temporal events cannot ultimately terminate in an Eternal cause, there is either infinite regress or no Necessary Existent at all — both unacceptable to the philosophers. Hence the philosophers themselves must "allow the proceeding of a temporal from an eternal" (§66). The philosophers respond that they only object to a *first* temporal event from an Eternal without mediating conditions; subsequent temporal events are unproblematic if preceded by a change in preparedness (§68). Al-Ghazālī retorts that this merely pushes the question back: the first change in preparedness itself must either regress infinitely or terminate in an Eternal producing a first temporal event (§70). The philosophers' counter-appeal to eternal circular heavenly motion as the bridge (§72–74) is rejected: if that circular motion is eternal, it cannot be the principle of why some temporal events occur at some times and not others; if it is temporal, it requires its own cause, and regress looms (§76).

### Proof 2: Time Is Pre-Eternal (§78–95)

**The argument**: "Prior to the world" either means the Creator is prior *in essence* (so that Creator and world are co-eternal in time) or prior *in time* — in which case there was a time before the world, and that prior time itself must have preceded by time, yielding an infinite regress of past times (§78–80). Either way, time (and hence motion and the world) must be pre-eternal.

**Ghazālī's objection**: "Time is originated and created, and before it there was no time at all" (§82). His key move: "before" creation does not refer to a real, extant temporal interval. Saying "God was and there was no world" means only the existence of the Creator's essence and the non-existence of the world's essence — no third thing (time) need be posited. The appearance of a "before" is produced by the **estimative faculty** (*wahm*), which cannot conceive an event lacking a precedent. But the estimative faculty is not reason. Al-Ghazālī draws an exact spatial parallel: just as reason accepts that the world has no "beyond" (no void or filled space outside the world), even though the estimative faculty cannot conceive a body with no exterior, so too reason can accept that the world's beginning has no "before" that is a real temporal interval, even though the estimative faculty insists on imagining one (§86–87, §92–94). "Clear reason does not disallow a first beginning that is preceded by nothing; and if the estimation falls short of grasping this, one must not heed it" (§94).

### Secondary Proof: Hypothetical Temporal Possibilities (§96–107)

**The argument**: God could have created the world earlier by any amount — a year, a century, a thousand years. These hypothetical intervals have measurable quantity and must have existed before the world; but measurable quantity is time. Hence time pre-existed the world (§96–98).

**Ghazālī's objection**: This argument is "the work of the estimation." He applies the same spatial parallel: God could have made the heavens larger or smaller by any cubit-measure, but this does not entail that spatial dimensions exist beyond the world. Hypothetical possibilities involve no real prior extension, temporal or spatial (§100). The philosophical claim that the world's exact size is the only possible size is rejected as leading to necessitarianism (§104–105).

### Proof 3: Possibility Has No Beginning (§108–111)

**The argument**: The world's existence is possible in every temporal state; there is no moment in which it was impossible. Since this possibility is eternal (has no beginning), the possible existent itself is eternal — for if the possibility had a beginning, there would have been a prior state of impossibility, which would mean God could not have created in that prior state (§108–109).

**Ghazālī's objection**: The world is eternally possible *as a temporally originated thing*, not as an eternal thing. Eternal possibility of origination is compatible with the world having a temporal beginning, just as the philosophers allow that the world's spatial extension could have been larger or smaller — infinite possibilities of size — without the world therefore having an actually infinite size (§110–111).

### Proof 4: Matter Is Pre-Eternal (§112–131)

**The argument**: Every temporal existent is preceded by the possibility of its existence; possibility is a relational description requiring a receptacle; that receptacle is matter. Hence matter (primary *hylē*) cannot itself be temporally originated, since its possibility would then require prior matter in turn, leading to regress or to a self-subsistent possibility, which is incomprehensible (§112–114).

**Ghazālī's objection**: Possibility, impossibility, and necessity are *judgments of the mind*, not real properties requiring a material substrate (§115–116). He offers three proofs: (1) impossibility would equally require a material substrate — but the impossibility of a partner to God has no such substrate (§117, §129); (2) the mind judges blackness in itself to be possible before any body is specified (§118); (3) on Avicenna's own view, human souls are temporally created, non-material substances, yet they have pre-existential possibility — without a material receptacle (§119, §132). The philosophers attempt replies (§120–125), which al-Ghazālī refutes in turn (§126–132), arguing that universals (genera, species) are also held by the philosophers to exist only in minds, so their move to objective possibility outside the mind is inconsistent.

## The Divine Will: Specifier Without Determinant

The deepest philosophical issue raised in the First Discussion concerns the **divine will** as the theological alternative to eternal necessitation.

The philosophers press: if all times are equal in their receptivity to creation, what could differentiate *this* moment of creation from any other? If God's eternal will cannot explain the selection without itself requiring an explanation, the problem merely regresses. They argue: "We know by necessity that a thing is not distinguished from what is similar to it except through that which specifies" (§38). If God's will is cited as the specifier, then the will's act of specifying also requires explanation — and if the question "why?" cannot be asked of the Eternal, let the world likewise be eternal and exempt from causal demands (§38–39).

Al-Ghazālī's answer is his doctrine of will as an irreducibly *specific* divine attribute:

> "Will stands as an expression for an attribute whose function — nay, its essence — is to differentiate a thing from its similar." (§41)

Just as knowledge is defined as the attribute whose function is to encompass its object as it is, will is defined as the attribute whose function is to render one possible specific over another — without requiring a further determinant. This is not arbitrary: it is precisely what will *is* by definition. If two perfectly equal dates lie before a thirsty person who cannot take both, he will inevitably take one — not because of any measurable difference, but because will is the power of choosing between equals (§46). To demand a further reason for will's selection is to demand a reason for what will *is*, which is incoherent.

Al-Ghazālī also presses back on the philosophers' own necessitarianism: the world has specific configurations (the poles, the directions of celestial motion) that are not necessitated by any rational difference between alternatives. The philosophers cannot explain why the celestial poles are where they are without appealing to some form of specification among equals (§52–64). This consequence is unavoidable on their own principles.

## Time, Creation, and the Ash'arite Position

A key commitment of al-Ghazālī's position — stated explicitly — is that time itself is created: "duration and time are both created" (§36). This blocks the philosophers' second proof: there was no pre-creation temporal interval in which God "waited" to create. The question "why didn't God create sooner?" is malformed; there is no "sooner" when time itself begins with creation.

This Ash'arite position stands in contrast to the philosophers' identification of time with the measure of eternal motion (*falsafa* follows Aristotle in making time co-extensive with the eternal circular motion of the celestial spheres).

## Hermeneutical Note

Al-Ghazālī argues entirely within the framework of rational demonstration (*burhān*) — the philosophers' own preferred method. He does not appeal to Quranic verses or prophetic tradition in the First Discussion's argumentation. The role of revealed law appears only in setting the bounds of inquiry: the question of whether the world will be post-eternal (Discussion 2) cannot be settled by reason alone and requires "the revealed law" (Disc. 2, §4). The divide between **divine will** (voluntarist; Ash'arite) and **divine necessitation** (emanationist; Avicennan) is the underlying fault line: for the philosophers, God's eternity necessitates the world's eternity; for al-Ghazālī, God's eternal will freely specifies a temporal moment without a determinant.

## Judaism — Maimonides' Suspension (Guide II:13–25)

[[maimonides|Maimonides]] reframes the same question within [[rabbinic-judaism|Judaism]]. He
names **three views**: Moses (*[[creation-ex-nihilo|creatio ex nihilo]]*), Plato (an eternal
pre-existent matter), and Aristotle (an eternal universe of fixed law). His decisive move is to
hold that **neither creation nor eternity has been demonstrated** — "Aristotle … never intended
to prove" eternity (II:15) — so he accepts creation "on the authority of Prophecy" (II:16),
arguing it is the more defensible and is required by the Law (an eternal, law-bound cosmos would
abolish miracle). Crucially, **time itself is created**, depending on the motion of the created
sphere (II:13).

This yields his famous **hermeneutical principle** (II:25): the creation texts may be allegorized
*only if* eternity were demonstrated — and since it is not, their literal sense stands. He
contrasts this with the divine-corporeality texts, which he *does* allegorize precisely because
incorporeality *was* demonstrated. Like [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazali]] he refuses the philosophers'
claim to demonstrate eternity, but unlike the [[kalam|Mutakallimun]] he equally refuses their
claim to *demonstrate* creation, resting it on prophecy instead. See [[guide-for-the-perplexed]].

## Christianity — Aquinas: neither side is demonstrable (Summa I, Q46)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] ([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q46, A1–2) reaches a
conclusion close to Maimonides' but on Christian ground, and against his own contemporary
**Bonaventure**, who held the world's temporal beginning to be rationally demonstrable. For Aquinas,
**that the world began in time is held by faith and cannot be demonstrated by reason — and neither
can its eternity.** Two arguments: (1) demonstration proceeds from a thing's essence/definition,
which abstracts from "here and now," so nothing in a creature's essence shows whether it always
existed; (2) the world depends on God's **will** regarding creatures, which is not necessary and so
not demonstrable. He calls Aristotle's eternity arguments merely *dialectical*, not apodictic
(*Topics* i.9), and distinguishes the *intolerable* error (that God did not make the world) from the
merely faith-contrary opinion (a created yet beginningless world), which reason cannot strictly
refute. He adds the same pastoral warning he gives about the [[trinity|Trinity]] (Q32): do not offer
bad "proofs" of faith-articles, lest they expose the faith to ridicule. He thus agrees with
Maimonides that creation-in-time is undemonstrated, but rests its *fact* purely on revelation rather
than judging it the more probable. See [[creation-ex-nihilo]].
