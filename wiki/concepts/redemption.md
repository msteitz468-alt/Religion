# Redemption

## Definition

*Redemption* (*apolytrosis*, *soteria*; Latin *redemptio*) in Christian theology designates liberation or deliverance — originally drawing on the image of paying a ransom to free a slave or prisoner (*lytron*, Mk. 10:45). The term carries overlapping meanings across the tradition: ransom from sin, liberation from bondage (to the law, to death, to the powers), and the final eschatological deliverance of the creation. In Barth's *Church Dogmatics*, redemption is distinguished from reconciliation and given a specific structural role as the economic act *appropriated to the Holy Spirit* (the third mode of being), designating the final, eschatological dimension of salvation — the Spirit's work of setting man free as a child of God.

## Barth's Structural Usage (Church Dogmatics I.1, §§11–12)

Barth distinguishes three modes of God's economic work:

| Mode of being | Economic work | Problem addressed |
|---------------|---------------|-------------------|
| Father | Creation | Creature's dependence on Creator |
| Son | Reconciliation | Sinner's enmity against God |
| Holy Spirit | **Redemption** | Man's incapacity to receive/inhabit revelation |

**Redemption is eschatological; reconciliation is present**: "Alongside this New Testament understanding of reconciliation ... there stands a second understanding of salvation ... which we can sum up in the concept of redemption or liberation or deliverance" (*CD* I.1, lines 5439–5441). Reconciliation has been accomplished in the event of God's revelation in Jesus Christ; redemption remains a future hope — the "glorious liberty of the children of God" (Rom. 8:21) that the Spirit inaugurates but does not yet fully realize.

**Redemption as freedom and divine sonship**: The Spirit's redemptive work is formally "freedom" (2 Cor. 3:17; Gal. 5:1) — specifically freedom *for* God and "for the glorious liberty of the children of God" (lines 7860–7862). Materially it is divine sonship: by the Spirit man becomes *capax verbi divini* (capable of the divine Word) and a child of God. "He is the child of God. As such he is free, he can believe" (*CD* I.1, lines 7871–7872). The Spirit of adoption (*pneuma huiothesias*, Rom. 8:15) enables the cry "Abba, Father!" — the distilled content of redemptive life.

**Redemption as eschatological sign**: All statements about the man who receives the Spirit are eschatological statements — they refer not to a present human possession but to what God has eternally determined and what will be fully manifest at the *eschaton*. "The Spirit remains the Holy Spirit, wholly and utterly the Spirit of promise" (*CD* I.1, lines 8251–8252). What man experiences in the present is "a human sign" of what God has given; the fully realized freedom of the children of God remains future. This prevents the turn of pneumatology into anthropology: "to have the Holy Spirit is to let God rather than our having God be our confidence" (lines 8132–8134).

**Why redemption requires the Spirit's full deity**: If the Spirit were a creature, man would retain a "reciprocal relation" to revelation — some capacity of freedom or cooperation that co-constitutes the reception of the Word. The Spirit's full deity eliminates this: "man can be present at God's revelation only as a servant is present at his master's work" (*CD* I.1, lines 8395–8396). The Spirit's immanent being (Eternal Spirit, §12.2) must correspond to His economic work: He is not made the Spirit of God in revelation but *is* antecedently and eternally the Spirit of God.

## Cross-Tradition Perspectives

- **Pauline theology**: *apolytrosis* encompasses freedom from sin (Rom. 3:24), from the law (Gal. 3:13), from death (Rom. 8:23), and the final renewal of the body. Paul uses both *apolytrosis* (accomplished) and future *apolytrosis* (awaited), reflecting the "already/not yet" tension Barth inherits.
- **Reformation**: Luther's "freedom of a Christian" emphasizes liberation from law as a means of justification — an emphasis Barth incorporates into his account of Spirit-freedom while insisting that freedom is *for* God, not a new human autonomy.
- **Liberal Protestantism**: Tends to collapse redemption into reconciliation or into moral transformation within history, losing the eschatological reserve. Barth's insistence on the eschatological character of all Spirit-statements is directed against this tendency.
- **Later Barth (CD I.1 plan)**: The projected but unwritten *Church Dogmatics* V was titled "The Doctrine of Redemption." CD I.1 §12 provides the structural groundwork.
