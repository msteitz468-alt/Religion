# Communicatio Idiomatum

The **communicatio idiomatum** ("communication/exchange of properties") is the Christological rule
that, because there is **one Person** subsisting in two natures (the [[hypostatic-union|hypostatic
union]]), the properties of *either* nature may be predicated of the one **Person** of
[[jesus|Christ]]. [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] gives the rule its precise scholastic form in the
[[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Tertia Pars Q16 (twelve articles on "what may be predicated of Christ").

## Aquinas's rules of predication (ST III Q16)

- **"God is man" and "Man is God" are both true** (A1–A2): "admitted by all Christians." The term
  "God" stands for the Person who has the human nature; the term "man" stands for the suppositum of
  human nature, which is the Person of the Son. Against Nestorius, who would divide
  the predicates between two subjects.
- **Concrete of concrete, not abstract of abstract** (A4–A5): what belongs to the human nature is
  predicated of the **Person** ("the Son of God suffered, was born, died") because the concrete term
  signifies the suppositum — but *not* of the divine nature in the abstract ("the Godhead suffered"
  is false; "the Godhead is the manhood" is false). The natures remain distinct even as their
  properties meet in the one Person.
- **"God was made man" is true; "Man was made God" is false** (A6–A7): the "making" terminates in the
  assumed humanity; but the suppositum of whom "to be God" is verified is the eternal Son, who was
  always God, so He cannot be said to have "begun to be God" (Damascene: "we do not say that man was
  deified, but that God was humanized").
- **Qualified predicates require the rider "in His human nature"** (A8–A12): "Christ is a creature,"
  "this Man began to be," "Christ as man is less than the Father" — said *absolutely* these lapse into
  Arianism; they are true only with the reduplicative *secundum quod homo*
  ("as man," referring to the nature). "Words spoken amiss lead to heresy" (Jerome).

## Significance

The communicatio idiomatum is the linguistic and doctrinal payoff of the hypostatic union: it secures
that it is genuinely *God* who is born of [[mary|Mary]] (hence *Theotokos*), who suffers, and who dies
for our salvation (see [[atonement]]) — while preserving the integrity and distinction of the two
natures. The same rule governs Marian doctrine (the divine motherhood) and the worship of Christ's
humanity with **latria** (see [[incarnation]]). The Lutheran tradition would later extend the
communicatio further (the *genus maiestaticum*, grounding ubiquity for its eucharistic theology) than
the Thomist account, which keeps the exchange strictly at the level of the Person.
