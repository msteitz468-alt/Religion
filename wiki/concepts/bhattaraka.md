## Definition

The **bhattāraka** (Sanskrit: "learned man"; cf. cognate Tamil *paṭṭar* used of Hindu temple priests) is the pivotal clerical-administrative figure of medieval and early-modern [[digambara|Digambara]] Jainism. Dundas describes him as "the pivotal figure in medieval Digambara Jainism" (ch. 5). The institution emerged as a practical response to the pressures of lay community life, altered political structures, and — according to one account — Muslim persecution, ultimately displacing the ideal of the naked wandering monk as the primary focus of Digambara religious authority.

## Origins and Development

The institution arose from the ancient practice of *dāna* (almsgiving and temporary shelter to ascetics), which over time evolved into the gifting of "rock-cut caverns" and the "building alongside temples of monasteries which were accompanied by substantial land endowments." This process gradually transformed the ancient ideal, with ascetics "accommodating themselves to their lay followers by adopting a quasi-householder mode of life."

In origin, the bhattāraka was "the head of any group of naked monks which lived permanently in one of the monasteries (maṭha) which had begun to be built near temple complexes from about the fifth century CE." The institutional logic was accelerated when a thirteenth-century bhattāraka — reporting Muslim persecution — "proclaimed that owing to Muslim persecution monks could wear clothes when outside the monastery and remove them when they returned to it."

The result was that "Digambara asceticism came to be associated in the medieval period less with naked monks than with the bhattārakas, who were celibate clerics who had received only token monastic initiation, were linked to pontifical centres, taking hereditary names from their predecessor, wearing orange robes both inside and outside the monastery and taking them off in honour of the ancient ideal only when eating and when initiating another bhattāraka."

## Functions and Role

The bhattāraka's primary functions included:
- **Conducting rituals** and supervising the lay performance of vows
- **Overseeing monastery affairs** and maintaining libraries
- **Religious education**
- **Community representation**: acting as "a mediating figure when dealing with Hindu rājas or the Muslim authorities"
- **Image consecration**: at the end of the fifteenth century, a bhattāraka in Rajasthan "consecrated a thousand images of fordmakers and sent them round India to repair the damage done by Muslim iconoclasm"

The bhattāraka "was very much like a king himself. He sat on a pontifical throne, enjoyed trappings of office and a great deal of pomp and ceremony attended his public activities." The bhattāraka of Kolhapur "built the gate of his monastery of equal height to the city gate … making a specific point about his equality of status with the local rāja."

## Sectarian Dimension

The bhattārakas maintained the vestigial Digambara sectarian divisions — principally between the Mūla Saṅgha and the Kāṣṭhā Saṅgha — sometimes across centuries. A seventeenth-century bhattāraka of Surat (Kāṣṭhā Saṅgha) doctored a manuscript copy of Devasena's critique, deleting objections to his sect and substituting abuse of the Mūla Saṅgha. Bhattāraka thrones inherited hereditary names (e.g., Cārukirti at Mūḍbiḍri and [[shravana-belgola|Śravaṇa Beḷgoḷa]]; Devendrakirti at Hombuja), reflecting the pontifical and territorial nature of the institution.

## Scale and Decline

"In the medieval period, there were thirty-six bhattāraka thrones in various parts of India." Most faded "to insignificance by the early twentieth century or even more recently" (e.g., Mahāvīrjī in Rajasthan, defunct in the 1970s). The most important surviving thrones are in south Karnataka: Mūḍbiḍri, [[shravana-belgola|Śravaṇa Beḷgoḷa]], and Hombuja. Two in Kolhapur also retain significance.

## Evaluation

Later Digambara writers stigmatised the institution as "a sign of decadence, an unhappy compromise in the corrupt world age when ascetic values were declining." Dundas presents a more functional reading: in an age when "the community of naked monks was gradually dwindling away, the bhattārakas played a vital role, not always based on any conspicuous spirituality, in the perpetuation of Digambara Jainism." Although formal intercession with government on behalf of the Jain community is no longer required, bhattārakas today "still play an important part in the promotion of Jainism, both through travelling inside and outside India and through general educational and publishing work."

## Cross-links

The bhattāraka institution is structurally parallel to the [[temple-dwelling-monks|temple-dwelling (caityavāsin) monks]] of the Svetambara tradition: both represent a pragmatic accommodation of the lay-ascetic relationship at the expense of strict wandering mendicancy.
