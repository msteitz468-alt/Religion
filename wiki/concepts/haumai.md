# Haumai

**Haumai** (Punjabi: "I am" — literally "I myself") is the term in Sikh theology for self-centredness, ego, or pride — the fundamental human predicament that causes suffering and perpetuates the cycle of death and rebirth (*transmigration*). It is the antithesis of submission to [[hukam|hukam]] and the principal obstacle to liberation.

## McLeod's Account

McLeod locates haumai as the root cause of the human condition in [[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]]'s system. Within the human *man* (the inner faculty — "a complex comprising heart, mind and spirit" ~L513–516), "evil exercises its vicious sway, and, seduced by the passions it generates, most are blind to the spiritual reality that lies within and about them" (~L518–521). "Driven by their evil impulses, they behave in a manner which, in accordance with the law of karma, earns appropriate penalties … The fundamental problem is **haumai** or self-centred concern for all that attracts the proud, the sensual and the selfish" (~L523–534).

The structural consequence is binding: "As long as the man is in the grip of haumai there can be no hope of escape. The endless round must continue" (~L535–537).

## Haumai and the Remedy

The solution to haumai is [[nam-simaran|nam simaran]]: "The person who learns how to appropriate the nam will be freed from the chains which bind that person to the wheel of transmigration" (~L542–543). Equally, a knowledge of the divine Name can be attained "because Akal Purakh is a God of grace, speaking the Word of divine understanding to all who are prepared to **shed their haumai** and listen in humility" (~L598–600). Shedding haumai is thus the precondition for receiving grace, not a precondition imposed before grace is offered — the two movements (divine grace, human response) are simultaneous.

## Sant-Tradition Background

The concept of haumai belongs to the broader inheritance Nanak received from the [[sant-tradition|Sant tradition]]. The Sants uniformly rejected external forms of piety as expressions of ego and wrong-orientation — trusting in temples, mosques, pilgrimages, or sacred scriptures rather than turning inward. Such reliance on externals was seen as a refined form of self-will: putting one's trust in one's own capacity to perform acts of merit rather than surrendering to the formless divine. Nanak sharpened and systematized this anti-haumai orientation into a coherent soteriological framework.
