# Exorcism

Exorcism — the expulsion of demonic spirits — is a recurring feature of [[jesus|Jesus]]' activity
in [[matthew|Matthew]], and the occasion of the gospel's sharpest controversy over the source of
his power (12:22-37). This page records the treatment of the theme in R. T. France, *The Gospel of
Matthew* (NICNT, 2007), the wiki's sole ingested source on it; interpretive claims are attributed
to [[rt-france|France]] throughout.

## Exorcism in the Ancient Setting

France notes that exorcism was an accepted feature of the activity of contemporary Jewish and
pagan holy men, yet that specific exorcism narratives are relatively few — so that Jesus "appears
in Christian sources as the exorcist par excellence." He observes that both the Beelzebul
controversy (12:22-32) and the parable of the returning spirit (12:43-45) indicate that the
reality of demon-possession and the need for exorcism "were taken for granted by both Jesus and
his audience."

France's distinguishing observation concerns method. In contrast with "the elaborate incantations
and techniques used by other exorcists at the time," Jesus expels demons simply "with a command" —
the same wording as the centurion's, again emphasizing authority. He emphasizes the "striking lack
of the quasimagical formulae and techniques (including the control of the demon by discovering its
name)," and notes that in Matthew's abbreviation even the command is only implied by the demons'
response. He distinguishes exorcism from physical healing generally: "Exorcism, as distinct from
physical healing, presupposes a hostile supernatural force" counterable only by a more powerful
spiritual authority, and Jewish exorcists were understood to act by God's power.

France also tracks Matthew's terminology as consistent: at 9:32-34 demonic possession causes
physical disability yet Matthew still speaks of exorcism, "as compared with 12:22 where in a
similar situation Matthew uses the verb 'heal'"; and dumbness is not always attributed to
possession (15:30-31). Of Matthew's borrowing of the Marcan phrase "unclean spirits" at 10:1
(elsewhere only 12:43) France declines to find significance: "it is simply an acceptable
synonym."

## The Gadarene Demoniacs (8:28-34)

France observes that the two possessed men are "little more than part of the scenery" — Jesus'
dialogue is with the demons — whereas the pigs are retained despite Matthew's usual abbreviation,
in order to prove the exorcism's reality and to explain the locals' hostility. He holds that
Matthew retains the tombs because "it was appropriate that unclean spirits should live in an
unclean place, just as they will later go into unclean animals (pigs)."

He reads the demons' *pro kairou* as their recognition that their licensed time is limited and
that Jesus' arrival begins the end: "Jesus in his earthly ministry is already introducing the
eschatological 'cleanup' of the forces of evil." On the fate of the demons France argues that
Matthew neither says nor implies that they died with the pigs — the plural "died" must share the
subject of "stampeded," and "demons, as supernatural beings, could not be destroyed by drowning."
He treats the pigs' behaviour as recorded for visible proof of the demons' departure, citing
parallels in Josephus (*Ant.* 8.48, an overturned basin) and Philostratus (*Vit. Apoll.* 4.20, a
knocked-over statue).

France notes that Matthew drops Mark's rehabilitation and commissioning of the demoniac, and draws
the contrast: "This is not a story about mission but about power." Among Jews the miracles attract
followers; in the Decapolis the locals want Jesus gone.

## The Beelzebul Controversy (12:22-37)

France notes that the trigger here is not an act criticizable in itself; unable to deny Jesus'
power, "the Pharisees, unable to deny his power, question its source." He frames the logic of the
accusation: "Supernatural power demands a supernatural source," and if the accusers will not admit
it is divine, there is only one alternative. He characterizes the charge as moving beyond
"academic" debate "to the realm of personal abuse and character assassination," intended to
destroy Jesus' credibility with a God-fearing public, and notes it was potentially extremely
serious "since sorcery was, according to the Mishnah, a capital offense." He reports that such a
charge remained for some centuries "a staple element in rabbinic Jewish polemic against Jesus as a
magician" who by black arts led Israel astray.

France reconstructs the tradition source-critically: vv. 25-29 cohere as direct responses to the
charge, while v. 30 (cf. Mark 9:40) and the unforgivable-sin saying of vv. 31-32 may have been
originally independent, Luke placing the latter in a different context (12:10).

### The divided kingdom

France summarizes the first counterargument as commonsense: "it is absurd to imagine that the
demon king would attack and defeat his own demonic forces," and civil war in the demonic kingdom
"can only be a recipe for disaster."

### "Your sons"

France reads 12:27 as an ad hominem argument that takes for granted that genuine exorcisms occur
in Jewish circles unconnected with Jesus; "'your sons' need not mean the actual sons of those to
whom he is speaking, but 'members of your community.'" He specifies where Jesus' uniqueness lies:
it "consists in the nature and authority of his exorcisms, not in the lack of any other
exorcists."

### "By the Spirit of God"

France notes that actual exorcism narratives do not elsewhere refer directly to the Spirit,
preferring Jesus' authoritative word of command, but that Matthew's framework lets the reader
infer the Spirit as the source of that authority. He observes that Luke has "the finger of God"
(echoing Exod 8:19) where Matthew needs the direct reference to the Spirit, both as a pickup from
v. 18 and as the basis for the charge in vv. 31-32. France holds the switch to "kingdom of God"
here — one of five Matthean occurrences — is required both to balance "Spirit of God" and by the
preceding "Satan's kingdom," so that "Jesus' saying thus vividly contrasts the personal kings of
the two kingdoms." He argues the aorist *phthanō* ("has caught up with") carries the same
implication as the perfect of *engizō* at 3:2; 4:17; 10:7: "God's kingship is already a reality."
He adds that the kingdom's coming is joy to those who embrace it but a threat to opponents, having
"caught up with" the [[pharisees|Pharisees]] and "breaking uncomfortably into their cozily
controlled world of tradition and turning everything upside down."

**France flags a difficulty in his own argument.** If Jesus' exorcisms carry eschatological
significance, why not those of other Jewish exorcists who also operate by God's Spirit? "This
powerful challenge follows strangely after v. 27." His proposed resolution is that no extant
literature records anyone else performing exorcisms on such a scale and with such decisive
authority, as against "the often bizarre rituals to which other exorcists resorted"; he quotes Luz
(2:124) for "something completely new and qualitatively different."

### The strong man

France identifies the strong-man imagery with Isa 49:24-25, where it symbolizes God's rescue of
his people from oppressors, so that Jesus' exorcisms are "a direct assault" on
[[satan|Satan]]'s possessions. He insists that "The tying up represents not an exorcistic
technique, but the comprehensive superiority of Jesus' authority" over Satan's, and thus the
coming into force of God's kingship. He then uses the binding to answer his own v. 27/v. 28
tension: "It is that 'tying up' that distinguishes Jesus' all-out assault on Satan's kingdom from
the little local forays of other exorcists of the time."

### The unforgivable sin (12:31-32)

France's headline verdict is that the saying "has often been inappropriately, and sometimes
disastrously, applied to contexts which have little to do with its original setting," and he flags
in advance the interpretive stake: "to recognize the specific setting of this troubling saying may
be important for its proper understanding," noting that Mark's editorial comment at 3:30 ties it
to the Beelzebul charge and that Matthew supports this by retaining the setting. He makes the
contextual argument explicit: "The opening 'therefore' indicates that in this context blasphemy
against the Holy Spirit ... is to be understood in terms of the Pharisees' charge in v. 24."

He states the crux of the offence as being that "they are deliberately denigrating the work not
just of a human being, but of the Spirit of God," and locates the unforgivability precisely: "it
is this diametrical opposition to the good purpose of God which is ultimately unforgivable" — the
thing that shows these Pharisees decisively "against" Jesus (v. 30). He draws the contrast that
gives the charge its weight: "the significance of Jesus' exorcisms was plain for all to see (v.
28); there could be no excuse for misinterpreting this work of the Holy Spirit and attributing it
to Beelzebul."

France's pastoral summary is that "This saying is a wake-up call to the arrogant, not a bogey to
frighten those of tender conscience." On the two ages he glosses them as Jewish terms applying
primarily to the contrast between this life and the next rather than to successive phases of life
on earth, so that the consequences of the sin extend past judgment (see
[[last-judgment|Last Judgment]]).

France links the leaders' failure to recognize the new phase to their being more culpable than
Israel's pagan neighbours, and to standing "perilously close to that fundamental rebellion against
God which cannot be forgiven."

## The Mute Demoniac and the Returning Spirit

The mute demoniac of 9:32-34 France treats as the pericope more properly called a Matthean doublet
(with 12:22-24), since little in it is distinctive — a judgment he sets against the usual view; see
[[miracles|Miracles]] for the surrounding argument.

On the parable of the returning spirit (12:43-45) France notes, "Contrary to the traditions of St.
Antony of Egypt (and the remarks of most commentators here), this demon is not comfortable out in
the desert" and is only truly at home with a human host. He glosses the seven other spirits as
"the number of perfection," recruited to render the cleaned house unclean enough to be habitable
again, and observes that the story is told "with wry humor."

**France attaches an explicit methodological caution**: "It is probably unwise to use this folksy
parable as in its own right a guide to demonology," since its function is to illustrate the danger
facing "this generation." He insists the rhetoric requires only that "this generation" be *like* a
newly exorcised person, not that they have been possessed; their "liberation" was seeing and
hearing a new power and orientation in Jesus' ministry. He reads the warning as bearing on
[[discipleship|discipleship]]: the void is to be filled by discipleship and specifically by the
Spirit of God, since "Half-hearted repentance without a new commitment will not last."

## Exorcism and the Disciples' Commission

The Twelve receive authority over unclean spirits at 10:1 — see
[[twelve-apostles|The Twelve Apostles]]. France notes that "heal all sorts of illnesses and
disabilities" is repeated verbatim from the summary of Jesus' own ministry, "showing that the
mission of the disciples is to be a continuation of that of Jesus."
