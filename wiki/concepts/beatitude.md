# Beatitude (The Last End of Man)

> **Not to be confused with** [[beatitudes|The Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–10)]], the macarisms opening the Sermon on the Mount. This page concerns the scholastic concept of *beatitudo* as the final end of man.

**Beatitude** (*beatitudo*, happiness, blessedness) is the final end of human life and the
starting point of [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s moral theology. The whole *Prima Secundae* of the
[[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] opens with it (I-II, Q1–5), because "the end is the rule of
whatever is ordained to the end." The structure mirrors the *exitus–reditus* of the work: having
treated God and the procession of creatures from God (Prima Pars), Aquinas now treats the rational
creature's movement *back* to God, beginning with the end that movement is for.

## Every agent acts for an end; man for a last end (Q1)

Properly human acts — those proceeding from deliberate will — are necessarily done for an end,
"since the object of the will is the end and the good" (Q1, A1). There must be a **last end**, for
an infinite regress of ends is impossible (Q1, A4), and a person can have only one last end, which
must so "fill man's appetite that nothing is left besides it" (Q1, A5). Crucially (Q1, A7), Aquinas
distinguishes the **formal aspect** of the last end (complete fulfillment — which *all* men desire)
from the **thing in which** it is realized (about which they disagree). Sinners do not abandon the
intention of happiness; they misidentify its locus. God is the last end as the thing desired, but
only the rational creature attains Him "by knowing and loving God" (Q1, A8).

## What happiness is not, and is (Q2–3)

Aquinas systematically excludes wealth, honor, fame, power, bodily goods, pleasure, and any created
good from being man's happiness: "God alone constitutes man's happiness," since every creature is
good only by participation (Q2, A8). Positively, happiness is an **operation** (Q3, A2) — and
specifically an act of the **speculative intellect** contemplating divine things (Q3, A5), with
delight in the will following as a concomitant (Q3, A4). Its perfect form "can consist in nothing
else than the vision of the Divine Essence" — the [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]] (Q3, A8). The
intellect's natural desire rests only when it reaches the *essence* of the first cause, not merely
its existence.

**Imperfect vs. perfect happiness.** The happiness attainable in this life (contemplation, virtue)
is real but imperfect — "we call men happy, but only as men"; perfect happiness is the next-life
vision (Q3, A2; Q5, A3).

## Attainment requires grace (Q5)

Man *can* attain happiness (Q5, A1), and one may be happier than another in degree though not in
object (Q5, A2). But "the vision of God's Essence surpasses the nature not only of man, but also of
every creature," so **no creature can attain final happiness by its natural powers** (Q5, A5) — God
alone bestows it (Q5, A6). Once attained it cannot be lost, since the vision of God concomitantly
rectifies the will (Q5, A4, against Origen). Good works (merits) are required, not as efficient
cause but because divine wisdom orders man to his end through his own ordered movements (Q5, A7).

## Relation to the Aristotelian tradition

The treatise reworks Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (happiness as the activity of the highest
faculty, contemplation) but transposes its terminus from the philosopher's this-worldly *eudaimonia*
to the supernatural vision of God, attainable only by grace — a fusion of Aristotelian *telos* with
the Augustinian *visio Dei* (cf. [[beatific-vision]] on Augustine).
