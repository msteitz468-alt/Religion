# Saṃsāra

**Saṃsāra** ("wandering / faring on") is the beginningless cycle of birth, death, and
**rebirth** that, in the Indian traditions, all unliberated beings undergo. In
[[buddhism|Buddhism]] it is the condition the path is meant to end: liberation
([[nirvana|nirvana]]) is precisely **release from saṃsāra**.

## In Buddhism

Saṃsāra is driven by craving (*taṇhā*) and ignorance (*avijjā*) and structured by
**[[karma|kamma]]** — intentional action whose ripening conditions future birth. Its
mechanism is **[[dependent-origination|dependent origination]]** (the twelve *nidānas*),
which traces how ignorance leads, link by link, to birth, aging, and death, and back
again. The Buddha taught that the round has no discoverable beginning and that beings have
wandered through it across countless lives.

Beings are reborn (without a transmigrating soul — see **[[anatta|non-self]]**) into the
**six realms** of traditional cosmology: gods (*devas*), titans (*asuras*), humans,
animals, hungry ghosts (*pretas*), and hell-beings. [[bhikkhu-bodhi|Bodhi]]'s anthology
devotes its fifth chapter ("The Way to a Fortunate Rebirth") to the *mundane* path of merit
and generosity that secures a good rebirth — a step below, but on the way to, the
[[noble-eightfold-path|supramundane path]] that ends rebirth altogether.

## Rebirth Without a Self

The distinctive Buddhist puzzle: if there is **[[anatta|no self]]**, what is reborn? The
canonical answer is that rebirth is the continuation of a *causal process*, not the
migration of a substance — "neither the same nor another" (*na ca so na ca añño*). A
craving-laden mental continuum conditions a new birth as one flame lights another, the
analogy [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]] and the [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] later press under the
[[two-truths|two truths]].

## Brāhmaṇa Precursors: Recurrent Death (*Punarmrtyu*)

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 6) traces the earliest textual anxiety
about what will become saṃsāra to the **Brāhmaṇas** (c. 800–500 BCE). What the Brāhmaṇas feared was not
death once, but **recurrent death** (*punarmrtyu* / *jaramṛtyu*, "old age and death") — a series of re-deaths
and the rebirths that are preludes to them.

Doniger's key intervention: the Euro-American comfort of reincarnation ("another chance at life") entirely
misreads the Hindu doctrine. If dying once is terrible, **dying over and over again is catastrophically
worse** — like being condemned to numerous life sentences running consecutively, not concurrently. The
Brāhmaṇas already mention transmigration: "When they die they come to life again, but they become the food
of this [Death] again and again." Rebirth is the *problem*, not the solution.

The Brāhmaṇic *answer* was sacrificial: through correctly performed sacrifice, a man could build a body in
heaven and "conquer recurring death." But the warrant is always conditional — "whoever knows this conquers
redeath" — and the catch-22 haunts it: one may die before completing the sacrifice that grants immortality.

**The Bhrigu-to-Nachiketas trajectory.** The Brāhmaṇic text of Bhrigu's journey to the other world (where
he witnesses humans being eaten by animals — reversal of the order of the sacrifice) is retold in a second
Brāhmaṇic text as the **Nachiketas** story: the boy Nachiketas is given to Death by his father; Death
teaches him how to avoid re-death. This Brāhmaṇic Nachiketas story is the direct precursor to the
**Kaṭha Upaniṣad**, which transforms what was a sacrificial/ritual answer into a philosophical one —
knowledge of the ātman is the true escape from recurrent death. The Upaniṣads transform Bhrigu's vision of
"what you eat in the other world" into the doctrine of karma and rebirth *in this world*.

## Upaniṣadic Development (Dasgupta, Vol. I, Ch. III)

[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] traces transmigration emerging in the [[upanishads|
Upaniṣads]] in two stages out of the older Vedic idea of an other-world recompense. The doctrine of the
**two paths** distinguishes the **way of the fathers** (*pitṛyāna*) — those who did charitable works pass
through smoke and the dark half-month to the **moon**, are recompensed, then **return** to be reborn —
from the **way of the gods** (*devayāna*) — the faithful and ascetic pass through regions of increasing
light to **Brahman, never to return**. Alongside this runs [[yajnavalkya|Yājñavalkya]]'s pure
transmigration account (Bṛh. IV.4): at death the self gathers its senses, and "just as a caterpillar
reaching a blade's tip draws itself over, … just as a goldsmith refashions gold into a newer, fairer
form," the self builds a new body shaped by its deeds and desires. Critically, **desire (*kāma*), not
karma, is named as the cause** (see [[karma|karma]]); karma is only the link. The self that is reborn is
a unity of moral-psychological tendencies *and* the physical elements — "the whole logic of the genesis
of karma and its fruits is held up within him."

## Doniger's Reading — Overcrowding and the Two Paths (Ch. 7)

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 7) reads the Upaniṣadic doctrine of
transmigration through two lenses that the tradition itself supplies:

**The overcrowding hypothesis.** The Upaniṣadic discussion of transmigration literally begins when a teacher
asks: "Do you know why the world beyond is not filled up, even when more and more people continuously go
there?" Doniger suggests this anxiety of *overcrowding* — the claustrophobia of an unprecedented urban
density in the Ganges Valley — may underlie the *theory* of reincarnation as a recycling solution. Herodotus
(5th c. BCE) called Indians "the most populous country on earth." The movements to renounce the cities may
have expressed a nostalgia for the open pastoral world, preserved in the texts but no longer lived: the
Āraṇyakas ("Jungle Books"), the village settings of the Upaniṣads, the forest imagery of early Buddhism.

**The two paths — and the third.** The Upaniṣads describe three possible post-mortem trajectories: (1) the
**path of flame** — those who know the Upaniṣadic doctrine pass through fire, day, sun, into the world of
brahman, and do **not return**; (2) the **path of smoke** — those who give gifts and perform sacrifices pass
through smoke, night, moon, become food for the gods, then rain, plants, food again, are reborn as humans in
wombs whose quality tracks the quality of their behavior; (3) the **third state** — those who take neither
path become "worms, insects, or snakes" cycling "ceaselessly." As the Chandogya adds: "A person should take
measures to avoid that" (CU 5.10.1–8).

**Samsara's positive face.** The dominant Orientalist view saw the Upaniṣads as univocally world-denying.
Doniger resists this. The *path of smoke*, of Vedic generosity and procreation and samsara, "survives intact
the journey from the Vedas to the Upanishads." The Upaniṣads contain eroticized passages of striking
openness: a woman's genitals are analogized to the sacrificial fire; sexual bliss is the closest available
approximation to deep dreamless sleep; a husband who wants his wife to love him uses a tender mantra — but if
he does *not* want to father a child, there is also a contraception mantra, "an intention that flies in the
face of the dharma texts." The two paths — flame and smoke, Release and rebirth, renunciation and the
"whole catastrophe" of family life — coexist as live options throughout the history of Hinduism.

## Cross-Tradition Note

Saṃsāra is shared vocabulary across **Hinduism** (escape via *moksha* and realization of
*ātman*/*brahman*), **Jainism** (escape by exhausting karmic matter), and **Sikhism**. The
Buddhist version is unique in denying the permanent self that the others make the vehicle of
liberation — making Buddhist saṃsāra a process "all the way down."
