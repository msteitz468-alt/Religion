# Anuvrata

## Definition

The *anuvratas* ("Lesser Vows") are the five foundational ethical commitments undertaken by the Jain layman (*sravaka*), forming the core of the twelvefold lay vow system. They parallel in content the five Great Vows (*mahavrata*) binding on ascetics, but are scaled to the conditions of householder life rather than demanding complete renunciation. As Dundas notes, the lay vows "parallel the five ascetic Great Vows both in their content and in the lifelong commitment which they require" (ch. 7).

In practice, it is "very rare today for anybody to take the Lesser Vows formally" (ch. 7), but they remain the theoretical backbone of canonical lay ethics and the basis of modern handbooks for Jain lay people.

## The Five Lesser Vows

**1. Non-violence (*ahimsa*).** While the ascetic must avoid any act of violence whatsoever, the layman must "try to the best of his ability to avoid any pointless destruction of life-forms." This is operationalised primarily through the regulation of livelihood. Monastic theoreticians prohibit occupations involving destruction of life — "gaining a livelihood through destroying plants, digging, milling, excessive use of fire or water, and breeding livestock or selling their products" (ch. 7). In practice, Jain lay people today do not generally scrutinise industrial or political activities through the lens of *ahimsa*, "placing the emphasis instead, if challenged, on their purity of intention" (ch. 7).

**2. Truth (*satya*).** Applies specifically to "social and business dealings," requiring avoidance of "sharp business practice such as wilfully misrepresenting the qualities of the goods one is selling" (ch. 7).

**3. Non-theft (*asteya*).** Prohibits stealing and is extended by some modern writers to include tax evasion.

**4. Chastity (*brahmacarya*).** Recognises that complete celibacy is impossible for a householder and instead makes it incumbent upon him "to restrict himself to one wife and curb his sex drive and, if possible, renounce sexual activity completely after the birth of a son" (ch. 7).

**5. Non-possessiveness (*aparigraha*).** Rather than abandoning all possessions, the layman "should not be over-attached to his wealth and, instead of hoarding it or glorying in it, he should lead a simple life and dispose of any surplus money by religious giving" (ch. 7).

## Supplementary Vows

The anuvratas are supplemented by three *gunavratas* ("Subsidiary Vows") and four *siksavratas* ("Vows of Instruction"), together forming the twelvefold system.

The **gunavratas** involve: (a) restriction of excessive travel to minimise destruction of life-forms; (b) avoidance of excessive enjoyment of food, clothes, etc.; and (c) abandonment of "deleterious forms of activity such as futile speculation, moping or idle and self-indulgent practices" (ch. 7).

The **siksavratas** are positive religious obligations: (a) restriction of one's activities to a defined area for a set period; (b) regular performance of the *samayika* ritual; (c) undertaking fasts on fixed lunar calendar days; and (d) "the performance of all sorts of religious and charitable giving" (ch. 7).

## The Lay Path and Its Stages (*pratima*)

An earlier model of the layman's life, formalised in the *Upasakadasanga* and elsewhere, described eleven successive stages (*pratima*) in which the layman "progressively abandons worldly needs and finally, after leaving his household, devotes himself to fasting and contemplation" (ch. 7). This ascetically oriented model became "largely otiose by the medieval period," though some modern lay people claim to pursue it.

The ideal lay person (*mahasravaka*) as depicted by Hemacandra "intensely pursues the twelvefold vow in its totality, with particular reference to religious giving both to ascetics and to the community by means of the building of temples and the installation of images, and carefully compartmentalises every moment of his day for religious purposes" (ch. 7). Hemacandra's account remains influential and "still forms the basis of publications produced by image-worshipping Svetambara ascetics for lay consumption today" (ch. 7).

## Terminology: Upasaka vs. Sravaka

A significant terminological shift marks the development of lay Jain identity. *Upasaka* ("one who attends, serves") reflects an early model of the layman as significant primarily "through catering to the needs of monks and partial imitation of them." *Sravaka* ("hearer") emphasises the layman as someone who "not only listens to the doctrine being expounded and acts upon it but who is also a listener by virtue of performance of worship in the temple, each example of which replicates the preaching assembly (*samavasarana*) of the fordmakers" (ch. 7). The term *sravaka* eventually displaced *upasaka* in general usage, reflecting a broader conception of lay religiosity centred on worship as well as service.

## Tension Between Ascetic Ideal and Lay Reality

Monastic theorists consistently characterised the layman as "by his very nature continually implicated in violence and destruction, even when he is acting for ostensibly pious motives" (ch. 7), pointing out that even feeding monks, building temples, and performing worship technically involve harm to life-forms. The monk's solution is renunciation; the layman's is purity of intention and public piety. What matters in practice is "not precise conformity to a canonical pattern of religiosity ... but rather the manifestation of pious intentions and correct ethical dispositions through public participation in religious ceremonies, worship and community activities" (ch. 7).
