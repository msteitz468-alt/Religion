# Inspiration of Scripture (Patristic Theories)

The early Church universally held that the Scriptures — both Testaments equally — were inspired by the Holy Spirit, but it developed competing accounts of *how* the Spirit acted upon the human authors. The spectrum runs from a "possession" or ecstatic model (the author as a passive instrument) to an orthodox correction insisting the authors retained full consciousness and free will, with Augustine and Theodore of Mopsuestia as the two most analytically ambitious theorists. Drawn primarily from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. III.

## The Common Patristic Baseline

The whole Bible, without exception, was regarded as inspired and as a unity — "not a collection of disparate segments, some of divine origin and others of merely human fabrication." The human author "served as God's instrument, and his tongue was…the pen of a ready writer." This inspiration extended to the smallest detail:

- **[[origen]]** and [[gregory-of-nazianzus|Gregory of Nazianzus]] perceived divine wisdom even in the "solecisms" of Scripture; Origen: "There is not one jot or tittle written in the Bible which does not accomplish its special work for those capable of using it."
- **Jerome**: "in the divine Scriptures every word, syllable, accent and point is packed with meaning."
- **[[john-chrysostom|Chrysostom]]**: even genealogies and chronological figures have profound value — he devoted two homilies to the salutations of Romans 16.

## The Ecstatic / Possession Model

Modelled on [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]], who held that prophets lost consciousness when seized by the Spirit, so that "they no longer spoke but God spoke through their lips."

- **Athenagoras**: the prophets prophesied "in a state of ecstasy (*kat' ekstasin*), and the Spirit…breathing through them much as a musician breathes through a pipe."
- The **[[montanism|Montanists]]** were the natural home of this theory — Montanus, Priscilla and Maximilla fell unconscious when prophesying — and **[[tertullian]]** defended it in his Montanist period.
- Echoes survive even in catholic writers: Chrysostom calls John and Paul "musical instruments played upon by the Holy Spirit."

## The Orthodox Correction: Authors Not Merely Passive

A reaction insisted the inspired writers kept their faculties and contributed personally:

- **Hippolytus**: the Word moved the prophets to "clarify their vision and instruct their understanding."
- **Origen**: explicitly rejected comparison with pagan ecstatic oracles; the Spirit "caused the former to apprehend divine truth more clearly without in any way suspending their free will."
- **Epiphanius**: true prophets "were in a state of normal consciousness, in full possession of their faculties, when they wrote."
- **Chrysostom**, **Cyril of Alexandria**, and **Jerome** emphasised the personal contribution, style, and background of Moses, John, and Paul.
- **[[augustine]]**: the evangelists used their own personal reminiscences; the Spirit's role was "to stimulate their memories and preserve them from error."

## The Two Pioneers

Kelly singles out Augustine and Theodore as "more or less isolated pioneers" who actually analysed the *manner* of inspiration; the majority were content to affirm the fact without examining it.

- **Augustine** distinguished three types of vision (corporal, spiritual, intellectual): the Spirit sometimes bestows a direct vision, sometimes instructs the intelligence, and sometimes (e.g. Caiaphas, John 11:51) "prompts him to utter divine truth without knowing it."
- **[[theodore-of-mopsuestia]]** held the Spirit's action "varied from writer to writer," distinguishing the special inspiration of the prophets (an ecstasy focusing the prophet on terrifying visions, affecting sight and then hearing) from the lesser "grace of prudence" given to Solomon — while still affirming all biblical authors wrote under one and the same Spirit.
