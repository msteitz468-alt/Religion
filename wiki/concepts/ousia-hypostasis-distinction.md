# Ousia and Hypostasis (the Cappadocian "Classical Theory")

The distinction between **ousia** (οὐσία — substance, being, essence) and **hypostasis** (ὑπόστασις — individual subsistence, concrete existence) is the central conceptual achievement of the [[cappadocian-fathers|Cappadocian Fathers]] and the foundation of Eastern Christian philosophy from the late 4th century onward. Its classic formula is **"one ousia in three hypostases"** for the Trinity: a single divine being or nature subsisting in three distinct, co-equal, consubstantial (*homoousios*) individual existences (Father, Son, Spirit).

In Johannes Zachhuber's analysis ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], 2020), this conceptual system — which he names **Cappadocian philosophy** and, once it became a shared intellectual *koine* across the East within a few decades, the **"classical theory"** — was not merely a terminological convention but a genuine *philosophy*: a logical and ontological account of being whose validity could be debated independently of the doctrine it was built to support. It is the hinge of his larger argument that Patristic thought was philosophy, and that this philosophy (continuous with Hellenism in its Cappadocian form) was later transformed by Christology into something that broke with ancient metaphysics.

## Background: Why the Distinction Was Needed

The terms were not biblical and had no fixed technical sense. Several pressures converged:

- **Origen** had introduced *hypostasis* into Christian usage to express the Son's real, separate existence from the Father ("two not only in thought but in reality"), and occasionally spoke of "three hypostases." He also distinguished *theos* (god, of the Son) from *ho theos* (the God, the Father) — a derivative, asymmetric account of the divine persons. See [[origen|Origen]].
- The **homoiousians** (Basil of Ancyra, George of Laodicea) argued that the Nicene *homoousios* ("consubstantial") implied collateral beings sharing a prior common substance — making Father and Son "brothers" — and so preferred "like in substance." They upheld "three hypostases" in the Origenist tradition.
- **Athanasius** defended *homoousios* as compatible with the Origenist tradition of graded hypostases, arguing likeness can indicate sameness (as among human beings).
- **[[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinarius of Laodicea]]**, in a philosophically sophisticated letter to the young Basil, proposed a *derivative* account of *ousia* (all humans are "one" as all are Adam; the son of David is David), drawing on the Neoplatonic notion of *ab uno* / *ad unum* (ἀφ' ἑνός / πρὸς ἕν) genera.
- **[[eunomius|Eunomius]]** and the anomoians ("unlike-in-substance" / heteroousians) pressed the binary logic of predication: terms used of Father and Son must be either univocal (implying full equality) or equivocal (homonyms). They held the "ingenerate" first *ousia* could not beget a second *ousia* like itself.

Basil's response to Eunomius — not direct dependence on Athanasius — generated the new theory.

## The Two Versions: Abstract and Concrete

Zachhuber's most important interpretive move is to distinguish **two complementary accounts** within the one Cappadocian theory. (They may also be called grammatical/physical or logical/metaphysical.)

### The Abstract Account (Basil of Caesarea)

Basil's account is **logical, epistemic, and grammatical** — focused on properties, knowledge, and right speech.

- The common divinity of Father and Son is expressed through **shared predicates** used in exactly the same sense (univocally): "whatever the substance of the Father is assumed to be, this must be assumed of the Son's substance as well." Basil deliberately invokes Aristotle's "formula of being" (*logos tēs ousias*, λόγος τῆς οὐσίας) from the *Categories*.
- Univocal predication implies **ontological co-ordination**: Father and Son are "light" in precisely the same sense, so there is no difference of rank or degree in their *ousia*. This was Basil's anti-Eunomian move: identical predicates indicate the *absence* of subordination.
- This created a new problem: if Father and Son share all predicates, how are they *distinguished*? Basil's solution: each *hypostasis* is marked by its own **particular property** (ἰδίωμα, *idiōma*) — fatherhood, sonship, sanctifying power (or: ingenerate/generate, cause/caused).
- The famous formula (Ep. 214, 236): "the relation the common item (*to koinon*) has to the particular (*to idion*), ousia has to hypostasis... each of us partakes of being through the common formula of being, but is one or another through the properties attached to him."

Crucially, Zachhuber stresses, Basil's abstract theory exercised **metaphysical restraint**: it provided a *grammatical rule* for Trinitarian (and creaturely) language without specifying what kind of *thing* the universal is or how it relates to particulars. This restraint helped its political success as a tool for uniting a divided Church — flexible enough that Gregory of Nazianzus could accept it while retaining his more Origenist, derivative emphasis on the Father as cause.

### The Concrete Account (Gregory of Nyssa)

Gregory of Nyssa's own contribution is **physical, metaphysical, and ontological** — an account of real, concrete being, developed most fully in his *cosmological* writings where Trinitarian terminology was not constraining.

- In the *Apology on the Hexaëmeron*, Gregory reads Genesis 1:1 as the simultaneous, non-extensional creation of the **entire ousia of created being** "in an instant" (compared to a geometrical point, atom, instant) — a dynamic seminal totality (a "whole," ὅλον, πλήρωμα) from which individuals (τόδε τι, "this something," Aristotle's term) emerge one by one in a divinely ordered sequence.
- In *On the Creation of Humanity* (ch. 16), the universal "man" of Genesis 1:26 (indefinite, *aoristos*) is **the whole of humanity** contained in the first creation — all humans are "one man" because human nature is a real ontological unity, "as if in one body," realized across history.
- In *To Ablabius* (answering "why not three Gods?"), Gregory boldly claims that, strictly, the universal term "man" should **not be used in the plural**: nature (*physis*) is "one, unified with itself, a precisely undivided monad, not increased by addition nor decreased by subtraction," even though it appears in a multitude (like a people, an army, an assembly; or like the gold of which coins are made — coins are not "golds" but "golden").

For the concrete account, the **continuity** between universal and particular is as important as their distinction; ontological priority belongs to the **unity of being**. This is why Zachhuber insists Cappadocian philosophy is at heart an **ontology of being-as-one**, fully continuous with the Greek emphasis on ontological unity — *not* a "turn to the person" or to the individual.

### The Synthesis (pseudo-Basil, *Epistle 38*)

The so-called **Epistle 38** (*On the Difference between Ousia and Hypostasis*), most likely written by Gregory of Nyssa though transmitted under Basil's name, is the classic statement that fuses the two accounts. It adds to Basil's abstract theory:
1. **Names and objects** as a third pole alongside concepts: the universal name ("man") has an *indefinite/scattered* meaning (ἐσκεδασμένη ἔννοια) — it fails to pick out an individual in a crowd — so proper names are needed.
2. A near-**definition of hypostasis** that shifts from the logic of *universal/particular* (which admits degrees) toward a more **binary distinction of being and existence**: hypostasis is "the conflux of properties around the particular" (συνδρομὴ τῶν ἰδιωμάτων), what gives the otherwise unstable, uncircumscribed common *ousia* its "stability" (στάσις) in a concrete thing. This is close to Porphyry's "bundle theory" of the individual and, Zachhuber argues, plants the seed later thinkers grew toward the medieval **essence/existence** distinction.

## Systematic Structure and Its Tensions

Zachhuber's §2.4 reconstructs the theory's structure and its built-in instabilities — the "Achilles' heel" later thinkers exploited:

- Both the common and the individual properties are seen in the *same* individual (Peter is fully man; the Son is fully God). So is the **hypostasis** the concrete individual, or only the bundle of particularizing properties? And is the concrete individual *also* ousia, or is ousia a "part" contained in it?
- If hypostasis = the concrete individual, then a hypostasis is also an ousia (the universal can be truly predicated of it) — which jars with the neat terminological distinction the Trinitarian settlement required.
- One development path leads (via the Aristotelian commentators' "particular ousia" — the universal as individuated and modified in each particular, "Peter's humanity is his and not Paul's") toward a **particularism** that abolishes the realistic universal — disastrous for Trinitarian theology.
- Another path (ousia as an immanent item in each individual; hypostasis as the primary being in which both universal substance and accidents inhere) anticipates Chalcedonian uses (more than one ousia in one hypostasis) but abandons the concrete account's realism.

These tensions "went largely unnoticed while the predominant concern was the universal," but came to the fore once **Christology forced attention onto the individual as individual** — the subject of the rest of Zachhuber's book.

## Why This Is *Not* a "Turn to the Person"

Against Vladimir Lossky and John Zizioulas (who locate an "ontological revolution," the identification of hypostasis with person, in the Cappadocian Trinity), Zachhuber argues the Cappadocians' individuals "are only hypostases of the 'one man'": they matter *as particulars in their plurality*, not *each in its individuality*. Their lack of interest in particularity-as-such follows mainstream ancient philosophy. The genuine revolution came later, in the Christological controversy — which is why the book is titled *The Rise of Christian Theology and the **End of Ancient Metaphysics***.

## From Cappadocian Theory to "Classical" Koine (5th century)

Within a few decades the Cappadocian framework spread across the Greek East as a shared *koine* — not a school affiliation. Zachhuber's evidence (Ch. 3) is that even theologians rooted in the rival **Alexandrian** and **Antiochene** traditions took it for granted:

- **[[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril of Alexandria]]** (an "unlikely candidate," deeply Athanasian) deploys *ousia*/*physis*/*hypostasis*/*idiōma* and the *Categories*-based univocal-predication argument, and even imports Gregory of Nyssa's *concrete* account (universal natures as real wholes; the unity of humanity as "identity" via descent from Adam).
- **[[theodoret-of-cyrus|Theodoret of Cyrus]]**, his Antiochene antagonist, opens the *Eranistes* by defining the same terms — "the same difference the universal has to the particular, *ousia* has to *hypostasis*" — while insisting it is an autonomous *Christian* philosophy, distinct from the pagans'.

The Cappadocians also became **symbols of a Christian *paideia*** — models for fusing classical rhetorical culture with Christian commitment — which carried with it the acceptance of applying technical (Aristotelian-Porphyrian) conceptual tools to doctrine.

## The Christological Crisis (forward pointer)

Trinitarian doctrine needed *unity despite plural hypostases*; Christology needed *a viable theory of the individual* to explain the one Christ in whom two consubstantial natures (divine and human) meet in one hypostasis. The two doctrines set up nearly **opposite** conceptual demands. Because the classical theory had **no account of individuation**, and because the [[double-homoousion|double homoousion]] implied (by the shared inference *two natures → two hypostases*) a duality of hypostases in Christ, the formula of [[chalcedonian-definition|Chalcedon]] ("two natures in one hypostasis") was left philosophically unsupported — the crisis that drove the sixth–eighth-century transformations. See [[hypostatic-union|Hypostatic Union]], [[chalcedonian-definition|the Chalcedonian Definition]], and later scopes of [[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|Zachhuber]].

## The Post-Chalcedonian Transformations (6th–8th centuries)

Zachhuber's later chapters trace how Christology, under the pressure of the **NNWH-principle** ("no nature without a hypostasis") and the [[tritheist-controversy|tritheist controversy]], drove the classical theory into several rival reconstructions — the "hidden revolution" by which the individual came to be conceived *as individual*. Three broad versions emerged:

1. **The miaphysite philosophy of particular natures.** [[severus-of-antioch|Severus]] gestured toward, and [[john-philoponus|Philoponus]] made explicit, *particular natures* — the universal individuated and divided in each instance — to preserve the ontological continuity of nature and hypostasis. This shifted weight from the universal to the particular and (with Philoponus's abandonment of the concrete universal) issued in tritheism. Anti-tritheist miaphysites ([[damian-of-alexandria|Damian]], [[peter-of-callinicus|Peter of Callinicus]]) tried to reassert the classical theory.

2. **The philosophy of the *enhypostaton*** (the "Leontian School": [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]] → [[pamphilus-the-theologian|Pamphilus]] → [[theodore-of-raithu|Theodore of Raïthu]]). Replacing the ousia/hypostasis duality with a **substance/accident** binary, these thinkers made *ousia* the self-subsistent (αὐθυπόστατον) carrier and **marginalized the hypostasis** to a compound of substance + accidents. See [[enhypostaton|enhypostaton]].

3. **The philosophy of the hypostasis** ([[john-the-grammarian-of-caesarea|John the Grammarian]] → [[leontius-of-jerusalem|Leontius of Jerusalem]] → [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]]). Doubling down on the hypostasis as the concrete principle of existence in which all other being inheres, abandoning the axiom that a hypostasis is the concrete realization of *one* nature — culminating in the Damascene's "world of hypostases" and a near explicit **essence/existence** distinction.

Standing somewhat apart, [[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus the Confessor]] "sought to return to the classical theory," reviving Gregory of Nyssa's concrete dimension and subordinating it to a **whole/part** ontology, while [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] "removed himself most radically from most of its principles." Both miaphysite and Damascene lines, Zachhuber notes, broke the classical theory "at the exact same point: the ontological continuity between ousia and hypostasis."
