# Karma-yoga (Desireless Action)

**Karma-yoga** — the "discipline of action" — is the **[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita's]]** signature
ethical teaching: act as duty (*svadharma*) requires, but **renounce attachment to the fruits** of
action (*niṣkāma karma*, "action without desire"). Its locus classicus is Gita 2.47: "Your right is to
action alone, never to its fruits." By acting without the craving and ego ("I am the doer, I am the
enjoyer") that bind one to [[karma|karma]] and [[samsara|saṃsāra]], one acts without accumulating
bondage.

## The Interpretive Fault Line: Means or End?

Karma-yoga is read in two sharply different ways, and the difference is the heart of Gita exegesis:

- **Preparatory (Śaṅkara / [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]).** For [[shankara|Śaṅkara]], desireless action
  cannot *itself* liberate; the Self is **actionless** (*akartṛ*), so no action reaches it. Karma-yoga
  only **purifies the mind** (*sattva-śuddhi*), making one fit for the knowledge that alone liberates,
  after which action is renounced (*sannyāsa*). Action is the ladder, [[jnana-karma-samuccaya|not the
  consummation]].
- **Theistic / ongoing ([[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Ramanuja]], devotional Vedanta).** Desireless action
  is **offered to God** and becomes a permanent mode of loving service (*bhakti*); it is not discarded
  on the way to a knowledge that supersedes it but integrated into communion with the Lord.

A third, **modern** reading (Tilak, Gandhi, Vivekananda) makes karma-yoga an **end in itself** — selfless
social action as a complete spiritual path — recovering it from Śaṅkara's renunciatory subordination.
This is the form in which the Gita became a charter for engaged, this-worldly Hindu ethics.

## The "Three Yogas" Frame

Traditional readings present the Gita as harmonizing three paths to [[moksha|liberation]]: **karma-yoga**
(action), **jñāna-yoga** (knowledge — the Upanishadic Self/[[brahman|Brahman]] insight), and
**bhakti-yoga** (loving devotion, which the Gita exalts in chapters 9–12). How these three rank — and
whether they are three real paths or one path described thrice — is exactly what divides the schools (see
[[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the rival commentaries]]).
