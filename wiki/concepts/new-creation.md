# New Creation (*Kainē Ktisis*)

"If anyone is in Christ, [there is] a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come" (2 Cor 5:17). This dense declaration is one of Paul's most theologically explosive statements: the eschatological renewal promised by the prophets has broken into the present world through the crucified and risen Christ. The "new creation" in Paul is simultaneously personal (the renewal of the individual human being), ecclesiological (the new covenant community), and cosmic (the renewal of all creation). It has arrived, but not yet universally; it is present "in Christ" while the old order persists alongside it.

## Jewish and OT Background

The "new creation" concept has deep roots in Second Temple Judaism and in the prophetic tradition, particularly Deutero-Isaiah:

- **Isaiah 43:18–19**: "Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?" — The new exodus as eschatological renewal
- **Isaiah 65:17**: "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind"
- **Isaiah 66:22**: "For as the new heavens and the new earth that I make shall remain before me, says the LORD, so shall your offspring and your name remain"

Paul's declaration in 2 Cor 5:17 is almost certainly an allusion to these Isaiah texts. He sees in the resurrection of Christ the fulfillment of Isaiah's eschatological vision: the "new thing" God was doing has now happened in the resurrection.

In Second Temple Judaism, "new creation" language appears in texts like 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, the Qumran Community Rule (1QS), and the Pseudepigrapha, where it refers to the eschatological renewal of the world at the end of the present age. Paul's distinctive move is to declare that this renewal has already begun — in Christ — without waiting for the full cosmic consummation.

## The Primary Pauline Texts

**2 Corinthians 5:17**: The most compact and direct statement. "If anyone is in Christ, [there is] a new creation" (*kainē ktisis*). The conditional form indicates that the new creation has broken in, but is localized: it is found only "in Christ," i.e., in the sphere of Christ and the proclamation of the Gospel. Those outside this sphere remain in "the old."

**Galatians 6:15**: "For neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation" (*kainē ktisis*). Here "new creation" is the eschatological reality that renders Jewish ethnic markers irrelevant. The social and ethnic distinctions of the old order have been overtaken.

**Romans 8:18–25**: The fullest cosmological development. "The creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God. For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God." Present creation groans together; believers groan as they wait for "the redemption of the body" (8:23). The new creation is partially present in the Spirit (*arrabōn*, down payment) but fully awaited in the resurrection.

**Ephesians 2:10**: Believers are "his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" — using the creation vocabulary for the new covenant community.

**Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10**: The "new self" that is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator — a new-creation anthropology that echoes Gen 1:26–27 (imago Dei). The renewal of the image is a new-creation event.

**Revelation 21:1–5**: "Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away." The new creation reaches its eschatological consummation: "Behold, I am making all things new."

## Seifrid's Reading (2 Corinthians 5:17)

[[mark-seifrid|Seifrid]] (PNTC, 2014) identifies several distinctive features of Paul's statement:

**The abrupt brevity is deliberate.** "If anyone is in Christ, a new creation" — Paul omits the verb, intensifying the declaration. The abruptness marks "the unqualified and undiminished entrance of the eschaton into the present world."

**The localization is theologically essential.** The new creation is found only "in Christ" — in the sphere of the crucified and risen Lord and the apostolic proclamation that communicates him. This is not a cosmological claim about the general renewal of creation (yet); it is the claim that the new creation is concretely present wherever the Gospel creates faith. Seifrid: "It is not found everywhere in the world. It is found in Christ, the crucified and risen Lord alone."

**The personal does not exclude the cosmic.** The re-creation of the human being necessarily implies the re-creation of all things. Paul's "jarring juxtaposition of 'person' and 'creation'" signals that human renewal is not an isolated individual event — it is the first installment of the cosmic renewal of which Romans 8 speaks.

**The connection to Isaiah is explicit.** Paul's "the old has passed away; behold, the new has come" almost certainly alludes to Isaiah 43:18–19 and 65:17. In Christ, Isaiah's "new thing" has arrived. The eschatological promises of the OT prophets are fulfilled — but in a form that is hidden under the cross, not yet openly manifest in cosmic transformation.

## New Heavens and New Earth in 2 Peter 3 (Green)

Outside the Pauline corpus, the major NT "new creation" text is **2 Peter 3:10–13**, which (with
Revelation 21) supplies the explicit "new heavens and a new earth" language drawn from Isaiah 65:17;
66:22. [[gene-green|Gene L. Green]] (BECNT, 2008) reads it against the false teachers' denial of the
parousia and their claim that "all things continue as they were from the beginning of creation"
(3:4). Peter's answer:
- The present world is **not immutable**: as the pre-flood world perished by water, the present
  heavens and earth are "stored up for fire" until the day of judgment (3:5–7). Green sets this in a
  **linear** cosmology of *three worlds* (past, present, future), explicitly *against* the Stoic
  *cyclical* conflagration (*ekpyrōsis*) and rebirth.
- On the "day of the Lord," "the heavens will pass away with a roar, the **elements** (*stoicheia*,
  the material constituents of the world) will be dissolved by burning, and the earth and the works
  in it will be **found**" (3:10). Green accepts the difficult, best-attested reading
  *heurethēsetai* ("will be found / laid bare / exposed" before God's judgment) over the smoother
  scribal variants ("burned up").
- The hope is **renovation, not annihilation**: "we await **new heavens and a new earth in which
  righteousness dwells**" (3:13; Isa. 65:17). As with Paul, destruction gives way to renewal; the
  new order is moral as well as material (only righteousness dwells there).
- The structure is ethical-eschatological: "since all these things are to be dissolved, what sort of
  people ought you to be in holiness and godliness?" (3:11). Eschatology grounds ethics.

This places 2 Peter alongside Romans 8 and Revelation 21 as a witness that the cosmic new creation is
*renewal* of the present order, not its replacement by a wholly other reality — though 2 Peter's
emphasis falls on the *future* consummation after judgment, where Paul stresses the *already* "in
Christ." See [[2-peter|2 Peter]].

## The Consummation in Revelation 21–22 (Osborne)

Revelation gives the new creation its climactic, visionary form. [[grant-osborne|Osborne]] (BECNT,
2002) traces the throne-room and consummation visions:
- **"A new heaven and a new earth" (21:1).** The fulfillment of Isaiah 65:17; 66:22 (cf. 2 Pet.
  3:13). On the 2026-07-18 fidelity re-read, Osborne surveys renovation vs replacement among modern
  commentators and sides **primarily with replacement** of the old order (with continuity inside the
  wholly new: physical resurrection; united heaven-and-earth), following the destruction tradition
  of 20:11; 21:1 (*ἀπῆλθαν* — "have passed away"). He discusses whether 21:1–5 envisions the **renovation** of the present cosmos or its
  replacement by a fresh **re-creation** — God first "destroys the old earth tainted with sin" (20:11;
  21:1) and then "makes all things new" (21:5), combining heaven and earth into a new created reality.
  The creation theme is, for Osborne, the ultimate proof of God's sovereignty: the God who made the
  world can re-make it.
- **The New Jerusalem as the Holy of Holies (21:9–27).** The cube-shaped city *is* the Most Holy
  Place; there is "**no temple** in the city, for its temple is the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb"
  (21:22) — the temple trajectory consummated in God's unmediated presence. See [[eschatological-temple|
  The Eschatological Temple]].
- **The New Jerusalem as the final Eden (22:1–5).** The river of the water of life and the tree of
  life (Gen. 2; Ezek. 47) — paradise restored and surpassed; "no more curse" (22:3). The new creation
  thus arcs from Eden lost (Gen. 3) to Eden regained and exceeded.

Where Paul stresses the **already** ("in Christ, a new creation," 2 Cor. 5:17) and 2 Peter the
**future** consummation after judgment, Revelation supplies the consummated **vision** of the new
heaven and earth as the eternal dwelling of God with his people (21:3) — the *telos* toward which the
whole biblical new-creation theme moves.

## The New Creation and Justification

The "new creation" in 2 Cor 5:17 stands in immediate proximity to the "great exchange" of 5:21 ("He made him to be sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God"). These two declarations are complementary aspects of the same saving event:
- **5:17**: The ontological dimension — in Christ, there is a new creation; the person is remade
- **5:21**: The forensic/soteriological dimension — in Christ, sin and righteousness are exchanged

Seifrid insists these cannot be separated: the forensic exchange (justification) and the ontological renewal (new creation) are one act of God, not two successive stages. This connects to [[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]]'s argument (from Romans) that the righteousness of God is both forensic and transformative — the two aspects belong together.

## The Already / Not Yet Structure

The new creation in Paul is characterized by eschatological tension:

**Already**: In Christ, the new creation has arrived. The Spirit is the *arrabōn* (down payment/guarantee) of the age to come (2 Cor 1:22; 5:5; Eph 1:14). Believers have been "raised with Christ" (Col 3:1; cf. Eph 2:6). The "inner person is being renewed day by day" (2 Cor 4:16).

**Not yet**: The "outer person is being destroyed" (2 Cor 4:16). Creation "groans" (Rom 8:22). Believers groan as they wait for "the redemption of the body" (Rom 8:23). The new creation is hidden under the cross and suffering — the *theologia crucis* applies to the new creation as well: it is real but not yet visible.

This tension defines the whole of Christian existence in Paul. It generates the eschatological reserve he constantly applies to the Corinthians: they have not yet arrived; the resurrection is still future; Paul has not yet attained (Phil 3:12–14).

## Relationship to Imago Dei

The "new creation" in Paul reconnects to the creation theology of Genesis 1–2. The human being was created in the image of God (*imago Dei*, Gen 1:26–27); through the fall, this image was distorted and creation was subjected to futility (Rom 8:20; cf. Gen 3). The new creation is the restoration and transcendence of the original creation: believers will bear "the image of the man of heaven" (1 Cor 15:49) — the risen Christ as the eschatological form of the imago Dei. See [[imago-dei|Imago Dei]].
