The *gurdwara* (literally "door of the Guru") is the central institution of Sikh communal life — at once a place of worship, a housing for the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]], and a community meeting-house. Per [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]], Sikhs believe that "because gurdwaras house the Guru Granth Sahib, they house the actual Guru" and therefore "deserve the profoundest respect" (McLeod ~L19–22).

## Structure and Furnishings

Every public gurdwara must fly the *Nishan Sahib* — a triangular or pennant-shaped flag in saffron (occasionally dark blue) on a flagpole (*mast*) also clad in cloth, replaced annually on Baisakhi Day. A large drum (*nagara*) is beaten on appropriate occasions. In addition to the main worship hall, a gurdwara always includes a [[langar|langar]] (dining hall with attached kitchen). The Guru Granth Sahib is placed on a lectern draped with a clean cloth and cushions, beneath a canopy; when not open, it is covered with a mantle (*rumala*). A *chauri* (fan whisk) is waved over the open scripture at all times as a sign of respect (McLeod ~L43–44).

## The Granthi

The *granthi* is the custodian and reader of the Guru Granth Sahib in a gurdwara. His duties include upkeep of the building, responsibility for regular services, and "respectful attendance on the Guru Granth Sahib" (McLeod ~L50–52). Crucially, a granthi is **not ordained** — "Sikhism is strictly a lay organization" with no Sikh clergy; references in the popular press to Sikh "clergy" or "priests" are, in McLeod's words, "mistaken usages" (~L54–58). The SGPC maintains a college to train granthis. The office is open to women as well as men, though in practice nearly all granthis are men (~L67–68).

## Conduct and Access

Before entering, everyone — Sikh and non-Sikh alike — must cover their heads and remove shoes. The first act upon entering is to bow before the Guru Granth Sahib, forehead touching the floor (*matha tekna*). All must sit at a lower level than the scripture, customarily on the floor. The congregation (*sangat* or *divan*) should be greeted with palms joined and the salutation *Vahiguru ji ka Khalsa, Vahiguru ji ki fateh* ("Hail to the Guru's Khalsa! Hail the Guru's victory!") (McLeod ~L79–80). Men usually sit on the right and women on the left, though this is not mandatory.

People of any faith, nationality, or caste may enter, provided they show due respect and bring neither tobacco, alcohol, nor other intoxicants. The sole access restriction is that only Amrit-dhari (initiated) Sikhs may enter the particularly sanctified inner area of a *takhat* (~L104–109). In an ordinary gurdwara no distinction is made between Sikhs and non-Sikhs, nor any discrimination on grounds of caste or status.

## Order of Service

For gurdwaras open for a limited period, the usual order is: ritual opening of the Guru Granth Sahib; extended *kirtan*; perhaps *katha* (a hermeneutic or hortative sermon); verses from Guru Amar Das's hymn *Anand Sahib*; *Ardas*; two salutations; and a final *hukam* (also called a *vak*) — the hukam taken by opening the Guru Granth Sahib at random and reading the hymn at the head of the left-hand page (~L129–136). *Kirtan* (hymn-singing) consists of passages from the Guru Granth Sahib or from the works of Guru Gobind Singh; the only additional works permitted in a gurdwara are those by Bhai Gurdas and Nand Lai (~L142–146).

Larger gurdwaras divide the day into five set *chaukis* ("sittings") for the singing of specific repertoires (~L15–16).

## Liturgical Readings

Each Sikh is encouraged to maintain a home gurdwara — a room or screened space set aside exclusively for this purpose — where daily reading and a *hukam* are taken. A recommended practice is to read the full 1,430-page scripture in daily instalments over four to eight weeks, one week (*saptahak path*), or up to a year (~L153–157). An *akhand path* (unbroken reading, ~48 hours) is undertaken for special occasions such as a marriage or the opening of a new business, with a team of readers assembled for the ceremony (~L158–175). It concludes with the *bhog* ("consummation") ceremony, during which *karah prasad* is distributed to all present (~L173–218).

## The Five Takhats

Five historic gurdwaras fulfil a special **temporal** role as *takhats*, headed by Akal Takhat opposite Harimandir Sahib in [[amritsar|Amritsar]], "where the Sarbat Khalsa would gather for its biannual meetings during the eighteenth century" (~L816–817). Akal Takhat is the supreme takhat from whose balcony "all matters of vital importance to the Panth as a whole are promulgated" (~L819). The remaining four takhats are located at places important in the life of [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]]: Harimandir Sahib in Patna (his birthplace), Kesgarh Sahib in Anandpur (where the Khalsa was inaugurated), Sri Damdama Sahib at Talvandi Sabo, and Sri Hazur Sahib at Nander (the place of his assassination). A fifth — Damdama Sahib — was elevated by the SGPC in 1966 (~L822–827).

## History of the Term

Until the eighteenth century the term *dharamsala* was used for any building where a *sangat* gathered for *kirtan*; "gurdwara" was reserved for structures commemorating an episode from a Guru's life. Gradually "gurdwara" supplanted "dharamsala" as the general term. Those commemorating a Guru's life event are now designated "historic" gurdwaras, as distinct from those serving as local congregational meeting-places (~L800–812).
