# Bahá'í Administrative Order

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Definition

The Bahá'í Administrative Order is the system of elected and appointed institutions through which the Bahá'í Faith is organized and governed globally. Elements were described or established by Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá, but a complete system only emerged during the Guardianship of [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]], increasingly replacing more personalized and informal patterns of local leadership that had previously prevailed (lines 2640–2645).

## Structure

Smith provides a diagram (labeled "The present-day structure of Baha'i administration") whose content is reproduced here (lines 2910–2936):

### Institutions of the "Rulers" (elected; direction and administrative authority)

**Local Spiritual Assemblies (est. 1920s)**
- Elected annually by local communities
- Form wherever there are at least nine adult Bahá'ís (aged twenty-one and above) in a locality
- Superintend all Bahá'í activities in their locality
- Establish their own funds and committees

**National Spiritual Assemblies (est. 1920s)**
- Elected annually by delegate conventions
- Operate wherever there is a sufficient national Bahá'í community
- Oversee national administrative centres; secretaries in some cases serve full-time

**The Universal House of Justice (est. 1963)**
- Elected quinquennially by international convention (the collected votes of all national spiritual assembly members: fifty-six in 1963, 165 in 1993)
- Nine members; elections held every five years at an international convention in Haifa
- The House has no officers but otherwise conducts its affairs similarly to spiritual assemblies
- Membership confined to men, in keeping with 'Abdu'l-Bahá's instruction
- Supreme administrative body; assumes permanent headship of the Faith in the absence of a Guardian

### Institutions of the "Learned" (appointed; advice and encouragement)

**Hands of the Cause of God**
- Ancient institution revived in 1951 as a functioning group of senior Bahá'ís
- Initially twelve, increased to twenty-seven by 1957, on all continents
- Responsible for assisting national spiritual assemblies and protecting the Faith from external and internal attacks
- After November 1964, the House of Justice ruled that no further Hands could be appointed; their functions are continued by the Continental Boards of Counsellors

**Continental Boards of Counsellors (est. 1968)**
- Quinquennial appointments; seventy-two members on five Boards (as of 1990s)
- Take over the work of the Hands on a continental basis, including direction of the Auxiliary Boards

**Auxiliary Boards for the Propagation and Protection of the Faith (est. 1954, 1957)**
- Two boards: one for propagation (1954), one for protection (1957)
- Organized on a continental level; members act as "deputies, assistants and advisers" to the Hands / Counsellors
- Numbers greatly increased: from seventy-two in 1957 to 846 in 1992

**Assistants to the Members of the Auxiliary Boards (est. 1973)**
- Annual or biennial appointments; empowered from 1973 onwards

**International Teaching Centre, Haifa (est. 1973)**
- Comprises all the Hands of the Cause and nine Counsellors; quinquennial appointments
- Directs the Continental and Auxiliary Boards; remaining Hands also serve on it

### Secretariat, Research and Other Departments

- Attached to the Universal House of Justice in Haifa
- An office of social and economic development established 1983; a public information office; other agencies
- By the mid-1990s, several hundred staff (compared with a handful under Shoghi Effendi)

## Authority, Elections, and the Principle of No Clergy

Elections to spiritual assemblies are by secret ballot; there is no nomination and no campaigning. The system has no clerical order: the growth of the assembly system greatly reduced the importance of clerically trained Bahá'ís who had initially exercised a leadership role in Iran. The "institutions of the learned" (Hands, Counsellors, Auxiliary Board members) are all appointed, not elected, and exercise no executive authority — that rests clearly with the elected assemblies (lines 2676–2685, 4283–4288).

The Universal House of Justice is empowered to legislate on matters not revealed in the Bahá'í scriptures, but up to the time of Smith's writing (1995) it had been extremely limited in exercising this function, preferring instead to provide directive guidance and to collect and publish extracts from scripture (lines 2893–2904).

## The Universal House of Justice: Composition and Authority

First elected in April 1963, the Universal House of Justice has acted as the supreme administrative body ever since. One of its earliest pronouncements (October 1963) was that there was no way to appoint further Guardians in succession to Shoghi Effendi, and it thus assumed permanent headship of the Faith (*Wellspring of Guidance* 11). The absence of a Guardian was described as a "grievous loss", but continued unity and guidance under the House is regarded as divinely assured (lines 2855–2864).

The constitution of the House was adopted on 26 November 1972. Of the original nine members elected in 1963, three still served at the time of Smith's writing. Total members to that date: sixteen men. By national origins: five Iranians from outside Iran, eight North Americans, two British, one Australian. The members cultivate an ethos of individual self-effacement — it is as a collectivity that the House exercises its leadership (lines 2865–2887).

### Legislation and Publications

The House has published:
- Three volumes of its letters to Bahá'í communities, institutions, individuals, and the entire Bahá'í world (regarded as divinely empowered)
- Compilations on Bahá'í administration, teaching, prayer, music, education, and family life
- Translations of writings by Bahá'u'lláh, the Báb, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá, including the *Kitáb-i-Aqdas* (1992)
- Its own constitution (1972)
- A volume of Bahá'u'lláh's letters to kings for presentation to contemporary heads of state (1967)
- *The Promise of World Peace* (1985), addressed "to the peoples of the world"
- *The Prosperity of Humankind* (1995)

By 1983, over 60,000 items of letters by Bahá'u'lláh, 'Abdu'l-Bahá, and Shoghi Effendi had been collected in Haifa (lines 2892–2953).

## Systematic Planning

Shoghi Effendi established a framework of systematic planning continued by the Universal House of Justice. Five international plans launched by the House to date (as of 1995):
- Nine Year Plan (1964–73)
- Five Year Plan (1974–9)
- Seven Year Plan (1979–86)
- Six Year Plan (1986–92)
- Three Year Plan (1993–6)

A subsidiary two-year plan (1990–2) co-ordinated Bahá'í activity in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union under new conditions of religious freedom following the collapse of communist regimes. Between 1963 and 1992, the number of localities in which Bahá'ís resided worldwide increased from 14,000 to over 120,000, and local spiritual assemblies increased from 4,437 to 20,435 (lines 2987–3008).

## Relationship to Covenant

The administrative order is explicitly linked to the Bahá'í doctrine of the Covenant. 'Abdu'l-Bahá's will named Shoghi Effendi as Guardian, and with the Universal House of Justice both are said to be under the unerring guidance and protection of Bahá'u'lláh and the Báb. The successive centres of the Faith were each believed to possess divine authority — a factor Smith notes in the lack of success of those who disputed their authority, designated "Covenant-breakers" (lines 2453–2467, 4289–4301).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

*The following material is drawn from Peter Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000), lines 1862–2271.*

### Early Organization

The Bábí movement's organization rested on clerical leadership (often of a highly charismatic kind) at both local and national level, and on itinerant couriers providing effective internal communications. With the Báb separated from his followers in imprisoned exile, such courier-based cohesion was essential (lines 1875–1884). Most 19th-century Bahá'í leaders (including the Hands of the Cause appointed by Bahá'u'lláh) were still clerics who had completed a traditional Islamic education prior to conversion; prominent Bahá'í merchants such as the Afnáns and the Nahrí family of Isfahan also assumed positions of importance (lines 1888–1896).

### Development of the Administrative Order: The 1920s

Shoghi Effendi regularized and extended the system of elected spiritual assemblies. The basic elements were laid out in 1922–3. All Bahá'í groups with at least nine adult Bahá'ís (twenty-one years old and above) were called upon to form local spiritual assemblies, each superintending all Bahá'í activities in its locality. In all "national" communities with a sufficient number of Bahá'ís, national spiritual assemblies were to be elected by a delegate convention (lines 1950–1965).

Both types of assembly would re-elect annually; have nine members; use prayer and consultation in their decision-making; establish their own funds; and elect their own officers (chairman, vice-chairman, secretary, treasurer) (lines 4079–4085).

Other developments from the 1920s: specific requirements for voting membership; national administrative centres (*Hazíratu'l-Quds*), each overseen by the elected secretary of the national assembly; membership rolls, enrolment cards, and credential cards; and the regular nineteen-day feast, which came to include a period of consultation on local Bahá'í activities (lines 1976–2001).

### Three New Institutions (1950s)

Three administrative institutions were newly created or reanimated during the period of Shoghi Effendi's guardianship (lines 2014–2059):

1. **The International Bahá'í Council (1950/51–1963)**: an initially appointed body, charged with assisting Shoghi Effendi in Haifa; also seen as the precursor of the Universal House of Justice.
2. **The Hands of the Cause of God**: revived in 1951 as a functioning group of senior Bahá'ís. An initial group of twelve was increased to twenty-seven by 1957, comprising individuals on all continents. They unexpectedly came to exercise overall headship of the Bahá'ís during the "interregnum" between Shoghi Effendi's death and the establishment of the Universal House of Justice (1957–63).
3. **The Auxiliary Boards**: members to act as "deputies, assistants and advisors" to the Hands. Two Boards were established on a continental basis: one for propagation (1954), one for protection (1957).

### The Universal House of Justice: Establishment

The formation of the House of Justice in 1963 marked a major transition, as overall leadership of the Faith for the first time became vested in an elected body. It confirmed its own authority as "the supreme institution" of the Administrative Order in its Constitution (1972). It stated in October 1963 that it was not possible to appoint further Guardians; in November 1964, that it was no longer possible to appoint Hands of the Cause (lines 2063–2081).

### The Rulers and the Learned

A distinction is made between the "rulers" and the "learned" of the Faith:
- **The rulers**: members of the Universal House of Justice and national and local assemblies — executive authority.
- **The learned**: Hands, Counsellors, Board members, and their assistants — all perform an educative and inspirational role.

Those "learned" who yearned for power analogous to Shi'i counterparts were out of place, as evidenced by the "rebellions" of Jamál Burqírí'í and 'Abdu'l-Husayn Aváríh (lines 2159–2185).

### Purpose and Spirit

Shoghi Effendi linked the Administrative Order to the emergence of Bahá'u'lláh's promised World Order, describing it as the "nucleus" and "Charter" of that Order, and the basis for the future "Bahá'í World Commonwealth" (lines 2187–2195). The administration was an instrument of the Faith, not a substitute for it. He warned Bahá'ís not to reify their present institutions or procedures. Administrators should exercise "extreme humility" and selfless devotion, together with loving consultation with the mass of the Bahá'ís (lines 2197–2213).

He also emphasized the distinctiveness of the Administrative Order: directly rooted in the writings of Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá; not like any former theocracy (no priesthood; the Guardians lacked the absolutist powers of popes or Imáms); "non-autocratic" and inclined toward "democratic methods" with elected assemblies (at local level by universal suffrage) — but assembly members answered to their own consciences, not to popular feeling (lines 2214–2253).

With the exception of the Universal House of Justice and the Guardianship, all Bahá'í administrative institutions are open to both women and men (lines 2254–2264).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### Consultation (lines 9661–9740)
Bahá'u'lláh taught that consultation enhances understanding and is a prerequisite of human well-being. 'Abdu'l-Bahá regarded it as a potent means of advancing any project. Proper consultation requires: mutual fellowship and unity among participants; expression of views with courtesy and moderation; willingness to adopt another's view if it is better; 'absolute freedom' of expression; avoiding feelings of ill-will. Shoghi Effendi described consultation as the 'bedrock' of the Bahá'í administrative order. Assembly members should consult not only among themselves but as much as possible with the wider community.

### Continental Boards of Counsellors (lines 9742–9794)
Institution created by the Universal House of Justice in 1968 to perpetuate the functions of the [[hands-of-the-cause|Hands of the Cause]], specifically the protection and propagation of the Faith. Responsible for: overseeing the Auxiliary Boards; consulting and collaborating with national spiritual assemblies; informing the Hands and the House of Justice of developments. Initially eleven Boards were formed, reduced to five (one per continent) in 1980. The number of Counsellors increased from thirty-six initially to seventy-two by 1985. Term of appointment: five years from 1980. The Boards' role is only advisory: administrative authority rests with the national assemblies. In 1973 the International Teaching Centre assumed responsibility for directing the Boards.

### Conventions (lines 9797–9876)
Three main forms:
1. **Unit conventions**: local/regional meetings to elect delegates for national convention, held annually
2. **National conventions**: meetings of delegates to elect national spiritual assembly members, normally held during the Riḍván period (21 April–2 May)
3. **International convention**: meeting of all national spiritual assembly members to elect the Universal House of Justice, held every five years (first in 1963)
The role of national conventions was described by Shoghi Effendi as that of a temporary advisory body to the national assembly.

### Constitutions (lines 9644–9660)
The prototype of all national Bahá'í constitutions was the Declaration of Trust adopted by the National Spiritual Assembly of the Bahá'ís of the United States and Canada in 1927. The prototype of all local constitutions was adopted by the New York local assembly in November 1931. The Universal House of Justice adopted its own constitution on 26 November 1972.

### Custodians (lines 10294–10327)
Those [[hands-of-the-cause|Hands of the Cause]] who exercised collective leadership of the Bahá'í Faith from the passing of Shoghi Effendi (November 1957) until the election of the Universal House of Justice (April 1963). Nine Custodians were initially selected at the first conclave of the Hands in November 1957. Originally selected: Hasan Balyuzi, Amelia Collins, 'Alí-Akbar Furútan, Paul Haney, Leroy Ioas, Jalál Kházeh, Adelbert Mühlschlegel, Charles Mason Remey, and Rúḥíyyih Khánum. Four were replaced over the course of the custodianship.

### Elections (lines 11665–11701)
Members of local and national assemblies, the Universal House of Justice, and some Regional Bahá'í Councils are elected by secret ballot. Nominations, canvassing, and all forms of electioneering are prohibited. There should be no discussion of personalities before the election. Each individual elector should prayerfully follow the dictates of their own conscience. Those with the most votes are elected if eligible to serve.
