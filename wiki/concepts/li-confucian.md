# Li (Ritual Propriety)

**Li** (禮) — "ritual," "rites," "propriety," "ceremony" — is, with **[[ren|ren]]**, one of the two
pillars of [[confucianism|Confucian]] ethics: the body of traditional **forms, manners, ceremonies, and
roles** through which character is shaped and society is harmonized. (Distinct from the Neo-Confucian
*li* 理, "principle"; this is *li* 禮, "rite.")

## Meaning

For [[confucius|Confucius]], outward ritual is not empty formality but the **discipline that forms the
inner virtue**: "to overcome the self and return to li is ren" ([[analects|Analects]] 12.1). Li covers
everything from sacrifices and mourning to court ceremony to everyday courtesy and the proper conduct of
the **five relationships** (ruler–subject, parent–child, husband–wife, elder–younger, friend–friend).
Performed with sincerity (and paired with **music**, *yue*), li cultivates respect, restraint, and social
trust. Ritual, not law, is Confucius's preferred instrument of order: "lead them with virtue and keep
them in line with li, and they will have a sense of shame and order themselves" (2.3).

## The Daoist Counterpoint

Li is precisely what [[daoism|Daoism]] criticizes: where Confucianism **cultivates** character through
deliberate ritual form, the Daodejing and Zhuangzi prize **[[ziran|ziran]]** (uncontrived naturalness)
and **[[wu-wei|wu-wei]]**, and read elaborate ritual as artificial decline from the spontaneous Way. The
**cultivation-vs-spontaneity** tension between li and ziran is one of the central debates of Chinese
thought (a candidate comparison/controversy page).
