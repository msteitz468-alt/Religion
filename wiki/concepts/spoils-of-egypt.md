## Definition

The "Spoils of Egypt" principle is a Christian hermeneutical and pedagogical doctrine holding that truths, valid reasoning, and useful knowledge found among pagan philosophers and scholars are not the property of the pagans but ultimately belong to God—and therefore may and should be appropriated by Christians for the service of the gospel. The name derives from the Exodus narrative (Ex. iii. 21–22; xii. 35–36), in which the Israelites, departing Egypt by God's command, took with them the Egyptians' gold, silver, and garments. Augustine treats that event as a "type prefiguring what happens now" (Bk. II, ch. 40, line ~2313).

## Augustine's Account in *On Christian Doctrine*, Book II

### The Core Statement (Ch. 40, §60)

Augustine's fullest articulation appears in Chapter 40:

> "Moreover, if those who are called philosophers, and especially the Platonists, have said aught that is true and in harmony with our faith, we are not only not to shrink from it, but to claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it. For, as the Egyptians had not only the idols and heavy burdens which the people of Israel hated and fled from, but also vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments, which the same people when going out of Egypt appropriated to themselves, designing them for a better use, not doing this on their own authority, but by the command of God, the Egyptians themselves, in their ignorance, providing them with things which they themselves were not making a good use of; in the same way all branches of heathen learning have not only false and superstitious fancies and heavy burdens of unnecessary toil, which every one of us, when going out under the leadership of Christ from the fellowship of the heathen, ought to abhor and avoid; but they contain also liberal instruction which is better adapted to the use of the truth, and some most excellent precepts of morality; and some truths in regard even to the worship of the One God are found among them. Now these are, so to speak, their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God's providence which are everywhere scattered abroad, and are perversely and unlawfully prostituting to the worship of devils." (Bk. II, ch. 40, lines 2275–2291)

Augustine extends the figure: pagan "garments"—that is, "human institutions such as are adapted to that intercourse with men which is indispensable in this life"—are likewise to be taken and turned to Christian use (line ~2292–2294).

### Epistemological Grounding (Ch. 18, §28)

The principle is grounded earlier in Book II when Augustine argues that useful arts and true knowledge must not be abandoned simply because pagans developed or misused them:

> "We ought not to give up music because of the superstition of the heathen, if we can derive anything from it that is of use for the understanding of Holy Scripture... For we ought not to refuse to learn letters because they say that Mercury discovered them... Nay, but let every good and true Christian understand that wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master." (Bk. II, ch. 18, lines 1695–1705)

This "wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master" formulation is the theological axiom underlying the Spoils image: truth has one source (God), and pagan scholars who arrive at it are, without knowing it, mining God's own providence.

### Typological Dimension (Ch. 40, §61; Ch. 41–42)

In §61 Augustine cites historical exemplars: Moses himself "was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts vii. 22, cited at line ~2300); and Christian writers Cyprian, Lactantius, Victorinus, Optatus, and Hilary all "came out of Egypt" laden with pagan learning put to Christian use. The pagan institutions that furnished this knowledge did so unknowingly: "they gave their gold and their silver and their garments to the people of God as they were going out of Egypt, not knowing how the things they gave would be turned to the service of Christ" (Bk. II, ch. 40, lines 2304–2315).

Augustine closes Book II (Ch. 42, §63) with a comparative judgment that puts the principle in proportion:

> "But just as poor as the store of gold and silver and garments which the people of Israel brought with them out of Egypt was in comparison with the riches which they afterwards attained at Jerusalem... so poor is all the useful knowledge which is gathered from the books of the heathen when compared with the knowledge of Holy Scripture." (lines 2360–2367)

Pagan learning is valuable but subordinate: Scripture contains whatever is useful in heathen letters, and vastly more besides.

### What May and May Not Be Taken

Augustine carefully delimits the principle. In the same chapter (Ch. 39, §58) he names what is genuinely useful from heathen sources:
- Information about past and present things accessible to the senses (natural history, geography, chronology)
- Useful mechanical arts
- The sciences of reasoning (logic) and number

What must be rejected:
- Anything involving fellowship with demons (magic, divination, astrology, augury)
- Mere superstition and luxury

The spoils principle therefore operates *after* this discrimination has been made; it does not license indiscriminate absorption of pagan culture, but the targeted appropriation of what is genuinely true and useful.

### Humility as the Condition (Ch. 41, §62)

Augustine adds a crucial caveat: riches brought from Egypt are spiritually dangerous without the humility of the Passover. Drawing on the hyssop imagery (a meek herb whose roots are nonetheless penetrating):

> "For so he will feel that, whatever may be the riches he brings with him out of Egypt, yet unless he has kept the passover, he cannot be safe." (Bk. II, ch. 41, line ~2320)

The "puffing up" of knowledge (1 Cor. viii. 1) is the risk; charity ("charity edifieth") and the easy yoke of Christ are the corrective. The Spoils of Egypt principle is thus not a triumphalist claim but a disciplined, humble act of conversion and re-dedication of what already belongs to God.
