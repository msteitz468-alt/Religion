# Mitzvah (the Deed as Response)

Part III ("Response") of [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]] makes the
**mitzvah** — the commanded deed — the human answer to [[revelation|revelation]] and to the
God who is in [[divine-pathos|search of man]]. "The basic term of Jewish living … is mitsvah
rather than law (*din*)": at once commandment, obligation, deed, and act of benevolence.
[[abraham-joshua-heschel|Heschel]] calls Jewish law "a science of deeds … not only how to
worship Him at certain times but how to live with Him at all times."

## The Leap of Action

The Jewish mode of religious existence is "a leap of action rather than a leap of thought":
"to do more than he understands in order to understand more than he does." The paradigm is
Israel's "we shall do and we shall hear" (Exodus 24:7) — practice preceding full
comprehension, "as it would be futile to explore the meaning of music and abstain from
listening to music." The deed is also "the test, the trial, and the risk," since its effects
vastly exceed intention.

## Partnership and the Divinity of Deeds

A mitzvah is "an act which God and man have in common … He is a partner to our act"; "a
mitsvah is an act in which man is present … while sin is an act in which God is alone; an act
of alienation." Beyond imitation of God, "sacred acts … do not only imitate; they represent
the Divine." Hence Heschel prefers belief in "the immanence of God in deeds" over the
immanence of God in nature.

## The Deed Redeems

Against both a faith-alone soteriology (Paul, Luther, Ritschl, Barth — "never a problem in
Judaism") and a pure ethics of intention (Kant, Fichte), Heschel holds the deed has
redemptive power that can precede pure motive: "the deed is wiser than the heart"; "the good
motive comes into being while doing the good." "Purity of motivation is the goal; constancy
of action is the way" — yet the deed must aim at transformation, not mere compliance: "the
purpose of the mitsvot is to sanctify man." On the inwardness the deed requires, see
[[kavanah]]; on the law–spirit polarity within which the mitzvah lives, see [[halakhah]].
