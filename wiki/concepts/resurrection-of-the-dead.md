# Resurrection of the Dead (*Tehiyyat ha-Metim*)

The **resurrection of the dead** is the belief that at the end of history God will raise
the dead bodily and restore them to full personal existence. In its classical Jewish form
(*tehiyyat ha-metim*) it is **bodily, communal, historical, and eschatological** — an event
within the redemption of the people Israel and the world, not merely the fate of an
individual soul. It must be carefully distinguished from the **immortality of the soul**,
with which it can coexist but to which it is not reducible. (For the cross-tradition
*personification* of evil and other figures, this page concerns the doctrine of rising,
not those figures.)

This page draws principally on [[jon-d-levenson|Jon D. Levenson]]'s
[[levenson-resurrection-and-the-restoration-of-israel|*Resurrection and the Restoration of
Israel*]] (2006).

## Resurrection vs. Immortality of the Soul

The single most important distinction in the topic:

| | **Resurrection of the dead** | **Immortality of the soul** |
|---|---|---|
| What survives / is restored | the **whole embodied person** | a disembodied, indestructible core (soul, spirit, *nous*) |
| Agency | God's **miraculous, gracious** act | a **natural property** of the self |
| Status of death | death is **real** and must be *overcome* | death is **not finally real** for the soul |
| Frame | **historical, communal, end-time**; bound to Israel's restoration | **individual, universal**, often a-historical |
| Provenance | Israelite / biblical | Greek / Platonic |

Levenson's polemical thesis: modern liberal Judaism (and much popular Christianity) has
**collapsed resurrection into immortality**, which "miss[es] the promissory character of
the expectation and its inextricable connection to a natural family, the Jews." To make the
governing question "*Will I* have life after death?" is already, in his words, "to get off
on the wrong foot."

## The Biblical Pre-History (Levenson)

Against the consensus that resurrection is a *late, foreign* importation, Levenson traces a
slow convergence of native biblical themes:

- **God's power over death** — "The Lord deals death and gives life, casts down into
  [[sheol|Sheol]] and raises up" (1 Sam 2:6); the never-resolved tension between "all come
  to Sheol" and "God can deliver from Sheol."
- **Fortunate vs. unfortunate death** — Sheol is not the universal, irreversible destiny of
  all; the patriarchs die "gathered to their kin," contented. See [[sheol|Sheol]].
- **Familial continuity** — a promise can be fulfilled *after* its recipient's death,
  through descendants; **infertility functions as death**, and **miraculous conception /
  the return of a lost child as resurrection** (the barren matriarchs; the binding of
  Isaac — cf. [[akedah-interpretation|the Akedah]]).
- **Prophetic resurrection miracles** — [[elijah|Elijah]] and [[elisha|Elisha]] raise dead
  sons (1 Kgs 17; 2 Kgs 4), evidencing an *early* faith in God's power over death.
- **National restoration** — the bereaved-widow-whose-children-return motif, climaxing in
  [[ezekiel-prophet|Ezekiel's]] **valley of dry bones** (Ezek 37): Israel raised from its
  graves back to the land.
- **The Divine Warrior** — the deepest root: God's eschatological victory (an Israelite
  transformation of the Canaanite **Baal–Mot** myth) revives a withered nature; within that
  "scenario of exceptions" the destruction of death itself (Isa 25:8) is "hardly
  exceptional." See [[the-combat-myth|the Combat Myth]].

## The First Explicit Affirmations

- **Daniel 12:1–3** — the first clear, canonical statement: "many of those who sleep in the
  dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting
  contempt" — a **double** resurrection (righteous and wicked) to differing verdicts, set in
  the context of national deliverance under persecution. See [[daniel|Daniel]].
- **Isaiah 26:19** (the Isaianic Apocalypse) — "your dead shall live, their corpses shall
  rise... the earth will give birth to the dead"; with Isa 25:8, "He will destroy death
  forever." An immediate antecedent of Daniel 12.
- **Isaiah 52:13–53:12** (the [[servant-songs|Suffering Servant]]) — the post-mortem
  vindication and new life of the faithful servant, read by Daniel 12 as resurrection.

The immediate catalysts (the **Maccabean martyr** crisis; the indirect milieu of
[[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]] eschatology) were "catalysts, not reagents": they accelerated
but did not create a hope long prepared in Israel's own tradition. On the Zoroastrian
question specifically see [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|the influence
page]].

## Tradition-Specific Development

- **Judaism.** The rabbis made resurrection a **defining dogma**: Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1
  excludes from the world to come anyone who says "the resurrection of the dead is not in
  the Torah." The second benediction of the Amidah (*Gevurot*) blesses God *meḥayyeh
  ha-metim*, "who revives the dead"; Maimonides lists it among the Thirteen Principles. The
  **[[sadducees|Sadducees]]** denied it (Acts 23:8; Josephus), the **[[pharisees|Pharisees]]**
  affirmed it, and [[rabbinic-judaism|rabbinic Judaism]] canonized it. **Modern** Reform
  (and ambivalently Conservative) liturgy has often replaced it with the immortality of the
  soul — a shift Levenson ties to the rejection of the national/ethnic (and Zionist)
  dimension of Jewish identity.
- **Christianity.** Bodily resurrection is foundational — Christ's resurrection as the
  "firstfruits" guaranteeing the general resurrection (1 Cor 15). The Christian *Resurrection
  of Jesus* so colored the word that many now mistake resurrection for a *Christian*
  invention. Debates over the nature of the risen body are filed at
  [[resurrection-body|The Resurrection Body (1 Corinthians 15)]].
- **Islam.** *Yawm al-Qiyama* (the Day of Resurrection) is a cardinal article of faith:
  bodily resurrection, judgment, and assignment to garden or fire.
- **Zoroastrianism.** Affirmed a bodily revival at the **[[frashokereti|Frashokereti]]**
  (the final "making wonderful"), with universal judgment and the defeat of death — the
  ancient parallel whose causal relation to the Jewish doctrine is debated (see below).

## Cross-Wiki Tensions

- **vs. the influence thesis.** The
  [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|influence page]] records the case that
  resurrection entered Judaism from Persia; Levenson sharply qualifies this for resurrection
  *specifically* (Zoroastrianism "at best the grandmother"; the Canaanite Divine Warrior the
  chief root) — flagged on both pages per the Contradiction Protocol.
- **vs. Sheol-as-universal-fate.** Resurrection presupposes that death is a real, grievous
  enemy; this stands against readings of [[sheol|Sheol]] as a neutral, theologically
  unproblematic destiny of all.
