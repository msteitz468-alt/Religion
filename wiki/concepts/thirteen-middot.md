The Thirteen Middot (hermeneutical measures or rules; Hebrew: מִדּוֹת) of Rabbi Ishmael are the formal logical-exegetical principles used in rabbinic Judaism to derive law from the Written Torah. They are appended as a *baraita* (external teaching) to the opening of the *Sifra* (the halakhic midrash on Leviticus) and are recited as part of the morning liturgy in traditional Jewish prayer.

## The Rules

The thirteen principles (in brief):

1. **Kal va-homer** (light and heavy, *a fortiori*): An inference from a less stringent case to a more stringent one, or vice versa
2. **Gezerah shavah** (verbal analogy): Two texts containing the same key word are interpreted by analogy with each other
3. **Binyan av mi-katuv echad** (building a category from one text): A general principle derived from a single verse
4. **Binyan av mi-shenei ketuvim** (building from two texts): A general principle derived from two verses
5. **Kelal u-ferat** (general and specific): A general statement followed by a specific one — the specific limits the general
6. **Perat u-kelal** (specific and general): A specific statement followed by a general one — the general expands the specific
7. **Kelal u-ferat u-khelal** (general, specific, general): Interpret according to the pattern in context
8. **Kelal she-tzarich li-ferat** (general requiring a specific): The general needs the specific to complete it
9. **Perat she-tzarich li-khelal** (specific requiring a general): The specific needs the general
10. **Kol davar she-haya bi-khelal ve-yatza min ha-khelal** (excluded items): Something that was in a general category but is excluded — the exclusion teaches something about the general
11. **Kol davar she-haya bi-khelal ve-yatza li-ton davar acher she-hu kheinyano**: A case excluded for an analogous case
12. **Kol davar she-haya bi-khelal ve-yatza li-ton davar acher she-hu she-lo kheinyano**: A case excluded for a non-analogous case
13. **Davar ha-lamed me-inyano**: Something learned from its context; something learned from what follows it

## Historical Context

Rabbi Ishmael (c. 90–135 CE) was a Tanna of the second generation whose academy was a major center of Halachic reasoning. An earlier, shorter set of seven hermeneutical rules is attributed to Hillel; Rabbi Ishmael's list of thirteen expanded and systematized Hillel's foundation. A third system, attributed to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yose ha-Gelili, lists thirty-two rules for Aggadic interpretation.

The thirteen middot became the canonical set for Halachic derivation. They are built on formal logical structures (analogy, inference, scope definition) and represent the systematization of interpretive moves that had been practiced informally throughout the Tannaitic period.

## Significance

The middot are the formal mechanism by which the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] claims derivability from the Written Torah. They provide the logical justification for Halachic innovation: a new ruling is legitimate if it can be shown to follow from Scripture by one of the recognized hermeneutical principles. This is the Talmud's answer to the question of how law can expand while remaining anchored in the biblical text.

The *kal va-homer* (rule 1) is the most frequently used and most intuitive. Talmudic argument often pauses to note: "Is this not a *kal va-homer*?" and then tests whether the inference holds.

## Comparison with Other Traditions

The Thirteen Middot are the most systematically formalized hermeneutical rules in ancient Near Eastern or Mediterranean religious literature before the Christian era. Comparison:

- **Hillel's seven rules** (pre-Christian): The precursor, less comprehensive
- **Rabbinic exegesis and NT use**: The NT employs *gezerah shavah* and *kal va-homer* (Paul's use of Gen 15:6 in Romans and Galatians is a form of verbal analogy)
- **Islamic *qiyas*** (analogical reasoning): A functional parallel in Islamic jurisprudence — deriving rulings from Quran and hadith through formal analogy — though the theoretical systems differ
