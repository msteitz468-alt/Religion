# Apatheia (Passionlessness)

*Apatheia* — freedom from the passions (*pathē*) — is the goal of the perfected spiritual life in
much of Greek Christian theology. The term and the ideal are inherited from **Stoicism**, but Christian
writers, above all [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]], transform it: where the Stoic seeks
*metriopatheia* (moderation of the passions), Clement seeks their complete eradication, grounded in
**assimilation to a passionless God**.

## Clement's Account

Clement insists on the stronger sense against the Stoic mean: "the complete eradication of desire reaps
as its fruit impassibility" — "impassibility, not moderation of passion" (*Strom.* VI). "The human
ideal of continence... teaches that one should fight desire... But our ideal is not to experience
desire at all" (*Strom.* III). He distinguishes rational propension (*orexis*, for genuine bodily needs
like hunger and thirst, which the perfect man retains) from irrational lust (*epithumia*, eradicated).

The ground is theological imitation: "God is impassible, free of anger, destitute of desire"; therefore
"man, when deified purely into a passionless state, becomes a unit" (*Strom.* IV). The Saviour himself
was "entirely impassible (*apathēs*); inaccessible to any movement of feeling — either pleasure or
pain." Apatheia is thus inseparable from [[theosis-divine-nature|deification]]: "Such an one is no
longer continent, but has reached a state of passionlessness, waiting to put on the divine image." Love
replaces desire — "love is not desire... but a relation of affection, restoring the Gnostic to the
unity of the faith." The state belongs to the true [[gnosis|Christian Gnostic]]; the short *Exhortation
to Endurance* counsels that the mind "must control the passions" (see [[clement-fragments|Fragments]]).

## Reception

Clement's Christian apatheia, mediated through [[origen|Origen]], became central to later Eastern
ascetic and contemplative theology (Evagrius, the desert tradition), where apatheia is the
precondition of pure prayer and contemplation. It remains in tension with biblical and later
affective spiritualities that valorize holy desire and divine [[divine-pathos|pathos]]; some Western
theology preferred the Augustinian language of rightly-ordered loves over passionlessness.
