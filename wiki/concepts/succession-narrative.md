# Succession Narrative

The **Succession Narrative** (German *Thronfolgegeschichte*), also called the **Court
History of David**, is the name given by the German scholar **Leonhard Rost** (*Die
Überlieferung von der Thronnachfolge Davids*, 1926) to a hypothesized originally
independent narrative source embedded in the Books of Samuel — comprising roughly
**[[2-samuel|2 Samuel]] 9–20 plus [[1-2-kings|1 Kings]] 1–2** — whose organizing question
is: *who will sit on David's throne after him?* On Rost's reading, the source is a
brilliant, near-eyewitness court history written to legitimate [[solomon|Solomon's]]
accession over his rivals.

## Rost's Thesis

Rost isolated two principal pre-existing sources in Samuel:

- the **Ark Narrative** ([[1-samuel|1 Samuel]] 4–6 + [[2-samuel|2 Samuel]] 6), and
- the **Succession Narrative** (2 Samuel 9–20; 1 Kings 1–2),

distinguished by their style, their human (rather than miraculous) causation, and their
thematic unity. The Succession Narrative was long celebrated as the earliest sustained
prose history in the Western tradition — Gerhard von Rad set its composition in the
enlightened "Solomonic enlightenment" of the 10th century BCE.

## The Court Narrative's Content

Whatever its compositional status, this stretch of text contains the most psychologically
intense material in Samuel: David's adultery with [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] and the murder of
[[uriah|Uriah]] (2 Sam 11); [[nathan|Nathan's]] parable and curse on the house of David
(2 Sam 12); [[amnon|Amnon's]] rape of Tamar and murder by [[absalom|Absalom]] (2 Sam 13);
Absalom's revolt, flight, and death at the hand of [[joab|Joab]] (2 Sam 14–18); and the
deathbed maneuvering that secures Solomon's throne (1 Kings 1–2).

## Critical Status and the Literary Challenge

The independent-source hypothesis, "long embraced by scholarly consensus," is now widely
questioned. [[robert-alter|Robert Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) judges
Rost's stylistic argument "unconvincing" and his notions of style "extremely vague," and
denies that *succession* is even the true center of the sequence — which is more powerfully
focused on **David's sin and the unfolding of the prophet's curse on his house**, a theme
Alter (citing Faulkner's *Absalom, Absalom!*) finds closer to Greek tragedy than to
dynastic apologetic. To read David's grief over his infant son (2 Sam 12) as part of a
detached pro-Solomonic succession document, Alter argues, "is to do palpable violence to the
beautiful integrity of the story as the probing representation of a human life." This is an
instance of the broader tension between source criticism and the
[[literary-reading-of-the-bible|literary reading]] of the Bible.
