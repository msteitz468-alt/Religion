# Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha)

**Buddha-nature** (Sanskrit *tathāgatagarbha*, "the womb/embryo of the Thus-Gone-One") is
the [[mahayana|Mahayana]] teaching that every sentient being possesses the innate factor
that enables it to become a fully awakened Buddha. It is the doctrinal ground of the
[[zen|Zen]] conviction that one need only "see one's nature" to awaken.

## The Teaching

In the *Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra* the Buddha teaches that hidden within the defilements of each
being is "the Tathāgata's wisdom, the Tathāgata's vision, and the Tathāgata's body …
eternally unsullied, and replete with virtues no different from my own." Buddhahood is
therefore not an external acquisition but the *uncovering* of what is already present;
practice removes the adventitious defilements obscuring an intrinsically pure mind. As
[[paul-williams|Williams]] stresses, this literature is primarily about **religious
encouragement and soteriology — realising one's spiritual potential — not ontology**.

## The "Self" Problem

Buddha-nature is the most striking strain in Buddhist thought because some
*tathāgatagarbha* sources are willing to call this factor a **Self (*ātman*)** — language
that appears to collide head-on with **[[anatta|not-Self]]**, the very doctrine held to be
the Buddha's unique discovery. How to interpret this — whether the "Self" here is a skillful
*upāya* to attract those frightened by emptiness, a merely cataphatic name for the
[[sunyata|empty]], purified mind, or something more — has been debated, [[paul-williams|as
Williams notes]], "to the present day." Some traditions read it as fully compatible with
emptiness (the pure mind is empty of defilement); critics worry it smuggles back the
permanent self that [[anatta|anatta]] denies. (Recorded without adjudication — Contradiction
Protocol.)

## Relationship to the Other Schools

Buddha-nature stands alongside [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] [[sunyata|emptiness]] and
[[yogacara|Yogācāra]] "mind-only" as a third major current of Indian Mahayana thought, and
was often harmonized with them (the pure, non-dual substratum of Yogācāra and the
luminous mind of the *tathāgatagarbha* readily merged). It became enormously influential in
**East Asian** Buddhism — the metaphysical basis of [[zen|Chan/Zen]] "sudden awakening" and
of much Tibetan [[vajrayana|Vajrayana]] (e.g. *rangtong* vs. *shentong* debates over whether
buddha-nature is "empty of self" or "empty of other").
