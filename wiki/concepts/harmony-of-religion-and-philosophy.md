# Harmony of Religion and Philosophy (Averroes)

[[averroes|Averroes]]'s account of how revealed religion and demonstrative philosophy relate —
the doctrine that organizes his replies to [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] in the
*[[incoherence-of-the-incoherence|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]]* and underlies the **masses-versus-elect**
distinction. It is the Islamic-philosophical counterpart to (and is often loosely called) a
"double truth," though Averroes himself holds that there is **one truth** reached in two registers,
not two contradictory truths.

> The clearest single statement of the threefold framing below is van den Bergh's summary of
> Averroes' final chapter; the substantive positions recur in Averroes' own replies (esp. Disc. 6,
> 11, 13, and the Natural-Sciences resurrection discussion).

---

## The three possible views of religion

Van den Bergh frames Averroes' position against two alternatives: (1) the sceptical view that
religion is merely useful illusion (an "opium for the people"); (2) the view that religious
statements express **absolute truth** literally; and (3) Averroes' **intermediate** view —
religious conceptions are "the **symbols** of a higher philosophical truth, symbols which have to
be taken for reality itself by the non-philosophers." Their literal acceptance is binding on the
many because "the sanctity of the State depends on them."

The conclusion van den Bergh draws — and that the whole *Tahāfut* exchange illustrates — is that
"it is sometimes more the formula than the essence of things which divides" the philosophers and
the theologians: both affirm that God creates the world, that He has knowledge and will (in a sense
unlike the human), that He is the supreme artificer who chose the best order, and that He is one.

---

## Masses and elect (the exoteric / esoteric layering)

Averroes repeatedly distinguishes two audiences and two modes of access to truth:

- **The masses**, for whom the literal sense of revelation, including material images of the
  afterlife, is the obligatory and sufficient path; and
- **The demonstrative class** ("one in a thousand," quoting Galen), who grasp the inner meaning by
  *burhān* but are **forbidden to publicize** it and obligated to uphold the religious principles
  publicly.

On the most exalted questions he enforces this directly: the topic of God's self-knowledge and of
others "is one of those questions which it is forbidden to discuss in a dialectical way, let alone
put… in a book"; and the Sufi-philosophical truth that "there is no reality besides Him… must not
be written down and it must not be made an obligation of faith." Scripture's ascription of hearing
and seeing to God is an accommodation for the masses, whose esoteric exegesis "is limited to the
learned." This is a tradition-specific deployment of the [[zahir-batin|Zāhir/Bāṭin]]
exoteric/esoteric distinction.

---

## Religion as a "necessary political art"

In the Natural-Sciences Fourth Discussion (on resurrection) Averroes gives the doctrine its
strongest political form: "the philosophers believe that religious laws are **necessary political
arts**, the principles of which are taken from natural reason and inspiration." Human beings cannot
attain happiness — practical or speculative — without the virtues, and the virtues require
religious worship; "philosophy only leads a certain number of intelligent people to the knowledge
of happiness… religions seek the instruction of the masses generally." Even the learned are not
perfected outside the religious community.

Consequences Averroes draws:

- The learned man is obligated to **choose the best religion of his period** (he cites the learned
  of Alexandria becoming Muslims, and those of the Roman Empire becoming Christians).
- To **dispute the principles of religion is heresy**: a learned man who doubts them "merits more
  than anyone else that the term unbeliever should be applied to him," and those who would destroy
  religious belief, having the power to do so, "both theologians and philosophers will no doubt
  kill."
- "**Every prophet is a sage, but not every sage a prophet; the learned… are the heirs of the
  prophets.**"

---

## Relation to the *takfir*

This framework is how Averroes can both defend the philosophers and affirm religion: the three
doctrines for which Ghazālī pronounced *[[takfir]]* (eternity of the world, God's ignorance of
particulars, denial of bodily resurrection) are, for Averroes, either misattributed to the genuine
philosophers (Avicenna's errors, or "heretical Muslims'") or matters where the philosophers'
inner sense and the religious literal sense are two registers of one truth. He charges Ghazālī
himself with confusing the registers — and with self-contradiction across his works.

## Christian parallel: Clement of Alexandria

The Averroist harmony has a much earlier Christian counterpart in [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of
Alexandria]], who first made Greek philosophy a *positive* theological resource rather than a diabolical
rival. Clement's signature thesis (in the [[stromata|Stromata]]): "before the advent of the Lord,
philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for righteousness... For this was a **schoolmaster to bring 'the
Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews, 'to Christ'**" — philosophy "a preparation, paving the way for
him who is perfected in Christ." Philosophy is "the **handmaid** of theology" (the Hagar/Sarah
allegory: secular culture the handmaid, wisdom the wife) and "a **covenant** peculiar to" the Greeks,
given by "the same God that furnished both the Covenants." "Rightly, then, to the Jews belonged the
Law, and to the Greeks Philosophy, until the Advent." Two differences from Averroes are instructive:
(1) Clement frames the harmony **historically/providentially** (philosophy as *preparatio evangelica*,
now superseded by Christ) rather than as a permanent masses/elect epistemology; and (2) he qualifies it
with the **"theft of the Greeks"** — the best Greek dogmas are derivative, plagiarized from [[moses|
Moses]] and the prophets. Yet Clement shares the esoteric/exoteric layering (the
[[zahir-batin|reserve]] of [[gnosis|gnosis]] for "the few") and the conviction that true philosophy and
true revelation cannot finally conflict. The same instinct recurs in [[origen|Origen]] and the later
Christian appropriation of Aristotle. See also [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement (commentator)]].
