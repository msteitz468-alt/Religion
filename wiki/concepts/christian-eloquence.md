# Christian Eloquence

The Christianized theory of rhetoric developed most fully by [[augustine]] in Book IV of [[on-christian-doctrine]]. Augustine argues that eloquence is not a neutral craft to be imported wholesale from secular culture, but a capacity that must be entirely subordinated to wisdom and truth. The concept draws on Cicero's rhetorical categories while fundamentally reorienting their purpose: where classical rhetoric aimed primarily at persuasion for civic ends, Augustine's "Christian eloquence" aims at the eternal welfare of hearers — teaching truth, shaping affections, and moving the will toward God.

---

## Eloquence Subordinated to Wisdom

Augustine opens Book IV by refusing to write a standard rhetoric treatise: "it is no part of his intention to write a treatise on the laws of rhetoric" (Bk. IV, Ch. 1). Rhetoric, he argues, can serve falsehood as easily as truth, and it would be absurd for "truth in the person of its defenders to take its stand unarmed against falsehood" (Bk. IV, Ch. 2). Christians are therefore permitted — even obligated — to use rhetorical skill; but it must remain the servant, not the master.

The ordering principle is stated clearly in Ch. 5, echoing Cicero's own concession: "though wisdom without eloquence is of little service to states, yet eloquence without wisdom is frequently a positive injury." Augustine extends this beyond civic life to the heavenly wisdom revealed in Scripture, arguing that "a man speaks with more or less wisdom just as he has made more or less progress in the knowledge of Scripture" (Bk. IV, Ch. 5). The preferred method of acquiring eloquence is imitation of eloquent writers — especially the biblical authors and the church fathers — rather than the formal study of rhetorical rules (Ch. 3).

The characteristic image of the relationship between wisdom and eloquence appears in Ch. 6, describing the biblical writers: "wisdom was walking out of its house — that is, the breast of the wise man — and eloquence, like an inseparable attendant, followed it without being called for."

In Ch. 28 the principle is summed up: "a teacher should govern his words, not let the words govern him."

## The Biblical Authors as Models of Eloquence

Augustine insists that the canonical writers are "not only wise but eloquent also, with an eloquence suited to a character and position like theirs" (Bk. IV, Ch. 7). The apparent obscurity of Scripture is itself a kind of eloquence "designed to profit our understandings, not only by the discovery of truth, but also by the exercise of their powers" (Bk. IV, Ch. 6).

He offers extended rhetorical analysis of two authors:

**Paul** (Bk. IV, Ch. 7): Augustine demonstrates that Romans 5:3–5 ("tribulation worketh patience; and patience, experience; and experience, hope") exhibits the Greek figure *klimax* (Latin *gradatio*) — a "ladder" of dependent clauses — combined with *periodos* (a rounded sentence). A long passage from 2 Corinthians 11:16–30 is analyzed clause by clause to show the varied interplay of *commata* (sections), *membra* (clauses), and *periodi* (periods). Augustine's conclusion: "I do not affirm that the apostle was guided by the rules of eloquence; so I do not deny that his wisdom naturally produced, and was accompanied by, eloquence."

**Amos** (Bk. IV, Chs. 16–20): Following Jerome's Latin translation (not the Septuagint), Augustine analyzes Amos 6:1–6 ("Woe to you who are at ease in Zion...") as a masterpiece of invective: names of places used "as lamps" illuminating the style; six clauses grouped into three periods of two members each; the shift in address (from second to third person) at v. 5 to distinguish wise music from voluptuous music; and the closing use of "Joseph" as a synecdoche for "brothers in general" — "a touch of marvelous beauty." "It flowed forth in wisdom and eloquence from the Divine mind; wisdom not aiming at eloquence, yet eloquence not shrinking from wisdom" (Bk. IV, Ch. 21).

## Clarity (Perspicuity) as the Prime Virtue of the Teacher

While the biblical authors are justified in their occasional obscurity (to exercise readers and veil mysteries from the godless), teachers and expositors of Scripture must never imitate this obscurity. Their "first and chief aim" must be "to be understood" (Bk. IV, Ch. 8). Citing Cicero's phrase, Augustine calls for "a kind of careful negligence" in style — stripping away ornament in the pursuit of clear communication, even at the cost of Latin purity (Bk. IV, Ch. 10). A teacher should use vernacular forms if the correct Latin would introduce ambiguity: "What advantage is there in purity of speech which does not lead to understanding in the hearer?" (Ch. 10).

Teaching, of the three ends of oratory, is the most essential: "To teach is a necessity, to delight is a beauty, to persuade is a triumph" (Ch. 12, quoting Cicero). A discourse that is not understood has literally said nothing to its hearer, however eloquent it may be in itself (Ch. 12). In public preaching especially — where hearers cannot ask questions — the speaker must watch the congregation's movements for signs of comprehension, restating material "in every shape and form and variety of expression" until understanding is achieved (Ch. 10).

## The Three Styles

Augustine adopts Cicero's threefold division of styles (*genera dicendi*) but assigns each a functional-theological role (Bk. IV, Ch. 17):

| Style | Latin | Cicero's purpose | Augustine's purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subdued / quiet | *summisse* | small matters | **teaching** and **proving** |
| Temperate / moderate | *temperate* | moderate matters | **delighting** and **praising/blaming** |
| Majestic / grand | *granditer* | great matters | **moving** and **exhorting** to action |

In Augustine's Christianized version, all matters preached to the people concern eternal salvation and are therefore intrinsically "great" — yet different rhetorical registers are still required depending on what the moment demands: instruction, praise, or urgent moral exhortation (Ch. 18–19).

### Subdued Style

Used when the aim is to teach and prove, to resolve knotty questions with precision. It employs few ornaments, relying on argument and scriptural citation. "For teaching, of course, true eloquence consists...in making clear what was obscure" (Ch. 11). Applied even to the doctrine of the Trinity when the goal is comprehension rather than emotional movement (Ch. 19).

**Scriptural examples**: Paul's allegory of Hagar and Sarah (Gal. 3–4), reasoning through the purpose of the law (Ch. 20, §39).

**Patristic examples**:
- [[cyprian]] in *Ad Caecilium* (Ep. 63): on whether the eucharistic cup should contain water only or water mingled with wine. "In this passage...the subdued style is maintained" (Ch. 21, §45).
- [[ambrose]] in *De Spiritu Sancto*: arguing for the equality of the Holy Spirit with Father and Son. "The object he has in view demands, not beauty of diction, nor the swaying of the mind by the stir of emotion, but facts and proofs" (Ch. 21, §46).

### Temperate Style

Used for praise, blame, and description — where the aim is to delight hearers with beauty of expression and to encourage virtuous conduct or censure vice. Distinguished from mere panegyric by the requirement that it serve truth. Its characteristic beauties are graceful antithesis and the harmonious pairing of related things.

**Scriptural examples**: Paul's exhortation in Romans 12:1 ("present your bodies a living sacrifice"), Romans 12:6–16 (gifts and duties), Romans 13:7–14 ("the night is far spent"). Augustine notes the lack of harmonious clausulae in the Latin translation but attributes this either to the translators or to deliberate authorial restraint (Ch. 20, §40–41).

**Patristic examples**:
- [[cyprian]], *De habitu Virginum* ch. vii and ch. xviii: the encomium on virgins — "flowers on the tree of the Church, the glory and ornament of spiritual grace" — and its closing exhortation. Cited as temperate because it describes the character of those who have already taken vows, rather than persuading new candidates (Ch. 21, §47).
- [[ambrose]], *De Virginibus* bk. ii, ch. i: the portrait of the model virgin — "serious in speech; prudent in disposition; sparing of words; delighting in study" — whose "outward appearance is an image of her mind, and a picture of purity." Cited as temperate for the same reason (Ch. 21, §48).

### Majestic / Grand Style

Used when hearers already know what they ought to do but are unwilling — when the will must be broken down by vehemence and emotional force. Not decorated by ornament so much as "exalted into vehemence by mental emotion" (Ch. 20, §42). The test of its success is not applause but tears, groans, and change of life (Ch. 24, §53). Augustine offers an autobiographical example: at Caesarea in Mauritania, preaching against the annual faction-fighting called the *Caterva*, he recognized his success not when the people applauded "but when I saw their tears" — and the custom, "handed down from their fathers," was overthrown (Ch. 24, §53).

**Scriptural examples**: Paul in 2 Corinthians 6:2–10 ("Behold, now is the accepted time") and Romans 8:28–39 ("Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?") — treated with "both power and beauty" (Ch. 20, §42–43). Galatians 4:10–20 ("My little children, of whom I travail in birth again") is "powerful" despite lacking formal ornaments, "because there is a glow of strong emotion that makes us feel the fervor of eloquence" (Ch. 20, §44).

**Patristic examples**:
- [[cyprian]], *De habitu Virginum* ch. xii: inveighing against women who paint their faces. "Dost thou think that thou wilt carry off with impunity so audacious an act of wickedness, such an insult to God the great artificer?" The style is "majestic throughout" (Ch. 21, §49).
- [[ambrose]], *De Virginibus* bk. ii: the parallel passage on the same vice, calling forth passionate denunciation: "What folly it is to change the features of nature into those of painting." Identified as majestic (Ch. 21, §50).

## Mixing the Styles

The three styles are not hermetically separate (Bk. IV, Chs. 22–23). Monotony weakens attention; variety strengthens it. Rules for mixing:

- The **majestic** style should almost always begin with a **temperate** introduction (Ch. 23, §52).
- **Knotty questions** require the **subdued** style as an interlude within any other style (Ch. 23).
- The **temperate** style may use the **subdued** for knotty questions but never uses the **majestic** — since its aim is to please, not excite (Ch. 23, §52).
- All three styles should, in every instance, strive for **perspicuity, beauty, and persuasive power** — these are not exclusively properties of one style each (Ch. 26, §55–58).

## Prayer and Holy Life as the Foundation of Eloquence

The deepest source of Christian eloquence is not rhetorical skill but prayer and moral integrity. Before speaking, the preacher "ought to pray for himself, and for those he is about to address, before he attempts to speak...lift up his thirsty soul to God, to drink in what he is about to pour forth, and to be himself filled with what he is about to distribute" (Bk. IV, Ch. 15, §32). The Holy Spirit who spoke through persecuted apostles also speaks through those who "deliver Christ's message to those who are willing to learn" (Ch. 15).

Human teaching and divine assistance are complementary, not competing: as medicines require God to give them virtue, "so the aids of teaching, applied through the instrumentality of man, are of advantage to the soul only when God works to make them of advantage" (Ch. 16, §33).

Above all, the speaker's life is itself an eloquence. A wicked but eloquent preacher may instruct some hearers, but loses far more: "there are numbers who seek an excuse for their own evil lives in comparing the teaching with the conduct of their instructors" (Ch. 27, §60). If a man cannot speak eloquently and wisely both, he should speak wisely without eloquence. If he cannot even do that, "let his life be such as shall...afford an example to others; and let his manner of living be an eloquent sermon in itself" (Ch. 29, §62).

## Note on Cyprian's Early Style

Augustine makes an important historical-critical observation about [[cyprian]]: an early letter (*ad Donat.*, Ep. 1) contains a passage of florid, redundant eloquence — "The neighboring solitudes afford a retreat where...the leafy covering has made a portico of vine" — which Augustine calls "wonderful fluency and exuberance" but "too florid to be pleasing to serious minds." He reads this as evidence of Cyprian's pre-Christian rhetorical education, subsequently disciplined by "the wholesome discipline of Christian teaching" into the "more dignified and modest form of eloquence" visible in his later letters (Bk. IV, Ch. 14, §31).
