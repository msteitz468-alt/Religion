# Sheol (שְׁאוֹל)

**Sheol** is the Hebrew Bible's term for the netherworld — the shadowy realm of the dead
beneath the earth. It is the biblical counterpart to the Mesopotamian and Canaanite
underworlds (cf. [[mesopotamian-underworld|the Mesopotamian underworld]]), and the
indispensable background to the later development of the
[[resurrection-of-the-dead|resurrection of the dead]]. Its character in the Hebrew Bible is
less uniform than the textbook account allows — a point argued at length by
[[jon-d-levenson|Jon D. Levenson]] in
[[levenson-resurrection-and-the-restoration-of-israel|*Resurrection and the Restoration of
Israel*]].

## The Standard Picture

On the long-standing scholarly consensus (Pedersen, Gray, et al.), Sheol is:
- the **universal** destination of the dead, "good and bad without discrimination";
- a place of **weakness, gloom, silence, and forgetfulness** — "slackness, sorrow,
  exhaustion, curses" (Pedersen), the opposite of the world of light;
- a **Land of No Return** ("whoever goes down to Sheol does not come up," Job 7:9);
- often **indistinguishable from the grave / the Pit** (*bor*, *shaḥat*), with which it
  overlaps (Pedersen: the dead are in both "not in two different places").

Sheol is conveyed through a cluster of overlapping metaphors — the grave, the Pit, "the
Land," watery depths, a devouring mouth (the earth opening to "swallow" Korah and his
faction, Num 16). Reading these requires **cultural translation**, not literalism: the
range of conditions the Bible groups under "death" and "Sheol" is broader than ours (those
in mortal danger already feel themselves "in Sheol").

## Levenson's Qualification: Not Universal, Not Wholly Irreversible

Levenson argues the consensus flattens a real biblical tension:

- **Fortunate vs. unfortunate death.** Not everyone is portrayed as descending to grim
  Sheol. The patriarchs die "old and contented," **"gathered to their kin"** — a *good*
  death that the texts do not assimilate to the horror of the Pit. Sheol in its most
  terrible mode is the destiny of the **God-forsaken**, the violent, and the cut-off, not
  obviously of the blessed. (Hence the book's question, "Are Abraham, Moses, and Job in
  Sheol?")
- **Not an absolute Land of No Return.** The psalms of thanksgiving repeatedly affirm that
  **God has brought the worshiper *up* from Sheol / the Pit** (e.g. Pss 30, 40, 88's hope).
  No one rises *naturally* — "no one moves naturally up from Sheol" — but God's **surprising
  grace** can reverse the descent. Were rescue the norm, "Sheol would lose its sting."

The result is a **never-resolved tension** between (1) the conviction that all eventually
come to Sheol and (2) the affirmation of God's unlimited power to deliver his faithful even
from death. This tension, Levenson argues, "gesture[s] toward" the later expectation of
[[resurrection-of-the-dead|resurrection]], in which *both* the inevitability of death and
God's power to overcome it are held together.

## Sheol and the Immortality of the Soul

The Hebrew Bible's terrified pleas against descending to Sheol are themselves evidence that
the speakers did **not** assume a naturally immortal soul: "if the speakers in these poems
were certain they possessed an immortal and indestructible soul, they would hardly be so
terrified of death." Sheol thus stands against a Greek/Platonic afterlife of the soul as
much as against a developed doctrine of resurrection — both of which arise only later. See
[[resurrection-of-the-dead|Resurrection of the Dead]] for the contrast.

## Block on Ezekiel's Sheol (Ezekiel 26–32)

The book of [[ezekiel|Ezekiel]] contains the Hebrew Bible's most sustained netherworld
tableau (the descent oracles against Tyre and Egypt, culminating in the "dishonor roll" of
Ezek 32:17–32). Daniel Block's NICOT commentary
([[block-ezekiel-25-48-nicot|Block, Ezekiel 25–48 (NICOT)]]) offers an extended synthesis:

- **Vocabulary.** Block catalogues Ezekiel's netherworld lexicon at the first occurrence of
  *šĕʾôl* in the book (31:15–18): *bôr* ("Pit," developed from cistern → grave → abode of the
  dead), *šaḥat*, *ʾereṣ taḥtît*, and "his own distinctive variation, *ʾereṣ taḥtîyôt*." In
  26:19–21 he adds *yōrĕdê bôr* ("those who descend to the Pit") and reads *ballāhôt*
  ("Terrors") with chthonic connotations via Job 18:14's *melek ballāhôt*, "the ruler of the
  netherworld, known as Mot and Nergal in Canaan and Mesopotamia."
- **A communal cemetery, stratified.** In Block's reading of 32:17–32, "Sheol is perceived
  as a massive communal cemetery, in which graves are arranged by nationality," subdivided
  into compartments by class — the uncircumcised and dishonorably buried in *yarkĕtê bôr*
  ("the remotest parts of the Pit"), apart from the honored "heroes of old." He finds the
  closest architectural analogue in Egyptian "pyramid complexes," and derives the bed
  (*miškāb*) imagery from ANE tomb benches with headrests. The roll "reflects the conviction
  that a person's status in the hereafter is determined by one's conduct during one's
  lifetime." The metaphorically "uncircumcised" occupy the worst compartment — see
  [[circumcision|Circumcision]].
- **Living corpses.** "In Sheol the deceased live on as 'living corpses'" — fully conscious,
  aware of relative positions, bearing disgrace.
- **Demythologization.** Block argues "Ezekiel deliberately demythologizes life after death":
  no divine king or queen of the netherworld appears, and Ezekiel avoids *rĕpāʾîm*, "perhaps
  … too closely associated with the cult of the dead." Against ANE dying-and-rising gods
  (Baal, Tammuz), "as far as Ezekiel and all orthodox Yahwists are concerned, Yahweh
  exercises full control over life and death … When Yahweh banishes someone to Sheol and
  closes the door to the Pit, it is sealed. No one consigned to Sheol ever returns" (on
  26:19–21, where even *tĕhôm* "has been thoroughly demythologized … but a tool in the hand
  of Yahweh").
- **Not yet hell.** Block cautions that "the primary aim of this oracle is not doctrinal but
  rhetorical" — "a literary cartoon rather than a literary photograph" — and that Ezekiel's
  "Sheol is not to be confused with Gehenna or hell. This prophet leaves no hints yet of a
  final eschatological judgment, or of eternal fiery punishment of the wicked. All of these
  developments must await a later day."

Block's picture of a sealed, no-return Sheol governed solely by Yahweh sits alongside
Levenson's stress (above) on the psalmic hope of rescue *from* Sheol; the two sources treat
different corpora (Ezekiel's descent oracles vs. the Psalms) and are recorded here without
adjudication.

## Isaiah 14 and 38 (Oswalt, NICOT)

The taunt-song over the king of Babylon (Isa 14:4b–21) contains the Hebrew Bible's most
vivid Sheol scene. Oswalt: "The departed kings are pictured as seated on their thrones in
Sheol, the underworld. When the mighty king appears they rise as though to do homage… But
instead they mock him" — "in the sight of death all human distinctions are meaningless"
(cf. 5:14, where Sheol swallows all ranks: "There is no distinction of rank and no place for
pomposity in the nether world"). The "depths of the Pit" (14:15) answers the heaven-storming
pretension of 14:13–14: "Death mocks every person's claim to be God." Oswalt distinguishes
the "pit" of 14:19 (the grave, not the underworld), notes the ANE horror of non-burial while
judging it "unclear" whether the Hebrews thought the unburied soul wandered, and observes
(endnote) that a Hebrew underworld *deity* is excluded because "the existence of only one
God rules out such a possibility."

Hezekiah's psalm (Isa 38:10–20) is, for Oswalt, "a classic statement of the OT belief that
the dead do not praise God" (38:18). Of the harmonizations he weighs, he prefers
progressive revelation — the verse "underlines the chasm between B.C. and A.D." — suggesting
the long silence kept Israel from letting the afterlife "distract his people from an
understanding of the importance of this life." Related: "dwellers in the dust" (26:19)
reflects the common ANE picture of the underworld as "a gray, dusty place" (Job 21:26;
Ps 22:16), and the pit/dungeon of 24:22 is "temporary imprisonment until final judgment."

## Sheol in Job (Hartley, NICOT Job, 1988)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[hartley-job-nicot|Hartley's NICOT Job]]. All
interpretive claims are [[john-hartley|John E. Hartley]]'s.*

Hartley treats Sheol as a **cosmological locus** in the book — one of the fixed coordinates
of Job's world rather than an object of doctrine. The principal passages he works through are
**3:14–26** (Sheol as the leveller and the place of rest Job craves), **ch. 7** (its
finality), **10:20–22** (its darkness), **14:13–17** (the hope passage), and **ch. 17** (hope
cannot descend there).

- **Finality and the land of no return.** "There a person ekes out a ghostly existence, no
  longer able to do good or to praise God… From an ancient perspective no one returns from
  Sheol" (p. 147, on 7:9–10). At 17:15–16 Hartley concludes that hope "could have no existence
  in Sheol" — a passage he later uses against a resurrection reading of Job 19:25.
- **Levelling without equalizing punishment.** On 3:17–18: "Although there may be degrees of
  punishment in the afterlife, the earthly social structure that allows one to lord it over
  others or a taskmaster to beat his slaves into doing his bidding no longer exists" (p. 98).
- **Darkness and disorder.** Hartley counts five darkness-words at 10:21–22 (*ḥōšeḵ*,
  *ṣalmāweṯ*, *ʿêp̄āṯâ*, *ʾōp̄el*, *lōʾ səḏārîm*): "Death is a realm of gloomy darkness.
  Having no order, it is a land of chaos" (p. 191). Elsewhere he notes Sheol's watery aspect,
  its pairing with Abaddon (26:5–6), and OT indications "that Sheol was believed to be
  composed of houses, each with a door and windows."
- **14:13–17 as wish, not doctrine.** Hartley reads Job's request as an "unrealistic wish":
  "Job is not thinking of Sheol as his final resting place, but as a **hideout** safe from the
  blasts of the divine anger," with an inscribed stela (*ḥōq*) so that "when his wrath has
  cooled, God will be reminded of his servant hidden in Sheol and restore him to life"
  (p. 236).

**Resuscitation, not resurrection — and Hartley's confessional dating.** This is Hartley's
most load-bearing classification, and the theological premise on which it rests is his own
evangelical commitment, not a neutral finding:

> "The NT teaches that Christ has defeated death… **But this doctrine becomes central to
> biblical faith only after the resurrection of Christ**, for it gains its validation in
> Christ's triumph over death. Without that foreknowledge Job can only conclude death is final
> for a human being. Furthermore, Job is thinking of an individual's returning back to life
> for a limited time. **His view here is closer to a concept of resuscitation than to
> resurrection.**" (p. 237, on 14:14)

He supports the reading philologically — "Since there was no technical word for resurrection
when the book of Job was written, the concept was expressed by the piling up of words
associated with 'awakening from sleep': *qûm*, *qîṣ*, *ʿûr*" (p. 235) — and concludes that
"Job sees no possibility for an individual to be brought back from the dead and given a
second life on earth." The same argument governs his rejection of a resurrection reading at
19:25; see [[kinsman-redeemer|The Kinsman-Redeemer]] and
[[job-19-25-redeemer|Job 19:25 and the Redeemer]].

Hartley's Job material coheres with Block's sealed, no-return Sheol above and stands at some
distance from the psalmic rescue-from-Sheol strand Levenson emphasizes; the sources treat
different corpora and are recorded without adjudication.
