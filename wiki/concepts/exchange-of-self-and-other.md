# Exchange of Self and Other

The **equalizing (*parātmasamatā*) and exchanging (*parātmaparivartana*) of self and other** is the
central transformative meditation of **[[shantideva|Śāntideva]]**'s [[bodhicaryavatara|Bodhicaryāvatāra]]
(chapter 8), and one of the most influential ethical-contemplative practices in [[mahayana|Mahayana]]
Buddhism. It is the engine by which **[[bodhicitta|bodhicitta]]** (the Awakening Mind) is generated.

## The Two Stages

1. **Equalizing self and other** (*parātmasamatā*): recognize that all beings equally desire happiness
   and shun suffering, so my happiness is no more important than anyone else's. Just as the hand
   spontaneously helps the foot although they are different limbs of one body, so one should serve
   others although they inhabit different bodies (8.91 ff.).
2. **Exchanging self and other** (*parātmaparivartana*): go further — *reverse* the ordinary
   self/other valuation, placing others before oneself. "Since I am one and others are infinite,"
   others in aggregate matter infinitely more. The rational, ego-free person therefore takes on
   others' suffering and gives them one's own happiness — the seed of the Tibetan **tonglen**
   ("giving and taking") visualization.

## The Argument from Nonself

The practice is grounded directly in **[[anatta|nonself]]**: Śāntideva holds there is "no enduring
entity to which the term *I* corresponds" — the self is "simply an imposition upon an ever-changing
flow of psycho-physical elements" (8.101–2; cf. [[sunyata|emptiness]], ch. 9). So the conviction that
*my* pain matters more *because it is mine* "has no grounds in reality." The boundary between self and
other, once seen as conventional, can be re-drawn:

> "All those who suffer in the world do so because of their desire for their own happiness. All those
> happy in the world are so because of their desire for the happiness of others." (8.129)

> "If the suffering of one ends the suffering of many, then one who has compassion for others and
> himself must cause that suffering to arise." (8.105)

The paradoxical result: pure altruism turns out, unintentionally, to be the fulfilment of one's own
deepest aspiration — "the very being of the bodhisattva is the welfare of others."

## Tsong-kha-pa's Framing (Lamrim Chenmo, Vol. II)

[[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] presents the exchange as **one of two methods** for generating
[[bodhicitta|bodhicitta]] — the Śāntideva lineage, alongside the [[atisha|Atiśa]] **seven cause-and-
effect** method. He stresses that "exchange" does not mean the absurd thought "I am others," but "a
change in the orientation of the two states of mind of cherishing yourself and neglecting others."
The remedies he supplies meet two obstacles: the sense that self and other are essentially distinct
(answered by their interdependence — "Like this side and the other side of a river, they are false")
and the sense that others' suffering does not harm "me" (answered by the hand–foot analogy of a body
without "a self-sufficient essence"). The Kadam masters voice it — Lang-ri-tang-ba's "horse strategy"
of keeping self-cherishing away, and Kam-lung-ba's warning that neglecting beings makes them neglect
us in turn.

## Influence

This chapter is the scriptural foundation of the Tibetan **lojong** (*blo sbyong*, "mind-training")
tradition — Atiśa, the Kadampa masters, and the *Seven Points of Mind Training* — and of **tonglen**
practice, championed in the modern era by the **Dalai Lama**.
