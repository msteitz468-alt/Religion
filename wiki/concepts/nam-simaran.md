# Nam Simaran

**Nam simaran** (*nām simaran*, "remembrance of the Name") is the central spiritual discipline of Sikhism as taught by [[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]] and inherited from the [[sant-tradition|Sant tradition]]. It is the prescribed means by which a person attunes themselves to the divine and thereby achieves liberation from the transmigratory cycle.

## The Nam (Divine Name)

The term **nam** is, as McLeod states, "a summary expression for the whole nature of [[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]] and all that constitutes the divine being. It is, to use another favoured expression, *sat*, or 'truth', and one commonly encounters the combination *sat-nam*, or 'True Name'" (~L544–549). "Anything that may be affirmed concerning Akal Purakh constitutes an aspect of the divine Name, and a sufficient understanding of the divine Name provides the essential means to deliverance" (~L550–552).

Nam is not merely a word or formula: it is the ever-present reality of Akal Purakh pervading creation. "The nam is the ever-present and all-pervading presence of Akal Purakh, and whoever perceives this presence gains access to the means of mystical unity with Akal Purakh" (~L565–572). The nam is simultaneously the supreme manifestation of the divine in creation and the means by which the devout believer approaches liberation.

## The Human Problem and Its Remedy

The human *man* (heart/mind/spirit complex) is "congenitally blind" and "deceived by the mischievous prompting of their weak and wayward spirits" (~L581–582). "Driven by evil impulses," people accumulate karma and remain locked in the "endless round of death and rebirth" (~L524–537). The fundamental problem is [[haumai|haumai]] — "self-centred concern for all that attracts the proud, the sensual and the selfish" (~L533–535). "The person who learns how to appropriate the nam will be freed from the chains which bind that person to the wheel of transmigration" (~L542–543).

A knowledge of the divine Name can be attained "because Akal Purakh is a God of grace, speaking the Word of divine understanding to all who are prepared to shed their haumai and listen in humility" (~L597–600).

## Three Forms of Practice

McLeod identifies three modes of nam simaran (~L615–654):

1. **Repetition**: "repeating a word or expression that summarizes the meaning of the divine Name and thus of Akal Purakh — terms such as *Waheguru* or *Hari*" (~L616–629, with OCR damage to specific terms).
2. **Singing of hymns** (*kirtan*): "the singing of appropriate hymns is another form of nam simaran, for in this manner also devout believers can attune themselves to the divine" (~L632–644).
3. **Meditation** (the "most sophisticated version"): "a technique of meditation that inwardly reflects upon the meaning of the divine Name, with the intention of bringing one's whole being into harmony with the divine harmony of the Name" (~L650–654).

## The Path of Progressive Attainment

"The discipline is not an easy task, nor can one expect to secure the ultimate reward without lengthy striving. It is, however, a sure reward for those who sincerely seek it, and it is a reward which does not require withdrawal from one's daily life" (~L655–661). "All who follow the discipline of nam simaran with devout persistence will progressively ascend to levels of spiritual experience which they alone can comprehend" (~L661–664).

Nanak in the concluding stanzas of *Japji* describes **five levels** of progressively elevated attainment and illumination. "The end is *sach khand* or the Realm of Truth, mystical union in the eternal bliss of total serenity. Thus is the cycle of transmigration broken. Thus does one merge in the divine Name" (~L671–676).

The two fruits of attainment: (1) "ever-growing wonder (*visamad*), leading eventually to the rapturous peace of total blending in the divine (the condition Nanak calls *sahaj*)" (~L678–683); (2) "the final ending of the cycle of transmigration with its painful sequence of death and rebirth" (~L684–694).

## Nam Simaran as the Core of Gurmat

McLeod emphasizes that "the complete and ultimate answer to all such needs lay in meditating on the divine Name and in experiencing the bliss that awaited the person who faithfully did so. This he [Nanak] repeated in a seemingly endless multitude of different ways" (~L734–737).

From Guru Nanak through all ten Gurus, the core teaching never changed: "liberation from the transmigratory cycle depended wholly upon disciplined remembrance of the divine Name in accordance with the instruction imparted by the Guru" (~L810–814). Nam simaran was one of the practices carried over explicitly into the Khalsa Rahit: Sainapati's *Gur Sobha* (c. 1711) lists "an insistence on the time-honoured practice of *nam simaran*" among the Khalsa obligations (~L1623–1625).

The *Sikh Rahit Maryada* enshrines nam simaran as a positive obligation in the Rahit alongside the Five Ks: "the obligation to practise nam simaran" is named as a principle of belief (~L1195–1196).

## Relation to External Religion

A key polemical marker of both the Sant tradition and Nanak's teaching is that nam simaran renders external religious observance soteriologically irrelevant. "External props and practices merely delude. In spiritual terms their effect must be fatal" (~L697–699). Vainly seeking liberation through "the temple, the mosque, devout ceremony or pious pilgrimage … None of these practices can achieve the end they seek" (~L588–591).
