The Aristotelian distinction between matter (*hyle*) and form (*morphe*) — in Maimonides' Hebrew philosophical usage, *ḥomer* (substance/matter) and *tzurah* (form) — is the metaphysical key through which [[maimonides|Maimonides]] explains the origin of evil, privation, destruction, and the limitations of the human condition. Matter is not evil in itself but is the necessary substrate of all privation; form is the principle of actuality, permanence, and rational capacity.

---

## Maimonides' Account in the *Guide for the Perplexed*

### Matter as the Source of Corruption and Evil

In Part III, Chapter VIII of the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide*]], Maimonides states the core thesis:

> "Transient bodies are only subject to destruction through their substance and not through their form, nor can the essence of their form be destroyed: in this respect they are permanent. The generic forms, as you know, are all permanent and stable. Form can only be destroyed accidentally, i.e., on account of its connexion with substance, the true nature of which consists in the property of never being without a disposition to receive form." (Ch. VIII)

Matter is defined by its restless receptivity: it always possesses some form, yet it is always disposed to receive a different one. It "never leaves off moving and casting off the form which it has in order to receive another" (Ch. VIII). This constant instability — the structural incapacity of matter to remain fixed in any form — is the metaphysical root of corruption, destruction, and evil.

### The Faithless-Wife Simile

The most memorable image in Maimonides' treatment is drawn from Solomon's Proverbs:

> "How wonderfully wise is the simile of King Solomon, in which he compares matter to a faithless wife: for matter is never found without form, and is therefore always like such a wife who is never without a husband, never single; and yet, though being wedded, constantly seeks another man in the place of her husband: she entices and attracts him in every possible manner till he obtains from her what her husband has obtained. The same is the case with matter. Whatever form it has, it is disposed to receive another form." (Ch. VIII)

This simile unifies the philosophical and exegetical registers: the "virtuous woman" of Prov. 31 is the well-governed soul (form dominant), while the "strange woman" of the Proverbs as a whole represents matter's promiscuous inconstancy.

### Form and Matter in Human Nature

For the human being, the matter/form distinction maps directly onto the body/intellect opposition:

> "Man's shortcomings and sins are all due to the substance of the body and not to its form; while all his merits are exclusively due to his form. Thus the knowledge of God, the formation of ideas, the mastery of desire and passion… all these man owes to his form; but eating, drinking, sexual intercourse, excessive lust, passion, and all vices, have their origin in the substance of his body." (Ch. VIII)

The human form is identified with the "image and likeness of God" — purely intellectual. Yet:

> "It was impossible, according to the wisdom of God, that substance should exist without form, or any of the forms of the bodies without substance, and it was necessary that the very noble form of man, which is the image and likeness of God… should be joined to the substance of dust and darkness, the source of all defect and loss." (Ch. VIII)

Matter is thus not a theological mistake but a metaphysical necessity: form requires a substrate. The Creator's response was to give form "power, rule, and dominion over the substance" (Ch. VIII) — the intellect's capacity to subdue bodily appetite is itself a divine gift.

### The Corporeal Element as Epistemic Screen

In Ch. IX, Maimonides extends the analysis to knowledge:

> "The corporeal element in man is a large screen and partition that prevents him from perfectly perceiving abstract ideals: this would be the case even if the corporeal element were as pure and superior as the substance of the spheres; how much more must this be the case with our dark and opaque body." (Ch. IX)

The prophets' language of God being "concealed in vapours, in darkness, in mist" (citing Ps. xcvii. 2; Ps. xviii. 12) refers not to a corporeal God but to the inherent limitation of embodied knowers: "on account of our bodies we are unable to comprehend His essence" (Ch. IX).

## Evil as Privation (Not Positive Reality)

In Ch. X, Maimonides builds on the matter/form framework to give a formal account of evil as *privation* — absence of form or of good properties — rather than as a positive force:

> "The [so-called] evils are evils only in relation to a certain thing, and that which is evil in reference to a certain existing thing, either includes the nonexistence of that thing or the non-existence of some of its good conditions. The proposition has therefore been laid down in the most general terms, 'All evils are negations.' Thus for man death is evil: death is his non-existence. Illness, poverty, and ignorance are evils for man: all these are privations of properties." (Ch. X)

This has immediate theological implications: God cannot be the direct cause of evil, because evil is not a positive thing:

> "It cannot be said of God that He directly creates evil, or He has the direct intention to produce evil: this is impossible. His works are all perfectly good. He only produces existence, and all existence is good: whilst evils are of a negative character, and cannot be acted upon." (Ch. X)

The Isaiah text "I form the light and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil" (Isa. xlv. 7) is exegeted through this lens: darkness and evil use the verb *bara* (create from nothing) not *'oseh* (make), "because darkness and evil are not things in positive existence" (Ch. X). The *bara* of Gen. 1:1 likewise denotes creation from nothing, and non-existence can only "be said to be produced" in an indirect sense.

God creates evil only indirectly: "He creates evil only in so far as He produces the corporeal element such as it actually is: it is always connected with negatives, and is on that account the source of all destruction and all evil" (Ch. X). Even this corporeal element, "low as it in reality is, because it is the source of death and all evils, is likewise good for the permanence of the Universe and the continuation of the order of things" (Ch. X). Rabbi Meir's exegesis that "even death was good" (on Gen. i. 31: "and behold it was very good") is cited approvingly.

Social evils — caused by human beings against one another — are likewise attributed to non-existence: "they originate in ignorance, which is absence of wisdom" (Ch. XI). "If men possessed wisdom, which stands in the same relation to the form of man as the sight to the eye, they would not cause any injury to themselves or to others" (Ch. XI).

## The Three Kinds of Evil

Chapter XII presents the most systematic taxonomy. Maimonides argues that evils appear more numerous than goods only to those who judge the whole universe by individual human experience. In fact, evil is rare relative to existence as a whole. The three classes:

### First Kind: Evil from the Body's Material Constitution

> "The first kind of evil is that which is caused to man by the circumstance that he is subject to genesis and destruction, or that he possesses a body. It is on account of the body that some persons happen to have great deformities or paralysis of some of the organs." (Ch. XII)

This includes natural disasters and constitutional defects. It is rare: "the evils of the above kind which befall man are very few and rare… deformed individuals are a strange and exceptional occurrence… not one-hundredth, not even one-thousandth part of those that are perfectly normal" (Ch. XII). Genesis requires destruction — without dissolution, no new form could arise. Hence even this kind of evil is necessitated by the overall good of the species.

### Second Kind: Evil People Inflict on Each Other

> "The second class of evils comprises such evils as people cause to each other, when, e.g., some of them use their strength against others. These evils are more numerous than those of the first kind." (Ch. XII)

This kind also "originate in ourselves," though the sufferer cannot avert them. Maimonides considers it "rare occurrence that a man plans to kill his neighbour or to rob him of his property by night" when the whole inhabited world is considered (Ch. XII).

### Third Kind: Evil Men Inflict on Themselves

> "The third class of evils comprises those which every one causes to himself by his own action. This is the largest class, and is far more numerous than the second class." (Ch. XII)

This is the dominant source of human suffering: "This class of evils originates in man's vices, such as excessive desire for eating, drinking, and love; indulgence in these things in undue measure, or in improper manner" (Ch. XII). Its proximate cause is the soul's habituation to superfluity:

> "The soul, when accustomed to superfluous things, acquires a strong habit of desiring things which are neither necessary for the preservation of the individual nor for that of the species. This desire is without a limit, whilst things which are necessary are few in number and restricted within certain limits; but what is superfluous is without end." (Ch. XII)

The structural proof: nature provides necessary things abundantly and cheaply (air, water, basic food), while superfluous things are rare and costly — evidence of divine ordering toward human welfare. Those who chase superfluity exhaust themselves and then blame God, when "we suffer from the evils which we, by our own free will, inflict on ourselves and ascribe them to God" (Ch. XII, citing Deut. xxxii. 5; Prov. xix. 3).

## Galen's Dictum and the Metaphysical Necessity of Imperfection

Maimonides grounds the first kind of evil in a physical principle attributed to Galen:

> "Whatever is formed of any matter receives the most perfect form possible in that species of matter: in each individual case the defects are in accordance with the defects of that individual matter." (Ch. XII, summarizing Galen, *De Usu Partium* III)

The best possible form from blood and semen is man: "living, reasonable, and mortal." Mortality is not a defect of divine will but a metaphysical consequence of the material substrate. This is Maimonides' answer to why God did not simply make man immortal: it is not possible given the matter.

## Relation to Providence

The matter/form and evil-as-privation framework feeds directly into Maimonides' account of [[divine-providence]]. Because evil originates in matter and in privation of intellect (form), those with greater intellectual development — greater actualization of form — are more protected by providence and less subject to the evils of the third kind. The ignorant and sensual, dominated by their material substrate, are exposed: "ignorant and disobedient persons are deficient in that Divine influence, their condition is inferior, and their rank equal to that of irrational beings" (Ch. XVIII). See also [[theodicy]].
