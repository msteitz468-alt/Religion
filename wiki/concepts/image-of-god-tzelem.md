The Hebrew terms *tselem* (צֶלֶם) and *demut* (דְּמוּת) appear together in Gen 1:26–27 ("Let us make man in our *tselem*, after our *demut*"). For [[maimonides|Maimonides]] in the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the Perplexed*]], the correct reading of these terms is the keystone of his anti-anthropomorphist theology: the divine image in which humanity was made refers exclusively to the **intellect**, not to bodily form.

## Maimonides' Lexical Analysis (Part I, Ch. I)

Maimonides begins by distinguishing three related Hebrew words:

- ***Toar*** — the ordinary word for shape or physical figure: "beautiful in *toar* and appearance" (Gen 39:6); "he marketh its *toar* with a line" (Isa 44:13). This term, Maimonides insists, "is not at all applicable to God."
- ***Tselem*** — "the specific form, viz., that which constitutes the essence of a thing, whereby the thing is what it is; the reality of a thing in so far as it is that particular being." In man, this form is "that constituent which gives him human perception": "In man the 'form' is that constituent which gives him human perception: and on account of this intellectual perception the term *tselem* is employed in the sentences 'In the *tselem* of God he created him' (Gen 1:27)."
- ***Demut*** — derived from *damah*, "he is like," denoting agreement with respect to **an abstract relation**, not physical resemblance. Maimonides cites "I am like a pelican of the wilderness" (Ps 102:7): the comparison is about sadness, not wings.

The key conclusion: "In the phrase 'Let us make man in our *tselem*' (Gen 1:26), the term signifies 'the specific form' of man, viz., his intellectual perception, and does not refer to his 'figure' or 'shape.'"

## The Intellect as Divine Image

Maimonides extends the argument in Part I, Ch. I: "As man's distinction consists in a property which no other creature on earth possesses, viz., intellectual perception, in the exercise of which he does not employ his senses, nor move his hand or his foot, this perception has been compared — though only apparently, not in truth — to the Divine perception, which requires no corporeal organ. On this account, i.e., on account of the Divine intellect with which man has been endowed, he is said to have been made in the form and likeness of the Almighty, but far from it be the notion that the Supreme Being is corporeal, having a material form."

This reading drives Maimonides' interpretation of Ps 49:13 ("Adam unable to dwell in dignity, was brought to the level of the dumb beast"): the "contempt" of one's *tselem* (Ps 63:20) "can only concern the soul — the specific form of man, not the properties and shape of his body."

## Adam's Pre-Fall Intellect and the Fall Narrative (Part I, Ch. II)

In a closely related discussion, Maimonides addresses an objection: did Adam only acquire rational capacity *after* the Fall, through eating from the Tree of Knowledge? His reply: "the intellect which was granted to man as the highest endowment, was bestowed on him before his disobedience. With reference to this gift the Bible states that 'man was created in the form and likeness of God.'"

The Fall did not grant reason; it granted knowledge of moral convention (*tob* and *ra'*) at the expense of some intellectual clarity. "When Adam was yet in a state of innocence, and was guided solely by reflection and reason … he was not at all able to follow or to understand the principles of apparent truths." After the Fall, he "was punished by the loss of part of that intellectual faculty which he had previously possessed."

## Seth and Intellectual Generation (Part I, Ch. VII)

Maimonides extends the image-of-God theme to Gen 5:3 ("Adam … begat a son in his own likeness, in his form"). The sons born before Seth were "not human in the true sense of the word; they had not 'the form of man.'" Seth, who "had been instructed, enlightened and brought to human perfection," genuinely bore the divine image. "It is acknowledged that a man who does not possess this 'form' (the nature of which has just been explained) is not human, but a mere animal in human shape and form."

## Hermeneutical Note

Maimonides' treatment exemplifies the *sod* (mystical/philosophical) register of PaRDeS hermeneutics: the plain sense (*peshat*) of "image" as physical likeness is explicitly rejected as the vulgar misreading that leads to anthropomorphism. The true meaning is accessible only to those trained in philosophy.
