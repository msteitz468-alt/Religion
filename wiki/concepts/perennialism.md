# Perennialism (the Perennial Philosophy)

**Perennialism** — the *philosophia perennis* — is the claim that beneath the doctrines,
rites, and institutions of the world's religions lies a **single, universal mystical
truth** to which all of them are, at their best, pointing. The differences are the
"foothills"; the summit is one. It is the most popular form of the conviction that "all
religions are one," and the chief polemical target of [[stephen-prothero|Stephen
Prothero]]'s *God Is Not One*.

## Definition and the Governing Metaphor

The signature image is **many paths up one mountain**: "It is possible to climb life's
mountain from any side, but when the top is reached the trails converge" (Huston Smith).
Differences in "theology, ritual, and organizational structure" are real but superficial;
the same goal beckons all climbers. A second stock image is the **blind men and the
elephant** — each tradition grasps a part of a reality too large for any to see whole (a
parable, Prothero notes, that perennialists read as proof of unity but that has itself been
told to opposite ends across traditions).

The term *philosophia perennis* is old (Agostino Steuco, 16th c.; taken up by Leibniz), but
the modern movement runs through:

- **Aldous Huxley**, *The Perennial Philosophy* (1945) — the anthology that named the
  modern current.
- The **Traditionalist School** — René Guénon, **Frithjof Schuon** (*The Transcendent Unity
  of Religions*), Ananda Coomaraswamy — who distinguish an esoteric, unanimous core from
  exoteric, divergent forms.
- **Huston Smith**, *The World's Religions* (1958; orig. *The Religions of Man*) — the
  best-selling popularizer; "religions at their best," foregrounding mystics (al-Ghazali,
  John of the Cross, Zhuangzi).
- **Joseph Campbell** (the "monomyth"), **William Blake** ("All Religions Are One," 1795;
  the "Poetic Genius"), **Ramakrishna** and **Swami Vivekananda** (a Vedanta-inflected
  source), and, in Prothero's reading, the popular work of [[karen-armstrong|Karen
  Armstrong]].

## Relation to the Other Pluralism Positions

Perennialism is **position 4** on the [[religious-pluralism-debate|pluralism debate]] page —
a cousin of, but distinct from, [[religious-pluralism|Hickian pluralism]]:

- **vs. [[john-hick|Hickian pluralism]].** Both deny that any one tradition is uniquely
  normative. But the perennialist common core is **knowable** — a mystical essence directly
  accessible at the heart of all traditions — whereas Hick's **[[the-real|Real]]** is
  *unknowable in itself* (we know only its culturally conditioned manifestations). Hick is,
  in effect, a perennialist who has lost his nerve about the contents of the summit.
- **vs. [[karen-armstrong|Armstrong]]'s developmental-experiential view (position 6).**
  Armstrong's recurring [[apophatic-theology|apophatic]] insight looks perennialist, and
  Prothero files her there; but she frames it historically and pragmatically rather than as
  a timeless *philosophia*.
- **vs. inclusivism.** Prothero (following **Hans Küng**) argues perennialism shares
  inclusivism's vice: just as **Karl Rahner**'s "anonymous Christians" conscripts outsiders
  into Christianity, the perennialist who says "all paths lead to God" is usually imagining a
  *particular* God — Swami Prabhavananda's is Hindu, Huston Smith's is the mystic's. It is
  "**pretend pluralism**": presumptuous beneath its generosity.

## The Particularist Critique (Prothero)

Prothero's case against perennialism — his "**Godthink**" — has several prongs:

1. **It is empirically false.** The religions diverge sharply on doctrine, ritual,
   mythology, experience, and law. They pose **different problems** and seek **different
   goals** ("Only Christians seek salvation"); they are climbing *different mountains*. See
   [[four-part-model-of-religion]].
2. **It is theology disguised as description.** Huston Smith "calls all religions one" — that
   is a *theological* claim, not neutral reporting, exactly as Dawkins's "religion is
   delusion" is. Perennialism presents a faith ("All Religions Are One") as analysis.
3. **It is selective.** Perennialists write about "religions at their best," foregrounding
   mystics and muting history, institutions, law, and "dirty laundry" — Smith's own
   admission.
4. **It is not even safe.** The standard defense — that affirming unity defuses conflict —
   fails: pretending Sunni and Shia, or Hindu and Muslim, differences are trivial does not
   make the world less violent but blinds us to real fault lines.
5. **It is presumptuous** (the Küng point above) — and it disrespects practitioners, for whom
   the *hajj*, baptism, *halakha*, and the rest are precisely *not* inessential.

## Defenders' Reply

Perennialists (and sympathetic pluralists) respond that (a) they never denied surface
differences — only relocated unity to the mystical/ethical depth; (b) the **mystics**
across traditions really do report convergent apophatic experience (the data behind
[[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]]); (c) the project is **ethically motivated** —
against triumphalism and "holy war" — a motive Prothero concedes is "to their credit"; and
(d) particularism risks reinstating exclusivist rivalry and ignoring genuine convergences
(the [[religious-pluralism-debate|Golden Rule]]).

## A Confessional Critic: Sharpe (1910)

Long before the modern pluralism debate, A. B. Sharpe's *[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True
Nature and Value]]* (1910) argued against the perennialist reading **of mysticism specifically**
— the claim that beneath their doctrinal trappings "Catholic, Protestant, Buddhist and
Mohammedan" mystics contemplate one and the same impersonal Absolute. Sharpe's two objections
(developed on [[mysticism]]): (1) **logical** — we possess only the mystics' own reports, and
cannot credit the bare "unity" while discarding the specific content (e.g. Teresa's explicit
intuition *of the Holy Trinity*); the recorded experiences "are so various as to be mutually
destructive"; (2) **moral** — if the great Christian contemplatives were deceived about Whom they
met, their expenditure of life is hard to justify. Unlike Prothero's pluralist-era critique,
Sharpe's is openly confessional: he rejects the common core *in order to* defend the reality of
specifically supernatural Christian mysticism. (Contradiction noted, not adjudicated: the
perennialist "defenders' reply" above rests precisely on the convergence Sharpe denies.)
