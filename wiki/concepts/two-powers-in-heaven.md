# Two Powers in Heaven / Divine Mediators in Judaism

This page covers the **gradation of divinity within ancient Judaism** — the spectrum of
divine and semi-divine beings that, on [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]'s argument in
[[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]], made it intelligible *within
Jewish monotheism itself* to speak of a human ([[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]]) as divine.
Its sharpest form is the **"two powers in heaven"** doctrine identified by Alan Segal:
the belief, widespread enough that the rabbis (2nd–4th c. CE) condemned it as heresy,
that "along with God in heaven there was a second power on the divine throne." Segal
traces it to first-century Palestine and to non-Christian as well as Christian Jews.

## Henotheism vs. Monotheism

Ehrman distinguishes **henotheism** ("there are other gods, but only one God is to be
worshiped") from **monotheism** ("there is, in fact, only one God"). He reads the Ten
Commandments ("you shall have no other gods before me") as henotheistic and the majority
view of the Hebrew Bible; strict monotheism (Isaiah's "I alone am God") is the minority
view, dominant by Jesus's time but still admitting **lower-level divinities** — angels,
cherubim, seraphim, "principalities, dominions, powers, and authorities," the great
angels Michael, Gabriel, Raphael. That some Jews **worshiped angels** is shown by the
laws prohibiting it ("You don't get laws prohibiting activities that are never
performed").

## Biblical and Para-biblical Warrants

- **Psalm 82** — God "in the midst of the gods [*elohim*]"; the divine-council members,
  also called "sons of God" (Job 1) and "children of the Most High," can be called gods,
  yet are made mortal.
- **The [[angel-of-the-lord|Angel of the Lord]]** — sometimes identified *as* God,
  sometimes appearing *as a human*: Hagar (Gen 16), Abraham's three visitors (Gen 18–19),
  the burning bush (Exod 3). "Either the Lord appears as an angel in the form of a human,
  or the Angel of the Lord is the Lord himself, God in human guise."
- **Daniel 7 / the [[son-of-man|Son of Man]]** — "one like a son of man" coming on the
  clouds and given everlasting dominion.
- **Genesis 1:26** ("Let us make humankind in our image") and **Exodus 24:9–10 vs. 33:20**
  (elders see the God of Israel, yet no one can see God and live) — read by two-powers
  exegetes as the *second* power being seen.

## Divine Hypostases: Wisdom and Logos

A **hypostasis** is "a feature or attribute of God that comes to take on its own distinct
existence apart from God."

- **Wisdom (Hokhmah/Sophia)** — Proverbs 8 (created "at the beginning," "a master worker"
  beside God); the Wisdom of Solomon ("a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty,"
  "beside God on his throne"), performing acts the Bible assigns to God's angel.
- **Logos/Word** — God creates by uttering his word (Gen 1); "since it is God's Logos, in
  a sense it is God," yet once emitted "it stands apart from God as a distinct entity."
  For the Stoic, Platonic, and especially **Philonic** development of the Logos as "second
  God" and mediator, see [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]] and
  [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]].

## Exalted Humans and Angelification

Within Judaism humans too could be divinized or **angelified**: [[enoch|Enoch]] (taken
without dying; in 2 Enoch "anointed … and put into the clothes of my glory," becoming
"like one of his glorious ones" — later identified with the Son of Man), [[moses|Moses]]
(enthroned in Ezekiel the Tragedian; "god and king of the whole nation" and "gradually
becoming divine" in Philo), and the **king of Israel** (Ps 2:7 "today I have begotten
you"; Ps 45:6 "Your throne, O God"; Isa 9:6 "Mighty God" — most likely Hezekiah's
coronation). Ehrman, citing John Collins, notes the king-as-god pattern ultimately
derives from Egyptian royal ideology, where graded divinity likewise applied.

Ehrman endorses [[larry-hurtado|Larry Hurtado]]'s thesis that "principal angel
speculation and other types of divine agency thinking … provided the earliest Christians
with a basic scheme for accommodating the resurrected Christ next to God" — "to make Jesus
divine, one simply needs to think of him as an angel in human form."
