# Ein Sof (אֵין סוֹף)

"Without end" / "Infinity." The utterly transcendent, unknowable divine Infinite in [[kabbalah|Kabbalistic]] theology — the hidden source from which the [[sefirot|ten sefirot]] emanate and within which all being is ultimately rooted. Ein Sof is not a sefirah; it is the ground of all sefirot, the divine reality before and beyond all names, attributes, or predicates. Of Ein Sof, the kabbalists consistently say: nothing can be affirmed, nothing denied, nothing even known.

The term originates as an adverbial phrase (*ein sof*, "without limit") and was nominalized in early Kabbalistic writing (c. early 13th century) to designate the infinite divine source as a proper name — or rather, as the only proper "name" for that which is beyond naming.

---

## Theological Definition

Ein Sof is distinguished from every prior Jewish and philosophical conception of God:

**vs. the God of the Hebrew Bible**: The biblical God acts in history, speaks, loves, judges, repents. Ein Sof does none of these. The "blessed Holy One" (*Qudsha Berikh Hu*) to whom prayer is addressed, who speaks at Sinai and delivers Israel from Egypt, is Tif'eret — the sixth sefirah, not Ein Sof. Ein Sof is the silent reservoir from which Tif'eret (and all the sefirot) flow.

**vs. Maimonides' God**: Maimonides' negative theology insists that God's essence is unknowable and that divine attributes are to be understood via *via negativa*. This is structurally similar to Ein Sof, but the difference is decisive: for Maimonides, the God of negative theology *is* the God of the Torah; for the kabbalists, the God of the Torah (YHVH, "blessed Holy One") is a sefirah — a knowable, expressible dimension of the divine, distinct from the truly unknowable Ein Sof. The kabbalists thereby both radicalize and relativize Maimonidean apophaticism.

**vs. Neoplatonic "the One"**: Neoplatonism's ultimate principle (Plotinus's "the One") is also beyond being and beyond knowing, and the sefirot are structurally analogous to the Neoplatonic hypostases (Intellect, Soul, Matter). The kabbalists were aware of Neoplatonism through Arabic and Hebrew intermediaries, and their language resonates with it. But Ein Sof is not "the One" of philosophical abstraction; it is the God of Israel in its most interior dimension, and the sefirot are not philosophically-derived hypostases but are mapped onto the patriarchs, divine names, and Torah commandments.

## Ein Sof and Creation

A distinctive problem: if Ein Sof is infinite and unlimited, how can anything other than Ein Sof exist? Creation out of nothing (*creatio ex nihilo*) in classical Jewish theology presupposes a God who acts to create a world distinct from himself. Kabbalah offers a different account: the sefirot *emanate* from Ein Sof (not ex nihilo creation but extension of divine self-expression), and the material world is the most remote and attenuated expression of this emanation.

The Zohar does not use the term *tsimtsum* (contraction) — that is a later Lurianic concept — but it does speak of Ein Sof "bursting out" of its aura (*avir*) to reveal the first point (Ḥokhmah):

> "Once this yod expanded, what remained was found to be: *or* (light), from that mystery of concealed *avir* (aura). After the primordial point, yod, emerged from it into being, it manifested upon it, touching yet not touching." (Zohar 1:15a–b)

The key dynamic: Ein Sof remains fully itself even as it "bursts" or "expands." The emanation is not a loss but a self-extension in which the source is not diminished. The traditional formula: "He fills all worlds and surrounds all worlds."

## "It" — the Grammatical Marker of Ein Sof

Because Ein Sof cannot be named, the Zohar often refers to it by the pronoun "It" (*hu*, impersonally used) rather than "He" or any divine name. When the Zohar says "the unknown concealed one created the palace," the subject is Ein Sof, not a named God. This grammatical move signals the profound otherness of the infinite ground from the personal God of biblical religion.

Similarly, the Zohar's re-reading of Genesis 1:1 — "With beginning, *It* created Elohim" — makes Ein Sof the grammatical subject: *It* (Ein Sof, the utterly unnamed) used *reshit* (Ḥokhmah) to create *Elohim* (Binah). The creation narrative begins not with God acting but with the Infinite coming to expression.

## The Paradox: Presence and Absence

Ein Sof is simultaneously:
- **Radically absent**: beyond all names, images, prayers, concepts, and predicates — "no will here, no thought" (Zohar)
- **Radically present**: nothing can exist outside of Ein Sof; "He is the place of the world" (rabbinic saying the kabbalists inherit); the material world is the most external expression of the Infinite, not separate from it

This double character is perhaps the deepest mystery in Kabbalistic theology. In the Lurianic system it is addressed by *tsimtsum* (God "contracts" to create space for otherness). In the Zohar it is held as an irreducible paradox: the spark of impenetrable darkness both is and is not; it "touches and does not touch" the first point; creation both happens and does not happen from the side of Ein Sof.

## Ein Sof and the Nine Palaces

One Zoharic passage (Parashat Noaḥ) describes Ein Sof as radiating through a curtain into "nine palaces of palaces — neither lights nor spirits nor souls; no one can fathom them." These nine palaces are related to the nine sefirot below Keter (Ḥokhmah through Malkhut), each of which is a "palace" — a structured space — through which the undifferentiated radiance of Ein Sof passes and becomes progressively more determinate and knowable. At the level of Ein Sof, even these palaces are beyond comprehension.

---

## Relation to the "Ayin" (Nothingness)

The kabbalists frequently designate either Keter or Ein Sof itself as *Ayin* (אַיִן, "Nothing") — a positive mystical nothing, not absence but pure potentiality beyond all determinacy. The move from *Ayin* (Nothingness) to *Yesh* (Being, the first sefirot) is the primal creative act. This echoes — and likely influenced — later Jewish philosophical discussions of creation, and bears comparison to apophatic traditions in Christian mysticism (Eckhart's *Gottheit* vs. *Gott*, the divine "desert" vs. the Trinitarian God) and Islamic mysticism (Sufi distinctions between *al-Haqq* the unknowable and the God of the Names).

## Cross-Tradition Comparison

**Pseudo-Dionysius / Christian Apophatic Theology**: The *Mystical Theology* of Pseudo-Dionysius (late 5th c.) reaches a similar apophatic extreme — the divine "darkness beyond light," beyond all names and non-names. The structural parallel with Ein Sof is notable; both emerge from the same Neo-Platonic inheritance (Proclus, Plotinus). Whether the kabbalists were directly influenced by Pseudo-Dionysius (possibly via Sufi intermediaries) is debated. Cross-link: [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]] for the Christian apophatic tradition.

**Philo's "God beyond the Logos"**: Philo already insisted that the true God transcends even the Logos; the divine essence (*ousia*) is utterly beyond predication. This is structurally close to Ein Sof vs. the sefirot, and Philo's influence on medieval Jewish thought (through Arabic philosophy) may be a partial channel. See [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]].
