# Docetism

**Docetism** (from Greek *dokeō*, "to seem/appear") is the Christology that **Christ was
not really human but only *appeared* to be** — "completely God," and God "could not be a
human any more than a human can be a rock." It is the mirror-image of
[[adoptionism|adoptionism]]: where adoptionism denies Christ's divinity, docetism denies
his **humanity** (one of the three "Christological dead ends" in
[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]'s [[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]).

Docetism grew out of the [[incarnation|incarnation Christologies]] of the later first
century. Ehrman finds its first clear opponents already in the New Testament: **1 John**
combats community members who deny that Christ "came in the flesh," calling them
"antichrists" (1 John 4:2–3). **[[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius of Antioch]]** (~110 CE)
insists Christ "was truly born, both ate and drank, was truly … crucified and died" — with
a personal stake: if Christ only *appeared* to suffer, Ignatius's own impending martyrdom
is meaningless.

## Marcion as Docetist

The major docetist was **[[marcion|Marcion]]** (see [[marcionites|Marcionites]] /
[[marcionism|Marcionism]]). Because his Christ belonged to the higher God of love and not to
the [[demiurge|creator God]] of the Old Testament, "he did not belong in any sense to the
creation itself": he "descended from heaven in the appearance of a full-grown adult, as a
kind of phantom who only appeared to have human flesh … He didn't really suffer and he
didn't really die. How could he? He didn't have a real body." Orthodox writers (chiefly
[[tertullian|Tertullian]], *Against Marcion*) answered that real salvation requires real
flesh and real blood.

Docetism is distinct from the **Gnostic [[gnostics|separationist]] Christology** (a divine
Christ temporarily inhabiting a human Jesus), though some Gnostics held fully docetic views.
