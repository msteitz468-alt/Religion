# Resurrection of the Body

The bodily resurrection of the dead at the end of the world — the subject of [[city-of-god|*City of
God*]] Book XXII, [[augustine|Augustine]]'s fullest defense of the doctrine against pagan philosophy.

## Against the philosophers

Augustine's chief opponents are the **Platonists**, who (citing Cicero, *De Republica*) hold that "a
body of earth" cannot exist in heaven and that the blessed soul must escape every body. He inverts the
order theory: if God already bound an incorporeal soul to an earthly body — the greater marvel — He can
surely raise an earthly body to heaven. He treats the **world's belief** in Christ's resurrection as
itself a miracle: that "a very few men, of mean birth… and no education" persuaded the world of so
incredible a thing is inexplicable unless it was true. He argues that **[[porphyry|Porphyry]]** (the
soul must flee all body) and **[[plato|Plato]]** (souls must return to bodies), if combined, yield the
Christian teaching — bodies in which the blessed "shall suffer no more."

## Objections answered

Augustine resolves a catalogue of puzzles about *which* body rises: **aborted fetuses and infants**
(raised not in their diminutive state but in the body time would have given them); **stature and age**
(all raised at the prime of Christ's age, ~30, defects removed but "the natural substance suffer[ing] no
diminution"); **hair and nails** (excess redistributed "to preserve the proportion of the parts" — "not
a hair shall perish" means number, not length); **monstrous births** (deformities removed); **bodies
eaten by beasts or cannibals** (the flesh "restored to the man in whom it first became human flesh… like
a pecuniary loan… returned to the lender"); **women** (raised as women — "the sex of woman is not a vice,
but nature" — but beyond marriage and childbearing); and **the martyrs' scars**, which "will not be a
deformity, but a mark of honor."

## The spiritual body

The resurrection body is "sown an animal body, it shall rise a spiritual body" — **"spiritual… but still
flesh, not spirit,"** subject to the spirit, needing no nutriment, "without any fleshly corruption." Its
freedom is the eschatological *non posse peccare*: free will is not withdrawn but perfected, "set free
from delight in sinning to take unfailing delight in not sinning" — the saints receive from God the
inability to sin. This culminates in the [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]] and the "eternal Sabbath"
(see below), the felicity of the [[two-cities|city of God]].

## Contemporary miracles as witness

Augustine argues miracles have **not ceased** (though they are now local and less published, the canon
of Scripture being closed), and catalogues healings he personally attests — many at the **relics of
[[stephen-protomartyr|Stephen the protomartyr]]** at Hippo and Calama (nearly seventy recorded "at the
hour at which I write"), including the Cappadocian siblings Paulus and Palladia cured before his
congregation at Easter. These miracles, he argues, "witness… to this faith which preaches Christ risen
in the flesh." See [[relic-veneration]].

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[darrell-bock|Bock's]] BECNT [[acts|Acts]]. All interpretive
claims are [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]]'s.*

In the apostolic preaching of Acts, Bock argues, **Jesus's resurrection is a unique, within-history
event**, distinct from the Jewish hope of a general end-time resurrection. Where the Pharisees held
a resurrection "at the consummation," the apostles proclaim that God raised *this one* — a singular
act that Bock (citing Wright at Acts 17 and 1 Thess.) calls "a fresh idea in Judaism," which
"lacked precedent" for an individual raised within history. The resurrection-exaltation-ascension is
"one continuous movement" (Bruce).

- **Psalm 16 as proof (2:25–31).** In the Pentecost sermon Peter argues from Ps. 16:8–11 that God
  "will not abandon my soul to hades, nor let your Holy One see corruption." Bock reads the psalm
  typologically-prophetically: it speaks of an individual "in a way that extends beyond that
  individual." Since David "is dead, buried, and his tomb is still with us" (2:29), the psalm
  "cannot ultimately be about David" but about Jesus, whom God raised — a syllogism (the Messiah
  will rise; God raised Jesus; therefore Jesus is the Messiah) grounded in the earliest testimony
  (1 Cor. 15:3–5). The same Ps. 16:10 "will not see corruption" argument recurs in Paul's Pisidian
  Antioch sermon (13:34–37), linked to Ps. 2:7 and Isa. 55:3 by gezerah shewa (see
  [[speeches-in-acts|the speeches in Acts]]).
- **The Pharisee/Sadducee crux.** At his Sanhedrin hearing Paul declares, "concerning the hope and
  the resurrection of the dead I am on trial" (23:6), splitting the council: Bock notes the
  Sadducees "say that there is no resurrection, or angel, or spirit" (23:8), while the
  [[pharisees|Pharisees]] affirm it. Bock reads "hope and resurrection" as a hendiadys — hope
  rooted in resurrection.
- **"Hope and resurrection" as the recurring trial charge.** Across the defense speeches (chs.
  24–26, 28) resurrection is the real issue: Bock cites Fitzmyer that "'the resurrection of the
  dead' thus echoes like a refrain in these latter chapters of Acts" (24:15, 21; 25:19; 26:6–8,
  22–23; 28:20). Paul insists the Christ "must suffer" and be "the first to rise" (26:23), so that
  the resurrection "indicates who Jesus is" and is God's public "proof" of Jesus's appointment as
  judge (17:31).
