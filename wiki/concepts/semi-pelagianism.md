# Semi-Pelagianism

The mediating position (so named only since the seventeenth century) that resisted both [[pelagian-controversy|Pelagius]] and the severer features of [[augustine|Augustine]]'s doctrine of [[grace]] and [[predestination]] — holding that grace assists rather than replaces free will, and that **the initial movement of faith (*credulitas*) is the sinner's own**. Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. XIII.

## The Objection to Augustine

Monks at Hadrumetum and in South Gaul protested that Augustine's [[predestination|predestinarianism]] "paralysed moral effort and verged on fatalism, not to say Manichaeism." Its classic spokesman was **John Cassian**, the monk of Marseilles, who (while firmly anti-Pelagian) urged:
1. the first movement toward good sometimes begins from God, sometimes from man's own volition (Zacchaeus), which God then strengthens;
2. Adam retained his knowledge of the good after the Fall;
3. the human will is "not so much dead as sick"; grace is "**cooperation**";
4. predestination must be *post praevisa merita* — in the light of foreseen merits.

## Outcome

Semi-Pelagianism was worn down by Augustine's growing authority. The **Council of Orange (529)** affirmed a moderated Augustinianism: death and sin pass to all Adam's descendants; the will is so weakened it cannot even believe without prevenient grace; "in every good action the first impulse comes from God"; yet "predestination to evil is to be anathematized." Augustine's harsher theses (irresistible grace, double predestination) were quietly dropped.
