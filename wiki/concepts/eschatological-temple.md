# The Eschatological Temple (The Church as God's Dwelling Place)

The identification of the Christian community — and the individual Christian — as the temple of God is one of the central theological claims of Pauline ecclesiology. In 1 Corinthians in particular, this identification is not merely a metaphor but a load-bearing theological premise: it grounds Paul's ethical instructions about sexual purity, idol feasts, worship order, and the treatment of other believers. The concept has deep OT roots in the expectation that God's presence, previously located in the wilderness tabernacle and Jerusalem temple, would come to dwell eschatologically with his people in a new and universal way.

## OT Background

The Hebrew Bible narrates a sequence of divine dwelling places: the Garden of Eden (God's original dwelling with humanity), the wilderness tabernacle (Exod 25–40; God's presence travels with Israel in the desert), the Jerusalem temple (Solomon's temple, 1 Kgs 6–8; 2 Chr 2–7; God's *kābôd* fills the house at its dedication), the vision of the eschatological temple in Ezekiel 40–48 (a restored, idealized temple; the glory returns), and the Isaianic vision of worldwide worship (Isa 56:6–7; 60; 66:18–24; God's house becomes a "house of prayer for all nations").

Key trajectories:
- **The divine *kābôd*** fills the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–38) and the temple (1 Kgs 8:10–11) at dedication, identifying the building as God's true dwelling. See [[divine-glory-kabod|Divine Glory (*Kābôd*)]].
- **Ezekiel's departing glory** (Ezek 10–11; the *kābôd* abandons the defiled temple) and **Ezekiel's returning glory** (Ezek 43:1–5; the *kābôd* returns to a new temple) frame the exile as a temple-defilement event and the restoration as a new temple-filling.
- **Haggai 2:7–9**: "I will fill this house with glory." The second temple disappoints; a greater filling is anticipated.
- **Isaiah 66:1–2**: "Heaven is my throne and the earth is my footstool; what is the house that you would build for me?" — questioning the adequacy of any human structure to house God.
- **Second Temple Judaism** developed the idea that the community of the faithful could constitute a "spiritual temple" — God's presence dwelling among the righteous few rather than in the stone building. 1QS 8–9 (the Dead Sea Scrolls, Community Rule) describes the Qumran community as a "holy house for Israel" and "a holy of holies for Aaron." This development primes Pauline usage.

## Pauline Development

Paul deploys the temple concept at several key points, all grounded in the conviction that the Spirit poured out at Pentecost (cf. 1 Cor 12:13) now dwells in the community and in individual believers — making them the *locus* of God's eschatological presence.

**1 Corinthians 3:16–17** — Collective temple:
"Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you? If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple."
The "you" (*hymeis*) is plural — the community, not the individual. The teachers creating factions (chs. 1–4) threaten to "destroy" the temple. Paul's warning is severe: God will destroy the destroyer of his dwelling.

**1 Corinthians 6:19** — Individual temple:
"Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God?"
Here *sōma* (body) of the individual believer is the temple. Sexual union with a prostitute (6:16–18) defiles the body-temple. The connection to sexual purity is direct: the body is a holy vessel in which God dwells.

**2 Corinthians 6:16**:
"For we are the temple of the living God; as God said, 'I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people'" — citing Leviticus 26:12 / Ezekiel 37:27.

**Ephesians 2:19–22** (expanded treatment from Thielman):
Ephesians develops the temple concept most extensively within a sustained argument about Jew-Gentile reconciliation. The passage follows immediately from the "middle wall" text (2:14–15): once the Mosaic law as social barrier is abolished and the "one new human being" created (2:15), the new humanity is constituted as a single household and temple:

- **2:19**: Former Gentile aliens (*xenoi*, *paroikoi*) are now "members of the household of God" (*oikeioi tou theou*) — the household metaphor introduces what becomes a building/temple image.
- **2:20**: The foundation is "the apostles and prophets" (NT; genitive of authorship — the revelation they received, not the OT prophets), with Christ Jesus himself as the "cornerstone" (*akrogōniaios*). The cornerstone — the stone that determines the orientation of the entire structure — is Christ. The Greek *akrogōniaios* could also mean "capstone" (the final stone crowning the structure), but *akrogōniaios* in LXX Isa 28:16 (which Paul has already cited in Rom 9:33 and 1 Pet 2:6–7) means the foundation cornerstone, and the sense "in whom the whole structure is joined together" (2:21) confirms the foundation/cornerstone reading.
- **2:21**: "In him the whole structure, being joined together (*synarmologoumenē*), grows into a holy temple in the Lord." *Synarmologoumenē* — joint-fitting, dovetailing — describes precise masonry. The temple is under construction; it grows (*auxei*). This is an unusual combination of building and growth metaphors: the temple is organic (a growing body) and architectural (a structure being fitted together).
- **2:22**: "In him you also are being built together (*synoikodomeisthe*) into a dwelling place (*katoikētērion*) for God by the Spirit." *Synoikodomeisthe* — "built together" — uses the *syn-* prefix again (cf. 3:6's *syn-* compounds in the mystery definition). The Gentile addressees are being built *together with* Jewish believers into a single structure. The telos: a *katoikētērion* (a permanent dwelling, not merely a *paroikia* / temporary lodging) for God by the Spirit.

[[frank-thielman|Thielman]] identifies the *oikonomia* metaphor as threading through chapters 1–3: God's *oikonomia* (household management, stewardship) is the administration of the mystery of Gentile inclusion (1:10; 3:2, 9), whose result is the *oikos theou* (household of God, 2:19), whose structure is the *naos* (temple, 2:21) — moving from management to membership to building. The three images are not interchangeable but sequential: the mystery's disclosure (1:9–10; 3:3–6) manages (produces) a household (2:19) that is simultaneously a temple (2:21–22).

The temple of Eph 2:19–22 surpasses 1 Corinthians' community-temple in two respects: (a) it is explicitly universal, not local (the whole structure, *pasa oikodomē*); (b) it is explicitly Jew-Gentile — the Gentiles are being built *into* what was already Israel's temple-story (Isa 28:16), not building an alternative structure. The Gentiles are not replacing Israel; they are being incorporated into the eschatological Israel. See [[ephesians-middle-wall|The Middle Wall in Eph 2:14]] for the barrier whose demolition makes this incorporation possible.

**1 Peter 2:4–5** (non-Pauline but parallel):
Believers as "living stones" being built into "a spiritual house" (*oikos pneumatikos*), a "holy priesthood" to offer "spiritual sacrifices."

## Significance in 1 Corinthians (Ciampa-Rosner)

[[roy-ciampa-brian-rosner|Ciampa and Rosner]] (PNTC, 2010) identify the eschatological temple as one of four key themes giving 1 Corinthians its theological unity (alongside the lordship of Christ, worldwide worship, and the glory of God). Paul's entire letter can be read as instructions for the maintenance of the temple's purity and its proper function as a locus of God's presence and glory.

- **Sexual immorality (chs. 5–7)**: The incestuous man defiles the community-temple (5:6–8 uses Passover/unleavened bread imagery, echoing the purging of leaven from God's holy house). Prostitution defiles the body-temple directly (6:19).
- **Idolatry / food offered to idols (chs. 8–10)**: Eating at an idol's table is incompatible with eating at the Lord's table (10:21). The Lord's Supper is a temple-meal; participation in idol feasts is a defilement of the temple community.
- **Worship order (chs. 11–14)**: The Lord's Supper must be discerned properly (11:29: "discerning the body"), because eating unworthily is a sacrilegious act within God's temple. Spiritual gifts must build up the body/temple (12:27; 14:12).
- **Resurrection and the body (ch. 15)**: The resurrection vindicates the body as permanently significant — God will raise and glorify the same bodies that now constitute his temple.

The temple concept also links individual ethics to community ethics: what I do with my body affects God's corporate dwelling; what we do in worship either honors or dishonors the indwelling Spirit.

## Eschatological Dimension

The NT temple-church concept is eschatological in two senses. First, it fulfills OT prophecies about a new or restored temple that would surpass the Jerusalem temple (Isa 66:1–2; Ezek 37:27; Hag 2:7–9). Second, it is proleptic: the current indwelling of the Spirit is the "down payment" (*arrabōn*, 2 Cor 1:22; 5:5; Eph 1:14) of the final consummation, when God will be fully "all in all" (1 Cor 15:28) and dwell with his people permanently (Rev 21:22: "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb"). The eschatological temple is simultaneously present (the Spirit already dwells) and future (the final glory not yet revealed).

## The Consummation: The New Jerusalem as the Holy of Holies (Osborne)

[[grant-osborne|Osborne]]'s Revelation (BECNT, 2002) shows the temple trajectory reaching its
*telos* in the New Jerusalem (Rev. 21:9–22:5). The city is a perfect **cube** (21:16) — the shape of
the **Most Holy Place** in Solomon's temple (1 Kgs. 6:20) — so the entire city is the Holy of Holies,
the place of God's unmediated presence. Hence its most striking feature: **"I saw no temple in the
city, for its temple is the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb"** (21:22). The whole sequence of divine
dwellings (Eden → tabernacle → temple → church-as-temple) culminates not in a *building* but in
**God and the Lamb themselves** dwelling directly with their people (21:3), with the city also
portrayed as the **final Eden** (the river and tree of life, 22:1–5). The eschatological temple is no
longer proleptic (the Spirit as down payment) but consummated: God dwells with humanity face to face.
See [[new-creation|New Creation]] and [[lamb-of-god|The Lamb of God]].

## Relationship to the Jerusalem Temple

The NT temple-church concept does not simply replace the Jerusalem temple without remainder. In Acts, Paul's own practice involves the temple and temple vows (Acts 21:26). The tension between the stone temple and the community-as-temple reflects Second Temple debates about the sufficiency of the existing temple structure. After the temple's destruction in AD 70, the church-as-temple concept became even more theologically central — especially in the Johannine tradition (John 2:19–22: Jesus's body as the new temple). The rabbinic tradition developed a parallel concept of the synagogue and Torah study as the locus of divine presence (the *Shekhinah*) after the temple's fall.
