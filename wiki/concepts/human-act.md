# The Human Act and the Sources of Morality (Aquinas)

After establishing [[beatitude|beatitude]] as the last end, [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] analyzes the
**human act** (*actus humanus*) — the deliberate, willed act through which a person advances toward
or strays from that end — at [[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I-II, Q6–21. Only acts
proceeding from a deliberate will (as distinct from mere *acts of a man*, like idle scratching) are
**moral** acts, specified and imputable.

## The voluntary and the involuntary (Q6)

The voluntary requires an intrinsic principle (the will) together with knowledge of the end. Aquinas
examines what diminishes or removes voluntariness:
- **Violence** cannot touch the will's own interior act, only commanded or external acts (Q6, A4).
- **Fear** produces "mixed" acts — voluntary simply, involuntary in a certain respect ("what is done
  through fear is voluntary") (Q6, A6).
- **Concupiscence** makes an act *more* voluntary, not less (Q6, A7).
- **Ignorance** excuses only when it is *antecedent* and non-negligent; *consequent* (voluntary)
  ignorance does not excuse (Q6, A8).

## The anatomy of the act (Q8–17)

Aquinas resolves a single human act into an ordered interplay of **intellect** and **will**, often
read as a twelve-moment structure: on the part of the end — apprehension of the good, *volition* of
it, *intention* of the end; in deliberating about means — *counsel* (deliberation), *consent*,
*choice* (*electio*); and in execution — *command* (*imperium*), *use* (*usus*), and *enjoyment*
(*fruitio*) of the attained end. Choice (the act most properly free) falls on the means; intention
bears on the end.

## The three sources (fonts) of morality (Q18–21)

The moral goodness or malice of an act derives from three sources (Q18):
1. **The object** (*objectum*) — what the act is in its kind; this gives the act its primary moral
   **species** and primary goodness (Q18, A2).
2. **The circumstances** (*circumstantiae*) — which add or subtract accidental goodness, and can
   even constitute a new species when they bear on a special order of reason (Q18, A10–11; the seven
   circumstances, Q7: who, what, where, by what aids, why, how, when).
3. **The end** (*finis*) — the goodness of the interior act of the will, the formal element (Q18,
   A6).

The governing principle, from Pseudo-Dionysius: *bonum ex integra causa, malum ex quocumque defectu*
— "good results from a cause that is whole; evil, from any single defect." An act is good only if
object, circumstances, and end are all in order.

**Reason and conscience** (Q19): the goodness of the will depends on its object as proposed by
reason; hence to act against an erring conscience is evil. An **erring conscience always binds**,
but it *excuses* only when the error is involuntary and non-culpable; culpable ignorance does not
excuse (Q19, A5–6).

## Significance

This analysis is the architecture of Catholic moral theology: the distinction of the three fonts is
the standard framework for evaluating the morality of acts, and underlies later debates over
intrinsically evil acts (object) versus proportionalist weighing (circumstances and end). It is the
bridge from the metaphysics of the will to the treatises on [[passions-aquinas|the passions]],
habits, virtues, and [[natural-law|law]] (Q90 ff.) that follow in the *Prima Secundae*.
