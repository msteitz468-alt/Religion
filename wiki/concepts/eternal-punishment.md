# Eternal Punishment (Hell)

The endless, bodily punishment of the damned — the subject of [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XXI,
the "end reserved for the city of the devil." *Hell* and *the second death* are treated here.

## The problem and the argument

Augustine treats eternal punishment **before** eternal blessedness because "it seems to be more
incredible that bodies endure in everlasting torments than that they continue… without any pain in
everlasting felicity." The **second death** "holds the soul in the body against her will" — death made
eternal, since the damned can "neither… enjoy God and live, nor… die and escape the pains of the body."
Against the objection that pain entails death, he argues that pain requires only a *living* subject; the
soul itself shows that something can suffer and yet be immortal.

The fire is **material** ("burning by contact") yet really torments immaterial spirits, as the immaterial
soul is now contained in material members; "one fire certainly shall be the lot of both" (Matt. 25:41).
Eternal punishment for temporal sin is just — no human law proportions a penalty's *duration* to the
crime's. The clincher is Matt. 25:46: since the same word "eternal" governs both destinies, "as the
eternal life of the saints shall be endless, so too the eternal punishment… shall have no end."

## The argument from natural marvels

To rebut the claim that perpetual burning without consumption is *impossible*, Augustine assembles a
celebrated catalogue of natural wonders that already do the seemingly impossible: the **salamander** living
in fire, the perpetual fires of **Etna**, the **peacock's** antiseptic flesh (which he kept thirty days
without decay), **charcoal** indestructible underground, **quicklime** that kindles when wetted and stays
cold in oil, the unbreakable **diamond**, the **magnet** (witnessed personally), asbestos, the apples of
Sodom, and more. "A portent happens not contrary to nature, but contrary to what we know as nature"; God
who created these natures can change them at will.

## Against the "merciful" (*misericordes*)

Augustine refutes, one by one, those who from tenderness soften hell: those who think all the baptized,
or all Catholics, or all Eucharist-partakers, or all almsgivers will eventually be freed. He affirms a
**limited cleansing**: some suffer *purgatorial* pains — but only **before** the final judgment, never as
an escape *from* hell after condemnation (see [[purgatory]]). On **[[origen|Origen]]**'s
[[universal-salvation-controversy|universalism]] (even the devil finally saved), he presses a reductio:
the logic of mercy, consistently applied, would free the fallen angels too — which none of the merciful
dare claim — so the position is unprincipled, and the Church "not without reason condemned him."

## Faith, works, and perseverance

The "foundation" of faith (1 Cor. 3:15) does not save those whose lives consist in "the works of the
flesh" (Gal. 5:19–21): "if any man say he has faith, and have not works, can faith save him?" (Jas.
2:14). Being **"saved by fire"** means the fire of *tribulation* that burns away lawful but excessive
earthly attachments from those who keep Christ as their foundation — *not* a fire from which anyone
escapes hell; "from that fire no man shall be saved." Almsgiving avails for past sins when life is
amended, never as a purchased license for ongoing sin.
