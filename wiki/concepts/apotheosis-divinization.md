# Apotheosis and Divinization (Greco-Roman)

**Apotheosis** (Greek, "becoming a god") and the broader Greco-Roman conception of
**divine humans** form the conceptual background against which
[[bart-ehrman|Bart Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]])
sets the earliest claims about Jesus. Ehrman's central claim is that the ancient world
did **not** conceive of divinity as a black-and-white "unbridgeable chasm" between God
and humans (the later, post-fourth-century Christian view) but as a **continuum**: "both
humanity and divinity are on a vertical continuum, and these two continuums sometimes
meet at the high end of the one and the low end of the other."

## The Divine Pyramid

Ehrman offers a **pyramid** as a model of pagan divinity (everyone but the Jews being a
polytheist):

1. **Apex** — one ultimate deity (Zeus/Jupiter, or "an unknown god"), virtually
   infinitely powerful, source of all.
2. **The great gods** — the twelve Olympians, patron gods of cities and state.
3. **Local gods** — of war, love, weather, health, childbirth, forests, rivers.
4. **Daimones** — lower divinities nearer to humans (e.g. Socrates's *daimon*).
5. **Divine humans** — exceptional generals, kings, emperors, philosophers, beauties
   elevated to or near the divine plane.

Divinity is **graded and mutable**: an inscription from Mytilene honors benefactors who
"have attained heavenly glory" and provides that the city "can further deify him." To
call the emperor a god "did not mean that the emperor was Zeus … He was a divine being
on a much lower level."

## Three Models of Divine–Human Overlap

Ehrman maps three pagan models (paralleled in [[two-powers-in-heaven|ancient Judaism]]):

1. **Gods who temporarily become human** — Jupiter and Mercury as mortals in Ovid's
   tale of Philemon and Baucis; the Lystra crowd taking Paul and Barnabas for Hermes
   and Zeus (Acts 14:11).
2. **Beings born of a god and a mortal** (demigods/heroes) — Hercules (son of Jupiter
   and Alcmena); Alexander the Great (rumored son of Zeus). Ehrman stresses: "In none
   of the stories … is the mortal a virgin."
3. **A human who becomes divine** (apotheosis/exaltation) — Romulus, Julius Caesar,
   Augustus. This third model "provided the most important conceptual framework that
   the earliest Christians had" for [[exaltation-christology|conceiving Jesus as both
   human and divine]].

## Illustrative Figures (Ehrman's examples)

- **Romulus** — at his death, caught up in a storm-cloud to heaven, "hailed … as a god
  and a god's son" (Livy), and afterward appearing to Proculus Julius; identified with
  the god **Quirinus**.
- **Julius Caesar** — claimed descent from Venus; "voted into divinity" by the Senate;
  a comet at Augustus's games taken as "the soul of Caesar … received into heaven."
- **Augustus** — birth legend of conception by Apollo as a serpent; hailed *divi
  filius* ("Son of God") and "savior … the birthday of the god marked for the world the
  beginning of good tidings." Ehrman: "Jesus was not the only 'savior-God' known to the
  ancient world." (NT scholar Michael Peppard: only two figures were actually called
  "Son of God" — the emperor and Jesus — "probably not an accident.")
- **Apollonius of Tyana** — Pythagorean sage with an annunciation, miraculous birth as
  "Son of Zeus," ascent to heaven, and a post-death appearance to a doubter; treated by
  the pagan Hierocles as a rival to Jesus and rebutted by Eusebius. Ehrman calls him the
  closest pagan analogue to a [[incarnation|god-man]], though "I don't know of any other
  cases … where an already existing divine being is said to be born of a mortal woman."

See [[roman-religion]] for the **emperor cult** (*cultus deorum*) as the institutional
expression of this conceptuality, and the satire of Peregrinus-style divine claims
in Lucian (whose invented post-death vision of the self-immolated Cynic mocks an
audience credulous enough to repeat it as fact).
