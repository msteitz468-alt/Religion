# Dogmatics as Science (*Wissenschaft*)

## Overview

Karl Barth argues that dogmatics has the right to call itself a science (*Wissenschaft*) without thereby subordinating itself to any external general concept of science, and without needing to justify its claims before a philosophical tribunal. The concept is developed in §7.2 of *Church Dogmatics* I.1 (lines 12606–13126).

## The Basis of the Claim

Dogmatics calls itself a science "because it has no interest in de facto self-segregation from the other human efforts at knowledge that bear this name" (line 12617–18) — that is, it affirms solidarity with other human knowledge enterprises without accepting their criteria as normative. It also asserts "a hidden but coming and therefore real unity of all such human efforts" (lines 12620–21). The scientific character of dogmatics does not depend on recognition by external philosophy: "Its scientific character consists in its orientation . . . to the question of dogma which is raised by the existence of the Church" (lines 12633–35).

## Regular and Irregular Dogmatics

Barth introduces a distinction within dogmatic practice:

- **Regular dogmatics**: the dogmatics of the theological school, aimed at systematic completeness — covering the full range of concepts significant for Church proclamation, the biblical witness, the history of dogmas, and methodological explicitness. Examples: Origen's *De principiis*, Peter Lombard's *Sentences*, the medieval Summas, Melanchthon's *Loci*, Calvin's *Institutio*, 17th-century Protestant orthodoxy (lines 12659–69).
- **Irregular dogmatics**: free discussion of specific problems without systematic completeness, taking the form of treatises, Bible commentaries, sermons, theses, or aphorisms. Examples: Athanasius, Luther, Hamann, Kohlbrugge, Blumhardt (lines 12724–32).

Historically, "irregular dogmatics has been the rule, and regular dogmatics the exception, in every age of the Church" (lines 12736–38). Scientific character belongs to neither type automatically: "the scientific nature of dogmatics lies in its special objectivity, namely, in its orientation to the question of dogma" (lines 12745–46), which either type may or may not possess.

Barth's own *Church Dogmatics* is an attempt at regular dogmatics, justified on the grounds that "especially for Protestantism to-day an orderly school dogmatics might be healthier than a further plethora of irregularities" (lines 12770–72), while cautioning that school dogmatics must regard itself only as "a necessary second form" (lines 12806–07).

## Three Marks of Scientific Dogmatics

Barth enumerates three demands on any dogmatics claiming scientific character:

1. **Orientation to Church proclamation**: Dogmatics must address the problem of Church proclamation and not dissolve into autonomous metaphysics. "Dogmatics as gnosis without regard for the task of Church proclamation, dogmatics as self-motivated and self-grounded metaphysics is unscientific as dogmatics" (lines 12826–28). All dogmatic statements are "preparation for Church proclamation" (line 12846).

2. **Critical function**: Scientific dogmatics must *criticise and correct* Church proclamation, not merely repeat it historically or ratify it. It must "unsettle rather than confirm Church proclamation as it meets it in its previous concretions" (lines 12886–88). A dogmatics that merely certifies the Church's existing proclamation — however pious — is complacent and therefore unscientific: "The true Church is waiting for something far different from certification" (line 12904).

3. **Scriptural criterion (decisive)**: The standard by which Church proclamation is measured must be "the revelation attested in Holy Scripture and not a philosophical, ethical, psychological or political theory" (lines 12954–56). This is the decisive mark. Barth cites Luther on theology's right to its own vocabulary (*phrasis Spiritus sancti*): *Non debet ars artem impedire, sed unaquaeque debet retinere suum quasi cursum et uti suis terminis* — "One art should not obstruct another, but each art ought to hold to its own course . . . and use its own terms" (lines 12990–91, Luther, *Comm. on Gen.* 1:14). What makes a theologian a theologian is "indemonstrable and unassuming attention to the sign of Holy Scripture" (lines 12972–73).

## Protest Against Theologische Korruption

Barth enters a formal protest against "the corruption of theology which has now been in full swing so long and which has been brought about by trying to understand and treat it simply as a branch of the humanities in general" (lines 13027–30). The formal autonomy of theology can be pointed out, but "the seriousness of the reference stands or falls with the attention that is actually paid to the witness of Holy Scripture and not just with speaking about it" (lines 13035–36). The only effective advocacy for theological relevance is "a wholly relevant theology" — not its description but its existence (lines 13038–40).
