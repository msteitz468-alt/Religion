# Purgatory / Purgatorial Punishment

Temporary, cleansing punishment after death for the regenerate, distinct from the eternal punishment of
the damned. [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XXI contains an influential early form of the idea —
"the germ of the doctrine of purgatory," as the NPNF editors note.

## Augustine's qualified affirmation

Within his refutation of those who deny [[eternal-punishment|eternal punishment]], [[augustine|Augustine]]
affirms that **some** of the regenerate "suffer temporary punishments after death" and so are *not* doomed
to the everlasting pains that follow the [[last-judgment|last judgment]] — citing Matt. 12:32 ("not
forgiven… neither in this world, neither in that which is to come," implying some sins *are* forgiven in
the world to come) and the refiner's fire of Malachi 3. The contested Pauline locus is **1 Cor. 3:11–15**
("saved… yet so as by fire"), which Augustine reads primarily as the fire of *tribulation* in this life.

He sets two firm limits: such cleansing avails only for those "regenerated in Christ" who lived neither so
badly as to be unworthy nor so well as to need nothing; and **purgatorial pains exist only *before* the
final judgment** — "let him not fancy that there are any purgatorial pains except before that final and
dreadful judgment." It is never a route *out of* hell once one is condemned. Baptized infants who die need
no such cleansing, "spiritual regeneration of itself" sufficing.

## Aquinas's account (Summa Theologiae, Supplement XP Q71 and Appendix)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] gives purgatory its mature scholastic form, following directly from his
distinction in the [[sacrament-of-penance|sacrament of penance]] between the *guilt* of sin (remitted
by absolution) and its *temporal punishment* (which may remain after death).

- **Suffrages for the dead** (XP Q71): **charity** is the bond extending the Church's communion beyond
  death, so the living can aid the dead. The **three chief suffrages** are the **Mass** (operating *ex
  opere operato*), **almsgiving**, and **prayer**. Suffrages can diminish or remit the *punishment* of
  souls in purgatory (without limit) but **cannot change a soul's fundamental state** — they do not
  help the damned (Origen's universalism is named and condemned), the children in [[limbo]], or the
  saints already in glory. Three medieval opinions restricting suffrages (Praepositivus; the Gilbertines;
  William of Auxerre) are rejected.
- **The nature of the punishment** (Appendix): its **existence** is proved from **2 Maccabees 12:46**
  and Gregory of Nyssa, and its denial called "erroneous and contrary to faith." Its pains — of **loss**
  (deferred vision of God) and of **sense** — **exceed all earthly suffering**, wrought by divine
  justice alone (not demons). Duration varies with how deeply sin is rooted. By the "common law"
  purgatory is in the **same fire and place as hell** (the damned consigned lower), the fire eternal in
  substance but temporary in its *cleansing* effect.

The dependence of this doctrine on 2 Maccabees ties it to the canon dispute: the book is
deuterocanonical for [[catholicism|Catholicism]] and [[eastern-orthodoxy|Orthodoxy]] but non-canonical
for [[protestantism|Protestants]], who rejected purgatory along with [[indulgences|indulgences]] and
the suffrages. See [[bodily-resurrection]] and [[hell]] for the surrounding eschatology.

## Reception

The idea was developed more fully by Gregory the Great and became defined doctrine in the Roman Catholic
Church (with prayers for the dead and indulgences), while the **Reformers rejected** it as lacking
scriptural warrant — a major point of canon-divergence between Catholicism and Protestantism. Augustine's
text is cited by both sides.
