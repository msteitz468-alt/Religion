# Ahiṃsā (Nonviolence)

**Ahiṃsā** ("the absence of the desire to kill") is the principle of nonviolence toward living beings —
one of the most important and contested ethical concepts in Indian religious history. The word is a
**double negative**: *a-hiṃsā*, the negation of *hiṃsā* (violence, injury). This structure is
characteristic: the Indian nationalist Bal Gangadhar Tilak translated it as "harmlessness" — another
double negative. The concept does not name a positive virtue (love, compassion) but the *absence of a
negative impulse* to harm.

## Historical Development

**Vedic period**: The Ṛg Veda shows no prohibition on violence toward animals; cattle raiding is
heroic, and animal sacrifice is a central rite. Early ahimsa stirrings appear in the Brāhmaṇas: "eat
animals the right way; don't stop eating them" — a limit on sacrifice, not an abolition.

**Upaniṣadic period (c. 600–200 BCE)**: The transmigration of souls (rebirth as animals) does not
automatically generate vegetarianism; the relationship between killing, sacrificing, eating, and
worshipping animals is debated with great complexity. The first sustained vegetarian arguments emerge
from the Jaina and Buddhist movements, which preach an uncompromising ahimsa that Hindu traditions
must then respond to.

**Epic period (c. 300 BCE–300 CE)**: The [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] is "simultaneously about
'grotesque, sanctioned violence' and an extended meditation on whether violence is ever avoidable."
The tension is productive but unresolved. **Aśoka** (c. 265–232 BCE) provides the first royal
ahimsa program — limited, hedged, and partial, but unprecedented in scale.

**Classical Hinduism**: Ahimsa is absorbed into Hindu ethics as a component of *sadharana dharma*
(universal dharma) — "nonviolence, truth, not stealing, purification, and the suppression of the
sensory powers" (Manu 10.63) — while *sva-dharma* (particular caste-duty) often requires violence,
especially for Kshatriyas. The paradox is structural and never resolved.

## The Violence Paradox

Ahimsa generates the central **moral aporia of the epics**: if the universal dharma gives "pride of
place to nonviolence," but a warrior's *sva-dharma* is to kill — how does he reconcile them? The
[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]] addresses this directly:

- Arjuna refuses to fight, invoking something like ahimsa and the horror of killing kinsmen.
- Kṛṣṇa's response is not to endorse ahimsa but to argue *around* it: since the soul cannot be
  killed, killing the body in war (like killing an animal in sacrifice) "is not really killing."
- The Gita then offers **karma-yoga** ("karma without kāma") as the resolution: act without
  attachment to results — including the results of killing.
- Crucially, the Gita **mentions ahimsa in only four lists of conventional virtues** (10.5, 13.7,
  16.2, 17.14) and **never discusses it** as a principle to be worked through.

Arjuna's counter-argument — that violence is simply inevitable — is equally forceful: "I see no being
that lives in the world without violence. Creatures exist at one another's expense; the mongoose eats
mice, just as the cat eats the mongoose; the dog devours the cat."

## Matsya-nyāya ("Fish Eat Fish")

The Sanskrit phrase *matsya-nyāya* (big fish eat little fish) is used across the Mahābhārata and
*Arthaśāstra* to mean two opposite things:
1. **Justification for coercive kingship**: Without a king wielding punishment, the strong will devour
   the weak; therefore, violence in the service of order is not merely permitted but required.
2. **Justification for anarchy**: Humans are animals and cannot be stopped from acting like animals;
   the "Geneva Convention" of war is a fiction.

Both usages reflect the same underlying realism about violence, deployed for opposite political agendas.

## Aśoka's Hedged Ahimsa

Aśoka's edicts are the first historical record of a political program of nonviolence. His approach was:
- Progressively reducing the animals killed in his palace kitchens (from "hundreds of thousands" to
  two peacocks and a deer — but "why go on killing these three? Perhaps because the emperor was fond
  of roasted peacock and venison").
- Protecting specific animal species from slaughter (a long list of birds, fish, mammals) while
  maintaining the right to kill others.
- Never discontinuing capital punishment or torture.

Doniger: "It is a very limited sort of nonviolence, not unlike that of the Brāhmaṇa text that
pointed out that eating animals is bad but then let you eat them in certain ways."

## Yudhiṣṭhira and the Limits of Ahimsa

The Mahābhārata meditates on ahimsa through Yudhiṣṭhira, who is "torn between violence and
nonviolence." His term for his refusal to abandon his dog is *a-nṛ-śaṃsa* — "not-cruelty" — a
**species-specific double negative** (specifying harm *to humans*), applied here (stretched) to an
animal. When he says "I am determined not to be cruel," the Sanskrit is more precisely "I am
determined not to harm humans."

"The story [of Yudhiṣṭhira's dog] shows just how rotten the caste system is but does not change it.
No dogs get into heaven." The text equivocates; it does not resolve the aporia; it reaches for a deus
ex machina (the dog was not really a dog; it was the god Dharma). "In case of a real dog… what then?"

## King Śibi and Self-Sacrifice

The reductio ad absurdum of the vegetarian position is the **King Śibi story**: Śibi protects a dove
(Agni in disguise) from a hawk (Indra in disguise) by cutting off pieces of his own flesh to equal
the dove's weight — giving up more and more, eventually offering his whole body. The hawk reveals
himself as Indra; it was all a test. This narrative pattern shows "how deeply embedded in Hinduism is
the ideal of self-sacrifice, often to the point of martyrdom and self-torture."

## Jaina vs. Hindu Ahimsa

- **Jainism** preaches an **uncompromising** ahimsa: no killing of any living being, including insects
  and plants (which have souls). Monks wear masks to avoid inhaling insects.
- **Buddhism** prohibits killing as a precept but is less strict about diet (monks may eat meat given
  to them if they have not seen, heard, or suspected the animal was killed for them).
- **Hinduism** negotiates: some professions require violence; some sacrifices require animal death; the
  soul does not die. "Ahimsa as a Hindu term claims a space closely associated with Jainism and Buddhism."

The Brahmin redactors of the Mahābhārata "created a text that accepted the political implications of
the violence of the universe, in part precisely in order to distinguish themselves from the nonviolent
Buddhists and Jainas."
