# Thomistic Anthropology (The Soul)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s account of the human soul, in the Treatise on Man
([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I, Q75–83), is the metaphysical core of his anthropology. It
fuses Aristotle's hylomorphism (*De Anima*) with the Christian doctrine of the immortal, individually
judged soul — producing his distinctive middle position between Platonic dualism and materialism.

## The soul as subsistent and immortal (Q75)

The human soul is **incorporeal and subsistent** (Q75, A2): the intellectual principle has an
operation (understanding universals) in which the body does not share, so it must subsist in itself.
Hence it is **incorruptible / immortal** (Q75, A6), by three arguments: as a subsistent form it cannot
be corrupted *per se*; it admits no contrary; and the natural desire for perpetual existence cannot be
in vain. The souls of brutes, by contrast, are *not* subsistent — they are produced by bodily power
and perish with the body, whereas the human soul is created directly by God.

## The soul as the substantial form of the body (Q76)

The pivotal thesis: "the intellectual principle… is united to the body as its form" (Q76, A1) — the
soul is not merely a mover using the body (Plato) but the one **substantial form** that makes the
human being to be at once a body, living, sentient, and rational. This entails the **unicity of
substantial form**: there is only one soul in man (not separate vegetative, sensitive, and rational
forms), and the rational soul communicates *existence* to the whole composite (Q76, A3–4).

## Against Averroist monopsychism (Q76, A2; Q79, A4–5)

Aquinas directly refutes the **Averroist** thesis (from [[averroes|Averroes]], "the Commentator")
that there is a *single* separate intellect — possible and active — shared by all humanity. He calls
it "absolutely impossible and unreasonable" (Q76, A2): it would destroy the fact that *this individual*
understands and is morally responsible. Each soul has its own **active intellect** (Q79, A4–5), a
power of the individual soul derived from God as the first separate intellect; Aristotle's calling the
agent intellect "separate" means only that it is not the act of a bodily organ, not that it is one
subsistent substance for all.

## Intellect, synderesis, conscience (Q79)

Among the powers Aquinas distinguishes the **possible** and **active** intellects, memory, and
reason. **Synderesis** (Q79, A12) is the natural *habit* of the first practical principles ("the law
of our intellect"); **conscience** (Q79, A13) is not a power but an *act* — the application of
knowledge to a particular case (*cum alio scientia*). This grounds his moral theology (cf.
[[human-act]], [[natural-law]]).

## Will and free-will (Q82–83)

The **will** is the rational appetite, moved by the good apprehended by intellect; it wills the last
end ([[beatitude|happiness]]) by necessity but the means freely. **Free-will** (*liberum arbitrium*)
is not a power distinct from the will but the will precisely as **choosing** among means — paralleling
how reason relates to intellect (Q83, A4). Man therefore acts freely and is master of his acts.

## Epistemology: knowledge from the senses (Q84–85)

Aquinas's account of how the embodied soul knows (Q84–88) is thoroughly **Aristotelian and
anti-Platonic**. The intellect is at first "a blank tablet on which nothing is written" (*De Anima*
iii); all our intellectual knowledge **originates in the senses** (the senses are its *material
cause*, Q84, A6), against both Democritus (knowledge as material images) and Plato (innate
participation in separate Forms). The **active intellect abstracts the universal intelligible
species from the phantasm** (Q85, A1); and in this life the intellect must always "turn to the
phantasms," even to use knowledge it already has (Q84, A7). This sense-grounded abstraction is the
epistemology underlying the [[five-ways]] (knowledge of God only from effects) and the rejection of
any direct vision of God in this life (see [[beatific-vision]]).

## The origin of the rational soul (Q90; Q118)

The human soul is **created immediately by God**, not produced by the parents and not transmitted
with the semen — to hold the latter (**traducianism**) Aquinas calls "heretical" (Q118, A2;
"the intellect alone comes from without," Aristotle). The soul is infused at the term of generation,
the prior (vegetative, then sensitive) forms being superseded by the one rational soul that God
creates — consistent with the unicity of substantial form.

## The state of innocence and original justice (Q94–95)

In Eden Adam did not see God's essence (which would have made sin impossible), but knew through
infused intelligible species "by the radiation of the first truth," a state midway between ours and
the [[beatific-vision|beatific vision]] (Q94). His integrity rested on **original justice** (Q95) —
a *supernatural* gift (a grace, not a property of nature) by which reason was subject to God, the
lower powers to reason, and the body to the soul. That this rectitude was grace, not nature, is shown
by its loss at the Fall while the demons retained their natural gifts — the integrity whose privation
*is* [[original-sin|original sin]] (cf. [[sin]]).

## Significance

The "soul as form of body" doctrine — defended at the cost of controversy with both the Augustinian
plurality-of-forms school and the Latin Averroists — was eventually adopted by the Council of Vienne
(1311) as Catholic teaching. It lets Aquinas affirm the unity of the human person (against dualism)
while preserving the soul's immortality and individual survival (against materialism and monopsychism).
