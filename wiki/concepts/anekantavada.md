# Anekantavada

**Anekāntavāda** — literally "the doctrine of many-pointedness" (also rendered "many-sidedness" or "non-one-sidedness") — is Jainism's signature epistemological and ontological position: that no single perspective can fully apprehend reality, and that correct understanding requires the integration of multiple, seemingly contradictory standpoints.

## Origins and Philosophical Basis

The doctrine emerges from two foundational Jain commitments: the claim of full omniscience (*kevalajñāna*) for the [[mahavira|fordmakers]], and the analysis of reality as simultaneously involving permanence and change. Because only an omniscient being can simultaneously apprehend all possible points of view, any judgement made by an ordinary *jīva* "while in the state of non-omniscience, while perfectly adequate for the purposes of ordinary, transactional activity, is inevitably flawed" (Dundas, ch. 8). The necessary consequence is that a multifaceted approach synthesising contradictory viewpoints, rather than dogmatic insistence on a single perspective, is the only means of gaining some understanding of reality's complexity.

**Textual germs.** Dundas traces nascent forms of the idea to the Śvetāmbara scriptures: the *Exposition of Explanations* (*Bhagavatī Sūtra* 2.1) depicts [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]] converting the brahman Skandaka Kātyāyana by showing that entities (world, soul, deliverance, liberated soul) are finite or infinite depending on the perspective from which they are approached. Among the early Digambaras, [[kundakunda]] employed a two-levels-of-truth model using the term *naya* that would stand at the centre of developed Jain relativism.

**Umasvati and Mallavadin.** The catalyst for philosophical relativism was Umasvāti's condensation, in the *Tattvārthasūtra*, of scattered scriptural remarks on substance and its modifications into "the definitive expression of a distinctively Jain model of reality as simultaneously involving the two apparent contradictories of permanence and change." The Śvetāmbara Mallavādin (fifth–sixth century CE) was apparently "the first to have rigorously formulated what became a standard system of seven *nayas*, standpoints through which judgements could be made about this complex reality, applying them with merciless force to intellectual systems which were opposed to the Jains" (Dundas, ch. 8).

## The Seven Nayas (Standpoints)

The *nayas* are not independent valid judgements but partial, incomplete perspectives that must be synthesised:

1. **First three nayas**: relate to how entities must be interpreted as conjoined with both general and specific properties, or with general properties only, or specific properties only.
2. **Fourth naya**: entities as necessarily rooted in the present when judgements are made.
3. **Fifth and sixth nayas**: verbal statements must be differentiated by tense, grammatical inflection, and etymology as means of gaining information about meaning or nuance.
4. **Seventh naya**: delimits individual words to one particular meaning — "a word only has its proper meaning when something is carrying out the function designated by it" (*TSRV* p. 99).

A judgement is valid "only if it encompasses all these perspectives and is 'manypointed' (*anekānta*)" (Dundas, ch. 8).

## Anekantavada as Philosophical Polemic

In Jain hands, the method "became a fearsome weapon of philosophical polemic with which the doctrines of [[hinduism]] and [[buddhism]] could be pared down to their ideological basics of simple permanence and impermanence respectively and thus be shown to be one-pointed and inadequate" (Dundas, ch. 8). [[hinduism|Hinduism]] was characterised as insisting on the permanence of entities; [[buddhism]] as insisting on impermanence. Both were thus *ekānta* (one-pointed) and epistemically deficient.

The doctrine was defended as immune from criticism because it did not present itself as a philosophical or dogmatic view in the usual sense. Akalanka defended its coherence by reference to "the method's insistence upon adherence to linguistic precision and its ability to convey the true, multifaceted nature of things on the basis of direct sensory perception alone" (*TSRV* p. 36).

## Complement: Syadvada

The provisional quality of all ordinary judgements gave rise to a complementary doctrine, [[syadvada]] ("the doctrine of *syāt*/*may be*"), in which statements are placed in the conditional mode to signal epistemic qualification. See the [[syadvada]] page for the full saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold predication) scheme.

## Modern Reinterpretation and Critique

In modern times, anekāntavāda has been widely reinterpreted as an extension of [[ahimsa|non-violence]] to the intellectual domain — "intellectual *ahimsā*" — and as a basis for universal religious tolerance. Dundas is critical of this move: "the validity of what has come to be called 'intellectual *ahimsā*' is problematic, based as it is on a misreading, admittedly well-meant, of the doctrine of manypointedness as intended to promote a benign attitude to other positions and, often, a universal religious tolerance" (ch. 8). Religious tolerance, he notes, "is effectively a political concept born in the European Enlightenment" that "does not transpose itself particularly comfortably into the traditional Indian context."

The great Digambara scholar **Todarmal** (eighteenth century) exemplifies the traditional stance: he "insisted on the impossibility of anybody from another religious tradition achieving the correct attitude (*samyaktva*) necessary for the ultimate achievement of spiritual deliverance owing to an inevitable inability to accept the authority of monks or the veracity of the Jain analysis of the workings of the universe" (Dundas, ch. 8). Jainism's consistent historical self-understanding is that "by the rigour of its analysis of reality in terms of the unique doctrine of manypointedness and the seriousness of the obligations required from its followers it is at the most profound level different from and superior to other paths."

Dundas also notes a potential philosophical limitation: the doctrine "functions [at] a level of abstraction [that] takes little or no account of the various motivations which lead an individual in everyday life to espouse one particular viewpoint as opposed to another. As a consequence, manysidedness could ultimately be regarded as operating in a moral vacuum."
