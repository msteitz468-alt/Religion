# Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity (*Parepidēmoi* and *Paroikoi*)

The governing self-understanding of the [[1-peter|First Letter of Peter]]: Christians are **"visiting
foreigners and resident aliens"** in the world — people whose new allegiance to God in Christ has so
loosened their ties to the surrounding society that they live as exiles in their own land. The motif
**frames the whole letter** (the "elect, foreigners of the dispersion," 1:1; "Babylon," 5:13) and
becomes its central appeal in 2:11. On [[karen-jobes|Jobes]]’s account (BECNT 2005), 1 Peter is the
only NT book to use foreignness as the structuring metaphor for the Christian’s relation to society
(Eph 2:19 uses related language differently).

## The Two Greek Terms

- ***parepidēmos*** — a "visiting foreigner," a sojourner passing through (1:1; 2:11). Jobes: in the
  first century a non-citizen resident with limited rights, not expected to hold host values, often
  viewed as potentially subversive. It is the Greek equivalent of the Latin *peregrinus* (free
  non-citizen) — and, in later Christian usage, the root of "pilgrim."
- ***paroikos*** — a "resident alien," one who dwells alongside a community without belonging to it
  (2:11; the cognate *paroikia*, "sojourning," 1:17; cf. 3 Macc 7:19 on Alexandrian Jews’ forced
  sojourn, noted by Jobes for verbal resemblance to 1:17).

Both terms describe ancient **Israel** in the LXX — Abraham as *paroikos*/*parepidēmos* (Gen. 23:4
LXX, the dual phrase of 2:11), the psalmist as a *parepidēmos* (Ps. 38:13 LXX [39:12]) — so Peter's
application of them to his readers conceptually **joins the church to God's ancient people**
scattered in exile. Alongside them, 2:9–10 heaps up further Exodus/Israel titles for the church: "a
chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's possession" (echoing Exod. 19:6;
Isa. 43; Hos. 1–2).

**Jobes’s stress on horizontal foreignness:** rather than reading *parepidēmos* primarily as
transitory earthly pilgrimage toward a heavenly home, she takes it first as defining the
**relationship between the Christian and unbelieving society** (with Goppelt: foreignness established
by election; nonconformity to handed-down lifestyles). The “heavenly home” reading is a natural
extension many commentators take, but not her primary accent.

## Literal, Metaphorical — or Both? (Jobes)

A long-standing interpretive question (see [[1-peter-authorship|the authorship/colonization
controversy]]):
- **Metaphorical only** (the modern consensus): the terms are spiritual imagery for the Christian's
  pilgrimage through earthly life toward a heavenly home; no concrete social referent is sought.
- **Literal only** (J. H. Elliott): *paroikoi* is a socioeconomic descriptor — the recipients were the
  disenfranchised resident-alien rural poor. Jobes reports Elliott’s argument as unconvincing to most
  (adornment instructions at 3:3 would be cruel if subsistence peasants; *parepidēmoi* hard to apply
  as one class across a vast multi-church area; rural *paroikoi* may not read Greek), while crediting
  him for forcing the social-historical question.
- **Both** ([[karen-jobes|Jobes]]): the figurative sense is “apt,” but the letter “must nevertheless
  have had some particular social and historical setting”; metaphorical need not exclude literal
  historical sense. She offers an **alternative sociopolitical background** from which the metaphor
  derived its power — not “thin air.”

### Jobes’s colonization / displacement reconstruction

Christians “were converted elsewhere, probably Rome, and then displaced to Asia Minor.” Peter uses
that disorientation to teach that **all** Christians are foreigners wherever Christian values conflict
with the dominant society. Pillars she cites:

1. Claudius colonized **all five** regions named in 1:1 (and perhaps uniquely so among emperors).
2. Colonies were often populated in part by deportations / noncitizen settlers (*peregrini* ↔
   *parepidēmoi*).
3. Roman writers link Claudius’s expulsion of Jews to disturbances under “Chrestus” (Suetonius;
   Acts 18:2) — possibly including Christians still classed as Jews.
4. Peter is the stated author; ancient tradition of Peter in Rome under Claudius remains arguable
   (Wenham, Thiede, Botermann) even if composition is in the 60s.
5. Once the letter circulated beyond original readers, the first (literal) sense receded; pure
   spiritual “foreigners of the Diaspora” became primary.

At 2:11 Jobes notes *ὡς* can be causal or comparative: original causal (“because you are…”) with
figurative overtones is possible; after circulation, comparative predominates. Either way, foreignness
is a **consequence of faith in Christ** — especially poignant if readers were deported over Chrestus
troubles. See [[1-peter-authorship|the colonization theory]] and
[[jobes-1-peter-becnt|Jobes BECNT source page]].

**Double purpose of “Diaspora” (Jobes on 1:1):** (1) possibly allude to actual displacement; (2)
interpret experience via Israel as God’s chosen → identify readers as God’s people. She rejects
treating Christian sufferings as OT-style exile-as-judgment for national sin: with Danker, the new
community suffers **in spite of** obedience, not because of disobedience; negative judgment
connotations of Diaspora are “swallowed up in the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ” (new birth,
1:3).

## Diaspora // Babylon Inclusio

Jobes: “Babylon” (5:13) is motivated by the **Diaspora framing** of 1:1 and functions as the **closing
inclusio** of that motif — pairing author and readers in shared exile status (*syneklektē*, “chosen
with you,” echoes *eklektois* of the opening). Modern interpreters are virtually unanimous that the
**referent** of Babylon is Rome; there is less agreement about significance.

- Not (for Jobes) a post-70 **apocalyptic code** for Rome-as-great-evil: 1 Peter is “not apocalyptic,”
  “portrays Rome as neither a great threat nor a great evil,” and urges submission to governors (2:13)
  and honor to the emperor (2:17). “Nothing is said of Babylon’s evil”; Michaels (via Jobes): “the only
  thing wrong with Babylon is that it is not home.”
- Davids (via Jobes): Rome-as-Babylon = capital of the place of exile away from the true heavenly
  inheritance.
- Kelly’s caution (recorded by Jobes): if the pair is only inclusio with Diaspora exile language, it
  may not designate a specific locality at all (cf. Ps 137 “waters of Babylon”); Rome as composition
  place then “leans much more heavily on tradition than on exegesis.”
- Genre: often called a **Diaspora letter** (Epistle of Jeremiah; 2 Macc 1; 2 Bar. 78–87) — recognized
  spiritual authority urging holy life in an uncongenial society; greeting form (*charis* + *eirēnē*
  *plēthyntheiē*) parallels Diaspora epistolary style (Gamaliel letters in b. Sanh. 11b).

## Ps 33 LXX (*paroikia*) and the Exile Matrix

Jobes’s LXX-first method treats **Psalm 33 LXX** (34 Eng.) as a saturating scriptural foundation for
1 Peter’s ethics and exile self-understanding — “much as Isa. 53 forms the basis of his Christology”
(she cross-refers her 2002 work on Ps 34 / 1 Peter).

- **LXX recontextualization:** Hebrew Ps 34:5 “from all my fears/terrors” is rendered in the Greek
  psalm with *paroikiōn* / *tēn paroikian* (“sojournings”) — **same lexical family as 1 Pet 1:17
  *paroikia* and *paroikous* (2:11)**. Jobes: the LXX translator contextualized the psalm for Greek
  Jews in the Diaspora; that re-pointing suits 1 Peter’s Diaspora frame (1:1; 5:13).
- **Echoes across the letter:** deliverance from hardships of sojourning exile (Ps 33:5 LXX) links
  the “time of your sojourning” (1:17); redemption of the Lord’s slaves and hope (Ps 33:23 LXX //
  1:13, 21); “taste that the Lord is good” (Ps 33:9 LXX at 2:3); full quotation grounding nonretaliation
  ethics (3:10–12). Redemption imagery at 1:18–19 conflates Isa 52:3 with this psalm matrix.
- **Application logic (not proof-text):** just as God delivered David from sojourn among the
  Philistines, God will deliver Asian Christians from faith-caused afflictions, because they are no
  less God’s covenant people than David. Peter grounds points in the original sense of the Hebrew
  *as contextualized in the LXX for Diaspora*, then applies it to contemporary readers.

## Theological Content

- **Election in exile.** Foreignness and chosenness are concomitant: *eklektois* and *parepidēmois* as
  two substantives in apposition (Jobes) — vertical (God) + horizontal (society). To be chosen by God
  and committed to Christ is *by definition* to become an alien in a world hostile to the gospel
  (1:1; 2:9–11). Election **necessarily** alienates from unbelieving society’s priorities → various
  trials (1:6).
- **New birth → new citizenship.** New birth into living hope (1:3) bestows an imperishable
  inheritance kept in heaven (1:4). Jobes: if readers were displaced from Rome, land-tied inheritance
  would be uncertain; even without physical displacement, conversion could jeopardize family
  inheritance. Salvation’s full *sōtēria* is deliverance “from this current state of existence as
  foreigners in a world hostile to God” into undissonant existence (1:5).
- **Christ the archetypal outsider.** Via Jobes, Miroslav Volf: the root of Christian alien-identity
  lies not so much in Abraham and Israel as in “the destiny of Jesus Christ, his mission and his
  rejection which ultimately brought him to the cross.” Believers follow in his footsteps (2:21); after
  identifying Jesus as individual Servant (Isa 53), Peter can identify Christians with corporate
  Servant language (Isa 41).
- **Engagement, not withdrawal.** Exile identity does *not* counsel sectarian retreat. Resident aliens
  “appreciate, respect, and value their host land but nevertheless maintain their own distinct
  identity within it” (Jobes on 2:11); live good lives among the Gentiles (2:12), seek the welfare of
  the city (cf. Jer 29:7 for “do good”), honor the emperor (2:17), answer gently (3:15) —
  **differentiated** engagement (see [[1-peter-christ-and-culture|Christ and Culture in 1 Peter]]).
  Christian community as “colony in a strange land, an island of one culture in the midst of another.”

## Background and Reception

- **OT/Jewish Diaspora**: scattering by Assyria and Babylon; Diaspora-letter genre (Jer. 29; Epistle
  of Jeremiah; 2 Macc. 1) — recognized authority writing to a scattered people to sustain holy life.
- **Babylon as exile-frame**: inclusio with Diaspora (above); Rome as capital of exile, not beast.
- **Later Christian "pilgrim" theology**: semantic drift of *peregrinus* → "pilgrim" (Jobes suggests
  *parepidēmos* may have contributed) carried the motif into the church’s self-image as a people
  journeying toward a heavenly homeland (cf. Hebrews 11:13–16; Augustine’s two cities). After the
  letter left its original setting, the figurative sense predominated.
