# Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity (*Parepidēmoi* and *Paroikoi*)

The governing self-understanding of the [[1-peter|First Letter of Peter]]: Christians are **"visiting
foreigners and resident aliens"** in the world — people whose new allegiance to God in Christ has so
loosened their ties to the surrounding society that they live as exiles in their own land. The motif
**frames the whole letter** (the "elect, foreigners of the dispersion," 1:1; "Babylon," 5:13) and
becomes its central appeal in 2:11. 1 Peter is the only NT book to use foreignness as the structuring
metaphor for the Christian's relation to society.

## The Two Greek Terms

- ***parepidēmos*** — a "visiting foreigner," a sojourner passing through (1:1; 2:11). It is the Greek
  equivalent of the Latin *peregrinus*, the legal term for a free non-citizen — and, in later
  Christian usage, the root of "pilgrim."
- ***paroikos*** — a "resident alien," one who dwells alongside a community without belonging to it
  (2:11; the cognate *paroikia*, "sojourning," 1:17).

Both terms describe ancient **Israel** in the LXX — Abraham as a *paroikos* (Gen. 23:4), the psalmist
as a *parepidēmos* (Ps. 38:13 LXX [39:12]) — so Peter's application of them to his readers
conceptually **joins the church to God's ancient people** scattered in exile. Alongside them, 2:9–10
heaps up further Exodus/Israel titles for the church: "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy
nation, a people for God's possession" (echoing Exod. 19:6; Isa. 43; Hos. 1–2).

## Literal, Metaphorical — or Both?

A long-standing interpretive question (see [[1-peter-authorship|the authorship/colonization
controversy]]):
- **Metaphorical only** (the modern consensus): the terms are spiritual imagery for the Christian's
  pilgrimage through earthly life toward a heavenly home; no concrete social referent is sought.
- **Literal only** (J. H. Elliott): *paroikoi* is a socioeconomic descriptor — the recipients were the
  disenfranchised resident-alien rural poor.
- **Both** ([[karen-jobes|Jobes]]): the terms carried a *real historical referent* — Christians
  displaced from Rome to Asia Minor by colonization/expulsion under Claudius, literally "foreigners
  and resident aliens" in their new home — *and* Peter transmutes that situation into the spiritual
  truth that **all** Christians are resident aliens wherever society's values clash with the gospel.
  On this reading the power of the metaphor derives from a lived experience, not "thin air." See
  [[1-peter-authorship|the colonization theory]].

## Theological Content

- **Election in exile.** Foreignness and chosenness are concomitant in 1 Peter: to be chosen by God
  and committed to Christ is *by definition* to become an alien in a world hostile to the gospel
  (1:1; 2:9–11). Disenfranchisement from society's entitlements is the flip side of citizenship in
  God's kingdom.
- **New birth → new citizenship.** The new birth into a living hope (1:3) bestows an imperishable
  inheritance "kept in heaven" (1:4) that relativizes every earthly status; "all the glory of Rome
  is but grass" (cf. 1:24).
- **Christ the archetypal outsider.** Miroslav Volf: the root of Christian alien-identity lies not so
  much in Abraham and Israel as in "the destiny of Jesus Christ, his mission and his rejection which
  ultimately brought him to the cross." The rejected, vindicated Christ is the pattern; believers
  follow in his footsteps (2:21).
- **Engagement, not withdrawal.** Crucially, the exile identity does *not* counsel sectarian retreat.
  Resident aliens are to live visibly good lives among the Gentiles (2:12), honor the emperor
  (2:17), and be ready with a gentle answer (3:15) — a *differentiated* engagement with culture (see
  [[1-peter-christ-and-culture|Christ and Culture in 1 Peter]]).

## Background and Reception

- **OT/Jewish Diaspora**: the scattering of Israel by Assyria and Babylon; the "Diaspora letter" genre
  (Jeremiah's letter to the exiles, Jer. 29; the Epistle of Jeremiah; 2 Macc. 1) — a recognized
  authority writing to a scattered people to sustain a holy life. 1 Peter adopts this form.
- **Babylon as exile-frame**: "Babylon" (5:13) closes the *inclusio* opened by "dispersion" (1:1) —
  as Babylon once marginalized Israel's faith, Rome now marginalizes the Christian faith.
- **Later Christian "pilgrim" theology**: the semantic drift of *peregrinus* → "pilgrim" carried the
  motif into the church's enduring self-image as a people journeying toward a heavenly homeland (cf.
  Hebrews 11:13–16, "strangers and exiles on the earth"; Augustine's two cities).
