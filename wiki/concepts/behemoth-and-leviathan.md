# Behemoth and Leviathan

**Behemoth** (Job 40:15–24) and **Leviathan** (Job 40:25–41:26 [Eng. 41:1–34]) are the two
creatures Yahweh describes at length in the second of the divine speeches that close the
[[book-of-job|book of Job]]. Their identity — earthly animals, cosmic monsters, or both —
is a standing interpretive crux.

This page rests on a single ingested source,
[[hartley-job-nicot|Hartley, *The Book of Job* (NICOT)]], and its substantive claims are
attributed to [[john-hartley|Hartley]] throughout. Where he reports the views of others,
those views are attributed to them.

## Hartley's Position — A Deliberate Both/And

Hartley frames the dispute as a spectrum: "The identification of the Behemoth and Leviathan
is disputed, ranging from earthly creatures to mythical monsters. As earthly beasts they are
identified as the hippopotamus and the crocodile respectively" (pp. 521). He then declines to
choose:

> "Realistic, detailed descriptions keep the portrait from becoming purely mythical.
> Nevertheless, into the factual description the author skillfully blends fanciful metaphors
> drawn from mythic accounts of monsters in order that these beasts may represent **both
> mighty terrestrial creatures and cosmic forces**. In this way Yahweh addresses the cosmic
> dimensions of Job's affliction." (pp. 521–522)

**Behemoth.** Hartley's default identification is the hippopotamus, which "is known to
inhabit Egypt and Africa… but in ancient times it lived in lower Egypt." He floats an
alternative of his own: "Possibly Behemoth is a buffalo, for in the Palestine of the 2nd
millennium B.C. buffaloes roamed about the Lake Huleh region, and there is some evidence that
they might have grazed in the Jordan Valley" (p. 524). He reads Behemoth as "the first of
God's ways (cf. Prov. 8:22), i.e., the crown of the animal creation," and as "king of the
wild animals" (pp. 525–526), while insisting on a parallel with the protagonist: "Behemoth,
like Job, is God's creation, and both are servants of God" (p. 525). He notes the Egyptian
background in which "only the divine pharaohs or the ruling god [Horus] could hunt this
powerful animal. A royal hunt was a religious ritual, considered to be a battle against evil"
(p. 524).

**Leviathan.** Hartley identifies it as "often identified as the crocodile," noting evidence
of crocodiles in Israel along the Yarkon and Kishon (p. 530) — while conceding that some
details strain the identification ("this would not be literally true if this creature is a
crocodile," p. 532). He reports the rival reading in full: "Another view interprets Leviathan
as a serpentine sea monster, the embodiment of the chaotic forces of evil. In the OT Yahweh
defeated the sea monster in the deliverance of his people from Egypt (cf. Ps. 74:14), and he
will defeat Leviathan again in the final apocalyptic battle (Isa. 27:1)" (p. 530) — the
eschatological Leviathan of **Isaiah 27:1** thus enters as Hartley's cross-reference, not as
his own thesis. His synthesis mirrors the Behemoth case: "The poetic description reveals that
the author skillfully weaves into the portrait of an earthly serpentine animal the features
of a mythical dragon. The implication of Yahweh's questioning is that Job could not master
this earthly creature, let alone its cosmic counterpart" (p. 530). He argues from Gen. 9:2
that Leviathan is **primordial**: "the fact that Leviathan is without fear indicates that it
is a primordial creature… Leviathan is the king over all that are proud" (p. 533).

## The Anti-*Chaoskampf* Line

Hartley's most distinctive claim is a denial that Yahweh's mastery is a combat victory. It is
his position, not a settled result, and belongs in tension with stronger combat-myth readings
(see [[the-combat-myth|The Combat Myth]]).

On the sea (38:8–11), after naming *Enuma Elish* and the Baal Cycle as background:

> "In contrast to mythical thought, the sea here is an infant in complete submission to
> Yahweh… Since he brought it into existence, **he never had to subdue it in mortal combat as
> the ruling gods Marduk or Baal** in ancient Near Eastern mythology had to do. It has always
> been subservient to him." (p. 496)

On Behemoth (40:18–19), the same move: "Because Yahweh is its Maker, its power and greatness
do not exist in opposition to him. In contrast to mythical thought Yahweh did not have to
defeat Behemoth to gain control over the forces of chaos. Rather Behemoth obeyed him from the
first moment of its origin" (p. 525).

## Function in the Argument — Stand-In for the Satan

Hartley reads the two beasts as the Yahweh speeches' functional answer to the prologue's
challenge, which the speeches never mention directly. "So Yahweh challenges Job to
demonstrate his prowess by defeating in mortal combat the ominous creatures Behemoth and
Leviathan. If he cannot master these symbols of cosmic powers, he will have to abandon his
complaint" (p. 518). And in the Aim: Yahweh "challenges him to show his mastery over the
great primordial monsters, Behemoth and Leviathan, which are symbolic of cosmic forces that
at times are hostile to Yahweh's rule… By questioning Job about the primordial monsters
Behemoth and Leviathan, Yahweh is trying to persuade Job that he is Master of all powers in
the world, both earthly and cosmic. **Certainly then he is Lord of all forces, earthly and
cosmic, that brought on Job's affliction**" (p. 534) — the affliction being, in the prologue,
the work of [[satan|the Satan]].

Hartley makes the same point in the Introduction, where the pair are called "symbols of
hostile cosmic forces," and where questioning about them "brings Job to realize that no human
being has a proper perspective to judge the course of matters in the universe" (pp. 49–50).
He also notes there that Yahweh "asserts that no corner of the world is outside his
authority (38:16–24), thereby refuting any theory that injustice and suffering exist because
God is in a struggle with a strong foe" — consistent with his anti-*Chaoskampf* reading.

Hartley reads the sheer length of the Leviathan portrait pastorally: "Yahweh drags out his
description of Leviathan waiting for Job's stubborn resolve to weaken… In his mercy Yahweh
does not leave off speaking to Job until he humbles himself" (p. 518).

## Comparanda Hartley Names

Within Job, Hartley assembles a chaos-name inventory: "In other passages the author has
alluded to God's defeat of and control over cosmic forces under various names: Leviathan
(3:8), Tannin (7:12), and Rahab (9:13; 26:12)" (p. 522). On 9:13 he writes that Rahab,
Leviathan, and Tannin "were thought to inhabit the depths of the sea. By reason of their role
in the myths of Israel's neighbors, in the OT these creatures symbolize the forces of chaos
in opposition to God. It is always affirmed that God has defeated them" (p. 173).

Outside Israel he names Ugaritic **[Lotan]** — "In Ugaritic literature this same creature,
known as Lotan, is a sea monster hostile to Baal. In fierce combat Baal, the ruling deity,
defeated this creature, described as a twisting serpent or a seven-headed dragon" (p. 530) —
together with [[marduk|Marduk]], Baal, [Horus], *[Enuma Elish]*, and the Baal Cycle.

**Not in Hartley's list.** Neither Yamm nor [[tiamat|Tiamat]] is named anywhere in the
extracted range of the commentary. The wiki's [[tiamat|Tiamat]] page is linked here as a
related comparandum drawn from **outside** Hartley's text, not as part of his inventory. The
"Rahab" of Job 26:12, whom Hartley calls "the embodiment of all evil forces," is a
chaos-monster and is **not** the Rahab of Joshua 2.

## Compositional Status

**No argument that the Behemoth and Leviathan passages are secondary appears in the
commentary section of the extracted range.** Hartley there treats them as integral — they are
what makes Yahweh's second speech "more than a continuation of the first" (p. 522). In the
**Introduction**, however, he *reports* Fohrer's reconstruction, on which Behemoth
(40:15–24) and Leviathan (40:25–41:26) are **secondary additions**, and rejects it, arguing
for two integral speeches on grounds of the book's twos/threes/fours architecture, stylistic
match with the ostrich and horse portraits, and the distinct function of each speech
(pp. 30–33).

Because the commentary's footnote apparatus was not within the ingested scope, this page does
not claim that Hartley reports no secondary-insertion argument anywhere in the volume — only
that none appears in the ranges read.
