# Integral Unity

A metaphysical framework — common to dharmic traditions in varying formulations — in which **reality is fundamentally One, and multiplicity is an expression of that unity rather than a collection of separate entities that must be assembled into unity**. Contrasted by [[rajiv-malhotra]] in [[being-different-malhotra]] with "synthetic unity," which he identifies as the structural assumption of Western/Abrahamic metaphysics.

## The Core Distinction

| Integral Unity | Synthetic Unity |
|---|---|
| Starting point: the Whole (*purna*) | Starting point: separate parts |
| Multiplicity emerges from unity | Unity assembled from parts |
| Unity is prior and non-derivable | Unity is constructed and derivative |
| Inherently stable | Inherently unstable (assembled things can disassemble) |
| West's anxiety over chaos is unintelligible | Chaos (disassembly) is an existential threat |
| Example: Brahman → world; Indra's Net | Example: atoms → molecules → life; social contract |

The *Purna* verse from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (5.1.1) is the paradigmatic statement: "That is *purna*. This is *purna*. *Purna* comes from *purna*. Take *purna* from *purna*, still *purna* remains." Unity is not diminished by emanation; the whole remains whole.

## Indra's Net

The Vedic image of Indra's Net (*indrajāla*), later elaborated in the Avatamsaka Sutra and the Huayan school of Chinese Buddhism (*Kegon* in Japan): an infinite net at whose every node hangs a jewel, each jewel reflecting all others in infinite regression. Each part contains the whole; each part is interdefined by all others. This is the model of integral unity as mutual interpenetration without loss of distinctiveness.

## Hindu Formulations

**Advaita Vedanta (Shankara)**: Brahman alone is ultimately real; the apparent world (*vyavaharika* — conventional reality) arises through *maya* (the power of superimposition, *adhyasa*). All three types of difference (between similar things, between different things, within a single thing) are ultimately unreal at the *paramarthika* (absolute) level. Liberation is the recognition that Atman = Brahman.

**Vishishta-Advaita (Ramanuja)**: Brahman is real with real attributes; the world and souls are real but constitute Brahman's "body" (*shareera*) — non-separate from Brahman as one's body is non-separate from the self. *Sajatiya* and *vijatiya* difference are denied; *svagata* (internal) difference is admitted — Brahman is genuinely complex. The Whole is truly the Whole but has real internal structure.

**Achintya-bhedabheda (Sri Jiva Goswami)**: "Inconceivable difference-in-non-difference" — the relationship between Brahman (as Bhagavan / personal God) and the world is simultaneously one of identity and difference, and this is *achintya* (beyond logical comprehension). Unity and distinctiveness coexist without contradiction.

**Integral evolution (Sri Aurobindo)**: Spirit "involves" into matter (the reverse of biological evolution); matter, life, mind, and supermind are successive levels of consciousness, each carrying the previous within it. Liberation is the re-emergence (*evolution*) of the original integral unity through and within matter — not escape from matter.

## Buddhist Formulation: Shunyata and Pratitya-Samutpada

Buddhist integral unity operates through the complementary principles of emptiness (*shunyata*) and dependent co-arising (*pratitya-samutpada*): phenomena have no inherent existence (*sva-bhava*) of their own; each arises in dependence on all others; therefore no phenomenon is a truly independent, self-subsisting entity. The Two Truths doctrine: conventional reality involves the appearance of distinct entities; ultimate reality reveals their mutual dependence and emptiness of inherent existence.

Nagarjuna's Madhyamika develops this through *catuskoti* (four-way logic) — see [[sunyata]] and [[two-truths]] for detailed treatment.

## Jain Formulation: Aneka-anta-vada

Jainism approaches integral unity through epistemological pluralism: because reality is too complex to be captured by any single perspective, all judgments must be made conditionally (*syad-vada*) and from multiple viewpoints (*aneka-anta-vada*). The complete truth is accessible only to a liberated soul that can entertain all possibilities simultaneously — i.e., one who has achieved integral vision. The seven-point logic (*sapta-bhangi*) systematically maps the space of possible views on any question.

## Bandhu: The Correspondence Principle

The *bandhu* ("connection/bond/correspondence") principle operationalizes integral unity in Vedic cosmology: the macrocosm and microcosm mirror each other; human physiology corresponds to cosmic structure; the year's 360 days correspond to the 360 bones of the infant; the breath corresponds to cosmic rhythms. Bandhu is not a metaphor but an ontological claim: the connections are real because all phenomena emerge from and remain connected to the One.

## Contrast with Synthetic Unity

The "synthetic unity" Malhotra diagnoses in Western metaphysics traces (in his account) to Aristotelian logic (the Law of the Excluded Middle — reality is divided into mutually exclusive categories); Ockhamist nominalism (radical separation of God and world; all beings are radically individuated); and Cartesian dualism (mind/body as separate substances that must be "bridged"). The history of Western philosophy and theology can be read as a series of attempts to construct unity from these separate parts — Hegel's dialectical synthesis, the Templeton Project's "science-religion dialogue," evolutionary cosmology — none of which fully succeeds because they begin from the wrong premise.
