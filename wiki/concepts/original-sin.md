# Original Sin and the Fall

The Christian doctrine that the first human transgression in Eden corrupted human nature itself and
that this corruption — guilt and a disordered will, with death as its penalty — is transmitted to all
of [[adam|Adam]]'s descendants. [[augustine|Augustine]] is the doctrine's decisive Western architect;
[[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Books XIII–XIV give his fullest narrative account (the dogmatic polemic
against Pelagius belongs to other works).

## The Fall (Book XIV)

For Augustine the **first evil was interior and prior to the outward act**:

> "Our first parents fell into open disobedience because already they were secretly corrupted; for the
> evil act had never been done had not an evil will preceded it."

The root of that evil will is **pride** — "the craving for undue exaltation," by which "the soul
abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself." The serpent
and the forbidden fruit were the occasion, not the cause; the sin was grave not because the food
mattered but because it violated **obedience**, "the mother and guardian of all the virtues." Eve was
deceived; Adam, "not deceived," yielded knowingly rather than be parted from his companion. "By craving
to be more, man becomes less."

## Death as Penalty (Book XIII)

Death is **not natural to man but penal** — the just sentence on the first sin. Augustine distinguishes
three deaths: of the **soul** (when God forsakes it), of the **body** (when the soul forsakes it), and
the **second death** (eternal). Had Adam not sinned he would have passed without dying into angelic
felicity; instead the whole race, "in that one man," became a *massa damnata* subject to mortality.

## Concupiscence and the Disordered Will (Book XIV)

The fall's punishment fits its crime: the will that disobeyed God is repaid by a body that disobeys the
will. The clearest sign is **concupiscence** — disordered sexual desire, which "importunes … in spite
of themselves, and sometimes fails them when they desire to feel it." This involuntariness, and the
**shame** that first made the naked pair cover themselves, is the diagnostic mark of the fallen
condition; before the fall the members "were never moved in opposition to a holy will." Generation in
paradise would have proceeded by the calm rule of the will, "not excited by lust."

## "Flesh" Means the Whole Self

Crucially, Augustine reads Paul's "flesh" (*caro*) **not as the body** but as **the whole man living
according to himself**. The "works of the flesh" include idolatry, envy, and heresy — vices of the
soul. Hence "the sinful soul made the flesh corruptible," not the reverse: this overturns the Platonic
and Manichaean blame of the body and locates sin in the will. To live "after the flesh" is to live by
[[two-cities|love of self]] — the founding love of the earthly city.

## Transmission and Sign

The corruption is propagated by ordinary generation to all Adam's seed. In Book XVI Augustine reads
**infant circumcision** (and its successor, infant baptism) as the remedy applied before any personal
fault: the uncircumcised infant is cut off "not personally in their own life, but according to the
common origin of the human race, [having] all broken God's covenant in that one in whom all have
sinned" (Rom. 5:12).

## Pelikan: A Doctrine Generated by Cult Practice

[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 6) argues that the doctrine of original sin was generated less by theological debate than by **the church's life and practice** — specifically two rites "present in the language of the church before they were ever exploited for their anthropological import": the **virgin birth** and **infant baptism**. [[cyprian|Cyprian]] was the first to forge "a correlation between the practice of infant baptism and the doctrine of original sin" (the infant is baptized for "the contagion of the ancient death" contracted "by his first birth"); [[ambrose|Ambrose]] drew the implication of the virgin birth (Christ's sinlessness as consequence of virginal conception). [[augustine|Augustine]] "learned from Ambrose" the first and "from Cyprian" the second. Pelikan stresses the parallel with Nicaea: as *[[homoousion]]* was the only way to justify the worship of Christ, so "the Augustinian theory provided a theological justification for an unchallengeable sacramental practice" (infant baptism) — another case of [[lex-orandi-lex-credendi|practice grounding doctrine]]. The pre-Augustinian tradition had "leaned noticeably… to the side of free will," partly because it had to answer Greco-Roman fatalism and [[gnosticism|Gnostic]] determinism. Celestius's six [[pelagian-controversy|Pelagian]] propositions and the Synod of Carthage (418) marked the doctrine's definitive Western establishment.

## The East/West Divide (Kelly) — Contradiction Protocol

[[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|Kelly]] (ch. XIII) sets the Augustinian doctrine against a markedly different Greek account, recorded here without adjudication:

- **Greek Fathers** ([[gregory-of-nyssa]], [[gregory-of-nazianzus]], [[john-chrysostom]], [[theodore-of-mopsuestia]], [[theodoret-of-cyrus]]): humanity inherits Adam's **mortality, concupiscence, and a bias (*rhopē*) toward sin**, but "there is hardly a hint… that mankind as a whole shares in Adam's *guilt*." Free will remains intact; newborns are sinless. Kelly calls this "the outline of a real theory of original sin," not its full Augustinian form.
- **West**: building on [[ambrose|Ambrose]] and especially **Ambrosiaster**'s reading of Rom. 5:12 as *in quo* ("in whom all sinned" — a Latin mistranslation of *eph' hō*), [[augustine|Augustine]] taught that all humanity is a *massa damnata* sharing Adam's **guilt (*reatus*)**, transmitted by the concupiscence of physical generation.

This divide is the deep background to the [[pelagian-controversy|Pelagian controversy]]; [[theodore-of-mopsuestia|Theodore]] was even branded "an Eastern Pelagius" (a charge Kelly doubts). See also [[traducianism]] (the soul-origin question that governs transmission), [[grace]], [[predestination]].

## Hillman's Revisionist Usage (Contradiction Protocol)

[[hillman-original-sin|Hillman, *Original Sin: Ritual Child Rape & the Church* (2012)]] uses the phrase "original sin" in a radically different sense from Augustine — not as a doctrinal category but as a polemical label for what he argues is the **founding institutional sin of the Church**: the ritual sodomy of young boys during catechetical initiation. Hillman's "original sin" = child rape as the constitutive founding act of early Christianity.

This usage has no connection to the Augustinian tradition's doctrinal definition. Hillman's argument is heterodox, non-peer-reviewed, and vigorously disputed by mainstream patristics scholars — see [[early-christian-sexual-ethics-pagan-critique|Early Christian Sexual Ethics and the Pagan Critique]] for the full controversy, including the mainstream reading of [[cyril-of-jerusalem|Cyril of Jerusalem]]'s catechetical rites that Hillman interprets as describing literal sodomy. (Contradiction Protocol applies: both readings are recorded without adjudication.)

Hillman does engage the Augustinian tradition's linkage of original sin to **concupiscence and sexual shame** — arguing that the anti-sexual theology of Tertullian, Jerome, and Ambrose was itself politically motivated (to suppress female oracular authority and pagan mystery cults), and that Augustine's later doctrinal formulation inherits and systematizes this political program.
