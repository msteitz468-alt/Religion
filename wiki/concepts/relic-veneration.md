# Relic Veneration and the Cult of the Martyrs

The veneration of the bodily remains and shrines of the martyrs, and the miracles attributed to them.
[[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XXII (chs. 8–10) is among the most extensive early patristic witnesses
to the practice, written by [[augustine|Augustine]] from firsthand observation.

## Miracles at the shrines

Against those who say miracles have ceased, Augustine argues they continue — "by His sacraments or by the
prayers or relics of His saints" — only less publicized now that the canon of Scripture is closed. He
catalogues cures he personally attests: the healing of **Innocentius** at Carthage (witnessed by Augustine
and Alypius), the cancer-cure of **Innocentia**, and a long series at the relics of
**[[stephen-protomartyr|Stephen the protomartyr]]**, newly brought to Hippo — nearly seventy recorded "at
the hour at which I write," including raisings of the dead and the celebrated cure of the Cappadocian
siblings **Paulus and Palladia** before his Easter congregation.

## The doctrinal guardrail

Augustine is careful to distinguish the cult of the martyrs from pagan worship: **"our martyrs are not our
gods."** "We build, not temples as if they were gods, but monuments as to dead men whose spirits live with
God." No altar is raised *to* a martyr; the **sacrifice** offered at a martyr's monument is the body of
Christ, "not offered to them, because they themselves are this body" — offered "to the one God of the
martyrs and of ourselves." The miracles, he argues, are not ends in themselves but **witnesses** to "this
faith which preaches Christ risen in the flesh" — the martyrs being literally *witnesses* (martyrs) of the
[[resurrection|resurrection]].

## Reception

This martyr-piety, with its relics and shrines, became central to Catholic and Orthodox devotion; the
distinction Augustine draws (veneration of the saints versus worship due to God alone) anticipates the
later *dulia / latria* distinction and was contested by the Reformers.
