# Sant Tradition

## Definition and Scope

The Sant tradition (or Sant movement) was a devotional movement of northern India that provided the immediate religious background from which [[sikhism|Sikhism]] emerged. W. H. McLeod states explicitly: "The background of Sikhism was the Sant movement of northern India. Guru Nanak was a representative of the Sant movement and he expresses in his works the characteristic doctrines of the Sants" (McLeod ~L316–320).

## Relationship to Sikhism

[[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]] is described by McLeod as "a unique figure in the history of religions, but his uniqueness lies in the clarity and comprehensiveness of his inspired teachings and in the beauty with which they were expressed" (McLeod ~L319–322) — that is, his uniqueness lay in the quality and completeness of his articulation of essentially Sant doctrines, not in departing from them.

McLeod explicitly argues against the common popular view that Nanak's teaching was a **syncretism** of Hindu and Islamic teachings: "Some of the Sants embodied a limited amount of Islam in their hymns, but the burden of their teachings was weighted heavily towards concepts found in Hindu ideals. This is not to deny that Muslim culture exercised an influence on the development of the Sikh community. It does, however, deny that Islam significantly influenced Guru Nanak" (McLeod ~L324–333).

## Characteristic Doctrines

McLeod's slice describes Nanak as expressing the characteristic doctrines of the Sants without enumerating a full doctrinal list in this opening section. The following are identifiable as Nanak's Sant-derived emphases:

- **Nam** (the divine Name) as the path to liberation, freely open to all regardless of caste or religion
- **Nam simaran** (remembrance / meditation on the Name) as the central spiritual discipline
- **Interiority**: dismissal of external ritual (temple ceremony, Brahmanical rites, orthodox Islamic forms) as false and dangerous
- **Anti-caste universalism**: liberation accessible without caste intermediation
- Corporate devotion: **satsang** (congregation of true believers) and **kirtan** (hymn-singing)

## Historical Context

The Sant tradition is situated in the broader context of northern Indian devotional religion prior to and during the 15th–16th centuries. McLeod notes that in the Hindu tradition "there have been many new panths" and that if Sikhism's lineage is traced through ideas rather than personalities, Nanak cannot unambiguously be called the "founder" of a wholly new religion (McLeod ~L308–315).

The Sants' influence is evident in the hymns collected in the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]], which includes compositions by "approved Sant poets" alongside the Sikh Gurus (McLeod ~L2017–2019). [[kabir|Kabir]] is among the Sant poets whose works are included.

## Detailed Doctrine (from McLeod's "Sikh Doctrine" chapter)

*The following section draws on McLeod's extended treatment of Sant doctrine in the "Sikh Doctrine" chapter (~L100–319 of the assigned slice).*

### Sant as Sadhan and Social Protest

McLeod characterizes the Sant tradition as simultaneously "a sadhan, or method of spiritual liberation, and a form of social protest. Both elements are inextricably linked" (~L103–105). Most leading Sants were from lower castes, and their soteriology "plainly rejects the relevance of caste status in matters pertaining to the soul's deliverance" (~L107–108). The condemnation of external religious authority extended equally to "Muslim as well as Hindu" (~L113–115).

### The Nirguna Doctrine

The starting-point of Sant doctrine is "a concept of God which insists upon a wholly formless quality. God is *nirguna*, as opposed to the *saguna* belief which envisages physical incarnations and accepts visible representations in the form of images" (~L269–276). This nirguna emphasis distinguishes the Sants from the Vaishnavas, who worshipped Vishnu in image form, even while both traditions share "uncompromising insistence on devotion as the way of liberation" (~L199–200).

### Interior Discipline and Rejection of External Forms

Sant devotion was "emphatically and exclusively interior" (~L135–136). The Sants rejected: incarnations and idol-worship; sacred scriptures (in their external sense); temples and pilgrimages; and all external forms of piety (~L152–163). "In place of outward piety, one must learn to turn one's attention inward, there to focus it entirely upon the *shabad* or divine Word uttered in the *man* (the 'heart' or 'mind') by the mystical Guru, the 'voice' of the divine Being" (~L167–172).

The ultimate goal was the same as Vaishnava Bhakti: moksha (liberation from transmigration). "The difference concerns the method whereby one breaks or terminates the cycle" (~L128–129).

### Sahaj

The condition of *sahaj* — "a word that leads us back to the Naths and beyond them to the earlier tradition of tantric Buddhism" (~L295–297) — is the end-state of Sant devotion: "permanent stilling of all emotion and all conflict, peace in an eternal equipoise that could be achieved by the devout Sant while still living out his present existence" (~L141–149). The Sants insisted *sahaj* is not attained through Nath hatha-yoga but "through inward devotion and the practice of meditation" (~L308–311).

### Sources of Sant Doctrine: Vaishnava, Nath, and Sufi

McLeod identifies three contributors to Sant doctrine:
1. **Vaishnava Bhakti**: "the system with the greatest resemblance to the Sant tradition" (~L30–31); supplied the emphasis on loving devotion to a personal deity and the goal of moksha
2. **Nath panth**: "a contemporary representative of the ancient tantric tradition" (~L205–207); contributed the interior emphasis, the irrelevance of caste and sacred languages, anti-externalism, and the concept of sahaj
3. **Sufi influence**: "may also have contributed to the development of Sant doctrine, though if this is indeed the case its results are much harder to detect in the terminology of the Sants than are features which derive from Vaishnava and Nath sources" (~L208–215)

The Naths (the "Split-ear" or *Kanphat* yogis, followers of Gorakhnath/Adinath) commanded "considerable influence in the Punjab and north India" in Nanak's time (~L221–231). The songs attributed to [[kabir|Kabir]] "are shot through with Nath concepts and terminology" (~L245–247), and "Nath influences are plainly evident in the works of Guru Nanak" (~L261–262). However, "the Sants were generally strong critics of the Naths" and went beyond mere borrowing (~L303–308).

### Kabir and the Sants

[[kabir|Kabir]] (c. 1440–1518) — "by caste a Julaha or weaver" (~L180–181) — is identified as "the most famous" of the Sants (if Nanak be excepted) (~L177–178). Kabir's low-caste status is representative: "most of the Sants were of low caste (Ravidas was actually an Outcaste)" (~L183–184). Nanak was exceptional in belonging to the "relatively high caste of Khatris" and being "soundly educated" (~L190–191).

### Nanak's Relationship to the Sant Tradition

McLeod frames the central question of Sikh origins: "Must we conclude that Nanak was a Sant?" (~L756–757). His answer is nuanced: if the question is about "a strictly neutral question of antecedents and influences, the answer must be in the affirmative" — Nanak "must be located within the Sant tradition" (~L775–779). But if the question implies a "lack of originality," the answer is "an emphatic negative" (~L780–782):

> "There is in them [Nanak's hymns] an integrated and coherent system no other Sant has produced; there is a clarity no other Sant has equalled; and there is a beauty no other Sant has matched." (~L784–787)

McLeod concludes: "It is Sant thought that we find in his works, but it is Sant thought expanded and interpreted. The result is a new synthesis, a synthesis that is cast within the pattern of Sant belief but which nevertheless possesses a significant originality and, in contrast with its Sant background, a unique clarity" (~L452–461).

The Sikh-specific distinctives of Nanak's version of Sant teaching include: (1) the systematic integration and clarification of doctrine; (2) the appointment of a successor (inaugurating the line of ten Gurus); and (3) the unique permanence of the Panth that emerged — "Nothing in the Sant experience can remotely compare to the Panth which was eventually to emerge from that decision of Nanak" (~L800–803).
