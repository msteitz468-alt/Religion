# Taiji — Supreme Polarity (太極)

**Taiji** (太極, romanized *t'ai chi* in older Wade-Giles) is the central cosmological concept of
the [[neo-confucianism|Daoxue/Neo-Confucian]] tradition, introduced into Confucian metaphysics through
[[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi's]] [[taijitu-shuo|Taijitu shuo]] ("Discussion of the Supreme Polarity
Diagram") and systematized by [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] as the name for the ultimate metaphysical principle
(*li*, 理) that is the source and ground of all things.

## Translation: "Supreme Polarity" vs. "Supreme Ultimate"

The standard English translation "Supreme Ultimate" is rejected by Joseph Alan Adler
(**[[adler-reconstructing-confucian-dao|2014]]**) as misleading. The key is the meaning of *ji* (極):

> *Ji* is the ridgepole of a roof — the highest point AND the pivot-junction dividing the roof into
> two complementary halves.

Thus *taiji* names the **principle of yin-yang bipolarity itself** — not a supreme monad *above*
the polarities, but the *principle* by which poles are constituted as poles. "Supreme Polarity"
captures this: the *taiji* is that which makes yin-yang polarity possible.

"Supreme Ultimate" implies a terminus or apex; "Supreme Polarity" preserves the generative
bipolarity that is the term's cosmological function.

## Wuji er Taiji

The *Taijitu shuo* opens: **"Wuji er taiji!"** — "**Nonpolar and yet Supreme Polarity!**"

- *Wuji* (無極) = "Nonpolar" — undifferentiated, without the polarization of yin and yang.
- The statement is not a contradiction but a paradox expressing that the ultimate is at once
  undifferentiated (formless, no fixed position) and the very principle of differentiation (the
  source of all yin-yang polarity).

Lu Jiuyuan attacked this opening line, arguing *wuji* was a Daoist term from *Laozi* ch. 28 with
no place in Confucian thought. Zhu Xi defended it, arguing it was not an added modifier but an
amplification: "to say that taiji is already supreme and yet say it is also *wu*, means simply that
there is no physical form or place for taiji — it is omnipresent principle."

## Cosmological Sequence (*Taijitu shuo*)

The Taiji Diagram generates all things in a sequence:

1. **Wuji er taiji** — Nonpolar and yet Supreme Polarity
2. **Activity generates yang; at the limit of activity there is stillness; stillness generates yin;
   at the limit of stillness it is also active** — the cycling of yin and yang
3. **The Five Phases** (Water, Fire, Wood, Metal, Earth) emerge from the interaction of yin and yang
4. **The four seasons revolve** from the Five Phases
5. **Heaven-and-earth and the ten thousand things** are generated from this process
6. **Human beings** receive the finest and most spiritually efficacious (*ling*) endowment; from this
   comes moral nature — humanity, rightness, ritual propriety, wisdom, faithfulness

The sequence moves from cosmological (cosmogony) to anthropological (human nature and virtue).

## Zhu Xi's Equation: Taiji = Li

[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi's]] most philosophically important move: **taiji = li (principle/order)**.

> "The Supreme Polarity is simply the *li* of Heaven-and-earth and the ten thousand things."  
> "Each of the ten thousand things has a taiji."

This is not a temporal or spatial claim (*li* and *qi* do not exist apart in time or space) but a
logical priority claim: *li* is the ordered, patterned character of things; *qi* is the
psychophysical stuff that actualizes that pattern. *Taiji* is the name for *li* in its cosmological,
generative totality.

This equation allowed Zhu Xi to link:
- [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi's]] cosmological *taijitu shuo* (activity-stillness → yin-yang → Five Phases)
- Cheng Yi's metaphysical *li* (the ordered, principled character of things)
- The practical-ethical cultivation framework: *li* is always already present in the human mind as
  human nature (*xing*); the task is to actualize it through *jing* (reverent composure) and
  *ge wu* (investigating things/affairs)

## Activity-Stillness Interpenetration

A central feature of the *Taijitu shuo* is the yin-yang **cycling** through activity and stillness:
"In activity it generates yang; at the limit of activity it is still. In stillness it generates yin;
at the limit of stillness it is also active." 

Crucially, Zhu Xi found in [[tongshu|Tongshu]] section 16 the further, deeper claim:

> "That which has no activity in activity, and no stillness in stillness, is **spirit**."

That is, at the level of principle (as opposed to formed things), activity and stillness
**interpenetrate** — there is stillness within activity and activity within stillness. This concept
was central to Zhu Xi's resolution of his spiritual crisis of the 1160s regarding how to cultivate
both the unexpressed (*weifa*) and expressed (*yifa*) phases of mind.

## Daoist Origins and the Taiji Diagram

The **visual diagram** that Zhou Dunyi wrote the *Taijitu shuo* to explain almost certainly had Daoist
origins: Chen Tuan (d. 989) → Chong Fang (965–1015) → Mu Xiu (979–1032) → Zhou Dunyi's circles.
Multiple similar diagrams survive in the Daoist Canon.

The familiar **yin-yang swirl** symbol (*taijitu* in the popular sense) first appears in a 1370s text
by Zhao Huiqian — much later than Zhou Dunyi's diagram.

Zhu Xi denied the Daoist origin to protect Zhou's position in the [[daotong|daotong]] succession.
Modern scholarship largely accepts the Daoist chain.
