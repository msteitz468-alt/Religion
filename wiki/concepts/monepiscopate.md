# Monepiscopate

## Definition

The *monepiscopate* (from Greek *monos* = "sole, single" + *episkopos* = "overseer, bishop") refers to the ecclesiastical structure in which a single bishop (*episkopos*) exercises authoritative leadership over the presbyters and deacons of a local church — or a group of churches in a city. It is distinguished from the earlier pattern of a *plurality of elders/bishops* (where *episkopos* and *presbyteros* were functionally synonymous).

The monepiscopate became the standard form of church government in the Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican traditions. Its historical development — specifically, whether it originated with the apostles or was a second-century development — is disputed.

## First-Century Background

The NT writings show a pattern of *plural* elders (*presbyteroi*) or bishops (*episkopoi*) governing local churches:
- Acts 14:23; 20:17 — Paul appoints plural elders in churches he plants
- Phil 1:1 — Paul greets "overseers and deacons" (plural). [[g-walter-hansen|Hansen]] (PNTC): only joint mention outside the Pastorals; **plurality** of shared leadership; leaders addressed as *together with* the church, not over it — part of the church and especially called to the humility of 2:3–4; greeted partly as those who administered the gift via Epaphroditus and who must help resolve disunity (e.g. Euodia/Syntyche). See [[philippians]], [[hansen-philippians-pntc]].
- Titus 1:5 — "appoint elders in every town"
- Acts 20:28 — the same group is called both *presbyterous* and *episkopous*, implying the terms are synonymous

The NT does show some individuals functioning as prominent leaders within the presbytery (James in Jerusalem; perhaps Timothy and Titus), but there is no evidence of a formally distinct, elevated bishop office separate from the presbytery at this stage.

## Development in the Second Century

### Stage 1: Continued Plurality (Early 2nd c.)

Several early second-century sources confirm plural leadership:
- **Didache** (c.100): "elect for yourselves *bishops* (plural) and deacons" — the role described is equivalent to elders
- **1 Clement** (c.96): represents the Roman church writing to Corinth; speaks of plural bishops/presbyters; even equates the two terms (1 Clem 44.5); refers to the Corinthian leaders as plural "presbyters" — no singular ruling bishop mentioned even in Rome itself
- **Shepherd of Hermas** (c.150): mentions plural "presbyters who lead the church"; uses bishop/elder interchangeably
- **Polycarp's Epistle to the Philippians** (c.110): calls his congregation to submit to "the presbyters" — not to a bishop; Polycarp never uses the term bishop for himself or anyone else

### Stage 2: Transitional Period — Ignatius (c.110-117)

[[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius of Antioch]] is the key transitional figure. Writing seven letters during his martyrdom journey to Rome, Ignatius advocates a three-fold ministry of bishop (singular), presbyters, and deacons:
- "You must not engage in any activity apart from the bishop, but be subject also to the presbytery" (Trall. 2.2-3)
- The bishop's authority is analogous to the church's relationship to Jesus; to reject the bishop is to reject God (Eph. 5.3; Trall 2.1)
- Only the bishop (or his designate) may administer baptism and the Eucharist (Smyrn. 8.2)

Some scholars (Brent) argue Ignatius invented the monepiscopate to deal with heretical challenges. Others (Robinson) argue he assumes it as already normative in the five Asian churches he addresses. Significantly, Ignatius does *not* mention a bishop in Rome — suggesting the monepiscopate was not yet universal even in the early 2nd c.

Importantly, Ignatius does not present the bishop as an entirely separate order *above* the presbytery but as *primus inter pares* — first among equals. He still calls audiences to submit to the presbytery alongside the bishop.

### Stage 3: Maturing Monepiscopate (Late 2nd c.)

By the end of the second century, the bishop had become a distinct and prominent office:
- **Hegesippus** (c.165): compiled a list of successive bishops in Rome back to Anicetus
- **[[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] of Lyons**: developed the concept of *apostolic succession* — a continuous identifiable lineage of bishops from the apostles to his own day; used it to counter gnostic claims of secret apostolic tradition. His list of Roman bishops is probably partly anachronistic (projecting the monepiscopate backwards) but reflects genuine concern for traceable tradition.
- **Apostolic Tradition** (attributed to Hippolytus, early 3rd c.): provides the most detailed early account of bishop-ordination and governance — outside our period but confirms the matured state.

## Jerome's Explanation

Jerome (*Commentary on Titus* 1.7), looking back, offered the most succinct explanation of the transition:
> "The presbyter is the same as the bishop. . . . But as each sought to appropriate those whom he had baptized . . . it was appointed that one of the presbyters, elected by his colleagues, should be set over all the others."

Jerome identified *ecclesial fragmentation and factionalism* — likely arising from the multiplication of house churches and the threat of heresy — as the driving motive. A singular bishop provided a clear symbolic and practical centre of unity. He also noted the transition was not by "divine institution" but by "tradition" (i.e., historical development).

## The Apostolic Succession Question

Irenaeus' use of the monepiscopate raises the question of *apostolic succession*: does a continuous chain of named bishops guarantee the transmission of apostolic teaching?

Kruger (following Lampe) suggests Irenaeus or Hegesippus may have retroactively identified *chairmen of the presbytery* (prominent elders) as "bishops" in the technical later sense, creating an apparent unbroken lineage. The earlier sources (1 Clement, Didache) support a more general principle: the apostolic teaching is preserved in the churches' leadership, not through a specific named lineage. Irenaeus' specific list is therefore "possibly anachronistic" but the underlying principle of preserving apostolic tradition through appointed teachers is ancient.

The Quartodeciman controversy (Easter date dispute between Polycarp/Anicetus and later Polycrates/Victor) illustrates that even near the end of the 2nd c., no single bishop — not even Rome's — had jurisdictional authority over churches in other locales. Victor of Rome's attempted excommunication of Asian churches was sharply rebuked by Irenaeus and others.

## Significance

The monepiscopate's development had lasting consequences:
1. **Unity**: A single bishop per city provided a visible symbol of ecclesial unity across multiple house churches
2. **Anti-heretical function**: The bishop became the guardian of the rule of faith; orthodox teaching was identified with episcopal succession
3. **Liturgical gatekeeping**: Ignatius tied sacramental validity (baptism, Eucharist) to episcopal oversight
4. **Polity trajectories**: Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican churches claim continuity of the episcopate from the apostles; Reformed and congregational traditions dispute both the historical and theological claim
