# Evil as Privation of Good (*privatio boni*)

The doctrine that **evil has no positive nature or substance of its own** but is the *corruption*,
*defect*, or *absence* of a good that ought to be present. Evil is not a thing God made, nor an
independent principle opposed to God, but the wounding or falling-away of something itself good. The
position is the backbone of [[augustine|Augustine]]'s answer to the problem of evil and his polemic
against [[manichaeism|Manichaean]] dualism.

## Augustine's Formulation (City of God XI–XII)

In [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Book XI, Augustine states it directly:

> "For evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name 'evil.'" (*Mali enim
> nulla natura est: sed amissio boni, mali nomen accepit.*)

The corollaries developed across Books XI–XII:

- **No nature is contrary to God.** Since God is supreme Being, "no nature is contrary save that which
  does not exist" — the only opposite of the highest *being* is **non-being**. There is therefore no
  co-eternal evil principle (against the Manichees).
- **All natures, as natures, are good.** "No nature at all is evil, and this is a name for nothing but
  the want of good." Even the least creature, having measure, form, and order, is good insofar as it
  exists. A vice can exist only in a nature that was previously good, and "the wicked will is a strong
  proof of the goodness of the nature."
- **Evil is a corruption that injures only the good it corrupts.** Vice is "an evil not to God, but to
  themselves … solely because it corrupts the good of their nature."

## The Deficient Cause of the Evil Will

Augustine's sharpest extension (Book XII) concerns the *origin* of evil in the will. The evil will has
**no efficient cause — only a "deficient" one**:

> "Let no one … look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient,
> as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect."

To turn from the immutable Good to a lesser, private good (oneself) is a *defection from being*, like
darkness or silence — known "by not knowing it." This is why the fall of the angels and of man requires
no positive cause beyond the will's own deficiency, and why God is not the author of evil though He is
the author of every nature. The defining defect is [[original-sin|pride]] — "the soul abandons Him to
whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself."

## Aquinas's Formulation (Summa Theologiae I, Q48–49)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] systematizes the privative doctrine, drawing primarily on Pseudo-Dionysius
(*Div. Nom.* iv). Evil is "the absence of the good which is natural and **due** to a thing" (Q48, A1)
— a *privation*, not a mere negation (the absence of sight is evil in an animal, but not in a stone).
Evil **is not a being or a nature** (Q48, A1); rather, **good is the subject of evil** (Q48, A3),
since a privation exists only in the good it deprives. Evil "cannot wholly consume good": the
subject-good always remains as that which is corrupted (Q48, A4).

In voluntary things, evil divides into **pain/penalty** (*malum poenae*, privation of form or
integrity) and **fault** (*malum culpae*, privation of due operation/order of the act). **Fault is
the greater evil**, because it makes the agent itself evil, and "God is the author of the evil of
penalty, but not of the evil of fault" (Q48, A5–6; Q49, A2).

On causation (Q49): **good is the only cause of evil — but only *accidentally* (*per accidens*)**.
Evil has no formal or final cause and no direct efficient cause; it arises as a defect in an action
whose agent and end are good (the agent intends a good, but a defect attends the effect). Decisively,
against [[manichaeism|Manichaean]] dualism, **there is no supreme evil or first principle of evil**
(Q49, A3): nothing can be essentially evil, a wholly evil thing would destroy its own subject and
hence itself, and evil's merely accidental causality disqualifies it from being any first principle.
Aquinas traces the dualist error to a failure to consider the universal cause of *being* (see
[[creation-ex-nihilo]]).

## Background and Reception

The privative analysis is shared with **Neoplatonism** (Plotinus, [[plotinus|Plotinus]], where evil is
linked to deficient being and matter as privation), which Augustine adapts within a doctrine of
creation *ex nihilo*: creatures are good because *from* God, yet mutable and corruptible because made
*out of nothing*, not out of God. The doctrine became standard in Western theology and was taken up by
later scholastics (notably Aquinas, *privatio boni*). It is in tension with any dualism that grants
evil an independent substance.

## Pseudo-Dionysius (*On the Divine Names*, Caput IV)

[[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius]] develops the most sustained Greek Christian account of evil as
privation in [[on-divine-names|*On the Divine Names*]] Caput IV (Sections XIX–XXXV), independently
of and parallel to Augustine (or as a source, if Parker's dating is accepted). The argument is
purely philosophical and exhaustive in scope, considering whether evil can be located in God, Angels,
demons, souls, irrational creatures, bodies, matter, or nature in general — and denying each.

Key moves in the Dionysian account, with verbatim anchors:

- **Evil is not an actual thing**: "The Evil qua evil makes no single essence or birth, but only, as
  far as it can, pollutes and destroys the subsistence of things existing" (Section XX).
- **Evil has a parasitical subsistence**: "the Evil altogether, is not, nor is good, nor good
  producing" — yet "coming into being for the sake of the Good, and not of itself" (Section XXXI).
  The parasite metaphor: "disease remains and is, by having the lowest possible order of essence, and
  in this continues to exist as a parasite" (Section XXVII).
- **All things are, in so far as they are, good**: "all things which are, in so far as they are,
  both are good, and from the Good; but, in so far as they are deprived of the Good, are neither
  good, nor do they exist" (Section XXVII).
- **Demons are evil not by nature but by turning aside**: "in them, is a turning aside and a stepping
  out of things befitting themselves, and a missing of aim, and imperfection and impotence, and a
  weakness and departure, and falling away from the power which preserves their integrity" (Section
  XXIII). Their angelic gifts remain intact and luminous; they simply do not use them.
- **The comprehensive enumeration**: "The Evil is privation and failure, and want of strength, and
  want of proportion, and want of attainment, and want of purpose; and without beauty, and without
  life, and without mind, and without reason, and without completeness, and without stability, and
  without cause, and without limit, and without production" (Section XXXII).
- **The singular cause principle**: "the Good is from the one and the whole Cause, but the Evil is
  from many and partial defects" (Section XXX) — there is no first principle of evil.
- **Providence encompasses evil**: "no single thing is without Providence... the Divine Providence
  uses even evils which happen for the benefit, either individual or general" (Section XXXIII).

Aquinas, who cites *Divine Names* IV extensively in the *Summa* treatment of evil (I Q48–49),
is the main channel through which Dionysian privation-language entered scholastic theology. See the
Aquinas section above.

## Perl on Dionysius's Privation Theory (*Theophany* Ch. 4)

Eric D. Perl's *Theophany* (SUNY Press, 2007), Chapter 4 — "The Problem of Evil" — treats DN IV.18–35 and addresses the question of how evil can figure in a system where **all being is theophany** (divine self-manifestation). If every being is God's self-showing, what becomes of evil? Perl's Chapter 4 endnotes (his assigned range) reveal the following moves:

**Evil as uncaused and unintelligible.** Perl endorses the reading that evil is not merely privative but *causally opaque*: it has no intelligible principle behind it. Dionysius's word for the fall is *anoētōs* — not "unintellectually" (which would merely invert the intellectual condition) but "unintelligibly": "the fall itself cannot be understood" [*Theophany*, endnote 21, p. 128; range line 5348–5351]. John Jones, summarizing Perl's Dionysian position: "The question 'Why is there evil at all?' is a mistaken question; for, it seeks an ultimate cause where there is none. However, in denying the legitimacy of this question we do not seek to explain evil away; rather, we indicate that evil is uncaused and unexplainable" [lines 5352–5357].

**How evil can "exist" in a theophanic system.** Evil is the failure of theophany, not a rival principle. The note on divine and creaturely causality (n. 18) frames it: all genuine creaturely activity is itself a manifestation of God's power — "The more man acts, the more God acts" (Trouillard, quoted approvingly) [lines 5338–5344]. Evil is the failure of this self-manifesting activity — activity's deficiency — and so falls outside theophany rather than constituting it. There is no being to be theophany.

**The demon's case.** [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius]] treats fallen angels as demons whose angelic nature remains intact (this is documented in the existing section above on DN IV.XIX–XXXV). Perl stresses that in Dionysius, "demons" are not the neutral *daimones* of Proclus ("wholly good spiritual beings" [line 5319]) but "fallen angels" [lines 5323–5325] — a genuinely Christian transformation. Evil in souls is characterized as "weakness of activity" (following Proclus, *De malorum subsistentia* 49.5–6) [line 5331]; it is a failure to act fully in accordance with one's nature.

**Comparison with Plotinus and Proclus.** Carlos Steel ("Proclus et Denys: De l'existence du mal") judges Dionysius's doctrine "greatly inferior to that of Proclus" [lines 5311–5312]; Perl's chapter evidently argues the opposite. A key Plotinian reference: for Plotinus, even matter is "a sort of ultimate form … not a positive reality other than form and … a product of the Good" (V.8.7.23–24) [lines 5314–5316], so evil's tie to matter is not a tie to some counter-principle. The Dionysius/Plotinus difference surfaces in the account of the soul: "Plotinus judges that souls are ill insofar as they are not sufficiently minds. Dionysius treats minds as souls" (Trouillard, "Le cosmos du Pseudo-Denys," 56) [lines 5328–5330] — for Dionysius the fall is not a failure to be intellectual enough but a departure from the proper orientation of the personal will.

**Comparison with Augustine.** Perl draws an explicit parallel that is not present in the existing sections of this page. Dionysius's "view of evil as deficiency of being and hence as uncaused and unintelligible is thus closely comparable to Augustine's, although Augustine thinks more of efficient causality and Dionysius more of final causality" [lines 5358–5360]. Augustine: "Let no one, then, seek an efficient cause of an evil will. For its cause is not efficient, but deficient … the will is made evil by nothing else than the defection by which God is forsaken: a defection of which the cause, too, is certainly deficient" (*City of God* XII.7–9) [lines 5361–5369]. This parallel reinforces what the existing sections on Augustine (above) document while adding the Dionysian dimension of *final* causality: things desire the Good, and evil is the failure to attain or be oriented to it.

Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], Ch. 4 endnotes, range lines 5303–5371.

### Body Text Analysis (Ch. 4)

*Source: Chapter 4 body text, range lines 2258–2752.*

**The philosophical stakes of the privation theory.** Perl's body text establishes that the doctrine of evil as non-being is not an optimistic dodge but follows necessarily from the central Neoplatonic-Dionysian identification of being with intelligibility: to be is to be intelligible, and intelligibility consists in goodness. Therefore "that which is altogether devoid of goodness has no intelligibility, no unity, no identity, and hence is not anything at all" [lines 2291–2293]. Perl traces this from Plotinus, for whom evil is identified with matter as privation — "matter has not even being — if it had it would by this means have a share in good; when we say it 'is' we are just using the same word for two different things, and the true way of speaking is to say it 'is not'" (I.8.5.8–13) [lines 2312–2316] — to Proclus, who improves on Plotinus by rejecting the identification of evil with matter, since matter as *necessary* for the cosmos cannot be evil: "evil is one thing, and the necessary another" (*De mal. sub.* 32.1–5) [lines 2397–2401]. Dionysius follows Proclus on this point expressly (DN IV.28, 729A) [lines 2484–2487] and extends the privation analysis to all levels of being: "all beings, insofar as they are, are good and from the Good; and insofar as they are deprived of the Good, they are neither good nor beings... But what is in every way deprived of the Good neither was nor is nor will be nor can be in any way whatsoever" (DN IV.20, 720B) [lines 2455–2459].

**Evil as failure of reversion and failure of theophany.** The conceptually decisive move in the body text is Perl's grounding of evil in the doctrine of reversion. Since every being must *actively* appropriate its constitutive determinations (revert to its cause), "there is a possibility of failure" [line 2543]. A being is evil insofar as it fails to exercise the activity by which it is itself. Since that activity just is its mode of being and of loving God, "as a being's partial lack of its proper perfections, evil is ultimately a failure of reversion, the being's failure to appropriate, to desire, to love God as the Goodness whereby it is" [lines 2548–2550]. This places evil squarely outside theophany: "insofar as anything is evil, i.e. insofar as it is not, God is not productively present in it" [lines 2598–2600]. Evil has no positive content that could constitute a theophany; it is the zone where divine self-manifestation fails to take hold because the being has failed to perform the reception. The significant corollary is that no activity *qua* activity can be evil: "Evil, therefore, lies not in a being's acting contrary to its nature but only in its not acting according to its nature, and so not fully being" [lines 2578–2580]. Evil is fundamentally passivity, the "many passions" that drive a person not from within but from without — "largely passive, driven about not by himself but by the passions, by what happens to him from without, he is vicious in his lack of unity, of interiority, of selfhood" [lines 2663–2657].

**Handling demons and fallen angels.** Dionysius's most distinctive contribution in DN IV.18–35, as Perl reads the body text, is extending privation to demons (fallen angels). Demons are evil "not insofar as they are, for they are from the Good and received a good reality, but insofar as they are not, by being weak in preserving their principle" (DN IV.23, 725A) [lines 2506–2508]; and "not evil by nature, but by the deficiency of angelic goods" (DN IV.23, 725B) [lines 2511–2512]. They retain positive perfections: "they are not altogether without a share in the Good, insofar as they both are and live and think" (DN IV.23, 725B) [lines 2518–2519]; it is only insofar as they lack their constitutive perfections that they fail to be. This differs importantly from Proclus, who holds that *daimones* (a term that in Proclus means purely good spiritual beings, not fallen angels) are incapable of evil — Dionysius brings Christian theology's category of fallen angel into the privation framework without granting evil a positive nature.

**Evil as causeless and unintelligible — the comparison with Augustine.** The climactic philosophical claim is that evil has no cause: "as non-being, as inactivity, evil is without cause (*anaition*)" (DN IV.30, 732A) [lines 2663–2664]. Perl treats this as the doctrine's philosophical strength, not its weakness. Since causality is intelligibility and intelligibility is goodness, an evil that had a cause would by that fact be good. "Unintelligibility, or causelessness, is therefore the very meaning of evil; and it is as unintelligibility that evil is non-being" [lines 2710–2712]. This is why Dionysius says man "unintelligibly fell from the divine goods" (EH III.3.11, 440C) [lines 2707–2709] — the fall as such cannot be understood. The parallel with Augustine (noted in the endnote analysis above) is confirmed in the body text's argument structure: both treat the will's defection from the Good as the font of evil and both deny that this defection has any efficient or intelligible cause, though Dionysius operates more from final causality (things fail to attain their telos) while Augustine operates more from efficient causality (the will fails to choose its proper end).

## Rolt's "Zero" Image and His Critique of Dionysius (1920)

[[rolt-dionysius|C. E. Rolt]] restates the Dionysian privation theory with a striking
illustration: evil is **like zero in mathematics** — "non-entity (since, added to numbers, it
makes no difference) and yet has an annihilating force (since it reduces to zero all numbers that
are multiplied by it). Even so evil is nothing and yet manifests itself in the annihilation of
the things it qualifies." Evil is "a tendency of things towards nothingness": sickness toward
death, sin toward spiritual death. Because "the ground of the soul is indestructible," no creature
(not even a demon) is "inherently bad" — "Were they such they would cease *ipso facto* to exist."

Unusually, Rolt also **criticizes** the doctrine he expounds. Dionysius's "great weakness," he
argues, is failing "to emphasize the ugliness of evil": "a lapse or failure has in it something
positive… as positive as the virtues from which it lapses." Quoting Augustine ("Sin is naught,
and men are naughts when they sin"), Rolt insists that in this finite world "the bad qualities of
things may… have as much actuality as their good qualities"; evil "ought to have no actuality
here, not that it has none." He attributes Dionysius's one-sidedness partly to anti-[[manichaeism|
Manichaean]] over-correction and to his being "so dazzled with his vision of ultimate Reality"
that he gives too little prominence "to the Cross of Christ." Rolt distinguishes three Dionysian
senses of **Non-Existence** (formless Aristotelian "Matter"; evil as formless; the Super-Essence
as beyond Existence) and notes the "dangerous, yet true" corollary — taught also by Augustine and
Dante — that "evil is a mistaken quest for Good." See [[rolt-dionysius|Rolt's edition]].
[[dante-alighieri|Dante]] dramatizes the doctrine in [[inferno|Inferno]] 34: his
[[satan|Satan]] is "pure negation," a parasitic parody of the [[trinity|Trinity]],
impotent and self-imprisoned — evil as the mere absence of the divine activity that
alone fully exists.
