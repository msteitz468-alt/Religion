The **Valiant Woman** (Hebrew *ʾēšet ḥayil*, "woman/wife of valor/strength") is the subject of
the acrostic poem that concludes the book of [[proverbs|Proverbs]] (31:10–31). Framed as the
book's epilogue, the poem praises a woman whose industrious, wise, and God-fearing activity
sustains her household and community. It is a first-class concept in the wiki because it carries
a distinct cluster of interpretive disputes — over genre, over whether the figure is an allegory
of wisdom or a real woman, and over its function as the counterpart to [[woman-wisdom|Woman
Wisdom]] in the frame of Proverbs.

## Definition and Text

The poem is a **Hebrew acrostic** — each of its 22 verses begins with a successive letter of the
alphabet — and, like the acrostic of Sirach 51:13–20, "draws the book to its conclusion"
([[bruce-waltke|Waltke]], NICOT). The phrase *ʾēšet ḥayil* also occurs at Proverbs 12:4 and,
famously, describes Ruth (Ruth 3:11). Its rendering has varied: LXX *gynē andreia* ("manly,
courageous woman"); Jerome *mulier fortis* ("strong woman"); KJV "virtuous woman" (from Latin
*virtus*, "manly excellence"); Jewish English "woman of valor." Waltke: *ḥayil* "denotes
'competent strength' and connotes wealth … and membership in a select group … including a
warrior class"; "'valiant' … fits the present heroic form."

Waltke's structural reading (attributed): an introduction on her value (vv.10–12); a body on her
activities (vv.13–27), with v.19 an "almost invisible seam" (janus) between her textile
manufacture (13–18) and her social achievements (20–27); and a conclusion of praise (28–31). The
body's second half is chiastic, with **v.23 (her husband respected at the city gate) as the
pivot** — "the valiant wife's accomplishments empower her husband to lead the nation in
righteousness and justice." The conclusion links to the introduction by three catchwords
(*ʾiššâ*, *ḥayil*, *baʿal*), forming an inclusio.

## Genre: Heroic Poetry, not Praise Hymn (attributed to Waltke / Wolters)

Waltke rejects the identification of the poem as a **praise hymn** (parallel to Psalm 112): it
lacks the opening call to praise the Lord, and the "hymnic participle" appears only once (v.27),
drawing the body to conclusion rather than dominating it. Following Al **Wolters**, he classes it
instead as **heroic/valor poetry** — "characterized by recounting the hero's mighty deeds,
usually his military exploits." The poem's military vocabulary is transposed into the domestic
sphere: *šālāl* ("spoil," v.11), *ṭerep* ("prey," v.15), and *ḥayil* / "she has done valiantly"
(v.29). On Wolters' reading (following R. G. Mark) *gebûrâ* is redefined "from 'heroic prowess'
to 'academic and moral victories'"; **Erika Moore** adds that the poem presents the godly wife
"as a spiritual heir of Israel's ancient heroes."

Wolters and Maryse Waegeman read the poem as **polemic**: against ANE erotic praise of women
"from an erotic point of view" (it praises instead her "activity in the ordinary affairs of
family, community and business life"), and against the Hellenistic ideal of the "silent,
'homebody' spouse" and "abstract theoretic wisdom rooted in impartial rationality" — over against
"concrete practical wisdom rooted in the fear of the Lord."

## The Central Crux: Allegory of Wisdom or a Real Woman?

This is the poem's principal controversy, and the wiki records the positions without adjudicating.

- **Symbolic / allegorical reading (McCreesh, and Whybray's leaning).** McCreesh rejects a literal
  reading "because 'the husband is left with little or nothing to do,'" arguing the wife
  corresponds point-for-point to sapiential values (valiant, rare, precious, trustworthy, wise
  teacher, pious) and so personifies [[woman-wisdom|Woman Wisdom]]. Whybray finds support in "the
  unmistakable return in the book's conclusion to the prominent role of the feminine figure in the
  Prologue."
- **Historical / real-wife reading (Waltke's position).** Waltke holds that his "exegesis …
  validates the traditional interpretation that the valiant wife belongs in the historical, not
  allegorical, realm." His arguments: the term *ʾēšet ḥayil* "denotes a real woman in its other
  occurrence (12:4)," and every Proverbs use of *ʾiššâ* is a real woman; McCreesh "undermines his
  own argument" by noting the parallel with Ruth, a real woman called *ʾēšet ḥayil* (Ruth 3:11);
  and Hawkins' distinctions hold (Woman Wisdom is never a wife or mother and is "a composite
  figure — prophet, teacher, and mediatrix — whereas the capable wife is exclusively a
  homemaker"). Waltke concedes the **inclusio** framing the book (Prologue's Woman Wisdom ↔
  epilogue's valiant wife) "is no coincidence but intentionally functions as the book's frame,"
  but denies it forces a symbolic reading — "Woman Wisdom in 9:1–6 is purely symbolic, but the
  foolish woman in 9:13–18 incarnates folly." His mediating conclusion: she "incarnates wisdom's
  ideals, without removing her from the historical realm."

This is the load-bearing distinction from [[woman-wisdom|Woman Wisdom]]: on Waltke's reading the
two feminine figures *frame* the book but are of different kinds — one a symbolic personification,
the other a historical exemplar who embodies what wisdom produces.

## Theological Significance

- **Fear of the LORD as the climactic value (31:30).** "Charm is deceitful and beauty is fleeting;
  as for a woman who fears the LORD, she should be praised." Waltke: this "vertical virtue …
  provides the necessary theological base for her horizontal social virtues," and the phrase
  brackets the whole book — [[fear-of-the-lord|fear of the LORD]] at its beginning (1:7; 9:10),
  middle (15:33), and end (31:30). Charm and beauty are called "deceitful" (*šeqer*) and "fleeting"
  (*hebel*) — though Waltke holds this in tension with the father's prayer for a wife with sex
  appeal (5:19) and with the Song of Songs.
- **A gift from God, sought in prayer.** "Such a wife is a gift from God (19:14) and must in part
  be sought by faithful prayer."
- **A theological anomaly of trust (31:11).** "His heart trusts in her" is "remarkable," Waltke
  notes, because "outside this text and Judg 20:36, Scripture condemns trust in anyone or anything
  apart from … the Lord"; the exception "elevates the valiant wife, who herself fears the Lord, to
  the highest level of spiritual and physical competence."
- **A canonized role model.** Waltke: "this valiant wife has been canonized as a role model for
  all Israel for all time. Wise daughters aspire to be like her, wise men seek to marry her, and
  all wise people aim to incarnate the wisdom she embodies, each in his own sphere." A wordplay
  closes the poem: "and praises her" (*wayehalelāh*) "sounds very similar to … 'and praise Yah.'"

## Reception

In Jewish practice the *ʾēšet ḥayil* is traditionally sung on Friday evenings before the Sabbath
meal. Its authorship is tied to the debate over [[lemuel|Lemuel]]'s Collection VII: whether the
acrostic belongs with the mother's instruction (31:2–9) or stands as an independent poem (see
[[lemuel|Lemuel]] and the Contradiction/authorship discussion there).
