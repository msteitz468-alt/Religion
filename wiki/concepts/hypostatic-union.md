# Hypostatic Union

The **hypostatic union** (Greek *henōsis kath' hypostasin*) is the union of the divine and human
natures of [[jesus|Christ]] **in one *hypostasis* (Person)** — the central settlement of the
Christological controversies, defined at the Council of Chalcedon (451): one Person "made known in
two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." The term
names *where* the union takes place: not in the nature, and not accidentally, but **in the
subsisting Person of the Word**. It is the doctrine the [[incarnation|Incarnation]] turns on.

## The conciliar background

The Chalcedonian formula was hammered out against two opposite errors:

- **Nestorianism** — so distinguishing the natures as to imply *two* persons /
  subjects loosely conjoined (a union of indwelling, dignity, or grace). Condemned at Ephesus (431).
- **Monophysitism / Eutychianism** — so uniting the natures as to leave *one*
  nature, the human absorbed into the divine ("one incarnate nature"). Condemned at Chalcedon (451).
- Constantinople II (553) and III (680–681) extended the settlement, the latter defining **two
  wills and two operations** (against Monothelitism).

The Eastern Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac), holding to Cyril's
*miaphysite* formula "one incarnate nature of the Word," reject Chalcedon — a division between
[[eastern-orthodoxy|Chalcedonian Orthodoxy]]/[[catholicism|Catholicism]] and the non-Chalcedonian
churches that persists to this day.

## Aquinas's metaphysical articulation (ST III Q2–Q3)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Tertia Pars Q2–Q3) gives the union its
classic scholastic analysis using the distinction between **nature** and **suppositum/person**:

- **Not in the nature** (Q2 A1): a union "in the nature" would mean either juxtaposition, mixture,
  or composition of parts yielding a third thing — all of which destroy the integrity of Godhead
  and manhood. So the natures remain distinct, "each retaining what belongs to it."
- **In the Person/hypostasis** (Q2 A2–A3): "whatever adheres to a person is united to it in
  person." The key tool is that a *nature* (the *quid est*, "what a thing is") is not the same as a
  *suppositum* (the subsisting *who*); in all composite beings the two really differ. Christ's human
  nature is real and complete, yet it does **not** subsist as a created human person — it subsists in
  the divine Person of the Word. Aquinas's image: "the hand of Socrates is not a person, because it
  does not exist by itself"; the assumed humanity, existing "in something nobler than oneself,"
  likewise constitutes no second person (Q2 A2). To posit a proper human hypostasis would be to
  posit a second person — the Nestorian error (Q2 A3, citing Ephesus's anathemas).
- **Not accidental** (Q2 A6): the Catholic position is the *via media* — neither in the essence/
  nature (Eutyches) nor accidentally as one thing joined to another (Nestorius), but **"in a
  subsistence or hypostasis."**
- **A composite Person "by reason of number"** (Q2 A4): there is one *esse personale* (personal
  being) subsisting in two natures — Damascene's "two natures and one hypostasis composed from
  both." The composition is not from integral parts (as soul + body make an animal) but from the one
  Person's subsisting in two.
- **The union is something created** (Q2 A7): the relation of the two natures is really in the
  created nature, only conceptually in God.
- **Which Person could assume** (Q3): the *act* of assuming belongs to the divine power common to
  all three Persons, but its *term* is the Person of the Son alone; any of the three Persons *could*
  have assumed, and it was most **fitting** that the Son — the Word, exemplar of all creation and of
  human wisdom — should (Q3 A4–A5, A8).

## Significance

The hypostatic union is the metaphysical ground of the [[communicatio-idiomatum|communicatio
idiomatum]] (because there is one Person, the properties of either nature are predicated of that
one Person), of the [[atonement|satisfaction]] wrought by Christ (the acts of the God-man have
"infinite efficiency"), and of the worship of Christ's humanity with **latria** (the Person, not a
mere creature, is adored — see [[incarnation]] §Adoration). It distinguishes the orthodox
confession from every Christology that either splits Christ into two subjects or merges the natures
into one.
