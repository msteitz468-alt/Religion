# Righteousness (*dikaiosynē* in Matthew)

## Definition

*Dikaiosynē* is the Greek noun conventionally rendered "righteousness." It occurs seven times in
the [[matthew|Gospel of Matthew]]: at 3:15, 5:6, 5:10, 5:20, 6:1, 6:33 and 21:32.

[[rt-france|R. T. France]] argues that the Matthean sense is behavioural rather than forensic:
*dikaiosynē* in this gospel "is overwhelmingly concerned with right conduct, with living the way God
requires." He explicitly distinguishes "the essentially behavioral sense of *dikaiosynē* in Matthew"
from "Paul's more theological and soteriological use of the term." This page records France's
reading of the term as a position within his commentary; it is not a synthesis across the wider
Christian tradition, and single-source claims are attributed to him throughout.

## France's rejection of the divine-action reading

At 5:6 ("hunger and thirst for righteousness") France reports that many interpreters take
*dikaiosynē* to represent not the behaviour of the disciple but the action of God — either his
justice or intervention (he cites NEB's "hunger and thirst to see right prevail") or Pauline
justification. He **rejects both**. His grounds are two: the dominant Matthean usage, which he
regards as decided by 5:20; and 5:10, where the righteousness for which disciples are persecuted
"can hardly be understood as divine action."

At 3:15 ("to fulfil all righteousness") France treats the case as partly exceptional. He notes that
while *dikaiosynē* elsewhere in Matthew means the conduct God expects, the phrase "fitting for us"
shows Jesus means something specific to his own and John's roles rather than a general principle:
"God requires him to be baptized by John." He acknowledges that "the explanation given does not
spell out why this is 'the right way for us to fulfill all that is required of us'." He floats a
possible echo of Isa 53:11's *yaṣdîq ṣaddîq* behind the term here but states that "The verbal
allusion is not certain," while holding servant ideology to be an appropriate background. See
[[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]].

## The greater righteousness (5:20)

France treats 5:20 as the interpretive fulcrum both of the term and of the
[[sermon-on-the-mount|Sermon on the Mount]] as a whole. He states the programmatic thesis at the
outset of his exposition of ch. 5: "The essence of life in the kingdom of heaven is in fact the
antithesis of a legalistic code, as 5:20 will state and 5:21-48 will repeatedly illustrate."

His central argument is that the demand cannot mean out-performing the [[pharisees|scribes and
Pharisees]] at rule-keeping: "Jesus is not talking about beating the scribes and Pharisees at their
own game, but about a different level or concept of righteousness altogether." The demand, he says,
"makes sense only if their basic premise as to what 'righteousness' consists of is put in question."
His sharpest formulation runs: "Those who can do no more than simply keep the rules, however
conscientiously, haven't even started as far as the kingdom of heaven is concerned."

France is notably even-handed about the foil. He holds that the scribes and Pharisees "would have
approved of what Jesus has just said in vv. 18-19"; that their traditions aimed not to supplant the
law but to guide observance; and that "in their own intention and ... in the eyes of the people at
large, they were staunch defenders and eager exponents of the role of the law as a practical guide
to holy living," respected for it. He also generalizes the critique beyond Judaism: the greater
righteousness is in a different league from that of the scribes and Pharisees "and of any other
religious traditions which understand the will of God in terms of the punctilious observance of
rules."

France identifies a tension inside 5:17-20 itself: v. 19 sounds like an endorsement of scribal
thoroughness, while v. 20 "warns that that sort of legal observance leaves one outside the kingdom
of heaven altogether." His resolution is the two-fronts thesis — vv. 17-19 confront those tempted
to set the law aside, v. 20 those "so preoccupied with its literal observance that they miss the
whole point of the fulfillment." See [[fulfillment|fulfilment]], [[old-law|the old law]] and
[[new-law|the new law]].

He characterizes the function of the commandments in what follows as "not as simple rules of conduct
but as pointers to a 'greater righteousness' which Jesus has brought into being and which supersedes
the old type of lawkeeping."

France quotes J. P. Meier approvingly on what the demand amounts to: "a radical interiorization, a
total obedience to God, a complete self-giving to neighbor, that carries the ethical thrust of the
Law to its God-willed conclusion, even when this means in some cases abrogating the letter of the
Law."

### Four features of "going beyond"

From the [[antitheses|six antitheses]] France extracts four common features of the greater
righteousness:

1. inward motive is weighted over outward observance;
2. the reading goes behind rules to far-reaching principles;
3. positive discovery of God's will replaces negative sin-avoidance;
4. a "100 percent achievable righteousness" is replaced by "a totally open-ended ideal (being
   'perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect') ... which will always remain beyond the grasp of
   the most committed disciple."

France's summary contrast is that "any definable set of rules could, in principle, be fully kept,"
whereas the kingdom's demand "has no such limit — or rather its limit is perfection, the perfection
of God himself." He reads 5:48 as both rounding off the sixth antithesis and summing up the whole,
putting "into a neat epigram the essential nature of the 'greater righteousness' introduced in
v. 20." He holds the wording recalls the Leviticus formula "You are to be holy, for I, the LORD your
God, am holy" (Lev 19:2; 11:44, 45; 20:26): "God's people were to reflect his character, and the
same is now true for those who are subjects of the kingdom of heaven." On *teleios* he argues it may
derive from Deut 18:13's *tāmîm* and "is a wider term than moral flawlessness," used for spiritual
maturity (1 Cor 2:6; 14:20; Phil 3:15; Hebrews). He explicitly denies a two-tier ethic: the standard
is demanded "not only from an upper echelon of spiritual elites but from all who belong to the
kingdom of God."

His diagnosis of what went wrong is that "Even the God-given law had been accommodated to a
practical ethical code with which Jewish society had come to feel comfortable," whereas Jesus
demands looking behind those laws to the mind and character of God himself.

## The two uses in chapter 6

**6:1.** France reads 6:1 as turning from the greater righteousness of 5:20-48 to a *wrong* kind of
"righteousness," undertaken not to conform to God's will but for human approval. He notes a shift of
register: "the focus has moved from ethical distinctives to the practice of religion, the
'righteousness' of 6:1 being not so much a moral orientation as a religious one, practical piety."
He observes that 6:1 is plural while the three following examples are singular, so that v.1
functions as a general exhortation introducing 6:2-18 "in much the same way as 5:20 introduced
5:21-48."

**6:33.** France notes the verb "seek" (*zēteō*) echoes the stronger compound *epizēteō* used of the
Gentiles' anxious quest in v. 32, marking the disciples' different orientation. He argues the further
nuance in *pleion* is that life is not only more important than food but consists of much "more,"
spelled out in v. 33 as the pursuit of God's kingship and righteousness. He explicitly rejects
reading "seek the kingdom" as "trying to bring in God's kingdom" as an eschatological event, arguing
from the present imperative and the verb's normal sense: "One must pray for the coming of God's
reign (6:10) because it is God who will bring it into being." His compact definition — "God's
kingship means God's people living under God's rule" — he treats as another way of saying the same
thing as seeking righteousness. See [[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]].

## Righteousness as the subject of the discourse's conclusion

France argues that 7:12 is "a far-reaching and memorable summary of the ethics of discipleship (the
'greater righteousness' of 5:20)," gathering the [[beatitudes|beatitudes]], salt and light,
forgiveness and the strictures on criticism.

Of the closing warnings he makes a striking observation: "The word 'righteousness' does not occur in
these concluding pericopes, but that is what they are about." He surveys Matthew's "fruit" metaphor
(3:8; 12:33; 13:8, 23; 21:19; 21:33-43) and concludes it is "predominantly an ethical metaphor,"
resting on the assumption that true loyalty to God issues in appropriate behaviour; only prophets
whose lives reveal kingdom righteousness are to be credited.

## The tension with Pauline grace

France does not harmonize Matthew with Paul. On the conditionality of forgiveness in 6:14-15 he
states plainly that "Only the forgiving will be forgiven," raises the "uncomfortable questions" this
poses, and offers this verdict: as with the traditional Paul–James antinomy, "there is not perhaps a
simple contradiction, but there is certainly a clear difference of perspective on what the
forgiveness of sins involves." He notes the same problem at 25:31-46 and 22:1-14, where "Salvation
... may be undeserved and unexpected, but it is not without conditions," and adds that if the
forgiveness achieved through Jesus' death (26:28) is not matched by an appropriately forgiving
attitude, "it cannot be presumed upon."

He registers the same tension over reward language. He notes that the commercial term "repay" at 6:4
"may suggest a 'quid pro quo' transaction which sits uncomfortably alongside a Pauline doctrine of
salvation by grace and not by merit," but holds it is counterbalanced by Matthew's *disproportionate*
rewards (19:29; 20:1-15; 25:21, 23). On treasure in heaven he concedes the language may suggest
earning but insists 20:1-15 shows a deliberate discrepancy between effort and recompense, so that
"there is no room for computing one's 'treasures in heaven' on the basis of earthly effort": they
are stored "not by performing meritorious acts (and certainly not only by almsgiving) but by
belonging to and living by the priorities of the kingdom of heaven." He also observes that "Unlike
many modern Christians, Matthew is not coy about the 'reward'."

At 7:21-23 France sharpens the point in the opposite direction: "Even good works by themselves are
not enough," since prophecy, exorcism and miracles "can hardly be called bad fruit" yet can be
performed by those lacking the relationship with Jesus that is the essential basis for belonging to
the kingdom. His redefinition is that "'Doing the will of my Father in heaven' is not a merely
ethical category; that will also includes to know and be known by Jesus the 'Lord.'" See
[[grace|grace]].

Elsewhere France balances his critique: at 9:13 he writes that "Righteousness is not of course in
itself a bad thing; indeed, properly understood it is the goal of discipleship," but that the
righteousness which puts sacrifice before mercy is not the kingdom's — "The point of 'calling
sinners' ... is not that they should remain sinners but that they may find true righteousness." At
11:28-30 he is emphatic that the "rest" Jesus offers is *not* a relaxation of the demands of
righteousness — "rather the opposite: see 5:20" — but a new relationship with God making fulfilment
possible.
