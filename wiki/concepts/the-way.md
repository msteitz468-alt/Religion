# The Way (*Hē Hodos*)

**"The Way"** (Greek *hē hodos*) is Luke's preferred self-designation for the early Christian
movement in [[acts|Acts]] — the name the community used of itself before it came to be regarded as a
distinct "faith" or "sect." As read by [[darrell-bock|Darrell Bock]] (BECNT *Acts*, 2007), the term
is an Old Testament-rooted metaphor for the path of God, and its use marks the movement's Jewish
self-understanding.

## The Term and Its Occurrences

Luke uses "the Way" absolutely (without further qualification) as a title for the movement at
several points in Acts:

- **9:2** — Saul seeks authority against "any belonging to the Way" in Damascus;
- **19:9, 23** — opposition arises in Ephesus, where "the Way" is the issue that provokes the
  Artemis riot (Bock: "'the Way' is the issue → Christians cause the stir");
- **22:4** — Paul recalls that he "persecuted this Way to the death";
- **24:14, 22** — before Felix, Paul confesses that "according to the Way, which they call a sect
  (*hairesis*), I worship the God of our fathers," and Felix is said to have "a rather accurate
  knowledge of the Way."

Bock notes that Paul deliberately **counters the label "sect" (*hairesis*) with "Way" (*hodos*)**:
the movement is "not a 'heretical' movement" but the true worship of Israel's God. In Bock's survey
of the many names for the community — brethren, believers, disciples, saints, the church,
Nazoreans, and (from outsiders, first at Antioch) "Christians" — "the Way" is the term Luke himself
most favors as the movement's own name.

## An OT-Rooted Metaphor for the Path of God

Bock reads "the Way" as an **Old Testament-rooted metaphor** for the path or road of God — the way
of the Lord that one walks in obedience. The absolute usage presents the movement not as a novel
religion but as the God-ordained road within Israel's own scriptural idiom. On Bock's reading this
coheres with Luke's broader presentation of Christianity as "a natural extension of Judaism"
(cf. Acts 15:5, where *hairesis* is used descriptively, not pejoratively, of Sadducees, Pharisees,
and Christians alike).

## Marker of the Movement's Jewish Self-Understanding

For Bock the self-designation "the Way" belongs to the period **before the movement became a
distinct "faith."** He connects this to Luke's larger thesis (drawing on L. T. Johnson) that "the
Way" was **"forced to become"** a separate faith by the reality of Jewish rejection — the community
"became the church ... because ... the realities of the reaction to the church forced it to become
separate." The name thus captures a moment of Jewish self-understanding: the followers of Jesus
saw themselves as walking the ancient path of God within Israel, not yet as members of a religion
over against Judaism. Later outsiders' labels — "sect," "Nazoreans," "Christians" — mark the
movement's gradual differentiation, but "the Way" preserves its original self-conception.
