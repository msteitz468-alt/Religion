# Charity and Cupidity (Caritas/Cupiditas)

## Definition

In [[augustine]]'s [[on-christian-doctrine]], *caritas* (charity/love) and *cupiditas* (cupidity/lust) name the two opposing orientations of human desire. *Caritas* is love rightly ordered toward God and neighbor; *cupiditas* is love wrongly fixed on temporal, creaturely goods as ends in themselves. The distinction is the moral and hermeneutical core of Book I: the end of all Scripture is *caritas*, and any interpretation that builds *caritas* is on the right path, even if it misses the author's precise intent.

## Augustine's Account

### Caritas: Love Ordered Toward God

*Caritas* is love directed toward God for His own sake, and toward neighbor and self for God's sake. It is the fulfillment of the two great commandments:
> "The end of the commandment is love, and that twofold, the love of God and the love of our neighbor." (Bk. I, ch. 26, lines 740–741)

Augustine's summary of what Book I has established is unambiguous:
> "Of all, then, that has been said since we entered upon the discussion about things, this is the sum: that we should clearly understand that the fulfillment and the end of the Law, and of all Holy Scripture, is the love of an object which is to be enjoyed, and the love of an object which can enjoy that other in fellowship with ourselves." (Bk. I, ch. 35, lines 950–953)

Caritas does not rest in any created thing but passes through it toward God. The interpreter of Scripture must have caritas: "he may come to the interpretation of these books with an easy mind" if he understands that the goal of all understanding is "charity, out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned" (Bk. I, ch. 40, lines 1043–1046, citing 1 Tim. 1:5).

### Cupiditas: Disordered Love

*Cupiditas* is love that rests in what should be only used — temporal, mutable goods treated as ultimate ends. It is the condition of "the wanderer" who becomes enchanted by the beauty of the journey and refuses to press on toward home (Bk. I, ch. 4). Men who "in their eagerness to enjoy the creature instead of the Creator had grown into the likeness of this world" are described as themselves becoming "the world" that did not recognize Christ (Bk. I, ch. 12, lines 486–488).

The soul in cupidity is also implicitly in *self-love wrongly ordered*: "it is inherent in the sinful soul to desire above all things, and to claim as due to itself, that which is properly due to God only. Now such love of itself is more correctly called hate" (Bk. I, ch. 23, lines 658–661).

### The Order of Love (Bk. I, ch. 27)

Augustine formulates a positive principle of *ordo amoris* (ordered love):
> "Now he is a man of just and holy life who forms an unprejudiced estimate of things, and keeps his affections also under strict control, so that he neither loves what he ought not to love, nor fails to love what he ought to love, nor loves that more which ought to be loved less, nor loves that equally which ought to be loved either less or more." (Bk. I, ch. 27, lines 754–757)

The ordering: God above all, then other persons (who can share in enjoying God), then one's own body (which cannot enjoy God but only lives through the soul that does). "No sinner is to be loved as a sinner; and every man is to be loved as a man for God's sake; but God is to be loved for His own sake" (Bk. I, ch. 27, lines 758–759).

Love of enemies is also grounded here: enemies are not feared (they cannot take away what we love — God) but pitied, "because the more they hate us the more are they separated from Him whom we love" (Bk. I, ch. 29, lines 794–796).

### Caritas as the Hermeneutical Rule

The central hermeneutical principle of *De Doctrina Christiana* flows directly from this: an interpretation that builds *caritas* is not perniciously wrong, even if it departs from the author's intent:
> "Whoever, then, thinks that he understands the Holy Scriptures, or any part of them, but puts such an interpretation upon them as does not tend to build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought." (Bk. I, ch. 36, lines 970–972)

Conversely:
> "If, on the other hand, a man draws a meaning from them that may be used for the building up of love, even though he does not happen upon the precise meaning which the author whom he reads intended to express in that place, his error is not pernicious, and he is wholly clear from the charge of deception." (Bk. I, ch. 36, lines 972–976)

The interpreter who wanders from the literal meaning but arrives at *caritas* is like a traveler who quits the high road but reaches the destination through the fields. He should be corrected — lest habit lead him further astray — but he has not sinned fatally (Bk. I, ch. 36, lines 985–991).

### Faith, Hope, and Love (Bk. I, chs. 37–40)

Augustine closes Book I by subordinating faith and hope to love. All knowledge and prophecy are subservient to faith, hope, and love (ch. 37, lines 1007–1008). But love is greatest: "sight shall displace faith; and hope shall be swallowed up in that perfect bliss to which we shall come: love, on the other hand, shall wax greater when these others fail" (Bk. I, ch. 38, lines 1010–1012). In the eternal world, faith and hope dissolve into direct vision and possession; love alone endures and intensifies. A person who has arrived at mature faith, hope, and love "does not need the Scriptures except for the purpose of instructing others" (Bk. I, ch. 39, lines 1024–1026).

## Hermeneutical Summary

*Caritas* is not merely a moral virtue but the *telos* of scriptural interpretation. The interpreter's readiness for the task is measured by whether she has "charity, out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned" (Bk. I, ch. 40). This makes the reader's moral formation a precondition of hermeneutical success — an anticipation of what later tradition will call the *spiritual sense* requiring spiritual readers.
