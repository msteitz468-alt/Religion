*Yôm YHWH*, the Day of the LORD — the prophetic announcement of God's decisive intervention
in history for judgment and, beyond judgment, blessing. Documented here from the book of
[[isaiah|Isaiah]] via Oswalt (NICOT, chs. 1–39); the phrase and its cognate "in that day"
formulas run across the prophetic corpus (Amos 5:18ff.; Zeph 1; Joel; Mal 3 — flagged by
Oswalt as parallels).

## Popular vs Prophetic Usage (attributed to Oswalt)

On Isa 2:12 Oswalt argues the phrase "must have been used popularly to denote a time when
God would vindicate his people… But the prophets' word was that the day of the Lord would be
a time of destruction and terror, and only after that time could blessing come." The day's
target in Isa 2 is human arrogance — the cedars, mountains, towers, and ships are figurative
("although not allegorical") of pride; the refrain is "the LORD alone will be exalted in
that day."

## Isaiah 13 as Set Piece (attributed)

In the Babylon oracle Oswalt describes the Day as "a day when human strength will be
helpless, when creation itself will tremble, when the almost boundless capacity for human
cruelty will be unleashed," its cause "the confrontation between the supremacy of God and
that thirst for supremacy which is lodged in the human heart." He notes the šōd/Šadday
wordplay (13:6), reads the darkened heavenly bodies as doubling as polemic against astral
deities ("There is no universal power in the stars that human beings can capture for
themselves"), and takes the depopulation language as "principle" rather than hyperbole. The
Medes function as a figure of "pitiless destruction characterizing the Day of the Lord,
without intending any specific prediction."

## "In That Day" and the Double Edge

The formula marks both dread (7:18ff.) and hope (4:2; 10:20; 11:10; 12:1): the same day
that judges also cleanses — "God's coming day would only be complete when cleansing and
restoration had taken place" (on 4:2). In chs. 24–27 the five "in that day" units of
19:16–25 and the trumpet-ingathering of 27:12–13 express Oswalt's summary conviction that
"all history will come to a point — a point where the just will be rewarded and the wicked
punished."
