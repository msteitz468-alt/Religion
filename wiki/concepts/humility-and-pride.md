# Humility and Pride (Aquinas)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats **humility** (*humilitas*) and its great opposite **pride**
(*superbia*) at the close of the treatise on temperance ([[summa-theologica|*ST*]] II-II Q161–162),
and connects pride directly to the **first sin** of the angels and of Adam (Q163–165). For Christian
ethics this pair is decisive: pride is the root of all sin, and humility the virtue that "lays the
foundation of the spiritual edifice."

## Humility (Q161)

Humility is a **genuine virtue**, seated in the appetite, that **restrains the inordinate movement of
hope** — tempering the desire for one's own excellence so that one does not reach beyond one's measure
(Q161, A1). Its **root is reverence for God**: recognizing what is one's own (defect) and what is
God's (every good one has). Aquinas ranks it **highest among the moral virtues after justice and the
theological and intellectual virtues**, because it disposes a man to be rightly subject to God in *all*
things — yet it is **not the greatest virtue simply**, since [[charity-aquinas|charity]] and the other
theological virtues surpass it (Q161, A5).

He reproduces **St. Benedict's twelve degrees of humility** (from inward reverence through outward
bearing to the lowest estimation of self), analysing them as ascending from root to appetite to
estimation to outward signs, with **Bernard's twelve degrees of pride** as their exact inverse.

### Humility and magnanimity

A standing puzzle is how humility coheres with the pagan-derived virtue of **magnanimity** (the
stretching of the soul toward great honours, treated under [[cardinal-virtues|fortitude]]). Aquinas
answers that they **regard different things**: magnanimity looks to the great gifts a man has **from
God** and dares to use them worthily; humility looks to his **own deficiency** and to what is due to
God. Rightly understood, the two virtues are not in conflict but jointly order a man's self-estimate.

## Pride (Q162)

Pride is the **inordinate desire for one's own excellence** — refusing to be subject to a superior, and
above all to God. It is a **special sin** (with its own object) that also has a **general influence**
over all sins, as their common root. Pride is **mortal by genus**, because aversion from God belongs to
it essentially: "all other sins flee from God, but pride alone sets itself up against God" (Q162). It
is therefore **the most grievous of sins on the side of aversion**, and **the first of all sins** —
"the beginning of all sin is pride" (Ecclus. 10:13). Aquinas distinguishes pride as the **"queen and
mother"** standing *above* the seven capital vices from **vainglory**, its chief offspring, which is
itself a capital vice.

## The first sin: the angels and Adam (Q163–165)

The first sin of our first parents was **pride** (Q163): in the state of innocence it could not have
been a bodily concupiscence, but an inordinate reaching for a spiritual good — to be "as gods,
knowing good and evil" — desiring a **likeness to God** beyond their measure (knowledge for Adam,
power as the devil had tempted). **Eve** sinned more grievously in species (more puffed up, sinning
also against her neighbour by leading Adam astray). The temptation's order is read morally: the
**serpent** as sensuality, the **woman** as the lower reason, the **man** as the higher reason
consenting (Q165). This connects Aquinas's moral theology to [[original-sin]] and the figures of
[[adam|Adam]] and the [[satan|devil]].

## Cross-tradition note

The exaltation of humility and the diagnosis of pride as the primal sin is distinctively
Judaeo-Christian; it contrasts sharply with the magnanimity-centred ethics of [[aristotle|Aristotle]]
(whose "great-souled man" disdains humility) — a contrast Aquinas must explicitly negotiate — and
invites comparison with the analyses of ego, self, and pride in other traditions (e.g. the Sikh
[[haumai]], the Buddhist deconstruction of self in [[anatta]]).
