# Sapeksha-Dharma

*Sapekṣa-dharma* (Sanskrit: *sapekṣa* = "with respect/regard for"; *dharma* = duty/righteousness/cosmic order) — **positive pluralism** that acknowledges genuine differences between traditions and engages them with mutual respect and reciprocity. Developed by [[rajiv-malhotra]] in [[being-different-malhotra]] as the dharmic alternative to Western secularism, Western religious pluralism, and Hindu pseudo-secularism.

## The Problem It Addresses

Malhotra identifies three inadequate responses to religious plurality in the Indian context:

1. **Western secularism** (*nirapeksha* dharma — "without regard for [specific dharmas]"): treats all religions as equally valid by treating them all as equally irrelevant to public life; pretends to be neutral but actually imports a post-Enlightenment Western assumption (the separation of church and state as the ideal) that itself has Abrahamic roots. Malhotra argues that India's adoption of Western-style secularism was a form of [[cultural-digestion]].

2. **Indian pseudo-secularism**: In Indian political practice, "secularism" has meant differential treatment of religious communities — Hindu practices subject to state control; minority religious institutions (Muslim waqfs, Christian schools) exempt. Malhotra argues this is not genuine pluralism but a power arrangement that disadvantages the majority while claiming neutrality.

3. **Perennialist sameness** (the "all religions say the same thing" approach): eliminates the significance of differences by declaring them superficial; is itself a form of cultural digestion because it absorbs dharmic traditions into a Western or universalist meta-framework. See [[cultural-digestion]].

## Sapeksha vs. Nirapeksha

| | Sapeksha-dharma | Nirapeksha-dharma |
|---|---|---|
| Attitude toward difference | Acknowledges and respects genuine difference | Treats differences as irrelevant or superficial |
| Engagement mode | Active, reciprocal dialogue with knowledge of the other's position (*purva paksha*) | Avoidance, indifference, or false equivalence |
| Model | Positive pluralism with mutual accountability | Secular neutrality |
| Risk | Requires genuine engagement; can be uncomfortable | Comfortable but leads to intellectual cowardice |

## The Reciprocity Requirement

Sapeksha-dharma requires *reciprocity*: other traditions must be willing to engage dharmic traditions on their own terms, not only through the lens of "tolerance" (which presupposes a tolerating authority in a superior position). Malhotra argues that Western pluralism often practices tolerance without reciprocity — willing to study Hinduism through Western academic frameworks (psychoanalysis, feminist theory, colonial studies) but resistant to having Western Christianity or Western secular values analyzed through dharmic frameworks.

The purva paksha model applies here: sapeksha-dharma requires both parties to study the other in its own terms. Malhotra's book is itself intended as a model of this practice: a dharmic scholar performing a sustained purva paksha of Western civilization.

## Gandhi as Exemplar

Gandhi's approach to British colonialism is cited as a practical model: Gandhi studied British civilization thoroughly (Hind Swaraj engages its intellectual, political, and moral claims in detail), articulated its strengths and contradictions, and then proposed dharmic alternatives (*svaraj*, *swadeshi*, *satyagraha*, *ahimsa*) grounded in his own framework — not as negations of British values but as positive alternative visions.

## Relationship to Indian Political Philosophy

Sapeksha-dharma implicitly critiques the uniform civil code debates in India (which Malhotra connects to British-era imposition of "canonized smritis"), Hindutva's assimilationist nationalism ([[hindutva]]), and Nehruvian secularism equally. The ideal is a polity in which different communities (*jatis*, traditions, sampradayas) engage under common principles while maintaining their distinctive identities — analogous to the dharmic "open architecture" model of spiritual life.
