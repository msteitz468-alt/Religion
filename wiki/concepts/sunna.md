# Sunna

## Definition

*Sunna* (Arabic: سُنَّة, "custom," "habitual practice," "way") denotes the normative example of the Prophet Muhammad — his words, acts, tacit approvals, and personal characteristics — as the second source of Islamic law and theology alongside the Qur'an. The concept rests on the theological conviction that the Prophet's conduct embodies the practical application of Qur'anic guidance and constitutes a binding model for Muslims.

## Sunna and Hadith: the Content and Its Record

*Sunna* is the substance — the normative practice itself. *Hadith* (plural: *ahadith*) is the literary form in which sunna is preserved: a discrete report consisting of (1) an *isnad* (chain of transmitters) and (2) a *matn* (the text of what the Prophet said or did). The two are related but distinct: sunna is what is normatively authoritative; hadith is the evidentiary vehicle that conveys it. Al-Bukhari's collection ([[sahih-al-bukhari|Sahih al-Bukhari]]) is the pre-eminent Sunni repository of hadiths and therefore the pre-eminent documentary record of sunna.

## Sunna as Standard in Sahih al-Bukhari

The collection opens, immediately after the *niyyah* (intention) hadith, with reports establishing the Prophet's example as the measure for conduct in every domain. Key examples from Volume 1, Books 1–9:

- **Ritual purity (wudu, ghusl)**: The standard method for ablution is invariably established by reference to what the Prophet did: "I saw Allah's Apostle performing ablution in this way" (Vol. 1, Book 4, No. 165). Individual descriptions by Uthman, Ibn Abbas, Abdullah bin Zaid, and Maimuna all converge on the Prophet's own practice as the normative reference.
- **Prayer structure**: The five daily prayers were established by the Mi'raj narrative (Vol. 1, Book 8, No. 345), in which Allah directly enjoined them. The number of *raka'at*, the direction of prayer (*qibla*), the role of the Imam, and even optional night prayers (*nawafil*) are all defined by what the Prophet "used to do" or explicitly commanded.
- **The Tayammum dispensation**: "The earth has been made for me (and for my followers) a place for praying and a thing to perform Tayammum" (Vol. 1, Book 7, No. 331) — a specific privilege granted to the Prophet and, through his sunna, to his community.
- **Moral conduct**: The parameters of iman, the marks of hypocrisy, the ethics of knowledge transmission — all defined by the Prophet's statements preserved in hadith (Books 2–3).

## Sunna's Scope: Words, Acts, and Tacit Approvals

Al-Bukhari's collection shows all three sunna types in operation:
- **Statements (*qawl*)**: "Religion is very easy and whoever overburdens himself in his religion will not be able to continue in that way" (Vol. 1, Book 2, No. 38).
- **Acts (*fi'l*)**: descriptions of how the Prophet performed wudu, ghusl, prayed, slept, etc.
- **Tacit approvals (*taqrir*)**: The Prophet's silence or non-objection when companions acted a certain way in his presence (e.g., Ibn Abbas riding a she-ass in front of a prayer row with no objection, Vol. 1, Book 9, No. 76).

## The Obligation to Follow Sunna

Multiple hadiths in Book 3 (Knowledge) establish the epistemological stakes of sunna-transmission. The Prophet explicitly warned: "Whoever tells a lie against me (intentionally) then (surely) let him occupy his seat in Hell-fire" (Vol. 1, Book 3, Nos. 106–110) — a cluster of five near-identical hadiths emphasizing that fabricating sunna is a grave sin. Conversely, Abu Huraira explains that he narrated many hadiths because Qur'an 2:159–160 forbids concealing "clear signs and guidance" (Vol. 1, Book 3, No. 118).

The portent that "religious knowledge will be taken away by the death of religious learned men" (Nos. 80–81, 100) frames sunna-transmission as the mechanism by which divine guidance remains accessible across generations.
