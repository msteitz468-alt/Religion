# Angels (Thomistic Angelology)

[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s Treatise on the Angels ([[summa-theologica|*Summa Theologiae*]] I,
Q50–64) is the most systematic medieval angelology — so central to his thought that he is nicknamed
the *Doctor Angelicus*. Angels are treated as the purely **spiritual creatures**, occupying the
metaphysical space between God and corporeal creation. (Patristic, scriptural, and other traditions'
angelologies should be added as those sources are ingested; this page records Aquinas's account.)

## Nature: subsisting immaterial forms (Q50)

Angels are **wholly incorporeal**, subsisting forms without matter (Q50, A1–2). Aquinas rejects the
"spiritual matter" (universal hylomorphism) of **Avicebron** (Ibn Gabirol, *Fons Vitae*) by name.
The most famous thesis follows (Q50, A4): since angels are individuated by **nothing but their own
form**, **each angel is its own species** — "there cannot be two angels of one species," just as
there cannot be two separate whitenesses. Angels are naturally **incorruptible** (Q50, A5).

## Knowledge (Q54–58)

Angelic knowledge is intellectual, not discursive: angels know through **innate intelligible
species** infused at creation (Q55), not by abstraction from sense, and grasp conclusions in their
principles "at once," without reasoning (Q58, A3). They know singulars and material things (Q57) but
not the secrets of hearts or future contingents, which belong to God alone. From Augustine, Aquinas
adopts the distinction of **morning and evening knowledge** (Q58, A6–7): *cognitio matutina* is
knowing things as they are in the Word (God); *cognitio vespertina* is knowing them in their own
created natures.

## Will, grace, and beatitude (Q59–62)

Angels have **will** and **love** (Q59–60); they were created in grace (or with grace offered) and
the good angels were **confirmed in glory** by their first meritorious turning to God, the beatific
vision then rendering them unable to sin (Q62).

## The fall of the angels (Q63–64)

The **first sin of the devil was pride** (Q63, A2) — "not to be subject to a superior when subjection
is due." Specifically, the devil desired to attain final beatitude **by his own natural power**,
which "is proper to God alone" (Q63, A3; accord with Anselm, *De casu diaboli*); envy followed pride.
The fall was **not in the first instant** of creation: in the first instant all angels were good; in
the second, some turned to the Word (confirmed in good) while others turned to self, "swelling up
with pride," and fell (Q63, A5–6). The fallen angels are the **demons**, whose punishment Aquinas
treats in Q64.

## The angelic hierarchy and angelic action (Q106–108, Q110–114)

In the Treatise on the Divine Government (Q106–114), Aquinas adopts Pseudo-Dionysius's **nine orders
of angels** in **three hierarchies** (Q108): the first — Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones; the second —
Dominations, Virtues, Powers; the third — Principalities, Archangels, Angels. Higher angels
**illuminate** lower ones (a lower angel never illumines a higher), the Dionysian principle of mediated
communication mirroring how God governs by design immediately but by execution through intermediate
causes. Angels also act upon the corporeal world and upon human beings (by enlightening the intellect
and moving the imagination/passions, though never the will directly), serve as **guardian angels**
(Q113), while the **demons** tempt (Q114).
