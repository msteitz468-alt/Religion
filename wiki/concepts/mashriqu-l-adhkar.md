# Mashriqu'l-Adhkár (Bahá'í House of Worship)

*All content from Peter Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (Oneworld, 2000), lines 21703–21911.*

## Definition and Concept

The Arabic term *Mashriqu'l-Adhkár* means 'Dawning-place of the remembrances [or mention] of God'. It refers to the Bahá'í temple or 'House of Worship' and its projected surrounding complex of buildings. The term is also used for designated prayer centres and for dawn prayers (lines 21709–21719).

[[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]] defined the Mashriqu'l-Adhkár as any building erected for the praise of God. Such houses of worship should be built everywhere; they should be 'as perfect as possible', and not adorned with images. It is a blessing to visit them at dawn to listen to the verses of God 'in silence' (KA 29 k31, 61 k115) (lines 21724–21742).

[[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]] specified that the central house of worship should be linked to subsidiary buildings including a hospital, drug dispensary, travellers' hospice, school and university — philanthropic institutions open to those of all religions. The house of worship itself should be a focus for communal unity and prayer, with a wider impact on its location (lines 21741–21751).

[[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] stressed the complementarity between the Mashriqu'l-Adhkár and the *Ḥaẓíratu'l-Quds*, and the close interaction between prayer, social service, education and Bahá'í administration embodied in the ideal of the temple complex (lines 21752–21759).

## Architectural Features

All Bahá'í temples are nine-sided (reflecting the significance of the number 9), with a central auditorium. Some are surrounded by gardens and ornamental pools. They have been built on a continental basis, designated as 'mother temples' for their respective regions. Numerous temple sites have been acquired throughout the world for future houses of worship (lines 21778–21789).

## Historical Development

Meeting places were designated as Mashriqu'l-Adhkárs by Bahá'ís in Iran and Asiatic Russia at an early date. 'Abdu'l-Bahá stated that every village and hamlet should have a prayer centre, even if it had to be underground to avoid persecution (lines 21761–21767).

## Individual Temples

### Ashkhabad, Turkmenistan (1902–7)
The first and most complete Bahá'í temple complex built to date. The main structure was built between 1902 and 1907, with external decoration completed in 1919. Schools and other satellite institutions were built adjacent to it. The project was supervised by Ḥájí Mírzá [[muhammad-taqi-bahai|Muḥammad-Taqí]], the Afnán, who also provided much of the funding. The building design was by Ustád 'Alí-Akbar Bannán. The temple was confiscated by Soviet authorities in 1928, leased back to the Bahá'ís until 1938, then fully expropriated and turned into an art gallery. It suffered earthquake damage in 1948 and was demolished in 1963 (lines 21790–21814).

### Wilmette, Illinois, USA (1920–1953)
Inspired by the Ashkhabad temple, the Chicago Bahá'ís decided to build their own (1903), eventually choosing a site in the north shore suburb of Wilmette (1908). The Bahá'í Temple Unity, representative of all American Bahá'ís, was established in 1909 to collect funds and superintend the project. 'Abdu'l-Bahá dedicated the site on 1 May 1912. The design by Louis Bourgeois was chosen in 1920. Financial problems delayed completion of the superstructure until 1931, external ornamentation to 1943, and the interior to 1951 (total construction costs: over $2.6 million). The temple was finally dedicated for worship on 1 May 1953. By 1982 it had received nearly 5 million visitors; it was designated a national historic place by the American government in 1978. Seating capacity: 1,191. Dome ribs rise 164 feet above the auditorium floor. The first dependency, a home for the aged, was inaugurated in 1959 (lines 21815–21855).

### Kampala, Uganda (1961)
Designed by C.M. [[remey|Remey]]. Foundation stone laid 26 January 1958; dedicated 14 January 1961. Seating capacity: over 400 (lines 21856–21863).

### Sydney, Australia (1961)
Designed by C.M. Remey. Construction began 1957; dedicated 15 September 1961. Seating capacity: 500 (lines 21864–21870).

### Frankfurt, Germany (1964)
Designed by Teuro Recholl. Construction began 1960 (after a long string of legal delays, seemingly caused by church opposition). Dedicated 4 July 1964. Seating capacity: 450–500. A home for the aged has been built as the first dependency (lines 21871–21882).

### Panama City, Panama (1972)
Designed by Peter Tillotson. Foundation stone laid 8 October 1967; construction began 1969; dedicated 29 April 1972. Seating capacity: 550 (lines 21883–21891).

### Apia, Samoa (1984)
Designed by Husayn Amanat. Foundation stone laid 27 January 1979 by Malietoa Tanumafili II. Dedicated 1 September 1984. Total construction costs: $6.5m. Seating capacity: 500–700 (lines 21892–21898).

### New Delhi, India (1986)
Designed by Faríboun Sahba. Foundation stone laid 17 October 1977; dedicated 24 December 1986. Construction costs: approximately $10m. Normal seating capacity: 1,200. Its unusual design — appearing as a giant opening lotus flower — has attracted considerable public interest (lines 21900–21911).
