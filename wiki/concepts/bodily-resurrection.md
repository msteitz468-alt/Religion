# Bodily Resurrection

The doctrine that the dead will be restored to embodied existence at the end of time for judgment, reward, and punishment. In Islamic theology (*qiyāma*), this entails the literal return of souls to physical bodies — not a purely spiritual survival — and the literal existence of paradise (*janna*) and hell (*jahannam*) as corporeal environments. The concept is central to [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa*]], where it marks the third and gravest charge of unbelief (takfir) against the [[falsafa|Islamic philosophers]].

---

## The Philosophers' Position: Allegorization of the Afterlife

In Discussion 20 of the *Tahafut*, [[al-ghazali]] presents the [[falsafa|philosophers']] doctrine of the afterlife before refuting it. They hold that the soul endures everlastingly after death "either in a pleasure so great that it is beyond description or in a pain so great that it [also] is beyond description" (Discussion 20, §3). This pleasure and pain are **purely intellectual and spiritual**: "The nourishment and enjoyment of the rational faculty consists in apprehending the intelligibles" (§4).

What prevents souls from this enjoyment in life is the body and its preoccupations. At death, the purified soul — freed from bodily distraction — experiences an explosion of intellectual bliss analogous to a convalescent suddenly restored to full appetite. The philosophers argue that intellectual pleasures are nobler than bodily pleasures on two grounds: (1) angels have no sensual pleasures, only the pleasure of self-awareness and knowledge of God, yet are nobler than beasts; (2) humans routinely sacrifice bodily pleasures for intellectual or honorific ones (Discussion 20, §§8–9).

Crucially, the philosophers **allegorize the Quranic descriptions of paradise**: "Regarding what has appeared in the religious law in terms of [sensible] representation, the intention [here] is to give analogies because of the failure [of most people] to understand these [intellectual] pleasures. They have thus been given examples in terms of what they understand" (§16). The houris, the gardens, the corporeal fire — all are *parables for the masses* (the *bāṭin* reading), not literal descriptions. The prophets, on this view, used sensory imagery to motivate the common people toward virtue, while the philosophical elite understand that only spiritual reward and punishment are real.

### The Zahir/Batin Axis

This is a direct application of the **zahir (exoteric) / batin (esoteric)** hermeneutical axis. The philosophers treat the Quranic eschatological texts as possessing an exoteric surface sense (*zahir*) appropriate for the multitude and an esoteric philosophical meaning (*bāṭin*) that reduces bodily resurrection to allegory. The philosophers compare this move to the standard practice of allegorizing the anthropomorphic Quranic verses (God's "hand," "face," "settling on the throne") — both, they argue, are parables proportionate to the understanding of created humanity (§§20–21).

---

## Ghazali's Rebuttal: The Zahir is Obligatory Here

Ghazali sharply distinguishes the two cases. He concedes that anthropomorphic utterances about God are legitimately interpreted metaphorically, because "rational proofs have shown the impossibility of [attributing] place, direction, visage, physical hand, physical eye, the possibility of transfer, and rest to God" (§25). But bodily resurrection and corporeal paradise are **not** impossible in terms of divine power; therefore, the *zahir* — the literal meaning — is obligatory:

> "What He has promised in the hereafter, however, is not impossible in terms of the power of God, exalted be He. Hence, one must follow the apparent [literal meaning of the revealed] speech—indeed, according to its signification, which is explicit." (Discussion 20, §25)

Ghazali further argues that what the Qur'an says about paradise and the fire "has attained a degree [of explicit statement] that does not [render it] subject to metaphorical interpretation" (§24). To treat it as allegory would amount to "obfuscation by making one imagine what is contrary to truth for the benefit of creatures. But this is what the position of prophethood is sanctified high above" (§24) — i.e., reducing prophetic speech to noble lies for the masses impugns the prophets themselves.

## The Philosophers' Arguments Against Bodily Resurrection

Discussion 20 presents two main philosophical objections:

**First objection (§§27–37): the three alternatives are all impossible.** The philosophers argue that resurrection must take one of three forms — (a) re-creation of an annihilated body plus its life-accident; (b) soul returned to the exact same material body; (c) soul returned to any body. They argue: (a) is not a *return* but a creation of a replica; (b) is physically impossible because the body decomposes, is eaten by worms, its matter recycled into other organisms and mixed with the earth; (c) amounts to metempsychosis (*tanāsukh*), which the philosophers themselves refute, and in any case the matter within the lunar sphere is finite while separating souls are infinite.

**Second objection (§§44–54): the fixed divine course (*sunnat Allāh*).** The divine will is eternal and does not change its course. "You will not find any change in God's way" (Qur'an 48:23). A bodily resurrection at the end of time would require God's way to radically alter — inconsistent with the eternal, fixed divine act proceeding from the eternal will.

---

## Ghazali's Defence of Bodily Resurrection

Against the three-alternatives argument (§§38–43): Ghazali endorses the third alternative — soul survives, returned to *any* body — and denies it constitutes forbidden transmigration. "The religious law has conveyed [as true] must be believed. Let it be 'transmigration.' We only deny transmigration in this world" (§41). The identity of the resurrected person consists in the soul, not the matter: "For [an individual] is what he is by virtue of his soul, not his body, since the parts of the body change over for him from childhood to old age" (§39). The philosophers' infinity-of-souls objection presupposes the world's pre-eternity — which Ghazali has already refuted — and in any case God "is able to create and resume the creation ex nihilo" (§40).

Against the fixed divine course (§§55–56): Ghazali repeats his earlier refutation of the world's eternity. It is not rationally improbable to posit three distinct temporal phases — God without the world, God with the world as we experience it, and God inaugurating the new order of paradise — "and then begins a new order which is the one promised in paradise" (§56). The habitual course of nature (*cadat Allāh*) can be disrupted; Ghazali points to prophetic miracles, magnetic attraction, and the wonders of embryology as cases where causes unknown to us produce effects that would otherwise seem impossible (§48).

Ghazali also insists that both bodily and spiritual happiness are promised and compatible: "What is contrary to the religious law among [the things they hold] is the denial of the resurrection of bodies, the denial of bodily pleasures in paradise and bodily torments in the fire, and the denial of the existence of paradise and the fire as described in the Qur'an. What, then, is there to stand in the way of realizing the combination of both [kinds] of happiness, the spiritual and bodily?" (§19). Combining them is *more perfect*, not contradictory.

## The Third Takfir: Denial of Bodily Resurrection as Unbelief

The Conclusion of the *Tahafut* enumerates three doctrines on which Ghazali pronounces the philosophers unbelievers (*kuffār*):

> "Pronouncing them infidels is necessary in three questions. One of them is the question of the world's pre-eternity and their statement that all substances are pre-eternal. The second is their statement that God's knowledge does not encompass the temporal particulars among individual [existents]. **The third is their denial of the resurrection of bodies and their assembly at the day of judgment.**" (*The Book's Conclusion*, §2)

The denial of bodily resurrection is singled out as the most unambiguous violation: "These three doctrines do not agree with Islam in any respect. The one who believes them believes that prophets utter falsehoods and that they said whatever they have said by way of [promoting common] utility, to give examples and explanation to the multitudes of created mankind. This is manifest infidelity which none of the Islamic sects have believed" (Conclusion, §3). The charge is especially sharp here because allegorizing the resurrection texts implies that prophetic speech is a pious deception — a noble lie for the masses — which Ghazali regards as the deepest form of the philosophers' impiety.

## Aquinas on the Resurrection and the glorified body (Summa Theologiae, Supplement)

The Christian comparandum is treated at length by [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] in the
[[summa-theologica|*ST*]] Supplement (XP Q75–Q96), where — unlike the eschatology al-Ghazali defends
against the philosophers — the resurrection is integrated into a full metaphysics of the
[[atonement|risen Christ]] as cause.

- **The resurrection itself** (XP Q75–Q80): universal (all the dead rise), miraculous (beyond every
  created natural power), and caused by **Christ's resurrection** as its quasi-instrumental efficient
  and exemplar cause. Against **metempsychosis** and Gnostic denial, the **same body** rises —
  identity guaranteed by the rational soul, which is the body's form; the risen body has integrity
  (all its members). The time is hidden (Joachim of Fiore's datings dismissed).
- **The four dowries (*dotes*) of the glorified body** (XP Q82–Q85), the gifts overflowing from the
  beatified soul (cf. Christ's body at the Transfiguration): **impassibility** (incapacity for
  suffering or corruption), **subtlety** (*subtilitas* — the body's full subjection to the spirit,
  as when the risen Christ passed through closed doors), **agility** (instantaneous movement at the
  soul's command), and **clarity** (*claritas* — the radiant beauty of the blessed body). The bodies
  of the **damned** also rise, but passible and inglorious. All rise at the perfect age (~33) and
  proper stature.
- **The General Judgment** (XP Q88–Q90): beyond the particular judgment each soul receives at death,
  there will be a **general judgment** at the end — because a person's life has effects that continue
  after death (in reputation, descendants, the works left behind, the body's condition), and these
  must finally be assessed; and because Christ, who was judged, must be seen to judge in His humanity
  by all, good and wicked. The world itself is **renewed** (XP Q91), the heavenly motions ceasing.
- The **[[beatific-vision]]**, the mansions of the blessed, the question whether the blessed see and
  rejoice in the punishment of the damned, and the **aureoles** (the special crowns of martyrs,
  virgins, and doctors) close the work (XP Q92–Q96). The fate of the damned is treated under
  [[hell]]; that of unbaptized infants under [[limbo]].

## Early Christian Models of the Resurrection Body ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) distinguishes
three early Christian understandings of the resurrected body of [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]]:

1. **Spiritual body** ([[paul-apostle|Paul]], 1 Cor 15) — a *transformed* body, raised
   imperishable, made of the "highly refined" stuff that ancient writers called spirit (not
   immaterial); "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God." Paul argues *against*
   Corinthians who thought believers were already spiritually raised.
2. **Spirit only / body abandoned** (Gnostic) — the divine spirit was never killed but escaped
   the flesh; no bodily resurrection (the *Coptic Apocalypse of Peter*: the "living Jesus"
   laughs above the cross while only the "clay vessel" is crucified). See [[docetism]] and
   [[gnostics]].
3. **Reanimated mortal body** (Luke/John) — Jesus has "flesh and bones," eats fish (Luke 24),
   invites Thomas to touch his wounds (John 20) — emphasizing *physical continuity* to counter
   spirit-only views. This became the dominant later view.

Ehrman further argues it was **[[resurrection-appearances-visions|visions]], not the empty
tomb**, that first produced resurrection belief, and that the resurrection was understood as
Jesus's **[[exaltation-christology|exaltation]]** to heaven.

## Cross-Tradition Note

The doctrine of bodily resurrection is shared across the Abrahamic traditions, though with significant variations. In Islam it is tied to the literal promises of the Qur'an regarding paradise (*janna*) and hell (*jahannam*). In Christianity, Paul's discussion in 1 Corinthians 15 of a "spiritual body" (*soma pneumatikon*) and the patristic debates over resurrection matter are comparanda. In rabbinic Judaism, *tehiyyat ha-metim* (resurrection of the dead) is among the thirteen principles of Maimonides and a subject of extensive talmudic and midrashic discussion. The philosophical tension Ghazali identifies — between allegorizing eschatology to serve elite intellectuals and insisting on literal bodily promises to honour the zahir of scripture — recurs across all three traditions. ## Averroes's Reply (Tahāfut al-Tahāfut, Natural Sciences Disc. 4)

This is the **third and climactic *[[takfir]]* charge**. **[[averroes|Averroes]]**'s reply is a
delicate balancing act:

- **Resurrection is religiously and politically necessary.** Belief in an afterlife motivates the
  virtue without which human society and happiness fail; revealed laws are "necessary political
  arts." To deny resurrection, having power to destroy religious belief, is heresy that "both
  theologians and philosophers will no doubt kill." See [[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy]].
- **But: simulacra, not numerically identical bodies.** "What arises from the dead is *simulacra* of
  these earthly bodies, not these bodies themselves, for that which has perished does not return
  individually" — the resurrected body is the same *specifically*, not *numerically*. This is sharper
  against the Ashʿarite atomists, on whose own principle (accidents not lasting two moments) the
  perished body cannot be numerically the same either.
- **Material images suit the masses.** Sensory depictions of paradise are "more appropriate" for
  motivating the many (Ibn ʿAbbās: "There is no relation in the other world to this world but the
  names").
- **Ghazālī contradicts himself.** Averroes notes Ghazālī claims in the *Tahāfut* that no Muslim
  holds a purely spiritual resurrection, yet elsewhere credits the Sufis with exactly that — "this
  man erred in religious questions as he erred in rational problems."

Averroes also gives a **historical note**: bodily resurrection "was first mentioned by the prophets
of Israel after Moses" (the Psalms), is affirmed in the New Testament, and is a Sabaean doctrine; it
is not found in "the oldest philosophers." Compare the controversy [[resurrection-body]].
