*Halacha* (Hebrew: הֲלָכָה, "the way of walking") is the body of Jewish legal material: rulings, case law, halakhic disputes, derived regulations, and normative practice. It constitutes one of the two primary content categories of the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] (the other being [[aggadah|Aggadah]]). In broader usage, *halacha* refers to Jewish law as a living system governing all domains of life.

## Derivation and Method

Halacha is derived from the Written Torah through formal hermeneutical rules ([[thirteen-middot|the Thirteen Middot of Rabbi Ishmael]]) and from the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] through transmitted tradition. The categories of Halachic material include:

- **De-oraita**: Laws explicitly derived from or clearly implied by the Written Torah — the highest authority level
- **De-rabbanan**: Laws enacted by the rabbis (takanot and gezerot) as fences around the Torah or to address situations not covered by Scripture
- **Minhag**: Custom that has attained the force of law in a given community

## Halacha and Aggadah

The distinction between Halacha and [[aggadah|Aggadah]] is fundamental to Talmudic literature but not always clean:

- **Halacha** is concerned with *what one must do*: legal rulings, norms of practice, adjudication of disputes
- **Aggadah** is concerned with *why and how to live*: narrative, theology, ethics, parable

The Talmud employs both. Aggadic narratives are sometimes used to derive Halachic rulings; Halachic discussions sometimes end with ethical or theological maxims. The boundary is functional, not absolute.

Whether Aggadah carries normative legal weight has been debated since antiquity. The classical position: Aggadah is not legally binding (*ein madinim me-haggadah*) — one may not derive binding rulings from homiletical or narrative material. In practice this principle is often violated by later authorities.

## Hermeneutical Character

Halacha operates by an **inductive, case-based method**: specific cases are presented and rulings derived; general principles are rarely stated explicitly but must be inferred. This contrasts with Roman law's deductive, principle-first approach. The [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] records both majority rulings and minority dissents, preserving rejected opinions for potential future use.

The Talmud's phrase "these and these are the words of the living God" (attributed to a heavenly voice settling the dispute between Beth Hillel and Beth Shammai, *Eruvin* 13b) acknowledges that multiple valid Halachic positions can exist simultaneously — only one is practically binding, but the others remain within the living tradition.

## Codification History

The desire to systematize Halacha into accessible codes drove a recurring debate between **accessibility** and **open study**:

- **Isaac Alfasi** (*Rif*, 1013–1103): *Halakhot* — extracted legal decisions from the Talmud, omitting theoretical debate
- **[[maimonides|Maimonides]]** (1135–1204): *Mishna Torah* — systematic codification of all Talmudic law in clear Hebrew, without sources or citations
- **Jacob ben Asher** (*Tur*, 1280–1340): *Arba'ah Turim* — organized by the four domains that structure [[joseph-caro|Caro's]] later code
- **[[joseph-caro|Joseph Caro]]** (1488–1575): *Shulchan Aruch* — the most authoritative practical guide, still used today

Each codifier was criticized for potentially displacing Talmud study. The tension remains live: the *Shulchan Aruch* is authoritative for practical questions; the Talmud remains the fundamental object of study.
