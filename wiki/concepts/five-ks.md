The *panj kakar* (Five Ks) are the five external insignia that every initiated member of the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] is required to wear at all times. They take their name from the fact that each item begins with the letter "K" in Punjabi: *kes* (uncut hair), *kangha* (comb), *kara* (steel wrist-ring), *kachh* (breeches), and *kirpan* (sword). Per [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]], among the obligations explained to initiants at [[amrit-sanskar|amrit sanskar]] is "a promise always to keep the Five Ks" (McLeod ~L363).

> **Note:** McLeod's individual enumeration and explanation of the five items falls in an earlier section of the source (before the "Worship, Ritual and Distinctive Customs" chapter). The account below draws on the references within this slice; further detail on each item's symbolic meaning is available in that prior treatment.

## Role in Rituals

The Five Ks appear across Sikh ritual life in this slice:

- **Amrit initiation**: both officiants (the Panj Piare) and those to be initiated must wear the Five Ks before the ceremony begins (~L297–299, L309–311).
- **Death rite**: "The corpse should be bathed and clad in clean garments, complete with all the Five Ks" before being laid on the bier (~L519).
- **Betrothal**: the girl's family presents the prospective husband with a *kirpan* and a *kara* (steel wrist-ring) at the betrothal ceremony, foregrounding two of the Five Ks as ceremonial gifts (~L429–431).

## The *Kirpan* in Liturgical Use

The *kirpan* (sword) appears in several ritual contexts beyond personal wear:
- The *karah prasad* is "touched with the tip of a *kirpan* to signify that it has been duly sanctified" before distribution (~L206–207).
- In the Khalsa birth ceremony, water stirred with a *kirpan* while reciting *Japji* is administered to the newborn (~L246–249).
- In the amrit ceremony, the officiants stir the *amrit* "with a two-edged sword (*khanda*)" (~L333–334).

## The *Kara* (Steel Wrist-ring)

The *kara* appears independently in the betrothal ceremony (gift to the groom, ~L430–431) and is implicitly referenced in the "Khalsa symbol of iron" whose relationship to *karah prasad* "is firmly maintained" (~L195–196).

## Obligation and Apostasy

Cutting one's hair is the first of the four cardinal prohibitions (*kurahit*) whose violation makes a Sikh an apostate (*patit*); any Kes-dhari Sikh who "trims or shaves his/her hair" is similarly branded patit (~L669–675). The Five Ks thus define the boundary between initiated and apostate Khalsa identity.
