# Kalam (Islamic Dialectical Theology)

*Ilm al-kalam* ("the science of discourse") is Islamic **dialectical / rational theology** —
the defence and systematization of doctrine by argument, practised by the *mutakallimun*
(theologians). Its two great schools are the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] and the
[[asharism|Ash'ariyya]].

## In the Tahafut (al-Ghazali)

[[al-ghazali|Al-Ghazali]]'s *[[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|Tahafut al-Falasifa]]* is a work
of Ash'arite kalam, but a methodologically unusual one: rather than arguing from kalam premises,
Ghazali wields the [[falsafa|philosophers']] own demonstrative logic (*burhan*) against them. He
treats logic as a neutral instrument and aims to show the *falasifa*' metaphysics is not
demonstratively proven. Kalam's classic concerns — God's [[divine-attributes-in-islam|attributes]],
unity (*tawhid*), will, and the createdness of the world — structure the twenty Discussions.

## Maimonides' Critique (Guide for the Perplexed I:71–76)

[[maimonides|Maimonides]], writing from within [[rabbinic-judaism|rabbinic Judaism]] but as an
Aristotelian, gives the most systematic Jewish critique of the Mutakallimun in
[[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide* I:71–76]]. He traces kalam historically to the apologetics of
"the learned Greek and Syrian Christians," who "laid the foundation for this science of
Dogmatics" to defend Church doctrine against the philosophers, whence the Muslims took it over;
the Jewish Geonim and [[karaism|Karaites]] then followed the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] because
they met that school first, while the [[asharism|Ash'ariyya]] left no mark on Jewish authors
(I:71).

His core methodological charge is that the Mutakallimun invert the order of inquiry: they "first
considered what must be the properties of the things which should yield proof for or against a
certain creed; and when this was found they asserted that the thing must be endowed with those
properties" — against Themistius' maxim that "our opinions must be adapted to the existing
properties" of things (I:71). He then enumerates and refutes the **twelve propositions** of
their atomism (indivisible atoms; a vacuum; time-atoms; substance never without accidents; the
non-endurance of accidents and God's constant recreation of them; "**admissibility**" — that
whatever can be imagined is possible — which he calls the load-bearing premise of the whole
system, confusing imagination with intellect), and their seven proofs of creation, five of
unity, and three of incorporeality (I:73–76). His verdict: they "denied the nature of the
existing things … and have weakened the arguments for the existence, the unity, and the
incorporeality of God" (I:76). Notably, Maimonides rejects the kalam claim to have *demonstrated*
[[creation-ex-nihilo|creatio ex nihilo]] as firmly as he rejects Aristotle's claim to have
demonstrated [[eternity-of-the-world|eternity]] — see [[divine-attributes]], [[negative-theology]].
