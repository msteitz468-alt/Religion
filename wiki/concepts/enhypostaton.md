# Enhypostaton and Insubsistence

The Greek term **enhypostaton** (ἐνυπόστατον) and the related **insubsistence Christology** are the conceptual tools sixth–eighth-century Chalcedonians devised to explain how Christ's human nature can be fully real yet have no hypostasis of its own — existing instead "in" the hypostasis of the Logos. In Johannes Zachhuber's analysis ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]]), the word carried **three distinct meanings** that only [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] finally fused, and the history of that fusion is central to the post-Chalcedonian transformation of the [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|classical theory]]. The notion gained modern prominence through Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics*.

## The Problem It Solves

Chalcedon confessed "two natures in one hypostasis." But the miaphysite **NNWH-principle** ("no nature without a hypostasis" — an inference of the classical theory's concrete dimension) implied two natures → two hypostases → either contradiction or Nestorianism. Chalcedonians needed to show that a complete human nature could be individuated and real without constituting a second hypostasis. See [[hypostatic-union|Hypostatic Union]] and [[chalcedonian-definition|the Chalcedonian Definition]].

## Three Meanings (Zachhuber)

1. **Traditional sense — "hypostatically existing."** In the fourth century *enhypostatos* was simply the opposite of *anhypostatos* (non-existent): it affirmed that the divine ousia truly exists in (three) hypostases. [[john-the-grammarian-of-caesarea|John the Grammarian of Caesarea]] still uses the word this way — *ἕνωσις ἐνυπόστατος* = *ἕνωσις καθ' ὑπόστασιν* = hypostatic union.

2. **Insubsistence Christology** (John the Grammarian, then [[leontius-of-jerusalem|Leontius of Jerusalem]]). A distinct *theory* — not, in John the Grammarian, attached to the word *enhypostatos*: Christ's humanity "subsisted not in another but in himself... has its individual existence only in the divine Logos." The humanity is individuated by its assumption into the Logos (its "only individual property"), treated, on the model of Aristotle's *Categories*, much like an accident inhering in a subject. Leontius of Jerusalem made this an "unequivocal" and elaborate doctrine.

3. **Leontius of Byzantium's technical *enhypostaton*.** In *CNE* 1, [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]] gives the word a new sense: not hypostatic existence, but the **individuated universal** — the ousia as embodied in the individual, distinct from both the universal as such and the hypostasis (pure existential realization), and distinct from an accident. "Hypostasis... and the enhypostaton are not the same thing, just as ousia and enousion are different."

## The Damascene Synthesis

"It is only John of Damascus who will bring these two traditions together." Following U. M. Lang, Zachhuber names John "the originator" of the *classical* enhypostaton doctrine: he reinterprets Leontius of Byzantium's term to mean "existing **in** [a hypostasis]," giving the formula that Christ's human nature is *enhypostaton* in the Logos. John lists two senses of the term (*Dial.* 45): that which "does not subsist on its own but is only seen in the hypostases" (the species in its individuals), and that which "is joined together with something else... to perfect one composite hypostasis" (soul and body). For the Trinity, John's symmetric pair *enousion / enhypostaton* expresses [[trinity|perichoresis]].

## Scholarly Disputes

- Uthemann (1997) equated John the Grammarian's *enhypostatos* with Leontius of Byzantium's technical notion; Zachhuber follows Gleede (2012) against this, keeping the three senses distinct.
- Whether the *enhypostaton* or the accident is the thing "having its being in another" in Leontius's key sentence is grammatically ambiguous and much debated; Zachhuber argues the productive reading starts from *enhypostaton* = the (individuated) ousia.
