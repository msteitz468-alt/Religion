# Theophany (*Theophania* — Divine Self-Manifestation)

**Theophany** (from Greek *theos*, God + *phainesthai*, to appear/manifest) means literally "appearance of God" or "divine self-showing." In the Dionysian context as interpreted by Eric D. Perl (*Theophany*, 2007), it names the metaphysical claim that **all beings are God's self-manifestation**: the entire created order is the transcendent God appearing or showing himself, without God ceasing to be transcendent. Being is theophany.

This is distinct from:
- **Theophanies in the OT sense** (visible divine appearances: the burning bush, the pillar of fire, the *kavod* filling the tabernacle) — particular, historical divine manifestations
- **Emanation** in the Plotinian sense — where the hierarchy of being flows from the One by necessity, not by gift or self-expression

## Theophany in Pseudo-Dionysius

[[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite]] does not use *theophany* as a technical term with the systematic weight Perl gives it, but the concept organizes the whole of the Dionysian system as Perl reads it. Key texts:

- **[[on-divine-names|Divine Names]] I**: God transcends all being and thought (*hyperousios* — beyond being), yet is immanent in all things as their cause and as the "processions" (*proodoi*) by which he gives himself to all
- **[[celestial-hierarchy|Celestial Hierarchy]] I–II**: the material world as a system of symbols, each of which manifests (*phainei*) the divine in a degree appropriate to its level of being

The governing paradox: God is **totally transcendent** (the One beyond being, beyond the Good, beyond all names) and **totally immanent** (present in all things as the cause of their being, which is his self-manifestation). Theophany is Perl's name for the resolution: God *is* present in all things *as* their being, without becoming them or being limited to them.

## Perl's Philosophical Analysis

Perl distinguishes Dionysian theophany from Plotinian emanation on the question of **participation and self-expression**:

- In Plotinus, the One overflows into Intellect, Soul, and Matter by a kind of necessary overflow (*ekphrasis*); lower levels are diminished reflections of the One's power
- In Dionysius (as Perl reads him), the processions are God's **self-gift**: "all being is theophany, the manifestation of God himself in and as the things that are." Being is not a diminished emanation but God's own self-showing at various degrees of intensity

This reading allows Perl to argue that several features often attributed to Dionysius's "Christian transformation" of Neoplatonism (God as immediate creator of all things; God as ecstatic love going out of himself; the equality of dignity among all participants in being) are in fact already present in non-Christian Neoplatonism, properly understood.

### Ch. 1–2: The Transcendence/Immanence Synthesis

Perl's Chapters 1 and 2 supply the philosophical spine of the theophany doctrine. Chapter 1 establishes that God's transcendence is not a devotional posture but a philosophical necessity. The foundational principle of the entire Neoplatonic tradition is that "to be is to be intelligible" (line 265), and this identification of being with intelligibility entails that being is necessarily finite and determinate. Since every being is what it is by virtue of a limiting determination distinguishing it from other beings, no being can be the first principle; the first principle cannot be a being. "Beyond being" is not a vague honorific but the precise metaphysical consequence: the One or God is "simply not anything, not 'there' at all" (lines 621–622), and thought, which is always the apprehension of some being, cannot attain God. Dionysius states this directly: "if all knowledges are of beings and have their limit in beings, that which is beyond all being also transcends all knowledge" (DN I.4, 593A, lines 606–608). Apophasis — even the negations — must be transcended, because even saying "God is not such-and-such" still treats God as a conceptual object. The conclusion is the silence of the mind, arising in "the cessation of every intellectual activity [*pases noeras energeias apopasin*]" (DN I.5, 593C, line 671).

Chapter 2 shows that this same radical transcendence is precisely what makes God's presence in all things possible. The argument proceeds through the Neoplatonic doctrine of participation: a Platonic form is both transcendent to and immanent in its instances. The form is transcendent in that, as one universal nature, it is not identified with or confined to any of its instances; it is immanent in that it is wholly present in each instance as the nature by which the instance is what it is. The form's transcendence is a strict implication of its immanence. Because the One is the universal determination of which all being is the differentiated unfolding, its not-being-any-one-thing is exactly what makes it present in every thing: it is "everywhere and nowhere" as the constitutive determination of all. Perl summarizes the consequence using the *complicatio-explicatio* structure: "the cause is the enfolding or *complicatio* of the effects, and the effects are the unfolding or *explicatio* of the cause" (lines 1071–1072). God is not merely "in all things" alongside their own content; the entire intelligible content of each thing is God's constitutive presence in it.

Dionysius expresses this in what Perl treats as the governing formula of the whole metaphysics: "the being of all things is the divinity beyond being [*to gar einai panton estin he huper to einai theotes*]" (CH I.4, 177D, lines 1282–1283). God is not a being but is the being that all beings have — beyond being in that he is not one of them, immanent in that their being is nothing but his self-giving presence. The resulting position is neither theism (which regards God as a being, however exalted) nor atheism (which denies the existence of such a being) nor monism (which identifies beings with God) nor dualism (which places God alongside the world as another being). Perl names it "theophanism" (line 1485): the doctrine that reality is the appearance of God, where appearance means genuine self-manifestation, not illusion. The key Dionysian summary: God is "all things in all things and nothing in any [*en pasi panta esti kai en oudeni ouden*]" (DN VII.3, 872A, lines 1359–1360) — all things in that whatever is found in any being is God present in it, nothing in any in that God is not any distinct being within the totality.

## Relation to Apophatic Theology

Theophany and [[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]] are the two sides of the same coin in Perl's reading:
- Apophasis: God transcends all names, all being, all thought — the via negativa tracks God's infinite excess over every conceptual capture
- Theophany: precisely because God is infinitely beyond all things, his presence in/as all things does not compromise his transcendence — the immanence is *of* the transcendent

The two together constitute what Dionysius calls the **symbolic theology**: things manifest God not by resembling him but by participating in his self-giving.

## Theophany Extended: Hierarchy, Symbolism, and Cognition (Perl Chapters 5–7)

The concept of theophany ramifies across Perl's later chapters in three directions: hierarchy as the *structure* of theophany, cognition as the *reception* of theophany, and symbolism as theophany's *expression* in the sensible order.

**Hierarchy as theophany's structure (Ch. 5).** Because all being is God's self-manifestation, the ordered levels of reality are degrees of divine presence, not diminishments of it. The endnotes to Chapter 5 make clear that Perl reads hierarchy not as a chain of command but as providential service: "the higher any being is in the order of reality, the more it is in the service of … all that is below it" [*Theophany* Ch. 5 n. 26; range line 5493–5496]. Each level of the hierarchy transmits, rather than filters out, the divine self-gift. Crucially, the activity of the creature is the activity of God-in-it: the sacramental principle — "*in persona Christi*" — is "extended to all reality, which, as theophany, is sacramental in nature" [lines 5523–5527]. Perl quotes Rutledge: "If we say that God creates each member of the hierarchy immediately, then … we must add immediately that each member is God, at exactly this level of manifestation or creation" [lines 5502–5505]. This is the metaphysical meaning of theophany: not that the world is *like* God, but that each thing *is* God at its level of self-showing.

**Cognition as reception of theophany (Ch. 6).** All modes of knowing — from bodily sensation to mystical *agnosia* — are modes of knowing God, because every object of knowledge is a theophany. Perl draws on Semmelroth: "a communion of knowledge is … a kind of communion of being" [line 5608–5609]. Jones states the conclusion explicitly: "For Pseudo-Dionysius, in all knowledge we always see and know the divinity, for the divinity is all in all" [lines 5629–5630]. Balthasar adds: "Things are not simply the occasion for his seeing God; rather, he sees God *in* things. Colours, shapes, essences and properties are for him immediate theophanies" [lines 5631–5634]. The highest cognition — Dionysian *agnosia*, the darkness of mystical union — is where theophany and the knower coincide: "in the absolute unification of cognition we do not merely see but become the darkness beyond being" [lines 5707–5709].

**Symbolism as sensible theophany (Ch. 7).** Material symbols in liturgy and scripture are not arbitrary human devices but grounded in the metaphysics of theophany: creation is God's self-veiling that is simultaneously self-revealing. Perl follows Semmelroth's "strong realism of symbols": "Between sign and signified in the symbol obtains not only a connection of knowledge, but also a connection of presence … the symbol is realistically bound with the symbolized" [lines 5702–5705]. The key verb *proballein* ("to project") captures this: creation is simultaneously "the veiling and the unveiling or unfolding of God through creation" (O'Rourke) [lines 5712–5714]. On this account, every material thing is a symbol — a mode of divine self-projection — and Dionysius's use of sensible images in the hierarchies is not a concession to human weakness but the recognition that the sensible order is *already* theophanic. Perl explicitly notes that Chapter 7 "overturns the interpretation of Dionysius as a despiser of the sensible" [lines 5743–5744].

### Body Text Details (Chapters 5–7)

*Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], body text, lines 2753–4566.*

**Chapter 5 — How the hierarchy argument works in the body text.** Perl's body text develops the hierarchy argument through DN V.1–3. The divine names Good, Being, Life, and Wisdom name God's constitutive presence in beings ranked by the universality of their participation — the Good reaching even matter as pure receptivity, Wisdom reaching only cognitive beings. The philosophical key is that these are not separate providential distributions but specifications of one another: Life is Being at a higher degree of intensity, consciousness is Life at a higher degree. "Life, in plants, therefore, is not superadded to Being, but is the more specific, intense mode of Being proper to them as compared to stones; Wisdom, i.e. consciousness, is not superadded to Life, but is the more specific, intense mode of Life and Being proper to cognitive things" [lines 2974–2977]. God is present "according to its rank (*kat' axian*)" to each being (DN VIII.7, 893D–896C [lines 3007–3008]), by illuminations "analogous to each of beings" (DN I.2, 588CD [lines 2998–3001]). The hierarchy does not mediate between God and lower beings by interposing intermediate steps; it is the structure of his immediate presence in all. Perl's formula: "The central principle of Dionysian hierarchy, then, is immediate mediation: it is by the hierarchical mediation of beings that God is immediately constitutively present to all" [lines 3391–3393]. The most striking consequence for the theophany doctrine: "In Dionysius' metaphysics, then, there is no such thing as an individual, a being conceived as a closed, self-contained unit… Each thing, indeed, is nothing but its relations to others, its place within the structure of immediate mediation. Hierarchy is thus, as its name signifies, the principle of sacredness, which is to say of being" [lines 3435–3441]. Being hierarchically ordered is not an imposition on theophany but its articulated form.

**Chapter 6 — How the continuum of cognition argument works in the body text.** Perl argues that the Platonic tradition never maintained a sharp dualism between sense and intellect as faculties aimed at different objects. Instead, "sense and intellect, with discursive reason as a mean between them, constitute a continuum of modes of cognition, articulated by the degrees of unity in which they apprehend reality" [lines 3472–3475]. Plotinus: "these sense-perceptions here are dim intellections, but the intellections there are clear sense-perceptions" (VI.7.7.29–31 [lines 3743–3744]). Dionysius: even "sense-perceptions themselves are an echo of Wisdom" (DN VII.2, 868C [lines 3780–3781]). Because all cognition grasps being and being is theophany, God is present to every mode of knowing — yet since God is not any being, he exceeds every mode equally. Dionysius in DN VII.3, 872A names God as the object of "intellection and reason and knowledge and touching and sense-perception and opinion and imagination," while also saying he is "neither thought nor spoken nor named" [lines 3882–3889]. Perl: "'Of God there is sense-perception': this stunning but wholly consistent affirmation cannot be overemphasized. It overturns all conventional misrepresentations of Neoplatonism in general, and Dionysius' thought in particular, as a 'gnostic' repudiation of the senses and the sensible world" [lines 3896–3899]. The continuum culminates in the union above intellect, which is the continuous completion of the cognitive ascent, not a rupture: "the mystical unknowing of God as no thing is the inseparable completion of the knowledge of God, by every mode of cognition, as all things in all things" [lines 4048–4050].

**Chapter 7 — How the symbolism argument is constructed in the body text.** Perl's body text for Chapter 7 collapses the distinction between "intelligible names" and "sensible symbols" by showing that both are equally inadequate and equally genuine manifestations of God. Since God is not any being, "it is as false, and false in the same sense, to say that God is Word, Mind, or Being as to say that he is lion, stone, fire, or worm" (CH II.3, 140CD [lines 4270–4272]). Conversely, since nothing is "altogether deprived of participation in the Beautiful" (CH II.3, 141C citing Genesis 1:31 [lines 4284–4287]), all things are appropriate expressions of God. "All things, then, are in effect symbols of God... and this is simply a restatement of the doctrine that being is theophany" [lines 4308–4310]. The verb *proballein* captures the dual nature of every symbol: "to throw forth" meaning simultaneously "present, put forward, expose" and "shield, screen, set up a defense" [lines 4388–4390]. Dionysius applies it to being itself (DN V.5, 820A and V.6, 820C [lines 4441–4446]), showing that the very being of all things is "a presentation which screens and a screen which presents God" [lines 4446–4448]. The symbol "conceals precisely in and as revealing, and reveals precisely in and as concealing" [lines 4315–4316]. Therefore "there can be no non-symbolic knowledge of God" [lines 4206–4207]; to ascend to union with God is not to strip away symbols but to "cross over into (*eisō... diabainein*) the sacred symbols" (Ep. IX.2, 1108C [lines 4512–4514]) — "the ascent from symbols is the penetration into them" [line 4517].

## Perl on Theophany: Transcendence as the Condition of Immanence (Chapters 1–2)

*The following draws on the endnotes to Chapters 1 and 2 of Perl, *Theophany*, lines 4742–5078.*

The philosophical engine of Perl's reading is the argument that runs from Chapter 1's account of transcendence directly into Chapter 2's account of immanence: God's absolute transcendence beyond being is precisely what makes his presence in all things as theophany possible, not what excludes it.

Chapter 1 establishes the philosophical basis for God's transcendence of being. The Platonic-Neoplatonic identification of being with intelligibility means that "to be is to be intelligible and hence finite" [4895–4896]. Because all being is necessarily determinate and finite, the infinite first principle cannot itself be a being. This is not a limitation on language or a feature of human inadequacy: "human concepts and minds are unable to capture God, not because they are human, but because they are concepts and minds" [4873–4875]. Apophasis is a philosophical necessity built into the structure of what it means to be a being. Even the negations must be negated — Plotinus states that the One is "really outside all multiplicity and any simplicity whatsoever" (V.3.16.15–16) [4842–4844] — and the conclusion is not a superior hidden being but silence: "Precisely because nothing can truly be described as ineffable, the only recourse is silence" [4923–4925].

Chapter 2 shows how this radical transcendence does not isolate God from the world but is the condition of his being all in all. Since God is not one being among others, he is not limited by or opposed to any being; since he is the source of all determination, all determination is his self-expression. The key Platinian structure is *complicatio-explicatio*: "the concept of complicatio-explicatio is entirely Plotinian even if the words are not" [4977–4978]. God "enfolds" all things as their source and all things "unfold" him as their manifest content. Dupré's formulation captures the consequence: "True infinity, therefore, preserves the finite within itself" [5048–5053].

The governing distinction that Chapter 2 enforces is between *appearance* and *illusion*. Dodds read Proclus as implying that "the separateness of the lower is an illusion resulting from a partial point of view" [5059–5060]. Perl corrects this: "it is true that for them 'the sensible and intelligible cosmos are both of them appearance,' but it does not follow that they are 'an illusion'" [5065–5067]. Theophany is manifestation in Balthasar's sense: "becoming visible in reality — not as maya, mere seeming illusion, but always as (real) manifestation of the unmanifest, of the ever greater God who can never be changed into simply comprehensible appearance" [5069–5072]. A technical clarification from the notes: the term *theophany* (θεοφάνεια) becomes terminologically central in Eriugena rather than in Dionysius himself, but "it quite neatly expresses what is meant here" [5043–5044]. And the "divine difference" which is constitutive of all beings as God's self-manifestation "has nothing to do with the trinitarian differentiation of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" [5017–5019]: the production of being is common to all three Persons indifferently. "Although trinitarian doctrine is fully present in Dionysius… it does not enter into his philosophical understanding of being as theophany" [5040–5041].

### Ch. 3: The Good and the Cosmic Eros

*Source: Chapter 3 body text, range lines 1492–2257.*

The body text of Chapter 3 deepens the theophany doctrine by showing how the names Good, Beautiful, and Love each articulate the *same* structure of divine self-diffusion that constitutes all beings as beings. The Good is God's most proper name because goodness just *is* the intelligibility that makes beings beings — "to be intelligible, to be one, to be good, and hence to be, are all the same" [line 1586] — so the Good is not one attribute among others but the universal formative principle by which any being is itself. Perl quotes Dionysius: "Every being is from the Beautiful and Good and in the Beautiful and Good and is reverted to the Beautiful and Good" (DN IV.10, 705D) [lines 1744–1745]. Theophany is not merely God's making beings and stepping back; it is the ongoing metaphysical motion — the procession/reversion cycle — that constitutes beings as beings at every moment. The triad of *monē* (remaining), *proodos* (procession), and *epistrophē* (reversion) is the philosophical structure of theophany itself: remaining is God's enfolding of all things as their undifferentiated source; procession is his unfolding into differentiated beings; reversion is each being's active return to God as the goodness whereby it is. Crucially, this cycle is "not temporally nor even ontologically subsequent" in its moments [line 1646] — they are one metaphysical motion, not three stages.

Beauty deepens the theophanic picture by showing how God's self-gift draws beings back to him. Since beauty is form, and all form is God's presence in things as their constitutive determination, "the beauty in each being is God in it" [line 1871], and Beauty-beyond-being is "that by which being is beautiful" [lines 1839–1840]. This is why Dionysius says God is called Beauty "calling (*kalōn*) all things toward itself" (DN IV.7, 701C) [lines 1882–1883]: the attractive power of beauty is theophany's revertive moment, God drawing all things back to himself by being already present in them as their proper perfection. The divine name Love (*erōs*) is the most complete expression of theophany as dynamic self-gift. God "becomes out of himself in his providences toward all beings...without going out from himself" (DN IV.13, 712AB) [lines 1974–1981], because God has no inner self that could be distinguished from his productive outgoing — he *is* the making of all things, pure exteriority and pure interiority at once. The consequence Perl draws is exact: "Every being whatsoever must in effect say, 'I am, and yet not I, but God is in me,' not as an obscure mystical paradox but as a precise metaphysical truth" [lines 2058–2061]. The whole cycle of Love — "an eternal circle, whirling around through the Good, from the Good, and in the Good and to the Good" (DN IV.14, 712C–713A) [lines 2088–2091] — is theophany described from the inside: God present in all things as their procession from and reversion to him, which is their very being.
