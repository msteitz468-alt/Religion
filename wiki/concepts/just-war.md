# Just War (bellum iustum)

The **just-war** doctrine asks under what conditions, if any, the use of armed force is morally
licit. Its classic Western statement is [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s ([[summa-theologica|*Summa*]]
II-II Q40), which gathers and systematizes [[augustine|Augustine]]'s scattered remarks into three
conditions; Aquinas treats war as a vice opposed to the **peace** that is the effect of
[[charity-aquinas|charity]].

## Aquinas's three conditions (ST II-II Q40, A1)

"In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary":

1. **Legitimate authority** (*auctoritas principis*) — "the authority of the sovereign by whose
   command the war is to be waged. For it is not the business of a private individual to declare
   war." As the ruler may use the sword against internal wrongdoers (Rom. 13:4), so he may summon
   the sword against external enemies for the common weal.
2. **Just cause** (*causa iusta*) — "those who are attacked should be attacked because they deserve
   it on account of some fault." Quoting Augustine: "a just war is wont to be described as one that
   avenges wrongs, when a nation or state has to be punished for refusing to make amends… or to
   restore what it has seized unjustly."
3. **Right intention** (*intentio recta*) — "the belligerents should have a rightful intention, so
   that they intend the advancement of good, or the avoidance of evil." Even with legitimate
   authority and just cause, a war is made wicked by "the passion for inflicting harm, the cruel
   thirst for vengeance,… the lust of power, and such like."

The end is always peace: "we do not seek peace in order to be at war, but we go to war that we may
have peace" (Augustine).

## Corollaries

- **Clerics may not fight** (Q40, A2) — not because fighting is in itself sinful, but because the
  shedding of blood is unbecoming those dedicated to the contemplative ministry of the altar, who
  "should be ready to shed their own blood for Christ." They may, however, exhort and spiritually
  assist just combatants.
- **Ambushes are lawful** in a just war (Q40, A3): concealing one's plans is permitted ("we are not
  always bound to declare our purpose"), but **lying or breaking faith is never lawful**, even with
  an enemy — "there are certain rights of war and covenants which ought to be observed even among
  enemies" (Ambrose).
- **Fighting on holy days** is lawful when the common safety requires it (Q40, A4).

Related disorders treated nearby: **strife** (Q41, private war from anger), **sedition** (Q42,
internal tumult — always mortal, except that resisting a tyrant is *not* sedition, since the tyrant
is the real seditionist).

## Background and reception

The two-or-three-condition framework descends from [[augustine|Augustine]] (against the radical
pacifism of some early Christians) and was carried into canon law via Gratian. After Aquinas it was
developed by the **School of Salamanca** (Vitoria, Suárez) into the foundations of the modern law of
nations, and the *ius ad bellum* / *ius in bello* distinction of later just-war theory grows from
this root.

## Cross-tradition comparison

The just-war question recurs across traditions and is a candidate for a comparison page: Aquinas's
*bellum iustum*; Islamic discourse on **jihad** and its conditions; the Hindu *dharma-yuddha*
("righteous war") of the [[mahabharata|Mahabharata]] and the [[karma-yoga|Gita]]; and the strong
Buddhist and Jain presumptions toward [[ahimsa|non-violence]].
