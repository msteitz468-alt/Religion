# The Anandpur Sahib Resolution

The **Anandpur Sahib Resolution** (named for [[anandpur|Anandpur Sahib]], where it was adopted) is the
1973 [[akali-movement|Akali Dal]] charter of Sikh demands that became the central document — and the
central ambiguity — of the Punjab crisis of the 1980s. Khushwant Singh (*A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2)
shows that its meaning was contested from the start, "the seeds of separatism" to some and a mere
federalist safeguard to others.

## The Threefold Interpretive Dispute

Singh frames the resolution as irreducibly ambiguous: "There are at least three versions… which Akali
leaders interpret differently." Some (Sant Fateh Singh) held it meant no more than "the Punjabi Suba…
was in fact all they wanted"; others read it as "a demand for an autonomous Sikh-dominated state within
the Indian Union"; "and yet others construed it as a demand for an independent, sovereign Sikh state to
be named [[khalistan|Khalistan]]." Its political sub-preamble invoked "*khalsaji ka bol bala* — where the
voice of the Khalsa Sikhs will be pre-eminent," which to "most non-Akalis… was clearly an attempt to
establish a theocracy in a secular India."

## Origins and Authorship

It was "drafted in English by **Kapur Singh**, a dismissed ICS officer turned Akali politician and
intellectual," adopted at the Anandpur conference in 1973 and passed without dissent at the All-India
Akali Conference at Ludhiana, 28–29 October 1978 (proposed by Tohra, seconded by Badal). Singh notes the
irony that Sant Fateh Singh, who "knew no language except Punjabi," approved the English draft "by
nodding." Kapur Singh, he writes, "made his personal grievance into a community grievance," declaring
Sikhs "*sui generis* a free and sovereign people."

## The Demands

Singh's summary of the political demands: redraw Punjab to include all Punjabi-speaking areas; restrict
the Centre to defence, foreign affairs, posts, currency and railways (a strongly federalist devolution);
safeguards for Sikhs outside Punjab; maintenance of Sikh proportions in the defence services; the right
to bear arms; an all-India Gurdwara Act to bring all historical shrines under the SGPC; and access to
Nankana Sahib in Pakistan (for which Kapur Singh demanded "Vatican Status"). The economic demands (raise
the land ceiling, nationalize basic industry, state control of grain trade) Singh judges "generous to
farmers, minimal to agricultural labour."

## The Political Use and the Crisis

Singh's verdict on Akali tactics: "They kept it as a not-too-secret weapon in their archives, to be
brought out and brandished when they were out of power and use its non-acceptance as a grievance
justifying agitation." A subsequent **45 demands** (1980–81) and **15 demands** (Oct 1981) were, he
insists, mostly "substantial" and legitimate — "There was nothing in either… that could possibly be
construed as separatist or unconstitutional." His central charge is that [[indira-gandhi|Indira Gandhi]]
repeatedly blocked settlement for electoral reasons: "on five occasions the terms of a settlement were
almost finalized and it was not the Akalis but Mrs Gandhi who resiled." The deadlock fed the **Dharam
Yudh Morcha** (launched 4 August 1982) and the rise of [[jarnail-singh-bhindranwale|Bhindranwale]],
culminating in [[operation-blue-star|Operation Blue Star]]. Sant Longowal's "authentic version,"
circulated to MPs, "denied categorically that the Resolution envisaged a separate or even an autonomous
Sikh state."
