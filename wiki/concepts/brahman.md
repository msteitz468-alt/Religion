# Brahman

**Brahman** is the ultimate reality of [[hinduism|Hindu]] thought — the unconditioned ground of all
being, from which the [[upanishads|Upanishads]] say everything arises, in which it abides, and into
which it returns. (Not to be confused with *Brahma* the creator-god, or the *brahmin* priestly class.)

## Vedic Emergence

The concept has a traceable prehistory. Per [[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] (Vol. I,
Ch. II), in the **[[rig-veda|Ṛgveda]]** "brahman" had not yet emerged from its sacrificial associations —
Sāyaṇa glosses it as food-offering, chant, magical formula, completed rite, or simply "great." It is only
in the **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** that *Brahman* (neuter) becomes the **supreme principle**, the moving force
behind the gods: "Verily in the beginning this universe was the Brahman; it created the gods…," there
identified with **[[prajapati|Prajāpati]]**, Puruṣa, and Prāṇa. From this Brāhmaṇic supreme principle the
[[upanishads|Upaniṣadic]] Brahman develops.

## The Upaniṣadic Quest (Dasgupta, Vol. I, Ch. III)

[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta]] reads the [[upanishads|Upaniṣads]] as the record of a
**quest** after Brahman that proceeds by failure. The sages first try to give Brahman a *positive*
content — meditating on prāṇa (vital breath), ākāśa (space), the sun, *manas* as Brahman — and on the
presiding deities of sun, moon, wind, fire; none satisfies the ideal. The resolution is the **negative
method**: Brahman can be indicated only by *neti neti* ("not this, not this"), "inaudible, intangible,
invisible… greater than the great." Dasgupta distinguishes **three unreconciled currents** in the texts —
the dominant **monism** (Brahman the sole reality), a **pantheism** (the universe *is* Brahman, "from it
all comes forth and to it returns"), and a **theism** (Brahman as *Īśa*, inner controller, *antaryāmin*) —
left "in the melting pot," which is why the later Vedānta schools could each claim the Upaniṣads. He notes
the doctrine of [[maya|māyā]] is only faintly hinted (once in Bṛhadāraṇyaka, thrice in Śvetāśvatara) yet
becomes the foundation of Śaṅkara's system.

## The Contested Center

What Brahman *is* — and how it relates to the self ([[atman|atman]]) and the world — is the question
that divides the Vedanta schools:

- **[[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]**: Brahman is **nirguna** (without qualities), the only reality;
  selves and world are [[maya|maya]]. *Atman is Brahman.*
- **[[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]]**: Brahman is **saguna** (with qualities), the personal
  God (Vishnu/Narayana) whose real *body* is the souls and the world.
- **[[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]]**: Brahman is Vishnu, the one **independent** reality, eternally
  **distinct** from dependent souls and matter.

See [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the controversy triad]].

## Cross-Tradition Comparison

Brahman invites comparison with the other traditions' names for ultimate reality — and the contrasts
are sharp:

- vs. [[ein-sof|Ein Sof]] (Kabbalah) — both an infinite ground beyond predication; but Brahman can be
  the *self* (atman), which Ein Sof never is.
- vs. Buddhist [[sunyata|emptiness]] — emptiness explicitly *denies* a Brahman-like ground of being;
  the central Hindu–Buddhist metaphysical dispute.
- vs. the Abrahamic **God** ([[tawhid|tawhid]], [[shema|the Shema]]) — a personal creator distinct from
  creation, closest to the Dvaita/Vishishtadvaita pole, farthest from nirguna Advaita.
