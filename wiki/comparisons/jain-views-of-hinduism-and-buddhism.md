# Jain Views of Hinduism and Buddhism

Based on Dundas, *The Jains*, ch. 8 ("Jain relativism and relations with Hinduism and Buddhism"). This page records specifically what Dundas presents as the Jain intellectual and literary position toward the other two great Indian traditions, drawn from the primary sources he cites.

## Anekantavada as Both Tolerance and Polemic

The signature Jain doctrine of [[anekantavada|anekāntavāda]] (many-pointedness) has a dual function that modern interpreters tend to obscure:

**As polemic**: In Jain philosophical usage, many-pointedness is "a fearsome weapon of philosophical polemic with which the doctrines of [[hinduism]] and [[buddhism]] could be pared down to their ideological basics of simple permanence and impermanence respectively and thus be shown to be one-pointed and inadequate as the overall interpretations of reality which they purported to be" (Dundas, ch. 8). Both traditions are diagnosed as *ekānta* (one-sided): Hinduism as insisting on permanence; Buddhism as insisting on impermanence. Both thus fail to grasp reality's complexity.

**As claimed immunity**: The many-pointed approach "was claimed by the Jains to be immune from criticism, since it did not present itself as a philosophical or dogmatic view" (Dundas, ch. 8). This asymmetry — the Jain system is meta-philosophical and so cannot itself be one-pointed — is philosophically controversial.

**Modern misreading**: In modern times anekāntavāda has been widely reinterpreted as "intellectual *ahimsā*" — tolerance of others' opinions. Dundas regards this as "a misreading, admittedly well-meant, of the doctrine of manypointedness as intended to promote a benign attitude to other positions." The historical Jain position is that by the rigour of its analysis "Jainism is at the most profound level different from and superior to other paths" (Dundas, ch. 8).

## The Jains and the Hindus

### General Relationship

Jain attitudes to Hinduism "were never uniform" and employed "a variety of idioms and strategies, including accommodation, rejection, satire and transmutation" (Dundas, ch. 8). Evidence of accommodation includes:

- The Jain Universal History drawing on Hindu epic and *Purāṇic* dynastic lore.
- From the eighth century, fordmakers linked with Hindu gods through Hindu names and epithets.
- An elaborate Jain iconography of guardian gods and goddesses showing extensive Śaiva influence.
- The Vaiṣṇavas incorporating the first fordmaker Ṛṣabha as a minor incarnation (*avatāra*) of Viṣṇu.
- The tenth-century Digambara Somadeva acknowledging that Jains could engage in popular or regional custom provided it did not infringe Jain basic tenets.

### Critique of the Veda

The Jains "consistently attacked the foundations upon which Hinduism rested" (Dundas, ch. 8). The Veda was challenged as "of the same human provenance as any other type of literature" and the brahman claim of authorlessness was rejected as logically untenable — "an argument that anonymous poems were not written by anybody" (*VTP* pp. 72–101). The Jain scriptures, conveyed by omniscient teachers, were deemed of greater worth. Some Jain writers even claimed that the Hindus did not know their own scriptures, being unfamiliar with references to the fordmakers in the Veda (e.g. *KKP* 35 pp. 170–1).

**Animal sacrifice** was the clinching argument: "the Jains refused to accept that, even in the controlled and delimited context of ritual action, the use of sanctified language in the form of mantras could neutralise the inevitable evil brought about by the slaughter of animals." Hemacandra characterises the lawgiver Manu's pro-sacrifice verses as *hiṃsāśāstra*, "the science of violence," and insists that "a dreadful rebirth can be the only possible result for those who destroy animals under the pretext of worship" (*YS* 2.33–40). Akalanka: "if killing can bring about a religious goal, then one should best take up a life of hunting and fishing" (*TSRV* p. 563).

### Hindu Mythology: Rewriting Śiva and Viṣṇu

Hindu gods were systematically derided within Jain literature, primarily through recasting their mythological careers in terms of the laws of rebirth and retribution.

**Śiva**: Jinadāsa describes how "Maheśvara" was the son of a Jain nun magically impregnated, who acquired his third eye through a spell and was killed by a prostitute called Umā (*AvCa* vol. 2 pp. 174–6). Harisena's Digambara version presents Śiva as the offspring of a monk and nun who broke their vow of chastity; he became a monk but through lust and violence sired children and ultimately died. The prevalence of the *liṅga* throughout north-west India is ascribed by Harisena to a Jain monk who set them up to exorcise the murdered Rudra's malign powers (*BKK* 97). Dundas notes the "remarkable feat" of simultaneously devalorising Śiva and giving Jainism credit for his phallic emblem's spread.

**Viṣṇu**: Jain attitudes were "rather more ambivalent, no doubt because of the close ties which the Jains came to form with the Vaiṣṇavas" — partly through the early association of Kṛṣṇa with the fordmaker Nemi. Jains mocked the claim of Viṣṇu's omnipresence (if omnipresent in water, is it appropriate to drink a god? *KKP* 29) but also reworked the dwarf-Viṣṇu myth to present him as "a mighty Jain monk, albeit a human one, who uses his magic power to overthrow a brahman minister who had attempted to expel the Jains from his master's kingdom" (*BKK* 11 and *DhMV* 93).

**Satirical literature**: Haribhadra's "Rogues' Story" (*DhA*) has lowlife characters cap each other's outrageous anecdotes with references to Hindu gods behaving disreputably, making the point that "if the gods are depicted as behaving in a disreputable manner, it is hardly surprising when humans do the same" — explicitly a means of "inculcating piety amongst the Jain community and subverting the authority of Hinduism" (Dundas, ch. 8).

### The Hindu Epics

The Śvetāmbara canonical *Nandī* sūtra links the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa with the Veda as examples of *mithyāsūtra* ("false scriptures which convey a message of violence") (*NSH* p. 64). Jain retellings of both epics systematically domesticated and re-moralised the narratives:

- The Mahābhārata's violence is attributed in the *Jñātādharmakathā* to Draupadī's foolishness in a previous birth (giving poisoned food to a monk).
- The Bhagavad Gītā was quoted occasionally but also assimilated by Yaśovijaya in his "Essence of the Inner Soul," approaching non-dualistic Vedānta. Today Jain ascetics sometimes "compare the Bhagavad Gītā favourably with Mahāvīra's teachings" (Dundas, ch. 8).
- The Rāmāyaṇa received the most extensive Jain retelling, notably Raviṣeṇa's seventh-century Digambara *Padmapurāṇa*, which: locates the whole story in a Jain cosmological frame; presents Rāvaṇa as a devout Jain who does not force himself on Sītā out of his vow; has Sītā become a Jain nun; and ends with Rāma renouncing the world and attaining enlightenment while Rāvaṇa and Lakṣmaṇa, having worked out their bad karma in hell, will eventually be reborn as Sītā's sons and attain deliverance (*PadP* 123).

In the sixteenth century, Jain writers in western India produced versions of the Mahābhārata libelling Viṣṇu and presenting Kṛṣṇa as "a devious and immoral schemer" — apparently reflecting "a particularly bitter period of sectarian struggle during which, according to the chroniclers, large numbers of Hindus in Rajasthan were being converted to Jainism" (Dundas, ch. 8).

## The Jains and the Buddhists

### Historical Relations

Mahāvīra and the Buddha were near-exact contemporaries in the Ganges region but there is no record of their having met. Buddhist Pali texts call Mahāvīra "Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta" and are familiar with his claim to omniscience; the *Dīgha Nikāya* (Saṅgītipariyāya Sutta) describes his death and the subsequent squabbling of his followers as reported to the Buddha. The Buddhist attitude was "one of disdain for their insistence on the practice of asceticism as a means to enlightenment and in the medieval period, no doubt out of contempt, they made virtually no effort to controvert the criticisms of the Śvetāmbaras and Digambaras" (Dundas, ch. 8). There is, however, "possible evidence that components of Jain practice may have filtered into early Buddhism" (Dundas, ch. 8).

### Early Jain Understanding of Buddhism

Jain early texts had "some limited familiarity with Buddhist teachings." The *Sūtrakṛtāṅga* alludes to the Buddhist analysis of the human personality into five factors but describes their holders as "fools" (*SKS* 1.1.1.17). Jain texts generally did not wish to accurately represent Buddhist ideas on karma, no doubt because "denial of the permanent self could logically leave no room for it" (*SKS* 1.12.4). One text travesties Buddhist teaching by making a Buddhist monk argue that cannibalism can be acceptable if carried out through error (*SKS* 2.6.26–9).

No early Jain text shows knowledge of the Buddha himself. The tenth-century Digambara Devasena dismisses him as "a lapsed follower of Pārśva" who "went to hell" (*DS* 6–9). Classical Jainism "refused to credit the Buddha with any authority because his knowledge was only partial and not complete like that of the omniscient fordmakers" (*Aṣṭaka* 1.3–4). The epithet *jina* ("conqueror") used by Buddhists for their founder was rejected: only the Jain fordmakers, who had truly conquered passions, senses, ascetic difficulties, and harming karmas, could legitimately claim the title (*LV* pp. 223–7).

### Standard Jain Critique of Buddhism

**Monastic laxity**: The standard medieval Jain view was that the Buddhist code of monastic law was "less stringent about matters which the Jains considered to be important such as diet and austerities, showed them to be lax and corrupt." The standard claim was that Buddhists were habitual meat-eaters (derived from the Buddhist rule that a monk can eat anything placed in his alms bowl not specially prepared for him). A well-known verse satirised Buddhist life as "a soft bed, food and drink, gambling and at the end of it all, deliverance" (Abhayadeva on *Sth* 607; cf. *MSP* p. 65 and *YS* 4.102.10) — Buddhist followers were regarded as "no more than householders" (Dundas, ch. 8).

**Philosophical critique via anekāntavāda**: The Buddhist doctrine of impermanence (and later momentariness) was "the classic example of a one-pointed (*ekānta*) and inadequate standpoint" (*YB* 464). Without some underlying permanent entity providing a locus for modification, change makes no sense, and without a permanent experiencer of the results of action, karma is unintelligible. "The very goal of Buddhist practice, *nirvāṇa*, would seem to be illogical, if momentariness dictates that there is no person who could experience such a state" (*VTP* pp. 285–306). Dundas notes that these criticisms were "hardly strange to the Buddhists and some of them had been voiced from within the Buddhist intellectual community itself" (ch. 8) — the Jains simply did not wish to consider whether Buddhist responses had any force.

**Hagiographic polemic**: Medieval hagiographies describe Jain teachers "vanquishing the Buddhists in debate or regaining control over holy places." Narrative literature includes stories of Buddhists falsely converting to Jainism to contract marriages, seeking revenge on genuine converts, and fomenting trouble (*BKK* 68, *KKP* 22, *MSP* pp. 72–3).

### More Ambivalent Attitudes: Haribhadra

The most nuanced Jain engagement with Buddhism centres on **Haribhadra**. In his yoga works, Haribhadra "evinces a willingness to acknowledge the areas in which Buddhism and Jainism show similarities" and suggests that the Jain who has attained correct belief and the Mahāyāna Buddhist *bodhisattva* "were essentially the same, since both were characterised by a willingness to do good to others" (*YB* 270, 273, 274). One of his most famous works has a title (Lalitavistara — "Extension of Play") nearly identical to a well-known Buddhist biography of the Buddha.

The medieval hagiographers responded to this by transforming Haribhadra into a scourge of the Buddhists who took violent revenge for the deaths of his nephews at Buddhist hands — a story intended to "reclaim Haribhadra fully as a prominent Jain teacher at a time when Jainism was trying to establish a firm identity" (Dundas, ch. 8). Dundas characterises this as evidence that Buddhism had "at times had a fascination" for the Jains — "something both alluring and dangerous" — illustrated by the elaborate story of the Buddhist book that blinded a Jain nun and then flew to heaven (*KhGPS* p. 28).
