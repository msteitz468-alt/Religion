[[genesis|Genesis 1–11]] shares significant formal, thematic, and narrative parallels with ancient Mesopotamian and Ugaritic texts. [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] and other commentators engage these parallels extensively — both to establish the cultural plausibility of Genesis' setting and to identify Genesis' distinctive theological polemic against polytheistic cosmology.

## The Enuma Elish (Babylonian Creation Epic)

*Date*: Compiled c. 12th century BCE; reflects older traditions.
*Summary*: The primordial gods Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water) generate the younger gods. Conflict ensues; Marduk defeats Tiamat, splitting her body to form heaven and earth. Humanity is created from the blood of Kingu (Tiamat's general) to serve the gods.

**Parallels with Genesis 1**:
- Both begin with primordial watery chaos
- Both involve a separation of waters to form sky and earth
- Both culminate in the creation of humanity and a divine "rest"

**Key differences — Genesis' polemic**:
- In Enuma Elish, creation results from **divine conflict** — the cosmos is built from a defeated deity's corpse. In Genesis, creation is by **divine word** — there is no conflict, no pre-existing divine opposition, no cosmic battle
- The Babylonian gods are themselves generated from primordial matter; YHWH has no origin — no theogony
- In Enuma Elish, humanity is created as the **slaves of the gods**, to relieve divine labor. In Genesis, humanity is created in God's **image**, with **dominion** and **dignity** (*imago dei*)
- The Genesis creation is **monotheistic**: one God creates everything; the Enuma Elish presents a pantheon

**Hamilton's assessment**: Genesis 1 is best read not as borrowed from but as **polemically related to** the Enuma Elish tradition. The verbal and structural echoes are too systematic to be accidental; the theological reversals are equally systematic.

## The Atrahasis Epic

*Date*: Old Babylonian period, c. 17th century BCE; one of the oldest complete ANE literary compositions.
*Summary*: A five-part structure — (1) creation of humanity to serve the gods, (2) divine problem (overpopulation/noise), (3) divine solutions (plague, drought, famine), (4) the Flood as final solution, (5) post-Flood resolution (death, infertility, infant mortality as population controls).

**Parallel with Genesis 1–11** (Kikawada and Quinn): The Atrahasis Epic's five-part structure closely parallels Genesis 1–11:
1. Creation (Gen 1–2)
2. Divine problem: human sin and its escalation (Gen 3–6)
3. Progressive judgments (Gen 3–6)
4. The Flood (Gen 6–9)
5. Post-Flood resolution: new humanity, new covenant, new start (Gen 9–11)

**Hamilton's use**: The Atrahasis structural parallel supports the argument that Genesis 1–11 is a **unified literary whole** — comparable to a known ancient literary genre — rather than a patchwork of incompatible source fragments. This is used as an argument against the fragmentary source-critical reading.

## The Gilgamesh Epic, Tablet XI

*Date*: Standard Babylonian version, c. 12th–7th century BCE; flood tablet is the best-known.
*Summary*: The hero Utnapishtim (equivalent of Noah) is warned by the god Ea to build a boat and take all living creatures aboard before a divinely sent flood. After the flood recedes, Utnapishtim sacrifices, and the gods gather like flies around the offering. Utnapishtim and his wife are granted immortality.

**Closest verbal parallels to Genesis 6–9** of any ANE text:
- Boat construction to divine specifications
- Animals taken aboard in pairs
- Sending out birds (raven and dove) to test for dry land
- Landing on a mountain
- Post-flood sacrifice

**Key theological differences**:
- In Gilgamesh, the flood is sent because humanity is **too noisy** — not because of moral corruption. The gods' motivation is purely irritation, not justice.
- In Genesis, the flood is sent because humanity's **wickedness is great** (6:5) — a moral-theological judgment
- Gilgamesh's gods "cower like dogs," are terrified by the flood they themselves unleashed, and quarrel over Utnapishtim's sacrifice. Genesis' God is sovereign throughout
- Noah's post-flood covenant (Gen 9) is a unilateral divine promise; Utnapishtim receives personal immortality as his reward

## Ugaritic El Tradition

The divine name **El** and its epithets in the Ugaritic texts (14th–13th centuries BCE, from Ras Shamra/ancient Ugarit) illuminate the patriarchal "God of the Fathers" tradition:
- El is depicted as an aged, benevolent deity, father of the gods and creator (*qnyt ʾlm* = "creator of the earth")
- El's epithets (*ʾāb šnm*, "father of years"; *lt*, "the Powerful One") parallel biblical epithets for YHWH in Genesis
- El appears in a tent at the source of two rivers — an image comparable to the Eden garden and its rivers (Gen 2:10-14)

**Frank Moore Cross** argued that the God of the patriarchs was originally worshiped as an El-deity before being identified with YHWH. Hamilton engages this argument cautiously.

## Summary: Genesis' Theological Distinctives

Despite shared cultural vocabulary, Genesis' theological posture is consistently **polemical and revisionary**:

| Feature | ANE tradition | Genesis |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmogony | Divine conflict; world from defeated deity | Word of God; no conflict |
| Divine nature | Plural, anthropomorphic, morally inconsistent | One God; transcendent; morally consistent |
| Human origin | Created to serve gods; relieve divine labor | Created in God's image; given dominion |
| Flood cause | Divine noise-irritation | Moral wickedness |
| Post-flood | Personal reward (immortality) | Universal covenant (never again) |
