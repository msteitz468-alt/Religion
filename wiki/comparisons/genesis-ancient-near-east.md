[[genesis|Genesis 1–11]] (and, in Hamilton’s second volume, patriarchal and Joseph material) shares significant formal, thematic, and narrative parallels with ancient Mesopotamian, Ugaritic, and Egyptian texts and realia. [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] ([[hamilton-genesis-1-17-nicot|1–17]]; [[hamilton-genesis-18-50-nicot|18–50]]) engages these parallels extensively — to establish cultural plausibility, to identify Genesis’ distinctive **antimythical / demythologizing** posture, and to **caution** against parallelomania (especially Nuzi).

## The Enuma Elish (Babylonian Creation Epic)

*Date*: Compiled c. 12th century BCE; reflects older traditions.
*Summary*: The primordial gods Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water) generate the younger gods. Conflict ensues; Marduk defeats Tiamat, splitting her body to form heaven and earth. Humanity is created from the blood of Kingu (Tiamat's general) to serve the gods.

**Parallels with Genesis 1**:
- Both begin with primordial watery chaos
- Both involve a separation of waters to form sky and earth
- Both culminate in the creation of humanity and a divine "rest"

**Key differences — Genesis' polemic (Hamilton densified)**:
- In Enuma Elish, creation results from **divine conflict** — the cosmos is built from a defeated deity's corpse. In Genesis, creation is by **divine word** / action without conflict, pre-existing divine opposition, or cosmic battle. Gen 1 and 2 lack conflict- and birth-modes of cosmogony entirely.
- The Babylonian gods are themselves generated from primordial matter; YHWH has no origin — no theogony. Gen 1:1 is, for Hamilton (with Gunkel), unmatched: “The cosmogonies of other people contain no word which would come close to the first word of the Bible.” Enuma elish 1–9 is syntactically closer to Gen 2:4bff. than to 1:1–3.
- *Tehôm* is not personified Tiamat: “the deep of Gen. 1 is so far removed in function from the Tiamat of Enuma elish that any possible relationship is blurred beyond recognition.” Closer linguistic kin: Ugaritic *thm*, Eblaite *ti-’a-matum*.
- Day 2 *rāqîaʿ* vs sky from half of Tiamat’s corpse with locks/guards — “wide discrepancy.”
- Day 4 **antimythical thrust**: luminaries created to serve, not be served; avoids *šemeš*/*yārēaḥ* (divinity-laden names) for “greater/lesser luminary”; order sun–moon–stars vs Enuma elish stars–moon–sun; stars almost afterthought.
- *Tannînim* (day 5): large marine creatures, not combatants — even if other OT/Ugaritic texts use dragon/Yamm battle language, Gen 1 has no battle; reappearance of *bārāʾ* underlines creation. “Gen. 1 could not be written with a more antimythical basis.”
- Anthropology: Enuma elish man as afterthought from Kingu’s blood to relieve gods’ toil — “The anthropologies of Gen. 1 and Enuma elish could not be wider apart.” Genesis: image, dominion, dignity.
- Seventh day: deliberate avoidance of “Sabbath day” term possibly demythologizes vs Akk. *šapattu*; Mesopotamian gods rest after creating man as surrogate laborers — opposite of biblical divine rest.

**Hamilton's assessment**: Genesis 1 is best read not as borrowed from but as **polemically related / demythologizing** relative to Enuma elish, Ugaritic combat myths, and astral cults — allowing shared motifs (watery deep, clay man, plants of life) at great functional distance. Westermann and Childs choose absolute *berēʾšîṯ* partly from repudiation of pagan cosmogony; Hamilton adds that lexical/grammatical/stylistic arguments also support “In the beginning,” not theology alone.

## The Atrahasis Epic

*Date*: Old Babylonian period, c. 17th century BCE; one of the oldest complete ANE literary compositions.
*Summary*: A five-part structure — (1) creation of humanity to serve the gods, (2) divine problem (overpopulation/noise), (3) divine solutions (plague, drought, famine), (4) the Flood as final solution, (5) post-Flood resolution (death, infertility, infant mortality as population controls).

**Parallel with Genesis 1–11** (Kikawada and Quinn; endorsed by Hamilton): five-part structure closely parallels Genesis 1–11:
1. Creation (Gen 1–2)
2. Divine problem: human sin and its escalation (Gen 3–6)
3. Progressive judgments (Gen 3–6)
4. The Flood (Gen 6–9)
5. Post-Flood resolution: new humanity, new covenant, new start (Gen 9–11)

**Hamilton's use**: structural parallel supports Genesis 1–11 as a **unified literary whole** rather than J+P patchwork — “the case for homogeneity is much greater than the case for heterogeneity.” Flood *cause* contrast: Atrahasis = noise/insomnia/overpopulation, not moral rebellion. Post-Flood contrast: Gen 9:1 fruitfulness vs Atrahasis population *controls* — Gen 9:1ff “looks like a conscious rejection of the Atrahasis Epic.” See [[flood-universality|Flood universality]] and [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]].

## The Gilgamesh Epic, Tablet XI

*Date*: Standard Babylonian version, c. 12th–7th century BCE; flood tablet is the best-known.
*Summary*: The hero Utnapishtim (equivalent of Noah) is warned by the god Ea to build a boat and take all living creatures aboard before a divinely sent flood. After the flood recedes, Utnapishtim sacrifices, and the gods gather like flies around the offering. Utnapishtim and his wife are granted immortality.

**Closest verbal parallels to Genesis 6–9** of any ANE text:
- Boat construction to divine specifications
- Animals taken aboard in pairs
- Sending out birds (raven and dove) to test for dry land
- Landing on a mountain
- Post-flood sacrifice

**Key theological and narrative differences (Hamilton densified)**:
- Flood motivation: Gilgamesh vague (“when their heart led the great gods…”) / Ea rebukes Enlil as “unreasoning”; Genesis 6:5 **moral** wickedness
- Ark: biblical dimensions “completely logical” for a seagoing vessel (~440×73×44 ft) vs Utnapishtim’s perfect 120-cubit **cube**
- Duration: forty days/nights more reasonable for claimed proportion than Gilgamesh/Atrahasis seven days/nights
- Navigation: Noah has no crew — survival is Yahweh’s grace, not human skill; bird-sending as navigational practice; raven-then-dove “more of the ring of truth” than dove→swallow→raven
- Landing: *hārê ʾarārāṭ* (mountains of Ararat/Urartu), not “Mt. Ararat”; Gilgamesh lands on Mt. Nisir
- Sacrifice: no gods swarming “like flies” starving after the flood; Yahweh’s soliloquy avoids framing sacrifice as magic manipulation of hungry deities
- Reward: Noahic **unilateral** covenant with all flesh vs Utnapishtim’s personal immortality
- Text-history note Hamilton cites: surviving bird-parallel in Gilgamesh XI copies is post-750 BC; earlier Sumerian flood lacks birds — so the “most telling parallel” may postdate the biblical account

Tigay’s Gilgamesh growth studies show multiedited epics are verifiable; Hamilton: “A multiedited Gilgamesh Epic is empirically verifiable. A multiedited Genesis remains, at best, a hypothesis.”

These parallels are deployed on both sides of the dispute over [[flood-universality|the Flood's scope]] — as evidence of a shared (locally-rooted) mythic tradition, or, via the Atrahasis structural parallel, as evidence of Genesis 1–11's literary unity.

## Ugaritic El Tradition

The divine name **El** and its epithets in the Ugaritic texts (14th–13th centuries BCE, from Ras Shamra/ancient Ugarit) illuminate the patriarchal "God of the Fathers" tradition:
- El is depicted as an aged, benevolent deity, father of the gods and creator (*qnyt ʾlm* = "creator of the earth")
- El's epithets (*ʾāb šnm*, "father of years"; *lt*, "the Powerful One") parallel biblical epithets for YHWH in Genesis
- El appears in a tent at the source of two rivers — an image comparable to the Eden garden and its rivers (Gen 2:10-14)
- Ugaritic *bn il* illuminates *bĕnê ʾĕlōhîm* language at Gen 6 without equating OT angels to a divine pantheon (see [[sons-of-god|Sons of God]])

**Frank Moore Cross** argued that the God of the patriarchs was originally worshiped as an El-deity before being identified with YHWH. Hamilton engages this argument cautiously. Garden of Eden vs Dilmun: Hamilton finds Dilmun as main antecedent “farfetched”; Gen 2 antimythical (man created before garden; life from God the planter, not magical plant as in Gilgamesh plant-of-youth / Adapa bread-and-water).

## Nuzi and Cuneiform Legal Parallels — Hamilton’s caution

Hamilton uses Nuzi and related cuneiform material **selectively** and often to **limit** overclaims (Speiser-style parallelomania; Van Seters’s critique is partly credited):

| Locus | Parallel often claimed | Hamilton’s caution / limited use |
|---|---|---|
| Gen 16 surrogacy | Nuzi Gilimninu/Shennima; Hammurapi §146; Old Assyrian Laqipum/Hatala; Neo-Assyrian Ṣubetu | Marriage customs conservative/long-lived; “impossible, simply on the basis of archeology, to date Gen. 16 to either the 2nd or the 1st millennium” — credits Van Seters against selective second-millennium dating |
| Gen 25 birthright sale | Nuzi Tupkitilla/Kurpazah property exchange | Brothers could sell inherited property, but differences (elder/younger, hunger motive, full inheritance) make a **limited** parallel — not proof of identical custom |
| Gen 27 deathbed blessing | Nuzi deathbed documents | “Nuzi deathbed parallels largely fail (Speiser overread)” |
| Gen 24 Rebekah consent | Nuzi consent notes | Both timing and leave are at issue; not simple one-to-one custom transfer |
| Gen 37 slave price | OB average male-slave price 20 shekels; rises later (Nuzi 30, Ugarit 40, Neo-Bab 50…) | Fits early 2nd-millennium range and Lev 27:5 valuation for males 5–20 (Joseph is 17) — chronological *plausibility*, not proof of DH dating |

Broader historiography: if the Amorite Hypothesis falls, “what has fallen by the wayside is a scholarly hypothesis, not the Biblical text” (Hamilton citing Sarna’s logic). Sumerian King List is structural parallel only (long pre-Flood lives, Flood, shorter post-Flood lives) with decisive differences (kingship + apotheosis vs genealogy of mankind from Adam; numbers dwarfed).

## Egyptian notes (Joseph story and adjacent)

From [[hamilton-genesis-18-50-nicot|Hamilton Genesis 18–50]] on Gen 37–50:

- **Geography**: Shechem and Dothan occupied in Middle Bronze (Joseph’s time) — archaeological plausibility of route.
- **Garment**: Mari *ketōnet passîm*-type parallel argues indirectly for early date of the Joseph story (vs late invention).
- **Sale price**: twenty shekels = OB average male-slave price (see table above).
- **Midianites/Ishmaelites**: overlapping terms (Judg 8), not forced J/E ethnic doublets — ANE ethnographic looseness.
- **Egyptian court customs**: eating with Hebrews an Egyptian *tôʿēbâ* (43:32); divination/lecanomancy (Joseph’s cup) not a Hebrew abomination in Egyptian eyes — “neither culture became Hebrew” in repudiating the other’s taboo.
- **Goshen / Delta**: Semitic name; Wadi Tumilat / E Delta; Merneptah-letter analogy for Asiatic presence; Pharaoh’s invitation as royal confirmation of Joseph’s initiative.
- **Agrarian reform (47)**: progressive dispossession; priests exempt; law “still in force today” — read as fruit of Jacob’s blessing on Pharaoh, not cynical reciprocity.
- **Embalming / mourning**: Egyptian physicians; 40 days work; 70 days mourning (royal-scale respect); Joseph’s *ʾārôn* (coffin/sarcophagus) — same word as ark of the covenant in later midrash.
- **Joseph’s lifespan 110**: Egyptian ideal age + numerical patriarchal square pattern.

These notes establish **cultural verisimilitude and early plausibility** without converting Genesis into a borrowed Egyptian novella. Theological center remains providence (50:20: brothers *ḥăšaḇtem* evil; God *ḥăšābāh* for good).

## Summary: Genesis' Theological Distinctives

Despite shared cultural vocabulary, Genesis' theological posture is consistently **polemical and revisionary** (Hamilton: antimythical):

| Feature | ANE tradition | Genesis |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmogony | Divine conflict; world from defeated deity | Word/action of God; no conflict; unique *berēʾšîṯ* |
| Divine nature | Plural, anthropomorphic, morally inconsistent | One God; transcendent; morally consistent |
| Human origin | Created to serve gods; relieve divine labor | Created in God's image; given dominion |
| Luminaries | Deified sun/moon/stars | Created servants; names demythologized |
| Flood cause | Divine noise-irritation / insomnia | Moral wickedness (6:5) |
| Post-flood | Personal reward (immortality); population controls | Universal covenant (never again); “be fruitful” |
| Legal/cultural parallels | Nuzi, Mari, Egypt illuminate customs | Limited analogies; no automatic dating or borrowing |
