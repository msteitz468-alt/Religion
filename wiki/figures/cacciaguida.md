# Cacciaguida

[[dante-alighieri|Dante]]'s great-great-grandfather, a twelfth-century Florentine
knight who died on the Second Crusade, and the central figure of the **Mars** cantos
of the [[paradiso|Paradiso]] (15–17) — the dramatic and structural heart of the
cantica, where Dante's exile is prophesied and the *Commedia* itself is commissioned.

## In the Divine Comedy

A light darts from the cross of warrior-saints and greets Dante in Latin — "*O
sanguis meus*" ("O blood of mine!") — explicitly echoing Anchises greeting Aeneas in
*Aeneid* 6. Cacciaguida recounts his birth "within the ancient ring" of an austere,
modest old [[florence|Florence]] (baptized in the Baptistery), his knighting by
Emperor Conrad III, and his death in battle "and came from martyrdom to this pure
peace" (15:148). He contrasts that virtuous old city with the corrupt present, dating
its ruin to immigration, mercantile wealth, and the founding Buondelmonte murder
(Cantos 15–16).

In **Canto 17** he delivers the poem's central **prophecy of Dante's exile**: "You'll
leave behind you all you hold most dear… And you will taste the saltiness of bread /
when offered by another's hand — as, too, / how hard it is to climb a stranger's
stair" (17:55–60), naming the Scaligeri of Verona (Bartolommeo and Can Grande) as
Dante's refuge. He then resolves Dante's hesitation about writing the truth, **commissioning
the poem**: "all lies put clean aside, / make plain what in your vision you have seen,
/ and let them scratch wherever they may itch" (17:127–129) — at once Dante's
self-authorization for the *Commedia* and its justification for naming famous souls.

## Significance

Kirkpatrick stresses that almost nothing is historically known of Cacciaguida; Dante
**reinvents** him as the voice of providential continuity against Fortune, his death
assimilated to the Cross that dominates the heaven of Mars. The episode is where the
poem reflects most directly on its own making and on the cost — exile — that
authorizes its prophetic voice.
