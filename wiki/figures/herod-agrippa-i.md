# Herod Agrippa I

Herod Agrippa I is the Judean king who, in Acts 12:1–23, persecutes the Jerusalem church,
executes [[james-son-of-zebedee|James son of Zebedee]], arrests [[peter-apostle|Peter]], and
is then "struck down" and "eaten by worms," dying in AD 44. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats his
death — datable against Josephus — as one of the externally attested chronological anchors
of [[acts|Acts]].

## Disambiguation

Bock and the text distinguish several Herods, all named as separate persons:

- **Herod the Great** — Agrippa I's grandfather.
- **Herod Antipas** — the tetrarch of Luke's Gospel; a distinct person.
- **Herod Agrippa I** — the subject of this page; king over the reunited Herodian realm,
  AD 41–44.
- **Herod Agrippa II** — his son, before whom Paul later appears (Acts 25–26); a distinct
  figure with his own slug.

## Biographical Overview

Herod Agrippa I (ca. 9 BC – AD 44) was the grandson of Herod the Great, "educated and
raised in Rome." Bock records that Caligula gave him Philip's and Lysanias's territory in
AD 37, and that under Claudius — his boyhood classmate — he received "full rule of Herod
the Great's empire" from AD 41, reigning until AD 44. Bock notes he was "very close to the
Pharisees" and "especially pious… when he was in Jerusalem" (Josephus, *Ant.* 19.6.1), and
quotes Josephus (*Ant.* 19.7.3 §§328–31) at length on his beneficence and popularity, in
contrast with the earlier Herod.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 12:1–2** — "lays hands on some of the church"; has James, the brother of
  [[john-apostle|John]], killed "by the sword."
- **Acts 12:3–5** — arrests Peter during the Feast of Unleavened Bread, guarding him with
  four squads of soldiers.
- **Acts 12:6–19** — Peter escapes by angelic deliverance; Herod cross-examines the sentries
  and orders them "led away" (Bock: a euphemism for execution), then goes to Caesarea.
- **Acts 12:20–23** — quarreling with Tyre and Sidon (probably an embargo, per Jervell),
  Herod delivers a public oration; the crowd hails "The voice of a god, and not a man"; he
  "accepts the praise, which is his downfall" (Bock), is struck by "an angel of the Lord"
  (πατάσσω), and dies "eaten by worms" (σκωληκόβρωτος, an NT hapax).

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock reads Agrippa's hostility to the church as politically motivated: his Jewish popularity
"may well explain his hostility to the new movement," as he sought favor with "the ruling
Sadducean party" (following Hengel and Schwemer). Bock cross-references Josephus
(*Ant.* 19.8.2 §§343–50), where the crowd hails Herod as "superior to mortal nature" and
Josephus adds the portent of an owl and a painful death. Bock's theological reading turns on
Herod's refusal to "give God the glory": accepting divine acclamation is rebuked in the
Tosefta (*t. Soṭah* 7.16), the Talmud (*b. Soṭah* 41b), and the OT (Ezek. 28:2–9, against
the prince of Tyre). Bock draws an explicit contrast with [[paul-apostle|Paul]] and
[[barnabas|Barnabas]] refusing worship at Lystra (Acts 14:11–15), and reads Herod's demise
as "typical for an enemy of God" (cf. Antiochus Epiphanes, Herod the Great), suggesting
"Luke is… suggesting that Herod also dies for persecuting Peter."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock structures Acts 12 around contrast — Peter rescued, Herod judged — following Polhill
("Herod found himself fighting against God") and Fernando's "rescue, no rescue" pairing.
Herod's judgment, side by side with Peter's deliverance, exhibits "Mercy and judgment…
side by side" (Polhill). His death "likely" in March AD 44 is, for Bock, "one of the key
dates" for the chronology of Acts.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock notes Johnson's observation of a historical core paralleling Josephus's account of
Herod's death (*Ant.* 19.8.2). Fitzmyer reads the crowd's cry as "a royal acclamation" and
places the occasion at either the quinquennial games at Caesarea (March) or Claudius's
birthday (August). Barrett observes that, unlike Greco-Roman rescue narratives, Peter's
deliverance is not a self-release but the work of an angel.
