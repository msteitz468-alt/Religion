# Nadab and Abihu

**Biographical Overview**:
Nadab and Abihu were the two eldest sons of Aaron and Elisheba. They accompanied Moses, Aaron, and the seventy elders up Mount Sinai to see God and eat the covenant meal (Exod 24). Shortly after their consecration as priests, they offered "unauthorized" (or "strange") fire before YHWH and were immediately consumed by divine fire (Lev 10). They died childless.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Exodus**: Invited up Mount Sinai with the elders to behold God without being destroyed (Exod 24:1, 9).
- **Leviticus**: Their central narrative. Ordained with Aaron (chs. 8-9); killed for offering strange fire (10:1-2). Aaron is commanded not to mourn them publicly.
- **Numbers**: Their death is recounted, noting they died without sons, leaving the priesthood to Eleazar and Ithamar (Num 3:4, 26:61).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Jewish**: Rabbinic literature heavily debates the exact nature of their sin, suggesting intoxication, rendering a halakhic decision in front of their teacher Moses, or inappropriate zeal to draw near to the divine.
- **Christian**: Used as a cautionary tale regarding the strictness of God's holiness, the danger of approaching God on one's own terms, and the necessity of proper reverence in worship.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Per commentators like Gordon Wenham, their deaths starkly define the boundaries of holiness. The same divine fire that consumed the acceptable sacrifice in Leviticus 9 consumes the disobedient priests in Leviticus 10. They illustrate the extreme danger of God's presence and the absolute necessity of approaching Him strictly according to divine instruction.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **The Nature of the "Strange Fire"**: Interpreters disagree on whether the fire was from the wrong source, offered at the wrong time, or involved unauthorized incense. The immediate subsequent command forbidding priests to drink alcohol on duty (Lev 10:9) leads some to infer they were drunk.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- Wenham highlights their story as the narrative hinge between the joy of the tabernacle's inauguration and the stringent purity laws that follow.
