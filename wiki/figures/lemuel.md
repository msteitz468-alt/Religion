Lemuel is the king named at the head of **Collection VII** of Proverbs ([[proverbs|Proverbs]]
31:1) — "The sayings of King Lemuel, an oracle that his mother taught him." He is otherwise
unattested and, on [[bruce-waltke|Waltke]]'s reading, probably a non-Israelite proselyte. His
collection preserves the only extended block of maternal royal instruction in the biblical
wisdom corpus, and (on Waltke's view) frames the book's concluding acrostic on the
[[valiant-woman|valiant wife]].

## Biographical Overview

- **Name.** *Lemuʾel* is "probably an otherwise unattested long form of Lael (Num 3:24), which
  means 'belonging to God' or 'dedicated to God'" — fitting the mother's address "son of my vows"
  (31:2). Waltke: were *melek* ("king") a title, Hebrew would require the article ("King
  Lemuel"); as an appositive it "identifies the sayings as 'royal instruction.'"
- **Non-Israelite provenance (Waltke, attributed).** "Since such a king is unattested in Israel's
  history, he is probably a proselyte to Israel's faith." The chapter's foreign loanwords
  (Aramaic *bar*, *melākîn*; Arabic *mah*; Ugaritic forms) "lend credence to the conviction that
  Lemuel and his mother are proselytes." Undatable on internal evidence.
- **The queen mother.** The instruction is delivered by Lemuel's mother — Waltke notes "no
  parallel exists in ancient Near Eastern literatures of a mother's wise sayings to her son,"
  though queen mothers elsewhere "influenced the policies and theological stance of the king."
  Her vow (31:2) "probably refers to a vow … that if God gave her son she would dedicate him to
  live according to God's wisdom (cf. 1 Sam 1:11)."

## Primary Source Appearances

Lemuel appears only in Proverbs 31. The commentary divides the chapter into two poems:

- **"The Noble King" (31:2–9)** — the mother's instruction proper:
  - *Restraint with women (31:3):* not celibacy but a caution against "unrestrained sexual
    gratification" and a large harem — Waltke cites David's lust and Solomon's many wives as
    kings whose sexual entanglements "made [them] callous toward justice" and true religion.
  - *Restraint with wine (31:4–7):* the absolute prohibition (v.4) qualified by v.5 to mean
    drinking "to the point of inebriation," since drunkenness makes a king "forget what is
    decreed" and "change a verdict" that protected the oppressed. Waltke reads the command to
    give strong drink to the perishing (vv.6–7) as **sarcasm** ("not a proposed welfare
    program … 'free beer as an opiate to the masses'"), aiming "to debunk intoxicants as useless."
  - *Defend the poor and the mute (31:8–9):* "Open your mouth" = "speak up"; the "mute" are those
    "socially and economically too weak to defend themselves against the rich and powerful."
    Meinhold: what is said of the king "is valid for each person in his sphere of activity."
- **"The Valiant Wife" (31:10–31)** — the concluding acrostic. On Waltke's authorship view this
  poem also belongs to Lemuel's collection (see below). It is treated fully on its own page:
  [[valiant-woman|the Valiant Woman]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

- **Royal-instruction genre.** Lemuel's sayings "resemble the Egyptian and Babylonian wisdom
  literature that also aim to equip rulers" — Waltke cites the *Instruction for King Meri-ka-re*,
  the *Instruction of Amen-em-het*, and the Babylonian *Advice to a Prince*. The distinctive
  Israelite note is the maternal source and the priority of protecting the poor: "the queen's
  admonitions primarily urge her royal son to preserve the current statutes that protect the poor
  (vv.4–5) and to produce new statutes to protect them (vv.8–9)." This aligns Collection VII with
  the just-rulership theology of chs 28–29 (see [[proverbs|Proverbs]]).
- **Canonization.** "The inspired editor of the Book of Proverbs added Lemuel's wisdom to the
  developing canon of Scripture" — the same "developing canon" claim Waltke draws from Agur
  (30:1–6). See [[agur|Agur]].

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Did Lemuel author the whole chapter, or only 31:2–9?** This is the central dispute. Many
  scholars credit only "The Noble King" (vv.2–9) to Lemuel's mother, excluding the valiant-wife
  acrostic "because of its distinct form and structure, its separation from verses 1–9 in the
  LXX, and an endemic scholarly skepticism about the Bible's own claims to its authorship."
  **Waltke's position:** Lemuel is "the ordering mind behind the whole chapter." His arguments:
  (1) the *orphan* argument — "if Lemuel is not the author of 'The Valiant Wife,' it is a unique
  orphan in Proverbs … it lacks a superscription ascribing its authorship"; (2) diverse forms do
  not prove diverse authorship (as Agur mixed confession and numerical sayings into a unity). He
  marshals Kitchen (a 9-verse "work" would be "ludicrously brief") and M. Lichtenstein (verbal
  and thematic links across the two poems: women at vv.3, 10; *ḥayil* at vv.3, 10; "open the
  mouth" at vv.8, 9, 26; defense of the poor at vv.9, 20). (Positions recorded, not adjudicated.)
- **"King of Massa" (Delitzsch), rejected.** Delitzsch emends *maśśāʾ* ("oracle") to a place name
  ("Lemuel, king of Massa"), thinking the apposition of *melek* to "sayings" impossible. Waltke
  keeps MT: the apposition "identifies the sayings as 'royal instruction.'"
- **The LXX's Solomonic "fiction."** The LXX reinterprets 31:1 and relocates 30:15–31:9 to keep
  the book under Solomon's name, subsuming the valiant-wife poem under "the miscellaneous
  instructions of Solomon" (25:1). Waltke judges the LXX "clearly secondary." See [[solomon|Solomon]].
