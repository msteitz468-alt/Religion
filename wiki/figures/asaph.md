# Asaph

## Biographical Overview
Asaph is an eponymous Levite musician and "seer" (prophet) who, according to the Chronicler, was appointed by King David to lead the musical worship before the Ark of the Covenant in Jerusalem. He is the founding head of the "Sons of Asaph," one of the major guilds of temple singers that survived the Babylonian exile and participated in the postexilic restoration.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **1 Chronicles 15-16**: Asaph is appointed by David alongside Heman and Ethan as chief musicians playing bronze cymbals when the ark is brought to Jerusalem. David delivers a psalm of thanksgiving into the hand of Asaph and his brethren (1 Chr 16:7).
- **2 Chronicles 29:30**: King Hezekiah commands the Levites to sing praises "with the words of David and of Asaph the seer," establishing Asaph's prophetic authority alongside David.
- **Ezra 2:41 / Nehemiah 7:44**: The "Sons of Asaph" return from the Babylonian exile as the official guild of temple singers.
- **Psalms**: Twelve psalms are attributed to Asaph (Ps 50, and the block of Pss 73–83). These psalms frequently invoke God as judge, emphasize the covenant and Israel's history, and employ prophetic and wisdom motifs.

## Tradition-Specific Reception
**Judaism and Christianity**: Asaph is recognized not just as a musician but as a prophetic voice (a "seer") whose psalms address some of the deepest theological crises in the canon. Psalm 73 is a cornerstone of biblical theodicy, and Psalm 79 is a pivotal communal lament over the destruction of Jerusalem. 

## Theological and Narrative Significance
In the canonical shape of the Psalter, the Asaphite psalms dominate the beginning of Book Three (Pss 73-83). Their placement is highly significant: they move the Psalter's narrative from the glorious Davidic-Solomonic era of Books One and Two into the period of the divided kingdom, national decline, and the trauma of exile. Through Asaph's voice, the community processes the failure of national institutions, turning from individual lament (Ps 73) to communal devastation (Ps 74, 79).
