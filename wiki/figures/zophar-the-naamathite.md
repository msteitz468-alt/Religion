# Zophar the Naamathite

The third of [[job|Job]]'s comforters in the [[book-of-job|book of Job]] and the only one who speaks
twice rather than three times. He is the friend who first concludes outright that Job is guilty, and
his silence in the third cycle is — on [[john-hartley|Hartley]]'s reading — a characterization, not
merely a textual accident.

## Biographical Overview

Zophar is introduced with the others at 2:11, identified by home rather than genealogy.

Hartley: *"Zophar, meaning 'young bird,' came from Naamah… A possible identification for Zophar's
home is the site 'Ain Ṣopar on the road between Beirut and Damascus"* ([p. 86], on 2:11). The name
[Naamah] is shared in the same discussion with two persons — the Cainite woman of Gen. 4:22 and
Solomon's Ammonite wife — who are distinct referents and should not be conflated with Zophar's town.
See [[uz|Uz]] for the wider geography.

On his standing among the friends Hartley is careful. *"While many have considered him to be the
youngest of Job's companions, little evidence supports this view. All the friends appear to be
contemporaries. Nevertheless, Zophar bears the least status, for he delivers only two speeches"*
([p. 193]). Status, not youth, is the operative variable.

## Primary Source Appearances

Zophar speaks twice. Hartley records 40 lines for the first speech and 56 for the second.

- **First speech (11:1–20).** A rebuke of Job's verbosity, a hymnic passage on God's unsearchable
  wisdom (vv. 7–12), a call to repentance with promises attached (vv. 13–19), and a closing threat
  (v. 20).
- **Second speech (20:1–29).** A single sustained discourse on the certain, swift downfall of the
  wicked, ending in the divine lawsuit imagery of vv. 27–29.
- **No third speech.** Nothing in chs. 22–27 is assigned to Zophar on Hartley's reconstruction.

### Method

Hartley distinguishes Zophar's epistemology from the other two friends: *"Unlike Eliphaz, Zophar does
not appeal to a mystical experience, and unlike Bildad, he does not recall the traditions of the
fathers. Instead, his basis is reasoned theology. He reasons deductively from a set of cherished
propositions"* ([p. 193]). This makes him the most systematic and, on Hartley's account, the least
flexible of the three.

Hartley also flags that Zophar misquotes Job. The supposed quotation at 11:4 *"is obviously his own
construction… Zophar is caricaturing Job's view in an attempt to make it look even more absurd. Job
never claims that his doctrine is pure."* The correction matters for the whole book: *"Job… prefers
the word* tām*, 'blameless' (9:20–21), a word that means personal integrity more than spotless
purity. **This is important, for Job never says that he has not sinned. His position is that he has
not committed any transgression worthy of such punishment.** Zophar then has misinterpreted Job in
quoting him as asserting his own moral purity"* ([p. 195]).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

This wiki's ingested sources carry no material on Jewish, Christian-patristic, or Islamic reception
of Zophar as a figure. The NICOT volume treats him exegetically and does not survey his afterlife in
any commentary tradition.

Within the book he is condemned with the other two at 42:7 — *"Yahweh holds all of them responsible
for the implications of their teachings and exhortations to Job"* ([p. 538]) — and then obeys,
letting Job offer sacrifice on his behalf.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The character's position

In the text Zophar holds that God assuredly punishes every evildoer, and therefore that Job's
suffering is not merely deserved but *under*-measured. Hartley on 11:6: *"God is punishing him for
only part of his wrongdoing, not for all of it… **Therefore, Zophar is the first of the friends to
conclude that Job has sinned even beyond the extent of his punishment**"* ([p. 197]). Hartley reads
the closing verse of the first speech as calculated pressure: *"this picture functions as a threat to
Job. Should he not repent, such will be his end. Zophar ends with this threat in order to overcome
any hesitation to repentance on Job's part."* He also detects an insinuation at 11:14 that *"Job's
wealth came through extortion of the less fortunate"* — the accusation [[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz]]
will later make explicit at 22:6–9.

The second speech is, in Hartley's analysis, a direct answer to Job's boldest confession: *"He is
indirectly rejecting Job's assertion that God will appear as his Redeemer to vindicate him… In his
view Job's hope is false, and it is deluding him"* ([p. 299]; see [[job-19-25-redeemer|Job 19:25]]).
Where Job pins everything on a heavenly witness, Zophar turns the same courtroom against him: *"the
heavens and the earth will rise up as witnesses and testify against the accused… This metaphor
recalls the imagery of the divine lawsuit"* ([p. 308]).

Hartley draws out one precise theological formulation from 20:15: *"While wicked deeds contain their
own punishment, God himself activates that punishment. It is important to observe that in this manner
of reasoning no clear distinction is maintained between primary and secondary causes."* See
[[doctrine-of-retribution|the doctrine of retribution]].

### Hartley's verdict

Hartley's judgment is explicit and negative, and parallels the one he passes on Eliphaz:

> *"It is clear that Zophar has taken the final step in the logic of the doctrine of retribution,
> concluding that Job is suffering for some hidden sin…"*
>
> *"**Zophar unwittingly aligns himself with the Satan's position found in the prologue by
> encouraging Job to seek God for personal gain. Unfortunately Zophar is blind to the implications of
> his reasoning. His failure shows that spiritual counsel must be offered in compassionate love,
> taking into account the peculiarities of a specific situation in order for it to bear the peaceable
> fruits of righteousness.**"* ([p. 204])

Two friends, on Hartley's reading, end up arguing [[satan|the Satan]]'s side of the prologue wager
without knowing it: Eliphaz and Zophar. Hartley also notes Zophar's coldness — *"With no apparent
sympathy for Job's lament, he coldly reasons that Job's present punishment is only partial, tempered
by God's abundant mercy"* — and that his implied charge is a specific one: *"In his view Job's crime
would be oppressing the poor to amass his great wealth"* ([p. 309]).

### Why there is no third speech

The absence of a third Zophar speech is the point Hartley presses hardest on this figure, and he
gives a *positive literary* reason for it rather than treating it purely as textual damage. Closing
his comment on the second speech:

> *"Perhaps then he has abandoned hope for Job and is defining his fate and its significance in order
> that Job might be aware of the outcome of his affliction. **If this is true, Zophar has closed his
> thinking in reference to Job's guilt. That is why he would have no reason to deliver a third
> speech. Having made up his mind that Job's fate is sealed, he would have nothing more to say.**"*
> ([p. 309])

Hartley supports the same conclusion from speech-length data — the other friends' speeches shorten
progressively while *"Zophar's second speech is significantly longer than his first (56 lines
compared to 40 lines). The author made Zophar's second speech longer as a signal that Zophar would
not speak again"* — and from character: *"since Zophar is the least creative of the three comforters
and the most certain that Job is guilty, there would be little loss if he delivered only two
speeches."*

Zophar's silence is therefore, in Hartley's overall reconstruction, the one feature of the disordered
third cycle that requires *no* emendation at all. This wiki records his account among the positions
on that dispute without adjudicating it — see [[job-third-cycle-integrity|the third-cycle integrity
controversy]].

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Hartley offers no argument about Zophar's historicity; he is treated throughout as a character in a
work set, by authorial design, in the patriarchal period. The identification of Naamah with 'Ain
Ṣopar is offered as *"a possible identification,"* not a settled one.

The live controversy attaching to Zophar is entirely the third-cycle question: whether his silence is
authorial characterization (Hartley), or whether a third Zophar speech once existed and has been lost
or absorbed into the surrounding chapters. Hartley reconstructs nothing for Zophar, in contrast with
commentators who assign him material from chs. 24–27.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Zophar's contribution to the book's argument is the hard edge of retribution logic, and Hartley
tracks its downstream effects. Job rejects Zophar's proverb personally at 12:3 and answers his
inaccessible-wisdom hymn at 12:7–9 with the counter-claim that God teaches through creation.
Structurally more important, Hartley finds Zophar's language reused in the reconstructed third speech
of [[bildad-the-shuhite|Bildad]]: 27:13–23 *"draws heavily on Zophar's words found in his last
speech (ch. 20)"* ([p. 359]), sharing the *ḥēleq*/*naḥălâ* and *rāšāʿ*/*ʿārîṣ* pairs of 20:29. On
Hartley's account the author has Bildad recycle Eliphaz in one half of his last speech and Zophar in
the other, *"to intimate that the comforters have nothing more to say."*
