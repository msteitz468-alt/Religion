# Rammohun Roy

**Raja Rammohun Roy** (1774–1833) was a Bengali Brahmin polyglot, social reformer, and religious
innovator who is often called "the Father of Modern India." He founded the **[[brahmo-samaj|Brahmo
Samaj]]** ("Society of God") in 1828, led the campaign against [[suttee|suttee]] (widow immolation),
and may have been the **first person to use the word "Hinduism"** to describe the religion (in 1816).

## Biographical Overview

Roy knew **Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Sanskrit**, and his native Bangla (Bengali). He
read the scriptures of multiple religions and concluded that there was "not much difference between
them" — a position that informed his eclectic theology. He settled in Calcutta in 1814, becoming
prominent in the movement for Western-style education: he urged Hindus to study mathematics, natural
philosophy, chemistry, anatomy, and "other useful sciences."

He always wore the sacred thread of a Brahmin and observed most Brahmin customs, but his theology
diverged sharply from Puranic Hinduism.

He visited Europe — one of the first upper-class Hindus to do so — and made a strong impression on
British and French intellectuals. He died in Bristol in 1833.

## Theology: Reformed Monotheism

Roy's theology drew on multiple sources:

- **Upanishadic Hinduism**: the monistic Absolute of the Upanishads (which he translated into Bangla
  in 1825) as the foundation of true Hinduism
- **Islam**: its strict monotheism shaped his opposition to polytheism and image worship
- **18th-century deism**: belief in a transcendent Creator God reachable through reason
- **Unitarianism**: emphasis on God's essential oneness
- **Freemasonry**: some deistic elements

He was fundamentally opposed to **image worship** (*pūjā*), polytheism, and the "external" practices
of Puranic religion — temples, pilgrimage, sacrifice. This positioned him as one of the first
Hindus to internalize what British Protestant critics said about "degraded" Hinduism — though he
framed it as a *return* to original Vedic monotheism rather than an adoption of foreign standards.

## The Campaign Against Suttee

Roy wrote two tracts against suttee:

1. *A Conference between an Advocate for, and an Opponent of the Practice of Burning Widows Alive*
   (two parts, 1818 and 1820) — written as a dialogue (the classical Hindu form) arguing against
   suttee *from within the Hindu scriptural tradition*: Manu does not mandate suttee; several dharma
   texts condemn it; the few late commentaries that endorse it are not authoritative.

2. *Abstract of the Arguments Regarding the Burning of Widows Considered as a Religious Rite* (1830)

He also wrote *Brief Remarks Regarding Modern Encroachments on the Ancient Rights of Females* — on
women's rights to property (arguing from both Mitakshara and Dayabhaga law).

Roy's writings may have been a major factor prompting the British to ban suttee in 1829. He was
unwilling to endorse government interference in religion directly but argued that suttee was not
genuinely required by scripture and that ascetic widowhood was the proper alternative.

## Brahmo Samaj

Roy founded the **Brahmo Samaj** in 1828, based on the doctrines of the Upanishads. Key
characteristics:

- Strict monotheism; rejection of image worship and polytheism
- Universal human reason as the path to God
- Social reform (opposition to caste discrimination, suttee, child marriage)
- Openness to Western education and scientific rationalism

After Roy's death, the Brahmo Samaj was led by Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905) and then by Keshab
Chunder Sen, who abolished caste within the society and admitted women as members. The Brahmo Samaj
had profound influence on the Bengali intellectual class and on Hindu reform more broadly.

## Significance in the History of Hinduism

Roy represents the complex phenomenon Doniger calls **"colonial Stockholm syndrome"**: some highly
placed Hindus so admired their British colonizers that they absorbed the Protestant critique of
"degraded" Hinduism and developed new forms of Hindu religion shaped by British Protestant standards.
The Brahmo Samaj elevated the Gita and Upanishads (Protestant-legible texts) and denigrated the
Puranas, image worship, and erotic mythology.

Yet Roy's project was also genuinely his own: his synthesis of Upanishadic philosophy, Islamic
monotheism, and Western deism was not simple imitation but a creative reformulation that took Hindu
resources seriously on their own terms.
