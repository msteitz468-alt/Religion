# Basilides

Basilides was an early-second-century Alexandrian Christian teacher, founder of one of the major
[[gnostics|Gnostic]] schools, active roughly a generation before [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of
Alexandria]]. His own works survive only in quotation and report; he is a recurring foil in Clement's
[[stromata|Stromata]], which preserves several otherwise-lost fragments of him and of his son and
pupil **Isidore**.

## What Clement Reports

Clement engages Basilides chiefly on three fronts:

- **Faith as a natural endowment of the elect.** Basilides and his followers "hold faith to be
  natural" and tied to a "supramundane selection" — the elect are "saved by nature." Clement refutes
  this at length: faith must be a free, voluntary act, not an endowment given to some by nature (see
  [[freedom|free will]]). This is one of Clement's principal arguments against the school.
- **A martyrdom theodicy** (quoted from the 23rd book of Basilides' *Exegetics*, *Strom.* IV): those
  who suffer are punished for sins — even, Basilides held, sins of a previous life or a latent "sinful
  principle"; a martyr who seems sinless is still "bad, on account of his wishing to sin." Basilides
  would "affirm anything rather than call Providence evil." Clement rejects this as effectively
  deifying the devil and calling the Lord "a sinful man," while noting Basilides at least preserves
  divine providence. The doctrine implies a form of transmigration.
- **Isidore's ethics of continence.** Clement quotes Isidore's *Ethics* on restraining "the fire of
  the seed," and pointedly uses the stricter Basilides/Isidore against the *libertine* Basilidians
  "who do not live purely" — distinguishing the teachers from their lax followers (*Strom.* III).

Basilides also appears in Clement's heresy-lists alongside [[valentinus|Valentinus]], Marcion, and
others as belonging to the sects that arose "in the times of Hadrian... and continued till the age of
the elder Antoninus" — later, Clement argues, than the one ancient apostolic Church.

## Wider Tradition

Basilides is also reported (with significant differences) by Irenaeus and Hippolytus, who describe an
elaborate cosmology of the unbegotten Father, the *Nous*, and a graded series of heavens (the famous
"365 heavens," with the name *Abrasax*). Those systems lie outside Clement's account and are not drawn
from the present source. Clement's value is as a comparatively sober witness who quotes Basilides and
Isidore directly and on ethical rather than cosmological questions.
