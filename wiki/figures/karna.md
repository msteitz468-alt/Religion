Karna is the great tragic hero of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], a matchless warrior whose life is defined by curses, concealed identity, and fierce loyalty to Duryodhana despite knowing the Pandavas are his half-brothers. He is simultaneously a figure of grandeur and pathos in Rajagopalachari's retelling.

## Biographical Overview

Karna is the son of the Sun-god (Surya) and Kunti. Born with divine armour (kavacha) and golden earrings (kundala) as natural endowments, he was abandoned by his mother, who placed him in a sealed box and set it afloat on a river out of shame. The box was found by a childless charioteer named Adhiratha, whose wife Radha raised the infant as her own. Karna is thus known as **Radheya** — son of Radha — throughout his life, and his origin as a foundling charioteer's son is the source of the social contempt he suffers from warriors who regard him as low-born. The Glossary identifies him as "a matchless warrior, son of the Sun-god and Kunti. Disciple of Parasurama. Also son of Radha, his foster-mother, and was known as Radheya." (Glossary, lines 11804–11806)

## Primary Source Appearances

### Battle with Bhima (Ch. LXXXVI)

The contest between Karna and Bhima on the fourteenth day is drawn in striking contrast. Rajagopalachari describes Karna's "handsome lotus-like face radiant with smiles when he attacked Bhima saying: 'Do not show your back,' 'Now, do not flee like a coward.'" Karna "did everything with a smiling air of ease whereas Bhima's face glowed with rage." (Ch. LXXXVI, lines 9443–9451) Karna fights on horseback, discharging well-aimed shafts while Bhima attempts to close with him physically.

As Duryodhana sends brothers one by one to save Karna, they fall dead in his defence. When Karna sees "so many of the sons of Dhritarashtra sacrificing themselves for him, his face was wet with tears and he mounted a fresh chariot and began to attack Bhima with deadly effect." (lines 9543–9545) Eighteen bows are broken during the battle. At the climax, Bhima is deprived of all arms and weapons; Karna taunts him: "Foolish glutton, you do not know the science of war... Get away!" But "mindful of his word to Kunti, forbore from killing him." (lines 9591–9596) The Glossary-framing is confirmed: "He had pledged his word to Kunti not to kill more than one of the Pandavas and he reserved that option for great Arjuna." (lines 9601–9603)

### The Death of Karna (Ch. XCI)

After Drona's death, the Kaurava princes install Karna as Generalissimo. He mounts his "gorgeous war chariot driven by Salya." (line 9952) His bearing heartens the Kauravas.

The decisive encounter with Arjuna is preceded by Karna sending a "dazzling arrow, which spat fire and made for Arjuna, like a serpent with its flaming double-tongue." Krishna saves Arjuna by pressing the chariot five fingers deep in the mud at the last instant. Then — as had been foretold — "the left wheel of his chariot suddenly sank in the bloody mire." (lines 9997–10003) Karna appeals to Arjuna: "Wait a minute! My chariot has sunk in the ground. Great warrior as you are, and knowing dharma as you do, you would certainly not take unfair advantage of this accident." (lines 10006–10009)

Krishna intervenes with a prolonged denunciation of Karna's past conduct — his participation in Draupadi's humiliation, the gambling conspiracy, the killing of Abhimanyu, and more. Karna "bent his head in shame and uttered not a word." (lines 10035–10036) He tries to recall the mantras of astras learnt from Parasurama but "his memory failed in the hour of his need, even as Parasurama had foretold." (lines 10044–10046) Arjuna kills him. Rajagopalachari adds an authorial note: "The poet had not the heart to impute this act to Arjuna who was the embodiment of nobility. It was the Lord Krishna that incited Arjuna to kill Karna when he was vainly trying to raise his chariot out of the mud." (lines 10052–10055)

### Revelation of Birth and Yudhishthira's Grief (Ch. XCVII)

After the battle, Yudhishthira confesses to Narada: "we took our own brother for an enemy and killed him, even Karna who stood rooted like a rock in his honour and at whose valour the world wondered." He recounts that "Karna's feet were so much like our mother's feet. In the large hall, when that great outrage was committed and my anger rose, when I looked at his feet, which were so much like Kunti's feet, my wrath subsided." (lines 9599–9609)

Narada then recounts the curses that shaped Karna's fate:

1. **Drona's refusal and Parasurama's deception**: Karna sought the Brahmastra from Drona, who declined since the teaching was reserved for brahmanas or penitent kshatriyas. Karna went to Parasurama, claiming to be a brahmana, and was taught archery and astras. When an insect bored into Karna's thigh while Parasurama slept on his lap, Karna bore the pain silently so as not to wake his teacher. Parasurama woke to find blood and recognized from the endurance that his pupil was a kshatriya. He pronounced the curse: "When your hour comes, your knowledge of astras will fail you and what you have learnt from me through deception will not avail you." (lines 9613–9637)

2. **The brahmana's cow**: While practising in the forest near Parasurama's ashrama, Karna accidentally killed a brahmana's cow. The brahmana cursed: "In battle, your chariot-wheels will stick in the mud and you will be done to death, even like this innocent cow which you have killed." (lines 9621–9624)

3. **Indra's gift of armour**: Indra, Arjuna's divine father, came in brahman disguise and begged Karna to give up his divinely-born earrings and armour. Karna gave them away. "From that time, Karna's strength was reduced." (lines 9641–9645)

Narada concludes: "Karna's pledge to his mother Kunti that he would not kill more than one of the five of you, Parasurama's curse, the anger of the brahmana whose cow was killed by Karna, the way in which his charioteer Salya depressed him by underrating his valour and Vasudeva's stratagems, these combined to bring about Karna's end." (lines 9646–9651)

Kunti adds: "His father, the sun lord himself, pleaded with him. He begged of him to give up the wicked-hearted Duryodhana and join you. I too tried hard. But he would not listen to us. He brought his end on himself." (lines 9653–9656) Yudhishthira, in anguish, curses women for being unable to keep secrets — this is Rajagopalachari's explanation of the tradition that Kunti's concealment of Karna's birth was the proximate cause of fratricidal war. (lines 9657–9670)

### Karna in Yudhishthira's Final Trial (Ch. CVI)

In Yudhishthira's passage through the heavenly regions, his voice is among those crying out from the apparent hell where his brothers suffer. When Yama/Dharma reveals the test, he says: "Neither the illustrious Savyasachi nor your beloved brother Bhima is really in hell. Nor Karna, the just, not any one else who you thought had been consigned to suffering." (lines 11479–11481) At the reunion, Yudhishthira sees "Karna and all his brothers" serene and free from anger. (lines 11487–11488)

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Rajagopalachari's retelling presents Karna as a figure of genuine greatness whose tragedy is multi-layered: his birth secret bars him from the status he deserves, his loyalty to a wrongheaded patron is absolute, and his honour coexists with participation in injustice. Rajagopalachari explicitly softens the moral blot on Arjuna by attributing the decision to kill Karna in his disadvantaged state to [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]]'s command. The editorial comment — "the poet had not the heart to impute this act to Arjuna who was the embodiment of nobility" — signals Rajagopalachari reading the original Vyasa through this lens. (Note: this is Rajagopalachari's interpretive frame, not a claim from the classical source independently.)

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Karna embodies the tragic tension between loyalty ([[dharma]] to a friend and patron) and kinship ([[dharma]] to blood relatives). His consistent choice to stand by Duryodhana despite knowing the Pandavas are kin — and despite his own mother and divine father urging him to switch sides — makes him a figure of flawed heroic integrity. His curses trace a pattern of concealment and its consequences: the concealment of his birth, the concealment of his caste to learn from Parasurama, and his hidden knowledge of kinship.

The episode in which Karna gives away his divine armour and earrings to Indra is paradigmatic of his generosity, a virtue Rajagopalachari stresses throughout. He bears the social contempt of low birth but never retaliates by abandoning his personal code. His restraint in battle with the Pandavas — keeping his pledge to kill only one — costs him strategic advantage.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Karna is a legendary figure of the epic tradition with no independent historical attestation. The question of his true caste — is he kshatriya by nature (born with armour, son of the Sun-god) or sutaputra (son of a charioteer) by upbringing — is a running tension in the narrative. Rajagopalachari's retelling does not resolve this but presents it as part of Karna's tragic identity.

The ethics of his death are explicitly flagged as a problem in this retelling: killing a man whose weapon is stuck in the mud violates the laws of war that Karna himself invokes. Rajagopalachari treats this as an instance of the broader lesson that "the battle for right, conducted through physical force leads to numerous wrongs and, in the net result, adharma increases." (lines 10059–10062)
