# Draupadī

**Draupadī** (also Kṛṣṇā — "the Dark Woman," for her dark complexion) is the wife of all five
Pāṇḍava brothers and one of the most remarkable female figures in all of Sanskrit literature. Born
from fire, motherless, and worshipped as a goddess in South India, she is the feminist counterweight
to [[sita|Sītā]] in the Hindu imagination: where Sītā is the model of wifely fidelity and suffering
endured in silence, Draupadī is "smart, aggressive, steadfast, eloquent, tough as nails, and resilient."
"How different the lives of actual women in India would have been had Draupadī, instead of Sītā, been
their official role model! Many Hindus name their daughters Sītā, but few name them Draupadī."

## Birth

Draupadī's father, King Drupada, performed a great sacrifice to produce a son who would kill his
enemy Drona. The priest made the oblation into the fire while the queen was still preparing herself.
A son (Dhṛṣṭadyumna) arose from the fire — and then, unasked for, a daughter: **Draupadī**. A
disembodied voice declared: "This superb woman will be the death of the Kshatriyas." She is
**born of no womb** (*ayonijā*), like [[sita|Sītā]], born of the earth: both are elemental goddesses
incarnate in mortal form.

She is named **Kṛṣṇā** for her dark skin. The name links her to Krishna (also Kṛṣṇa) and to Kālī.

## Polyandry

Draupadī is married to all five Pāṇḍava brothers — Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva.
The Mahābhārata offers three separate justifications for this scandalous arrangement:

1. **The mother's inadvertent command**: Arjuna won Draupadī and called out "Look what we got!" to his
   mother, who said "Share it among you all" without looking up.
2. **Divine precedent**: The five Pāṇḍavas are all incarnations of the single god Indra; Draupadī is
   the incarnation of Śrī (prosperity, Indra's wife) — so the polyandry is cosmically appropriate.
3. **Her own previous life**: In a previous life, she had asked Śiva for "a virtuous husband" five
   times in succession; Śiva granted her five husbands and restored her virginity for each husband.

Doniger reads the persistent polyandry in the lineage of Mahābhārata heroines (Satyavatī, Kuntī, Madri,
Madhāvī, Draupadī) as "a remarkably positive fantasy of female equality, which is to say, a major
resistance to patriarchy" — even if it also represented "male redactors' nightmare vision of where all
that autonomy might lead."

## The Disrobing

The crisis moment of the Mahābhārata: the Kauravas have Draupadī dragged into the assembly hall after
Yudhiṣṭhira has gambled away first his kingdom, then his brothers, then Draupadī herself. The villain
Duḥśāsana attempts to **strip her sari** before the assembled court. Every time he pulls off a layer,
another appears to cover her, until there is a great heap of silk beside him and he gives up — her
chastity protecting her miraculously.

The Kauravas justify their assault by calling her a slut: "a woman who sleeps with five men must be
promiscuous." Yet the text never vindicates these insults; all her children are legitimate, and her
unwavering devotion to her husband(s) is emphasized.

## Role in the Epic

Draupadī's humiliation at the disrobing is the emotional engine that drives the Pāṇḍavas toward war.
"She is the heart and soul of the Pandavas." The prediction that she "will lead the Kshatriyas to
their destruction" is fulfilled — the great battle that destroys nearly all the warrior class is, at
its emotional root, revenge for her dishonor.

Her character is consistently active: she questions, pressures, and drives her husbands rather than
enduring passively. When Yudhiṣṭhira wants to renounce the world after the war, she refuses to accept
resignation as an answer.

## Goddess Cult

Draupadī became a **goddess with a sect of her own**, worshipped throughout South India — "primarily
by lower castes, Pariahs, and Muslims." She is closely connected with the dark goddess **Kālī** and
is part of a wider tradition of fire-born, dark, peripheral goddesses (the *Mātr̥kās* or "Little
Mothers") who are dangerous especially to children and who represent the unchained, demonic feminine
that the Brahmin imaginary typically suppresses.

The Mahābhārata mentions other dark goddesses in its later layers (Mahā-Kālī), suggesting that
Draupadī's cult is indigenous and pre-textual, absorbed into the epic rather than generated by it.

**Puranic confirmation:** The early Puranas (c. 5th–7th c. CE) are explicit where the Mahābhārata
was implicit: Draupadī "was a goddess from the start" (*āditya-śakti*). The buffalo-sacrifice ritual
associated with the Draupadī cult (particularly in Tamil Nadu) is directly analogous to — and
probably continuous with — the buffalo sacrifice of [[durga|Chandika/Durga]] described in the
*Devīmāhātmya*: both goddesses receive blood sacrifice, both were born "without a womb" (*ayonijā*),
and both are paradigmatic tooth-goddess figures absorbed into an epic/Puranic frame that incompletely
domesticated them.
