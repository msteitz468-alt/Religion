# Yājñavalkya

**Yājñavalkya** is the foremost sage of the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad** and, in
[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta's]] account (Vol. I, Ch. III), the chief voice of the
classical Upaniṣadic doctrine of the self. He appears chiefly at the court of **King Janaka** of Videha
and in dialogue with his wife **Maitreyī** and the woman philosopher **Gārgī**.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **The negative method (*neti neti*).** Yājñavalkya gives the Upaniṣads' defining characterization of
  [[brahman|Brahman]]/[[atman|ātman]] by *denial*: "He the ātman is not this, nor this (*neti neti*) — he
  is inconceivable, for he cannot be conceived; unchangeable, for he is not changed; untouched, for
  nothing touches him" (Bṛh. IV.5.15). (Dasgupta notes in passing that Deussen, Müller and Roer
  misread *asito* in this verse as a participle, where it is the ablative of *asi*, "a sword.")
- **Maitreyī dialogue** (Bṛh. II.4, IV.5): the teaching that the self is dearer than son, wealth, or
  spouse — that all things are dear "for the sake of the self" — and the image of phenomenal knowledge
  dissolving in the self "like a lump of salt in water," leaving "no consciousness after death" in the
  phenomenal sense.
- **On transmigration** (Bṛh. IV.4): the self at death gathers its senses and, "as a caterpillar reaching
  the tip of a blade draws itself over… as a goldsmith refashions gold into a newer, fairer form,"
  fashions a new body. Here Yājñavalkya makes **desire (*kāma*), not [[karma|karma]], the root of rebirth**
  — "as he desires, so he wills; as he wills, so he acts" — and emancipation the extinction of desire
  (see [[samsara|saṃsāra]], [[moksha|mokṣa]]).

## Doniger's Reading — Women, Non-Brahmins, and the Upaniṣadic Reform

[[wendy-doniger|Doniger]] (*[[doniger-the-hindus|The Hindus]]*, Ch. 7) foregrounds the social dimensions
of Yājñavalkya's appearances in the Upaniṣads, which Dasgupta's philosophical account brackets:

**Maitreyī and Katyāyanī.** The famous vignette of Yājñavalkya's two wives (BU 2.4 and 4.5) is, for
Doniger, a feminist passage as well as a theological one. He is about to leave for the wandering ascetic
life and proposes to divide his property between the two women. **Maitreyī** — "a woman who took part in
theological discussions" — refuses the property and asks instead for the knowledge of immortality.
**Katyāyanī** — "whose understanding was limited to women's affairs" — never even appears in the dialogue;
"presumably she takes care of the house (which, also presumably, she will inherit when their husband
abandons both wives to take the ascetic path) while the other woman talks theology." Doniger reads this as
the Upaniṣads opening the door to women of intellectual talent while keeping it shut for most.

**Gārgī — the feistiest woman in the Upaniṣads.** Gārgī Vācaknavī is a woman philosopher who publicly
questions Yājñavalkya in the presence of distinguished Brahmins. She likens herself to "a fierce warrior,
stringing his unstrung bow and taking two deadly arrows in his hand, rising to challenge an enemy" — "an
extraordinarily masculine, and violent, simile for a woman." When she presses him past a safe point, he
says, "Don't ask too many questions, Gārgī, or your head will shatter apart!" But when he answers her
questions to her satisfaction, she cries out: "Distinguished Brahmins! You should count yourselves lucky
if you escape from this man without paying him anything more than your respects. None of you will ever
defeat him in a theological debate (BU 3.8)." Doniger: "This is one tough lady, cast from the same mold
as Urvashi and, later, Draupadī."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Yājñavalkya is the personification, in Dasgupta's reading, of the Upaniṣadic turn from sacrifice to
self-knowledge: the sage who can answer (or out-silence) every questioner about Brahman, and who locates
ultimate reality in the witnessing self — "the seer of all seeing, the hearer of all hearing," which
"sees but is not seen." His *neti neti* becomes foundational for [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]'s apophatic
account of *nirguṇa* Brahman.
