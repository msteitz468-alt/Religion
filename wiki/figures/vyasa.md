Vyasa (also Vedavyasa, Krishna-Dvaipayana) is simultaneously the legendary author of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], compiler of the Vedas, and a recurring character within the text itself — appearing at moments of crisis to console, instruct, and guide the Pandavas. The Glossary identifies him as "Compiler of the Vedas, son of sage Parasara." (line 12277) He stands apart from all factions as a wise grandfather figure whose counsel is solicited by both sides.

## Biographical Overview

According to the Glossary, "Vaisampayana — Chief disciple of sage Vyasa who revealed the epic for the benefit of humanity." (lines 12202–12203) The epic's narrative frame is thus: Vyasa composed the Mahabharata, dictated it to Ganapati (Ganesha) as amanuensis — "Ganapati — Amanuensis of Vyasa who agreed to write down without pause or hesitation the story of the Mahabharata dictated by Vyasa" (lines 11696–11698) — and Vaisampayana later recited it to king Janamejaya. In Rajagopalachari's retelling, the narrator at times explicitly cites "Vaisampayana, who recited Vyasa's Mahabharata to the king." (line 11093) His son Suka is noted in the Glossary as the sage "who related the Srimad Bhagavata to King Parikshit, grandson of Arjuna." (lines 12163–12164)

## Primary Source Appearances

### Consoling Yudhishthira after Abhimanyu's Death (Ch. LXXXI)

Rajagopalachari introduces a characteristic role at lines 8865–8888: "It was always the custom with Vyasa to come and comfort the Pandavas, whenever they were in great sorrow. He was their great teacher as well as grandsire." After Abhimanyu's death, Vyasa appears before the grief-stricken Yudhishthira and offers a cosmological teaching on the nature of death: Brahma created life and was filled with anxiety about overpopulation; the resulting fire threatened all creation until Rudra intervened, and Brahma controlled it into the law of Death. "Death is thus an inescapable law of existence, ordained for the good of the world. It is not true wisdom to be impatient with Death or to grieve immoderately for those who die." (lines 8884–8886) Having delivered this consolation, "Krishna-Dwaipayana retired." (line 8888)

### As Ashvatthama's Shield (Ch. XCV)

When the Pandavas search for Ashvatthama after the night massacre, they find him "on the bank of the Ganga, hiding himself behind Vyasa." (line 10469–10470) Vyasa functions here as a kind of sanctuary figure — Ashvatthama shelters behind the authority of the grandfather sage. The confrontation then occurs in Vyasa's presence.

### Consoling Dhritarashtra at Kurukshetra (Ch. XCVI)

After the battle, Vyasa approaches the grieving Dhritarashtra at Kurukshetra: "Dear son, there is nothing that you do not know and which you have to learn from me. You know very well that all living beings must die. This great battle came to reduce earth's burden as I have heard from lord Vishnu Himself. That is why this calamity could not be prevented. Henceforth, Yudhishthira is your son. You should try to love him and in that way bear the burden of life, giving up grief." (lines 10509–10515)

### Advising Yudhishthira to Rule (Ch. XCVIII)

When Yudhishthira is paralyzed by remorse and wishes to renounce the kingdom and go to the forest to do penance, "Vyasa spoke to Yudhishthira at length and explained where his duty lay, pointing out precedents, and persuaded him to go to the city and take up the burden of ruling the land." (lines 10698–10700)

### The Brihaspati Story (Ch. XCIX)

"After the libation ceremony for Bhishma was over, Vyasa narrated to grief-stricken Yudhishthira an episode in Brihaspati's life." (lines 10726–10727) Vyasa tells the story of the divine preceptor Brihaspati and his younger brother Samvarta — a teaching about envy's destructive power — to console and instruct the king after his grief.

### Advising Yudhishthira on Dhritarashtra's Retirement (Ch. CIII)

When Dhritarashtra wishes to retire to the forest and Yudhishthira resists giving permission, Vyasa enters and advises: "Do what Dhritarashtra, eldest of the Kurus, desires. Let him go to the forest. He is old...The dharma of kings is to die in battle or to spend their last days in retirement in the forest...Let him go with your hearty consent, and without anger in his heart." (lines 11205–11217) Yudhishthira consents and "Vyasa returned to his hermitage." (line 11218)

### Presence with Gandhari (Ch. XCVI)

"Vyasa was with Gandhari. 'Oh queen,' said the rishi, 'be not angry with the Pandavas. Did you not tell them even when the battle began: Where there is dharma, there surely will be victory? And so it has happened. It is not right to let the mind dwell on what is past and nurse one's anger. You must now call to aid your great fortitude.'" (lines 10535–10539)

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Rajagopalachari uses Vyasa as a recurrent instrument of the narrative's teaching voice. Each appearance involves Vyasa consoling a grief-stricken character with cosmological, dharmic, or ethical reasoning. He functions as the embodiment of the text's own wisdom — simultaneously the composer and a sage within the composition. This dual status (author and character) is a traditional Indian literary device that gives the text self-authority: the story's moral lessons are delivered by the man who wrote the story.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Vyasa's role as consoler carries consistent theological content in this range:
- Death is an ordained law (to Yudhishthira after Abhimanyu's death)
- The battle was cosmically necessary to "reduce earth's burden" — willed by Vishnu himself (to Dhritarashtra)
- Dharmic duty requires active life, not renunciation of sorrow (to Yudhishthira tempted to take sanyasa)
- Envy is among the deepest afflictions, even of the divine (to Yudhishthira via the Brihaspati story)
- Kings must pursue the vanaprastha stage and the forest when the time comes (to Yudhishthira on Dhritarashtra's departure)

He is also associated with the pedagogical frame: the Mahabharata as Vyasa tells it to Vaisampayana, who recites it to Janamejaya, is the transmission chain that Rajagopalachari's retelling embeds in its narrative references.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Vyasa is a semi-divine legendary figure in Hindu tradition. The attribution to him of the Vedas, Mahabharata, Puranas, and other texts is a traditional claim of comprehensive authorship. Modern scholarship treats the Mahabharata as the product of a long compositional tradition, but the figure of Vyasa as narrator-author is preserved as part of the text's internal frame. Rajagopalachari does not engage this critical question — his retelling treats Vyasa as a historical sage and character.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Vyasa is cited across Hindu commentary traditions as ultimate textual authority. The Suka-Parikshit relationship (Suka recites Srimad Bhagavata to Parikshit) continues the transmission chain from the Mahabharata into the Bhagavata Purana tradition.
