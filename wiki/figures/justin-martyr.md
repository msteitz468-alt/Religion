# Justin Martyr

## Biographical Overview

Justin was born c. AD 100 in Flavia Neapolis (modern Nablus, near ancient Shechem) in Samaria to pagan Greek-speaking parents. His *First Apology* indicates he worked through several philosophical schools — Stoicism, Aristotelianism, Pythagoreanism — before finding Platonism intellectually compelling. His conversion to Christianity came through encounter with an old man who pointed him to the Hebrew prophets and argued that Christian revelation fulfilled and surpassed Greek philosophy. Justin thereafter styled himself a "Christian philosopher," continuing to wear the philosopher's cloak (*pallium*) and opening what amounted to a philosophical school in Rome.

He was beheaded at Rome c. AD 165 under Emperor Marcus Aurelius, along with six companions — hence "Martyr." The record of his trial (*Acta Justini*) survives. He apparently refused to sacrifice to the gods when given the opportunity to recant, consistent with the Christian exclusivism in worship he had argued for in his writings.

His exact relationship to orthodox ecclesiastical structures is unclear; he moved in educated lay circles and engaged both the imperial court and Jewish interlocutors in his writings.

## Primary Source Appearances

Justin does not appear as a character in any canonical text. His primary "appearances" are:
- His own writings (most important below), which are themselves the primary evidence.
- References in Eusebius' *Ecclesiastical History* (4.11, 4.16–17), which quotes him and summarizes his life.
- His *Acts* (*Martyrium Iustini*), a court record of his trial.

## Major Works

1. **First Apology** (c. 155–160 AD) — addressed formally to Emperor Antoninus Pius and the Senate. Defends Christian beliefs and practices against popular accusations (atheism, orgies, cannibalism) and philosophical objections. Key for Hurtado as describing Roman Christian worship: corporate reading of "memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets," followed by preaching, prayer, eucharist, and collection for the poor. Justin calls these "memoirs" *apomnēmoneumata* (recollections), a term drawn from Xenophon's *Memorabilia* of Socrates, thereby presenting the Gospels as respectable literary products comparable to the Socratic tradition.
2. **Second Apology** (shorter; likely appendix to *First Apology*) — more personal; responds to specific incidents of Christian prosecution.
3. **Dialogue with Trypho** — longest surviving work; a literary dialogue with a Jewish interlocutor named Trypho (modeled on a real encounter in Ephesus). Extensive argument that Jesus is the Messiah prophesied in the Hebrew scriptures, including interpretation of Psalms, Isaiah's Servant Songs, and Daniel 7. Hurtado cites this for Justin's knowledge of multiple Gospels (both those ascribed to apostles and those by their followers = possibly Matthew/John + Mark/Luke).

Lost works: a *Treatise against Marcion*, a *Treatise on the Soul*, a *Discourse to the Greeks*, and others referenced by Eusebius.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Second-Century Context
Justin represents the proto-orthodox mainstream response to the dual challenges of (1) pagan hostility and (2) gnostic/Marcionite versions of Christianity. He insisted on:
- The Hebrew scriptures as Christian property, contra Marcion who rejected them.
- Multiple Gospels (at least four), contra Marcion who accepted only a version of Luke.
- A philosophical Christianity capable of defending itself before educated pagans.

### His Significance for Hurtado's Argument
[[larry-hurtado|Hurtado]] uses Justin as evidence for:
- The regular reading of Gospels as scripture in corporate worship by mid-2nd century Rome.
- The description of Christian baptism and eucharist as distinctly religious rites.
- The pagan critics' awareness of Christianity: Justin's writings were read by Celsus, who critiques *The Dialogue of Jason and Papiscus* (a slightly earlier Christian apology).
- The "apologetic acceleration": by the end of the 2nd century Christianity had moved from marginal movement to a force requiring serious intellectual engagement.

### Later Reception
- **Eusebius of Caesarea** cites Justin extensively as a champion of orthodoxy and provides the main biographical summary.
- **Medieval tradition** venerates him as a saint (feast day June 1 in Western Christianity; June 1 in Eastern).
- **Modern scholarship** views Justin as a key witness to the diversity and creativity of mid-2nd century Christianity and to the development of the NT canon. His knowledge of the four canonical Gospels (inferrable from his citations) is important evidence for their early widespread circulation.
- **Feminist/social-history scholarship** notes that Justin, despite his philosophical sophistication, operated in contexts where Christianity was crossing social boundaries — he himself was not of the Roman elite, and his school attracted socially diverse adherents.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Justin's **Logos theology** is his most distinctive theological contribution: Jesus is the incarnation of the divine Logos (Word/Reason) that underlies all creation and was partially grasped by the best pagan philosophers (hence Socrates and Abraham are proto-Christians in Justin's view). This theological move allowed Justin to claim all truth as ultimately Christian truth, while distinguishing the fullness of divine revelation given in Christ from the partial illuminations of philosophy.

Justin's theology of the Logos prepared the ground for later developments in Trinitarian thought and apologetics, though he never systematized it.

## Justin and the Rule of Faith (Kruger)

[[michael-kruger|Kruger]] (*Christianity at the Crossroads*, ch. 2, 5, 7) uses Justin as a major witness to three of Kruger's five transitional themes:

### As Apologist (*superstitio* transition)
Justin wrote at the height of the Roman *superstitio* accusation against Christianity. His *First Apology* is addressed to the Emperor Antoninus Pius and the Senate (c.150-155 CE); his goal is to show Christianity is not a dangerous *superstitio* but a philosophy superior to paganism. He argues: Christians are law-abiding (*First Apol.* 17); they do not deserve persecution without evidence of wrongdoing; the charges of immorality (incest, cannibalism) are false.

When brought before the prefect Rusticus c.165 CE, Justin's trial testimony (preserved in the *Acta Justini*) includes a spontaneous recitation of the core Christian beliefs that closely matches the [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]]: "We believe in one God, maker of all things... and Jesus Christ, the Son of God, who was proclaimed by the prophets..." — an early, forensic instance of the rule in a non-literary context.

### As Canon Witness
Justin's *First Apology* 67 is the most detailed surviving account of mid-2nd-century Christian worship:
> "On the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs and exhorts to the imitation of these good things..."

Two features are critical for canon history:
1. The "memoirs of the apostles" (*apomnēmoneumata tōn apostolōn*) are read **alongside "the writings of the prophets" (= OT)** — they function at the same liturgical level as Scripture
2. The "memoirs of the apostles" = the Gospels, plural; Justin cites Matt, Mark, Luke (and possibly John) throughout his works

This does not use the formal citation formula "as it is written" for the Gospels, but the *liturgical* status — public reading alongside OT Scripture as the basis for preaching — implies scriptural treatment. Justin represents the functional canon in operation. Kruger (ch. 7) cites this as evidence for a pre-Irenaean liturgical canon.

Additionally, Justin's reference to "those who have composed and those who are followers of the apostles" (*Dial.* 103) — as sources for the Gospels — implies knowledge of both apostle-authored and apostle-associated Gospels (i.e., Matthew/John + Mark/Luke), consistent with all four canonical Gospels circulating in mid-2nd c. Rome.

### Against Marcion
Justin explicitly names [[marcion|Marcion]] as active in his own time and using "another god" as the Father of Jesus (*First Apol.* 26; 58). This is among the earliest datable references to Marcion and confirms he was a known presence in Rome c.150 CE — consistent with the c.144 CE excommunication date. Justin reportedly wrote a specific *Treatise Against All Heresies* and *Against Marcion* — both lost — confirming that the Marcionite challenge was sufficiently serious to demand a dedicated response.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Authenticity of the works**: The two Apologies and the Dialogue with Trypho are universally accepted as genuine. Several other works attributed to Justin (*Discourse to the Greeks*, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, *On the Sole Government of God*) are disputed or considered spurious.
- **"Trypho"**: The identity of Justin's Jewish interlocutor is debated; some see a historical figure, others a literary construct.
- **The relationship of Justin's Christianity to later orthodoxy**: His subordinationist-sounding Logos theology (the Word as numerically distinct from and derivative of God) has sometimes been read as proto-Arian; others read it as a pre-Nicene articulation of what became orthodoxy.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Justin is the most widely cited 2nd-century Christian source in early Christian apologetics. His works influenced:
- **Athenagoras, Theophilus, Tertullian** — the next generation of apologists.
- **Irenaeus** — who shared Justin's multiple-Gospel canon and anti-Marcionite stance.
- **Later dogmatic theology** — his Logos Christology influenced Origen, Clement of Alexandria, and through them the Nicene tradition.
