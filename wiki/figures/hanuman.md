# Hanumān

**Hanumān** (also Hanuman) is the monkey hero of the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]], son of the wind god [[vayu|Vāyu]] and the nymph Añjanā. He serves as the most devoted servant (*sevaka*) of [[rama|Rāma]], and in these chapters performs the defining acts of his career: the great leap across the ocean to [[lanka|Laṅkā]], the discovery of [[sita|Sītā]], the burning of Laṅkā, and the return bearing proof of Sītā's survival.

## Origin, Birth, and Early Life

*(Content from lines 5731–8500 — the Book of the Forest and Book of Kishkindhya)*

### The First Monkey and the Birth of the Monkey Race

Buck's text narrates a cosmogonic origin for the monkey race. **Lord Brahma**, resting on **Mount Meru** — the golden center of the Earth, whose soil has been turned to gold by the Sun over ages — shed a tear, "and where it touched the golden ground, right then and there the first monkey was born" (7405–7407). Brahma named him **Riksharaja** and kept him company on Meru.

### Riksharaja and the Birth of Vali and Sugriva

One morning Riksharaja bent to drink from a lake, saw his reflection, and thinking it an enemy jumped in to attack. "When that monkey came back out onto the shore again, he had been changed into a female" (7419). Both [[indra|Indra]] and [[surya|Surya]] the Sun descended and made love to her that same morning. Two gold-colored sons were quickly born: **Vali** (Indra's son) and **Sugriva** (Surya's son) (7422–7429). Brahma gave Vali the city of **Kishkindhya**; Indra gave Vali a victory-garland of golden lotus flowers (7433). Vali became monkey King.

### Hanuman's Parentage

Watching from heaven, Riksharaja observed that Vali received everything. He asked [[vayu|Vāyu]], the Wind, to father a son — "a monkey who would always be Sugriva's true friend" (7438–7439). Vayu surveyed the monkey women of Kishkindhya; the most beautiful was **Anjana**, "who in human form could have the love of any man" (7442–7443). She was walking atop a mountain near Kishkindhya "when Vayu came to her. He gently stole away her clothes and embraced her in his long arms" (7444–7445). Anjana was married to another monkey and left Hanuman at the mouth of a small cave on the hill after his birth (7447–7450). Hanuman was born "a little monkey with white fur and a red face and brownish-yellow eyes" (7447).

### The Leap at the Sun

Newborn and alone, hungry at dawn, Hanuman saw the Sun rise: "He saw the glorious Sun rise into the air like a big ripe mango fruit. Newborn though he was, he knew what fruit was. He licked his sharp bright little teeth, crouched down and jumped with a leap and a bound up into the sky and went flying straight at the Sun" (7457–7461). His father the Wind blew from the north to protect him from burning (7463). Hanuman tumbled unhurt inside the Sun's fire.

### Rahu, Indra, and the Breaking of the Jaw

During a solar eclipse, the Asura **Rahu** advanced to swallow the Sun. Hanuman accidentally put his foot in Rahu's eye (7476), then attacked Rahu and bit his ear (7485). Indra rode out on his white elephant **Airavata**; Hanuman attacked them both. "Indra pushed Hanuman away with the flat side of his thunderbolt" (7491–7492). Hanuman fell to Earth and broke his jaw on a stone by his cave.

### Vayu's Anger: The Stilling of the Winds

Vayu, seeing his son injured, "went to his son Hanuman and took him inside the little cave, and held him. The Wind hid himself. The bodies of living creatures became hard as wood and their joints stiffened into knots. Like a banner lowered and put on the ground, the ever-moving air moved no more and lay still" (7495–7503). The cessation of wind threatened all life in all worlds.

### Brahma's Gifts and Shiva's Teaching

**Lord Brahma** came to Hanuman's cave, healed his jaw with a touch, and declared: "Hanuman on the lap of the Wind, shining white, you will live as long as you wish to live; you cannot be killed" (7511–7512). Then [[surya|Surya]] entered the cave in his jeweled clothes, smiled at Hanuman, and set three mangos beside the sleeping child (7513–7520). The Wind was appeased and returned to the world.

Vayu then carried Hanuman to **Lord Śiva**, who "taught Hanuman to change form at pleasure" (7524–7525). **Nandin** the bull taught him languages (7525). Hanuman also learned poetry, and "discovered that he had been wearing golden earrings all his life without knowing it. No one else can see those little gold rings except some person as yet unknown to Hanuman, who will meet him and be his friend and master, so said the bull Nandin" (7527–7531) — a prophecy fulfilled when Rāma immediately recognizes Hanuman on Rishyamuka Hill (7718).

### Exile with Sugriva

Hanuman "grew up and lived with Sugriva in Kishkindhya. Vali was the King" (7532–7533). When Vali turned petty and exiled Sugriva for supposed treason (mistakenly thinking Sugriva had sealed him into a cave to die while fighting the demon Mayavi — lines 7620–7650), Vali also held Sugriva's wife Ruma prisoner. "Only Hanuman has stayed with Sugriva" (7649). They took refuge on Rishyamuka Hill in Matanga's Wood, where Vali's curse of death prevented him from entering (7650–7651).

### First Appearance to Rāma

On Rishyamuka Hill, Hanuman spots Rāma and Lakshmana climbing and disguises himself as a poor woodcutter. When Rāma looks at him, he says simply: "Oh Hanuman of golden earrings" — fulfilling Nandin's prophecy (7718–7719). Hanuman replies, "Rama, I remember you now" and shakes himself back to his true form: "white with glossy fur, a long tail curved gracefully, long thin arms, feet like hands, a red face and white teeth and light eyes almost all yellow" (7720–7722). When told Rāvaṇa has taken Sītā, Hanuman grows large immediately: "He grew bigger. 'Get on my shoulders,' he said, and took them on uphill to meet Sugriva" (7725–7727).

At the council with Sugriva, Hanuman says of the search: "Wherever he took her we can find her" (7743). After the killing of Vali, Hanuman leads Rāma and Lakshmana to their cave on Prasravana Hill and makes a significant admission: "I could have saved Sita by now more than once" (7922).

Savari the hermit woman had earlier described him to Rāma: "That white monkey? Oh, no one can equal him. He is Sugriva's one loyal friend. He is brave and kind. He is the child of the Wind" (7389–7392).

### The Southern Dispatch

When Sugriva dispatches the four search parties, he reserves Hanuman for the southern direction — the crucial one, since Rāvaṇa was seen flying south (8366–8368): "Hanuman the Son of the Wind will surely find Sita." Sugriva gives Hanuman a scroll — a message for Rāvaṇa if encountered (8372–8378). Rāma gives Hanuman his own gold wedding ring inscribed "Rama, Rama, Rama" three times, so Sītā will recognize it (8399–8402). Hanuman leads Angada, Jambavan, and a quarter of the army south; the other three parties return empty-handed. In the final lines of this narrative range, Sugriva says: "Now it is only Hanuman the Son of the Wind who will succeed in finding Sita" (8496–8497).

## The Dispatch and the Leap Across the Ocean

### Context: The Search Party Loses Heart

The monkey search party for Sītā, led by Prince Angada, has wandered into the underground cavern of Maya the Asura — "Maya's Cave of Trees" (line 8505) — and lost half a month. Emerging on the southern shore, facing a hundred leagues of sea to Laṅkā, Angada despairs and resolves to die by fasting. All the animals but Hanumān and the bear king Jambavan join him in suicidal meditation.

Jambavan turns to Hanumān and challenges him: "Little monkey, are you going to stand there looking at us like an idiot? I didn't hear you say anything just now about how far you can jump" (lines 8798–8803). Hanumān answers simply: "I'll do it" (8805).

The text describes his divine nature at this moment: "Oh King Rama, Wind is Fire's best friend. Wind shatters the hills and moves across the eye of heaven. He cannot be seen, but only the heavy things he moves are visible. Hanumān is the son of the fast-racing generous Wind. He equals his father in flight" (8806–8810).

### The Leap

Hanumān climbs the Malaya Hills to gather power. As he grows large and heavy, serpents are crushed from underground, rivers pour from the hillside, and heavenly beings (Gandharvas and Apsarasas) rise to watch (8823–8844). Then:

> "Hanumān stood on the hilltop. He held his breath and sucked in his stomach. He frisked his tail and raised it a little at the end. He bent his knees and swung back his arms, and on one finger gleamed Rama's gold ring. Then without pausing to think he drew in his neck, laid back his ears and jumped. It was grand! It was the greatest leap ever taken." (8845–8851)

The speed of the leap pulls flowers into the air; "red clouds flamed over the sky" (8856); the ocean pitches and rolls beneath him; electric light crackles around him (8864–8866). "That white monkey was like a comet, pushing the sky from his way and bumping clouds aside" (8858–8859).

### Obstacles in Flight

Two obstacles are overcome on the crossing:

1. **Mainaka the mountain**: The golden winged mountain Mainaka rises from the ocean floor to offer Hanumān rest, in repayment of a debt to his father the Wind (8874–8876). Hanumān declines: "Forgive me, but I must not break my flight" (8895). He touches the mountain only with a fingertip and speeds on. Indra rewards Mainaka for the welcome.

2. **Sinhika the Rakshasi**: Living twenty leagues off Laṅkā, Sinhika seizes Hanumān's shadow from below, dragging him toward her open mouth. Hanumān becomes "quickly small as a thumb and flew down her throat like a tiny hurricane. He crushed her heart with his sharp fingernails, turned, and darted up out from her ear" (8919–8921). Her blood spreads through the water; the fish eat her.

## Entering Laṅkā

Hanumān lands below Laṅkā, observes its golden walls, shark-filled moat, and massive gate-towers (8966–8988). He shrinks to the form of "a small long-haired silver tabby cat" (8993–8994), ties Rama's ring in his fur, and prays: "Three times I take refuge in Rāma, who is the safe shelter of all who flee for help from any thing" (8997–8999).

At the wall he encounters the spirit of the city itself — a dark-skinned goddess, "Lanka herself, surrounded by protections, ruled by the hand of Ravana" (9015–9016) — who threatens to kill him. He strikes her between the eyes and knocks her down. She yields: "When a monkey knocks you down, know that a fear and a curse have come to the Rakshasas" (9026–9027). Hanumān enters, touching the ground first with his left foot, "putting that foot on his enemies' heads" (9032).

Inside he pauses and prays again: "I trust in Rāma . . . he is very strong — and alas, that for their one king's wrong this noble race of demons must perish!" (9041–9045).

## Searching Laṅkā by Night

At midnight Hanumān ranges through Laṅkā: broad streets, palace gardens, armories, temples, Rakshasa patrols armed with mantras and hidden weapons (9064–9124). He enters the inner palace compound and searches the Pushpaka chariot — Ravana's stolen aerial vehicle, described in extended and luminous detail (9147–9198) — then follows his nose to Ravana's bedroom.

There he sees Rāvaṇa's countless wives sleeping in disarray across the floor. Hanumān fears moral wrong in gazing at sleeping women but reasons: "I am saving Rāma's life by seeking for Sītā, and surely a woman may be found among other women" (9274–9275). He examines each face and finds Rāvaṇa himself — "heroic and dark, wearing white, with twenty arms like gate pillars . . . ten devilishly handsome heads" (9316–9319) — asleep on his crystal bed. The text describes him: "Rāvaṇa lay like a collection of wrongs, a mass of harm and injury and brutality and darkness of heart" (9335–9336).

Hanumān observes Rāvaṇa for a long time, then mistakenly identifies Queen Mandodari as Sītā before correcting himself: "That's not her. What a shameful apish idea . . . Sītā will drink no Laṅkā wine or ever love another husband" (9363–9368).

Running out of places to search, Hanumān collapses on the wall of the Asoka Grove behind the palace in apparent despair. His father Vāyu responds: a great wind sweeps through Laṅkā — "the first time since Rāvaṇa had come to Laṅkā that the Wind had dared to blow hard" (9425–9427) — and blows Hanumān over the wall into the grove.

## Finding Sītā

At dawn Hanumān climbs the Sinsapa tree in the center of the grove. He sees:

> "a fair woman of the human race arise from sleeping in the dirt under another nearby tree. She came over and sat with her back against the Sinsapa tree, her legs drawn up and her worn dusty yellow robe covering all her body, her glossy black hair tied in a long braid." (9520–9525)

He identifies her as Sītā, "much changed since I saw her carried away" (9526–9527): "thin from grief and fasting, lean and trembling" (9538), yet still recognizably beautiful.

He watches Rāvaṇa approach and court Sītā, who repels him with fierce words: "You see everything reversed, because you are so near to your own death" (9572–9574). He watches Rakshasi guards threaten her. He is on the verge of leaping down to fight when the good old demoness Trijata steps in and calms the others with her prophetic dream of Laṅkā's doom (9655–9703).

Alone with Sītā, Hanumān faces the problem of approach: "Demons can take any form; how can I speak to her so she will trust me? If I talk in Sanskrit like a brahmana she'll think I'm the Rakshasa King" (9706–9709). So he speaks the animal language, very softly: "Rāma-Rāma-" (9712).

Sītā looks up and hears facts only Rāma's messenger would know — the golden deer, Jatāyu, Sugrīva's alliance. She doubts whether Hanumān is real or a dream: "I fear you are also only a phantom in my mind" (9733–9734). Hanumān comes closer: "Listen, too much crying's bad for your eyes! Everything's going to be all right" (9735–9737). Sītā reads his "inmost thoughts and feelings" by her own heart and is convinced (9741–9742).

He gives her Rāma's ring — the gold band inscribed "Rāma, Rāma, Rāma" — and she reads it: "Rāma seemed to be there touching her" (9767–9768).

## The Conversation with Sītā

Hanumān offers to carry Sītā back on his back — he demonstrates by growing to "a white hill eighty spans high" with "nails and teeth like thunders, yellow lantern eyes like dazzling brass plates" (9801–9803). Sītā laughs at his "monkey nature" (9799).

Her refusal is theologically precise: "No, let Rāma recover me if he will — but now that beautiful man is freed from a wife like me" (9809–9810). The rescue must be Rāma's act, not Hanumān's: only Rāma's direct intervention can restore the cosmic order upset by the abduction. This insistence is not passivity but a statement about the proper structure of the rescue — its meaning depends on who performs it.

She gives Hanumān two things to take to Rāma: a message invoking the crow incident (the time Rāma sent the Brahmastra weapon after a crow that clawed her), and the wedding pearl her father Janaka had tied into her hair on their wedding day. The pearl's message: "I will live on here for a month and not longer. Do you still think of me at all? I send this pearl so you may know — you are loved by me, you are loved by me" (9290–9293).

## Destruction of Laṅkā's Gardens and Capture

Rather than leave immediately, Hanumān makes a "monkey decision" (9881) to leave a mark. He destroys Rāvaṇa's garden — "plowed through Ravana's lawns and flowerbeds and pushed over his pavilions . . . threw benches and bricks through the palace windows" (9894–9896) — drawing out Rāvaṇa's palace bodyguard of eighty thousand fighters, whom he kills with an iron gate-bar (9915–9916). He then destroys the city temple, spinning a marble column until it catches fire (9934–9939).

Rāvaṇa sends his warrior son Indrajit. Knowing Hanumān cannot be killed, Indrajit shoots "the spell of Shiva's noose" from a single-use mantra-arrow; Hanumān falls bound. But before Indrajit can reach him, other Rakshasas pile on chains — inadvertently canceling Shiva's noose (whose power is negated by any additional bond). Indrajit recognizes the blunder in silence: "Those fools — I bow to Shiva who places us all in true danger now" (10051–10054). Hanumān, now free though he does not show it, is dragged before Rāvaṇa.

## Before Rāvaṇa's Throne

In Rāvaṇa's court Hanumān delivers Sugrīva's message: return Sītā. When Rāvaṇa asks how he crossed the sea, Hanumān answers: "I jumped" (10116). He tells Rāvaṇa: "I crossed the ocean, as a person without attachment to worldly desires easily crosses the ocean of existence. Withdraw your heart from Sītā, or that will be a costly theft, for it's by her energy that I jumped over the sea" (10123–10127). Rāvaṇa's brother Vibhīṣaṇa intervenes to prevent Hanumān's execution: a messenger cannot be killed. Rāvaṇa instead orders Hanumān's tail set afire and has him paraded through Laṅkā.

When Rāvaṇa asks how Rāma compares to himself, Hanumān answers: "Truly, you are the bright Full Moon, the light of the night world; and my Rāma is the New Moon, a thin shred of light, no more" (10156–10158) — a riddle that Rāvaṇa takes as flattery but which means Rāma's light is waxing.

## The Burning of Laṅkā

Wrapped in oily rags and carried through the streets with his tail afire, Hanumān feels no heat — in the Asoka Grove, Sītā has prayed to the fire: "If I've kept faith, be cool to Hanumān" (10175). The flame bends to the right, signaling assent (10178). "Fire let the Wind be cool right next to his son's tail" (10180).

Hanumān breaks his chains, "lashed out with his fiery tail, and sprang into the sky like a lightning streak. Hanumān flew, setting fire to rows of mansions and houses with his burning tail. Flames devoured Laṅkā like a cremation yard" (10184–10188). The destruction is comprehensive: "Burnt were sweet sandalwood and diamonds, corals and fine silks, golden vessels and piles of weapons, furniture and ornaments . . . all burnt" (10189–10192). Indrajit performs a water ritual that quenches the fires.

Hanumān lands back in Laṅkā horrified: "Never again! What have I done? I've burned Sītā!" (10247). He races to the Asoka Grove. Sītā is unharmed: "How could Fire have burned Sītā? It is she who can burn Fire if she will" (10255–10256).

Sītā consoles him: they set his tail afire and he burned them back — "throw off your care as a snake abandons his old worn skin" (10277). She gives him the wedding pearl and her final message. Hanumān "walked round her in three right-turning circles, bristled up his fur and flew off to the north" (10296–10297).

## Return to Rāma

The monkeys and bears on the shore hear Hanumān returning, form a circle holding hands, and he lands among them. "I talked to her; she's alive!" (10304). They celebrate; Hanumān leads them into Sugrīva's Honey Park where they drink the ancient honey wines and create comic chaos (10329–10383).

Back with Rāma, Hanumān reports in full: Sītā's location (the Asoka Grove, guarded by Rakshasis), her steadfast refusal of Rāvaṇa, her fidelity ("her heart carries her love to you . . . she's true to you, but she is all alone, like a tuneful lute without a player," 10435–10437), and a detailed physical description of her beauty even in captivity. He gives Rāma the wedding pearl. He speaks in silent mental speech, rebuking Rāma's passivity in startlingly sharp terms (10482–10498). Then aloud: "Now make me a promise, Rāma, an unfailing promise . . . Kill Rāvaṇa!" (10507–10510).

In the campaign planning Hanumān describes Laṅkā's defenses in detail (10571–10606). He carries Rāma on his shoulders in the march south (10616). During the first assault on Laṅkā he remains at Rāma's side (11338). When Vibhīṣaṇa defects to Rāma's side, it is Hanumān who vouches for him and leads him to Rāma by the hand (10857–10861).

## Divine Identity and Powers

As revealed in these chapters, Hanumān's powers include:

- **Infinite size-shifting**: from thumb-small to eighty spans high, at will (8918, 9801)
- **Physical invulnerability**: cannot be killed; Shiva's noose merely stops him temporarily (10037)
- **Flight**: equals his father the Wind in speed
- **Breath-hold**: can suck in power and energy on demand
- **Animal communication**: speaks in the language of animals distinct from Sanskrit
- **Brief prophetic trance**: falls into "a short animal trance" and speaks of Kali devouring the Rakshasas (9782–9789)
- **Divine parentage actively expressed**: the Wind responds to his despair and literally blows him into the Asoka Grove (9422–9442)

## Theological Significance: Hanumān as Perfect Devotee

The Ram Dass foreword reading of Hanumān as the embodiment of selfless service (*seva*) finds extensive textual support in this range. Hanumān's defining features here are:

1. **Instrumental self-effacement**: he consistently frames his extraordinary feats as being "Rāma's slave" and acting in Rāma's service. In Rāvaṇa's court: "I am Hanumān, I am Rāma's slave" (10108). His first prayer entering Laṅkā: "I trust in Rāma, I trust in Rāma" (9042–9043).

2. **Love that motivates heroism**: the text explicitly names love as the motor of Hanumān's action: "Whatever the obstacle, Hanumān likes strong love leaps over it and goes on" (10411–10412). His tears when he fears Sītā is dead (9396–9406) and his joy when he finds her (9765) are not incidental — they are the source of his power.

3. **Obedience to dharmic structure**: Despite the power to carry Sītā home himself, he accepts her refusal without challenge. The rescue must be Rāma's; Hanumān's role is preparation and report.

4. **Willingness to rebuke the master**: His silent mental speech to Rāma (10482–10498) — sharply rebuking Rāma's despondency — shows that seva does not mean uncritical flattery. The true servant tells the truth.

5. **Prayer before every threshold**: Each major action in this range is preceded by taking refuge in Rāma (entering Laṅkā: 8997–8999; 9041–9043). This repetition is structural — it marks Hanumān's acts as devotional rather than heroic in the human sense.

The text also captures Hanumān's charm, humor, and monkey nature — his brief gleeful celebration when he (wrongly) thinks he's found Sītā (9358–9361), his "monkey decision" to destroy the garden, his leading the animals into Sugrīva's honey park. This is not despite his devotion but part of it: the monkey's spontaneity is consecrated by love for Rāma.

## The Battle of Lanka: Against Kumbhakarna

When the giant **Kumbhakarna** steps over Laṅkā's wall and his war cry routs the monkey army, Hanumān stands his ground beside Prince Angada — "came from somewhere and stood quietly beside Angada" (l. 11778). With Rāma exposed, Hanumān acts: "from where he stood Hanuman jumped hard and high at Kumbhakarna and bit a piece out of his ear" (ll. 11783–11785). Kumbhakarna strikes him down with his iron spear, but the blow breaks the shaft — the only physical damage Kumbhakarna sustains before Rāma dismembers him. Hanumān falls stunned and plays no further role in that combat.

## After Indrajit's First Assault

Indrajit attacks invisibly from a tiger-chariot, killing Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa, and virtually the entire army. Hanumān attempts to find him in the clouds. He sees what appears to be a magic sword flying independently — he grabs it, but "that weapon became a fair young woman" who cried, "Let me go! It's wrong to hold an unwilling woman!" — and Hanumān, hearing the word *Wrong*, releases her. She becomes a sword again and cuts him down from the sky (ll. 11990–11996). Since Hanumān cannot be killed, Vibhīṣaṇa finds him alive that night, "covered with sword-cuts" (l. 12047).

The dying Bear King Jambavan's first question is not about Rāma or Lakṣmaṇa, but: "Tell me, does Hanumān the Son of the Wind still live?" (ll. 12055–12056). When Vibhīṣaṇa questions this priority, Jambavan explains: "While Hanumān lives, this army also lives; but if he is dead we are truly destroyed" (ll. 12060–12061) — naming Hanumān as the indispensable axis of the army.

## The Mountain-Lifting Episode

Jambavan sends Hanumān alone on the crucial mission: fly to the far Himālayas and return with the **Medicine Hill of Life** — "crowned with annuals and herbs, bright and luminous, long ago transplanted from the Moon" — which are "potent to heal any hurt and restore life to the dead" (ll. 12075–12084).

Hanumān "bent his back, opened his mouth and jumped straight for the snowy lap of the Himalya Hills. He flew like a blazing comet, fast and generous, speedy and bright!" (ll. 12086–12088). He sees Śiva standing tall and smiling with the new moon in his hair. On the horizon he finds the glowing Medicine Hill: "there is no difference between day and night" on it; "their new leaves sparkle and gleam, their stems glow like liquid silver, like moonlight on the waves as they bend in the wind, and the whole hill shines like a gem" (ll. 12098–12106).

But as Hanumān approaches, the plants feel him coming and draw themselves underground, hiding their lights: "You foolish grasses with no compassion!" — and in three shakes of his shoulders he uproots the entire mountain and holds it over his head (ll. 12112–12115). He flies back so fast the hill grows hot and the herbs begin to steam. Arriving over Laṅkā with nowhere to set the mountain down, he hovers — but the scent of the herbs spreading over the battlefield alone is sufficient "to restore the dead animals to life and heal all their wounds" (ll. 12119–12125). The dead Rākṣasas, however, are "under the sea in other worlds, beyond the reach of any medicine of Earth" (ll. 12124–12125).

When Jambavan sees him holding "a huge hill bearing all its trees and stones and elephants and colored veins of ore and pockets of snow and falling rivers" and shouts, "Where do you want it?" — Jambavan laughs until he cries: "Oh blessings, best of monkeys. We are saved! Turn away, put it back again where it belongs!" (ll. 12127–12134).

## Theological Statement: Love as Power

Sītā, watching from Pushpaka chariot as Hanumān flies toward Laṅkā carrying the glowing mountain, says: "It must be by the power of his wholehearted love for Rāma" (l. 12220). Buck's text frames Hanumān's physical superhuman feats entirely as a function of *bhakti* (devotion), not merely innate strength.

After the healing, Rāma embraces Hanumān and says: "Were I to lose any of you, then without you my life would be empty and worthless. Never seeking in the world again could I find other friends so true" (ll. 12150–12154). Hanumān's reply places the friendship in a cosmic frame: "We are your forefathers. We are your ancestors the animals, and you are our child Man. As for our friendship, why we've known you a long long time, Rāma, and the number of those days is lost in Silence" (ll. 12161–12164).

## The Final Battle

During [[ravana|Rāvaṇa]]'s midnight dance which calls down the North Wind to extinguish "every fire in the Universe and every light," Hanumān "bent over Rāma to protect him" in the gale — though even he could barely breathe in the wind his own father made (ll. 12899–12904). In combat with Rākṣasa magicians, Hanumān kills Lightning-Tongue: "No fear and follow me" — catches his throat in both hands, twists his head off, and throws it into Laṅkā like a ball (ll. 12702–12707). After Rāvaṇa's death, Hanumān holds the ornaments Sītā had dropped during her abduction (l. 13593).
