# Absalom

**Absalom** is the third son of [[david|David]] (by Maacah, a princess of Geshur), the
strikingly handsome prince whose revolt against his father is the central catastrophe of
the second half of the David story (2 Samuel 13–19). His career runs from avenging his
sister Tamar, through usurpation of his father's throne, to his death — caught by his
famous hair in a terebinth and dispatched by [[joab|Joab]] against David's explicit orders.

## Biographical Overview and Primary Appearances

- **Avenging Tamar** — When his half-brother [[amnon|Amnon]] rapes their sister **Tamar**
  and David fails to act, Absalom waits two years and has Amnon murdered at a sheep-shearing
  feast, then flees to his grandfather in Geshur (2 Sam 13).
- **Return and reconciliation** — [[joab|Joab]], through the wise woman of Tekoa, secures
  Absalom's recall; after a further two years and a dramatic gesture (setting Joab's field
  alight) Absalom is restored to David's presence (2 Sam 14).
- **The revolt** — Absalom cultivates the people at the gate, "stealing the hearts of the
  men of Israel," then has himself proclaimed king at Hebron. David flees Jerusalem.
  Absalom, on the counsel of Ahithophel (later undercut by Hushai), takes
  possession of David's concubines on the palace roof — fulfilling Nathan's curse — and
  pursues his father across the Jordan (2 Sam 15–17).
- **Death** — In the battle in the forest of Ephraim, Absalom's head (or hair) is caught in
  a great terebinth; Joab thrusts three darts into his heart, defying David's command to
  "deal gently... with the lad." David's grief — **"O my son Absalom, my son, my son
  Absalom! Would I had died instead of you"** — is among the most wrenching laments in the
  Hebrew Bible (2 Sam 18).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Absalom's revolt is the working-out of [[nathan|Nathan's]] curse that "the sword shall never
depart from your house" — David's sin with [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] returning upon him through
his own children (incest, fratricide, rebellion, the public taking of his concubines). The
arc dramatizes the collision between David as **father** and David as **king**: politically
Absalom's death is deliverance, but as a father David is destroyed by it.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) reads David's flight from
Absalom as "not merely a story of political intrigue... but also a tale of anguished
conflict between father and king in the same man," culminating in "David's horrendous stutter
of grief over Absalom's death" — the broken repetition "my son, my son" — "followed by
[[joab|Joab's]] harsh rebuke." Alter also notes the **structural rhyme** with the [[amnon|Amnon]]
episode: both the rape of Tamar and David's taking of Bathsheba are "prohibited sexual acts"
that "lead to murder and political disarray," the writer linking the king's sin to his son's
through deliberate analogy. (Faulkner's *Absalom, Absalom!*, Alter remarks, was a keener
reader of this material than the source critics.)

## Reception

- **Judaism** — Absalom becomes a byword for the rebellious son and for vanity (his
  celebrated hair, in which he is caught and killed); a monument near Jerusalem was popularly
  called "Absalom's Pillar." Psalm 3's superscription sets it during David's flight from
  Absalom.
- **Christianity** — Patristic and medieval readers used Absalom typologically as a figure
  of the traitor (sometimes paired with Judas) and as a moral emblem of pride and filial
  rebellion; David's lament was read as an image of divine grief over the lost.
