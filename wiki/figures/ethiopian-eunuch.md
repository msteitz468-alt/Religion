# Ethiopian Eunuch

The Ethiopian eunuch is the unnamed court official converted by [[philip-the-evangelist|
Philip the evangelist]] on the Gaza road (Acts 8:26–40). [[darrell-bock|Bock]] reads the
episode as one of a set of "individual conversions" (eunuch, Saul, [[cornelius|Cornelius]])
that show "the more personal side of evangelism" and as "an important intermediate stop in
the journey to Gentile inclusion."

## Biographical Overview

He is a treasurer of "the Candace" — which Bock, citing Pliny, notes is "a hereditary
dynastic title" of Ethiopian queens, **not a personal name**. "Ethiopia" here is ancient
Cush/Nubia, capital Meroë, in central modern Sudan south of Egypt; Bock notes (with
Witherington) that the man "probably is black." Eunuchs, Bock observes, "often served as
treasurers." Bock reads him as a literal eunuch, against Jervell's symbolic-celibate
interpretation.

## Ethnic and Religious Status (Bock's key judgment)

Bock judges it "unlikely that the Ethiopian is a pure Gentile," precisely because Cornelius
(Acts 10) is emphasized as *the* Gentile breakthrough. Surveying Jervell's options (Diaspora
Jew, proselyte, or [[god-fearers|God-fearer]]), Bock concludes it is "quite likely that he
has been significantly touched by Judaism, since he is reading Isaiah and coming from
Jerusalem… he would not be seen as a pure Gentile." A eunuch "could not be a proselyte"
(Deut. 23:1; 1QSa 2.5–6) and could only have embraced Judaism short of full membership.
Bock adds an eschatological note: the eunuch's condition barred full temple worship, but
"In the eschaton, eunuchs will be restored to full worship (Isa. 56:3b–5)," and the story
"likely is an indication that the hope of worship expressed in Isa. 56 is beginning to take
place," even though "there is no such explicit note" in the text.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 8:26–40** — Philip, directed by an angel and then the Spirit, joins the eunuch's
  chariot as he reads Isaiah aloud; the eunuch asks whom the prophet describes; Philip
  "gospels Jesus" from the passage; at water the eunuch asks "what prevents me from being
  baptized?" and is baptized; Philip is caught away and the eunuch goes "rejoicing."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

The christological core, on Bock's reading, is the passage the eunuch reads —
**Isaiah 53:7b–8a** (περιοχή, an NT hapax), cited "in a form like the LXX, but the sense
reflects the MT"; the differences, Bock says, "are not crucial because the issue is not the
details but the identity of the figure." Bock notes the rare word "lamb" (ἀμνός) suggests
"a traditional source," and that while "the comparison to a lamb might suggest atonement,
this is not the most emphasized idea" (Luke clarifies atonement elsewhere). Bock's primary
reading of the quoted lines is *injustice*, not vindication: "it is justice that is removed,
as the unjust death of an innocent takes place." Yet he sees irony — "in the generation's
act of taking Jesus's life from earth, there is also, for Jesus, God's vindication of that
death." On the eunuch's question (Acts 8:34), Bock (with Fitzmyer and Jeremias) lists three
candidate referents Jews might propose — the prophet, Israel, or another individual (a
revived Elijah or a Messiah, "but not one who suffered") — before Philip identifies the
figure as Jesus. Bock reads the whole account as showing "some parallelism to Luke 24 and
the Emmaus road."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The eunuch's baptism — "a baptism performed by a non-apostle," with no laying-on-of-hands
(contrast Samaria) — demonstrates for Bock the reach of the Acts 1:8 program toward "the
end of the earth" (Witherington's gloss on Ethiopia). Bock notes the man is "a significant
government official… just what someone such as Theophilus, to whom Acts is addressed, needs
to hear."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock defends the episode against Haenchen, who "doubts historicity chiefly because of the
supernatural activity," replying that "The Acts of the Apostles could just as well be named
the Acts of God." Against Jervell, Bock reads a literal (not symbolic-celibate) eunuch. The
so-called "Additional Note" confession at Acts 8:37 ("I believe that Jesus Christ is the
Son of God") appears, Bock observes with Barrett and Bruce, "only in MSS of the Western
family," is not original, and is "probably a second-century addition" reflecting later
baptismal practice.
