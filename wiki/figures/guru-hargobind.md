## Biographical Overview

Guru Hargobind was the sixth Guru of the Sikhs, succeeding his father [[guru-arjan|Guru Arjan]]. His tenure marked the most radical single shift in the direction of the Panth. According to McLeod, "Under the sixth Guru, Hargobind, the Panth radically shifted direction. The Mughal rulers were proving hostile and in order to protect itself the Panth took up arms under the direct leadership of the Guru" (McLeod ~L239–241).

The Guru attracted growing numbers of Jat converts — typically rural, martial people — whose "loyalty to the leader rather than deep awareness of the teachings of the divine Name" shaped the period's character (McLeod ~L31–32). McLeod treats this as a sociological explanation for the conflicts of the era rather than requiring magnification into "full-scale battles" (McLeod ~L2–7).

Hargobind had married three wives and had six children, five of them sons. The eldest, Gurditta — initially marked as successor — predeceased him in 1638. Other sons either died early or were deemed unsuitable: Tegh Bahadur was thought "too withdrawn" (though his later selection as ninth Guru casts doubt on this tradition), and Dhir Mai was considered hostile to the Panth's interests. Har Rai, Gurditta's younger son, was chosen as successor (McLeod ~L43–80).

Because troubles with Mughal authorities were growing increasingly serious, Hargobind abandoned Amritsar and withdrew to Kiratpur on the edge of the Shivalik hills, where he remained until his death in 1644 (McLeod ~L39–41).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

McLeod treats Hargobind's militarization as a historically explicable response to Mughal hostility, dismissing accounts of "full-scale battles" as exaggeration. The Sikh theological answer to any apparent inconsistency with Guru Nanak's interior spirituality is that "there is only one Guru, successively inhabiting ten human bodies" (McLeod ~L912–913) — so what Hargobind decreed was what Nanak himself would have decreed in comparable circumstances.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Hargobind introduced the [[miri-piri|miri-piri]] doctrine — the dual authority of temporal sword (*miri*) and spiritual sword (*piri*) — which permanently altered the Panth's self-understanding as both a religious and a martial community. This militarization set the template that Guru Gobind Singh would institutionalize in 1699 with the founding of the [[khalsa|Khalsa]].

## Historicity and Controversies

McLeod cautions that many accounts exaggerate the conflicts of this period, suggesting that "a combination of disturbed circumstances in the Punjab together with the accession of growing numbers of typically turbulent Jats to the Guru's Panth provides a sufficient explanation" without invoking full battles (McLeod ~L1–11).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Hargobind's militarization is a touchstone in debates over the relationship between Guru Nanak's interior, name-centred spirituality and the external martial identity of the Khalsa.

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Ch. 4)

Where McLeod cautions against magnifying the period's conflicts, Khushwant Singh narrates the militarization
in full. Becoming Guru at eleven after Arjan's martyrdom, Hargobind girded **two swords** at his investiture
— declaring himself *mīrī pīrī dā mālik*, "the lord of the spiritual and secular domains" (Singh, ch. 4,
fn.3; the founding gesture of [[miri-piri|miri-piri]]) — accepted "offerings of arms and horses instead of
money," built the **Lohgarh** fortress at [[amritsar|Amritsar]], and erected the **Akāl Taḵẖt** ("the throne
of the Timeless God") facing the Harimandir, where "the congregation heard ballads extolling feats of heroism…
and discussed plans of military conquests." Jahangir imprisoned him at Gwalior over Arjan's unpaid fine.
Singh records open clashes with Shah Jahan's forces (Amritsar 1628; Lahira; Kartarpur) and his retirement to
**Kiratpur**, where he died in 1644. Singh's verdict: the shift "from a peaceful propagation of the faith to
the forthright declaration of the right to defend that faith by force of arms proved to be extremely
popular."
