# Michal

**Michal**, the younger daughter of [[saul|Saul]], is [[david|David's]] first wife — the one
character of whom the text says **she "loved David"** (1 Sam 18:20), the only woman in the
Hebrew Bible explicitly said to love a man. Her story arcs from that love, through her
saving David's life and being given to another man by her father, to a bitter final
estrangement and childlessness — a casualty of the dynastic struggle between the house of
Saul and the house of David.

## Biographical Overview and Primary Appearances

- **Marriage** — Saul, learning Michal loves David, sets a bride-price of a hundred
  Philistine foreskins, hoping David will die collecting it; David brings two hundred and
  marries her (1 Sam 18).
- **Saving David** — When Saul sends men to kill David, Michal lowers him through a window to
  escape and places a household idol (*teraphim*) in the bed to deceive the assassins,
  buying him time (1 Sam 19).
- **Given away** — In David's absence Saul gives Michal to **Paltiel son of Laish** (1 Sam
  25:44).
- **Returned** — As a condition of [[abner|Abner's]] defection, David demands Michal back;
  she is taken from Paltiel, who follows her weeping until Abner orders him home (2 Sam 3).
- **Estrangement** — When David dances "with all his might" before the ark entering
  Jerusalem, Michal watches "from the window" and "despised him in her heart," then rebukes
  him for exposing himself before the slave-girls. David retorts that he danced before the
  LORD who chose him over her father's house. The text closes: **"Michal daughter of Saul had
  no child to the day of her death"** (2 Sam 6).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Michal embodies the human cost of dynastic politics. Her trajectory — loving David,
risking herself for him, then traded and finally rejected — tracks the displacement of the
house of Saul. The closing notice of her childlessness seals the end of any union of the two
royal houses through her: no descendant of Saul will sit beside the line of David. Her clash
with David over the dance frames a confrontation between **Saulide dignity** and David's
exuberant, self-abasing devotion before YHWH.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) uses Michal's story to
illustrate the writer's "freighted imagining of the detail not strictly necessary to the
historical account." Of her removal from Paltiel he singles out the "few, indelible words":
**"And her husband went with her, weeping as he went after her, as far as Bahurim. And Abner
said to him, 'Go back!' And he went back."** To a sober historian, Alter writes, "this moment
might well seem superfluous"; to "a great imaginative writer" it is the kind of detail that
opens a whole inner world — Michal's silence and Paltiel's grief set against David's purely
political motive in reclaiming her. The final scene of contempt at the window, Alter notes,
gives Michal the last reported feeling of any Saulide toward David.

## Reception

Rabbinic tradition is divided over the window scene — some praising Michal's piety and
faulting David's display, others justifying David and reading her childlessness as judgment
for despising the LORD's anointed (or, in one tradition, harmonizing the texts so that
Michal raised her sister's sons). She is remembered as a tragic figure of love crushed
between two kings.
