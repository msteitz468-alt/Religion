# Mitra

**Mitra** ("Contract," "Friend") is the Vedic god of **covenants, oaths, friendship, and the binding
agreements that hold society together** — the close partner of **[[varuna|Varuna]]**, with whom he forms the
sovereign pair (the *Adityas*) upholding **[[rita|rita]]**. Where Varuna is the awesome, punishing guardian of
cosmic order, Mitra is the **benevolent god of human concord** — he watches over the keeping of promises,
presides over the daytime, and "brings people together," holding the social and moral world in order through
the **sanctity of the contract**. His Iranian cousin **Mithra** (a great god of covenant, truth, and the sun)
gave rise, by a long and indirect route, to the Roman mystery god of [[mithraism|Mithraism]] — a striking
Indo-Iranian-to-Roman thread.

## The Iranian Mithra (Mihr)

In [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]] tradition **Mithra** (later **Mihr**) is the foremost of
the **[[amesha-spentas|yazatas]]** — the lesser beneficent divinities under
[[ahura-mazda|Ahura Mazda]]. Per [[rose-zoroastrianism-an-introduction|Rose]], the word
*mithra* means **"contract / covenant"** (also "ally, bond"), and the long **Mihr Yasht**
(Yt 10) celebrates him as:

- the **protector and sustainer of the one who keeps the contract**, and the relentless
  judge who **smashes the covenant-breaker** with his mace — the bull-headed "mace of
  Mithra" still bestowed on Parsi priests at initiation;
- an **upholder of [[asha-druj|asha]]** and defender of those who reject the Lie;
- intimately linked with the **sun**: he rises beyond **Mt. Hara** ahead of the sun and
  surveys the whole material world from the mountaintops with **ten thousand eyes**.

Mithra is named among the **judges of the soul** at the [[chinvat-bridge|Chinvat
crossing]] (Vd 19.28–9). The **seventh month** and the sixteenth day of the
[[persians|Achaemenid]] Avestan calendar bear his name, and Greek sources from the fourth
century BCE attest an official festival, the **Mithrakana** (later Mihragan). The Iranian
royal cult invokes him beside **[[anahita|Anahita]]** (Artaxerxes II), and a Sasanian
relief of Mithra at **Taq-e Bostan** later became the model for modern icons of
[[zarathustra|Zarathushtra]] himself.
