# Joshua

Joshua (originally Hoshea son of Nun of the tribe of Ephraim) is the divinely appointed successor to Moses, the military leader of the conquest of Canaan, and a model of faithful obedience and covenant leadership. He first appears as Moses' aide and commander in the battle against Amalek (Exod 17), as one of the two faithful spies (Num 13–14), and is publicly commissioned by Moses before the people (Num 27; Deut 31, 34). His story culminates in the book of Joshua, where he leads Israel across the Jordan, conquers the land, divides it among the tribes, and renews the covenant at Shechem.

## Biographical Overview

Born in Egypt during the oppression, Joshua (whose name means "YHWH saves" or "YHWH is salvation") rises as a trusted lieutenant to Moses. He ascends Sinai with Moses (Exod 24), guards the tent of meeting (Exod 33), and commands the army against Amalek (Exod 17:8–16). As one of the twelve spies sent from Kadesh-barnea, he and [[caleb|Caleb]] alone give a positive report and urge immediate conquest, earning God's promise that they alone of their generation will enter the land (Num 13–14; Deut 1:36–38). Moses publicly commissions him as leader in the sight of the people (Num 27:18–23; Deut 31:7–8, 14–15, 23), laying hands on him and charging him to be strong and courageous. After Moses' death, God confirms the commission directly (Josh 1:1–9), promising presence and success if Joshua meditates on the law day and night.

Per [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] ([[woudstra-joshua-nicot|Joshua NICOT]]): Josh 1 already plants the book's leading motifs—succession, Jordan crossing, conquest, “all Israel,” covenant obedience, allotment, and land as God's gift. Continuity of leadership is assured: Joshua completes Moses' unfinished mission, yet Moses retains the honorific “servant of the Lord,” while Joshua is still “Moses’ aide” until 24:29. Direct divine speech to Joshua (as to Moses) means that in all instances “Israel’s true Leader is the Lord.” The thrice-repeated “be strong and courageous” is not superfluous: Joshua will bring the fathers’ oath-promise to fruition; courage is linked to strict adherence to the whole law of Moses—tôrâ as covenant statute of grace, not work-righteousness; the book of the law is to stay in his mouth and be meditated on day and night. Martial aspects of 1:10–11 are muted: ownership by promise must become actual possession, the land remaining God’s gift. The Transjordan tribes are charged so that “rest” already granted east of Jordan may not be fully enjoyed until all Israel achieves it together.

Joshua leads the crossing of the Jordan (Josh 3–4, with the ark and memorial stones), the conquest of Jericho (ch. 6, with the famous walls falling after seven days of marching and trumpet blasts — liturgical procession rather than conventional siege), the initial defeat and subsequent victory at Ai (chs. 7–8, after Achan’s sin), the southern campaign (ch. 10, including the long day at Gibeon and the defeat of the five Amorite kings), and the northern campaign (ch. 11, defeating Jabin of Hazor and allies). Immediately after crossing he performs the cultic acts of mass circumcision and Passover (5:1–12). Woudstra reads the circumcision as a Joshua–Moses parallel: as Moses was reminded to circumcise before fully entering his deliverer role (Exod 4:24–26), Joshua must circumcise before his role as captain of the Lord’s people in the Conquest; “rolling away Egypt’s shame” at Gilgal means bondage broken in principle at the Exodus is finally removed once Israel is safely in Canaan. He encounters the captain of the LORD’s army (5:13–15)—for Woudstra a self-contained theophany // Exod 3:1–22 (shoes off, holy ground), the drawn sword directed against Israel’s enemies, not a sanctuary-etiology remnant. At Jericho, Woudstra stresses liturgical/holy-war form: not magical circumambulation (command comes from the Lord; ark’s role precludes magic); first city of the [[promised-land|promised land]] by a shout at Joshua’s command. Joshua’s curse on fortifying Jericho (6:26) is potent because the Lord bears the word (fulfilled 1 Kgs 16:34); closing fame of Joshua (6:27) is triumphant but not hero-worship (cf. ch. 7). At Ai, “as another Moses,” Joshua holds the javelin until the ban is executed (8:26 // Exod 17). On Ebal and Gerizim (8:30–35) he proclaims blessings and curses: Woudstra holds possession of the land tied to proclamation of and subjection to God’s covenant claims; altar // patriarchal claim-altars and Sinai; whole *qāhāl* including sojourners.

In the southern campaign Woudstra notes Joshua as naive/rash in the Gibeon treaty (no Lord’s counsel, 9:14) yet oath-keeper and deliverer of the Gibeonites from Israel’s murderous intent—stature enhanced without whitewashing failure. He divides the land among the tribes (chs. 13–21), with special allotments for Caleb (Hebron) and the Levites; Eleazar the priest takes priority in distribution (14:1–2; 19:51). By analogy with Caleb at eighty-five, Woudstra judges Joshua of similar age when allotment is commanded (13:1); he allots rather than fully conquers remaining land—possession by allotment. Structurally, Joshua’s inheritance at Timnath-serah (19:49–51) frames the allotment chapters with Caleb’s claim (14:6–15): the two good spies bracket “the book of the land.” At the end of his life he renews the covenant at Shechem (ch. 24), setting up a stone as witness and charging the people to “choose this day whom you will serve” (24:15)—for Woudstra house solidarity as favorable inverse of Achan’s household solidarity in evil. Woudstra catalogs Moses//Joshua parallels: both cross water; both have “burning bush”–type encounters; both hold staff/javelin in battle; both build altars; farewell addresses // Deut 31–32. He dies at 110 and is buried in his inheritance at Timnath-serah (24:29–30); only then is he titled “servant of the LORD”—previously reserved for Moses. Woudstra stresses that the “prophetical” narrator presents history theocentrically—from God’s perspective—with deliberate literary design supporting promise fulfillment and covenant responsibility.

From [[peter-craigie|Craigie]] (Deuteronomy): Joshua is the faithful executor of the covenant, embodying the transition from Moses' prophetic/legislative role to military and administrative leadership. Craigie notes the transfer of authority in Deut 31 and 34 as a model of orderly succession under divine appointment, with Joshua charged to "be strong and courageous" (Deut 31:7, 23; Josh 1:6–9), echoing the treaty stipulations and the call to exclusive loyalty to YHWH. The conquest is framed as covenant enforcement, fulfilling the promises to the patriarchs while warning of the consequences of disobedience.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Exodus**: Moses' aide and military commander against Amalek (17:8–16); ascends Sinai with Moses (24:13); guards the tent of meeting (33:11).
- **Numbers**: One of the twelve spies sent from Kadesh (13–14); intercedes with Moses and Caleb against the people's rebellion; granted entry to the land (14:6–9, 30, 38); publicly commissioned as Moses' successor (27:18–23).
- **Deuteronomy**: Charged by Moses before the people (31:7–8); witness to the song of Moses and the covenant renewal (31:14–15, 23; 32:44); present at Moses' death and burial (34:9); the people "listen to him" as they did to Moses.
- **Joshua**: Central figure throughout — charge and law-meditation (1); spies/[[rahab|Rahab]] (2); Jordan crossing and exaltation “as with Moses” (3–4); circumcision/Passover and captain of the LORD’s army (5); Jericho and Rahab spared (6); Achan and Ai (7–8); Ebal/Gerizim law (8:30–35); Gibeon treaty and southern/northern campaigns (9–12); allotment with Eleazar, Caleb’s grant, own inheritance Timnath-serah (13–19); cities of asylum and Levitical cities (20–21); Transjordan dismissal and altar crisis (22); farewell and Shechem covenant (23–24); death and burial as “servant of the LORD” (24:29–30).
- **Judges**: Mentioned in the context of the incomplete conquest and the faithfulness of his generation (Judg 1–2:7).
- **1 Kings**: Reference in the context of covenant faithfulness and the "book of the law of Moses" (context of 1 Kgs 2 or later reflections); curse on Jericho’s fortification fulfilled (1 Kgs 16:34).
- **Acts**: Stephen's speech references the conquest under Joshua (Acts 7:45).
- **Hebrews**: Joshua's rest is contrasted with the Sabbath rest that remains for God's people (Heb 4:8–10); listed among the heroes of faith (Heb 11:30, walls of Jericho fell by faith).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Judaism**: Joshua is the faithful servant who completes Moses' work and leads Israel into the promised land. The book of Joshua is read in synagogue on certain festivals. Rabbinic tradition emphasizes his humility (he never sought leadership), his role in the circumcision at Gilgal and the first Passover in the land, and his covenant renewal at Shechem as a model for later generations. In the Passover Haggadah and midrash, he is celebrated as the one who fulfills the promise to the patriarchs. Samaritan tradition holds him in high regard as a prophet and leader who established worship on Mount Gerizim.
- **Christianity (evangelical, per Craigie and Woudstra)**: Joshua is a type of Christ — the "captain of the Lord's host" (Josh 5:13–15), leading God's people into their inheritance. Craigie (Deuteronomy) and other commentators see the conquest as fulfillment of the Abrahamic promise and a paradigm of spiritual warfare, rest (Heb 4), and covenant faithfulness. His charge "be strong and courageous" (Josh 1:6–9) is applied to Christian leadership and perseverance. The book illustrates both the gift of the land and the responsibility of obedience; incomplete conquest (remaining Canaanites) leads to later oppression in Judges. **Woudstra** (NICOT) adds: law-meditation as concrete covenant fellowship (Soggin’s sola-scriptura analogy noted with approval); Moses//Joshua redemptive-historical continuity without collapsing into bare moral example; “already–not yet” land fulfilment (11:23 vs 13:1); complete and incomplete conquest held side by side as equally valid (23:4–5); rest with expanding horizons through David/Solomon to Heb 4; Shechem survey puts God at the center rather than reducing history to moral examples; positive evaluation of Israel’s fidelity “to this day” (23:8; 24:31) with hopeful book message—later calamities not laid to God’s charge.
- **Other Christian**: In patristic and medieval exegesis, Joshua prefigures Jesus (the name "Joshua" = "Jesus" in Greek); the crossing of Jordan is baptism or death to sin, the conquest is the Christian life, Rahab is the Church of the Gentiles, the walls of Jericho fall by faith (Heb 11). In liberation theology, Joshua is sometimes critiqued for the violence of conquest; evangelical responses emphasize it as judgment on Canaanite sin (Gen 15:16) and fulfillment of divine promise, while noting the book's own warnings about partial obedience.
- **Islamic**: Yusha' ibn Nun (Joshua son of Nun), companion and successor to Musa (Moses). Mentioned in Islamic tradition as the one who leads the Israelites into the land; some sources identify him with the "servant of God" in the Quranic story of Khidr (Surah 18). Venerated as a righteous military leader and prophet.
- **Critical scholarship**: The historicity of the conquest is heavily debated (archaeological evidence for destruction layers at Jericho, Ai, etc., is sparse or contradictory; many see the book as etiological or ideological, written to justify later Israelite claims to the land). Craigie and conservative scholars defend a core historical nucleus consistent with Late Bronze Age conditions, while acknowledging literary shaping and the book's theological agenda. The "ban" ([[herem|ḥerem]]) raises ethical questions about violence and genocide in modern readings. Woudstra’s counter-positions (attributed, not adjudicated here): chs. 3–4 as literary-theological whole vs composite sources; Jericho/Ai as historical narrative *and* theological presentation; monuments “until this day” caused by events, not inventing stories; ḥerem not “sub-Christian” without involving God in evolution from cruel to benign.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Joshua completes the Pentateuchal arc: the promises to the patriarchs (Genesis), deliverance and covenant at Sinai (Exodus–Deuteronomy) are realized in the conquest and settlement. He is the model of obedient leadership — "not one word of all the good promises that the Lord had made to the house of Israel had failed" (Josh 21:45; 23:14). Theologically, the book explores the tension between divine gift (the land is given) and human responsibility (it must be taken by faith and obedience). Per Woudstra, land-as-gift is continuous with patriarchal *nāṯan* language; comparison of 13:1 with 11:23 yields only partial fulfilment—an “already–not yet” dimension typical of the OT; conditionality of covenant promises is not in conflict with sovereign grace. The "rest" motif (Josh 11:23; 21:44; 22:4; 23:1; cf. Heb 4) has expanding horizons and points beyond the temporal land to eschatological rest in Christ. Joshua's covenant renewal at Shechem (ch. 24) echoes Deuteronomy and calls for exclusive loyalty: "Choose this day whom you will serve" (24:15). His story warns against incomplete obedience (Gibeonites, remaining Canaanites, Achan’s *māʿal*) and models intercession, covenant fidelity, and the centrality of the law ("this Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth," Josh 1:8). Three graves at book’s end (Joshua, Joseph, Eleazar)—for Woudstra, symbolic fulfilment: remains of the instrument of obtaining land, the faith that looked to the promise (Gen 50), and the priest of distribution laid to rest in the land promised to the fathers.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Conquest historicity**: Archaeological debates (Jericho's walls fell centuries earlier than the traditional date; no destruction at Ai in the right period; Hazor has a destruction layer but dating disputed). Traditional view: the biblical account is substantially historical, with possible later editing for theological emphasis. Critical view: largely legendary or ideological, composed in the monarchic or exilic period to legitimize claims to the land and explain the presence of Canaanite populations. **Woudstra**: Jericho usually Tell eṣ-Ṣultan but identification remains open; belief in miracle does not exclude natural causes (earthquake etc.), yet the story is silent and means to celebrate God’s act; archaeology has limited but valuable role. Usual Ai = et-Tell yields an uninhabited site at entry—solutions (wrong identification; Bethel camp; tradition transfer) debated; king of Ai makes pure transfer theories difficult. Southern-campaign variations mark reliability for Woudstra, not later streamlining.
- **The "ban" ([[herem|ḥerem]])**: Total destruction of certain Canaanite cities raises ethical questions (genocide? divine command to violence?). Craigie and others argue it was limited, applied only to certain cities as judgment on Canaanite sin (Deut 9:4–5; 20:16–18), and not practiced uniformly ([[rahab|Rahab]] and Gibeonites spared by covenant or oath). **Woudstra**: ḥerem = absolute irrevocable consecration (Lev 27:28–29) *and* sentence to utter destruction (Deut 13:16); both senses; degrees of application; Jericho most rigorous by way of example; temporal destruction as prelude/foreshadowing of final judgment when unrighteousness is full (Gen 15:16); cannot correctly call ḥerem “sub-Christian” without involving God in evolution from cruel to benign; Ai milder (spoil permitted). Modern interpreters debate whether it is prescriptive for all time or descriptive of a specific historical-theological moment.
- **Succession from Moses**: The transfer of authority (Deut 31, 34; Josh 1) is seen as a model of orderly leadership transition under divine appointment. Some critical scholars see it as a literary device to link the Pentateuch to the Former Prophets (Deuteronomistic History). Woudstra stresses continuous work of God and exaltation of Joshua “in the eyes of all Israel” at the Jordan (3:7 // Exod 14:31), with Joshua less instrumentally direct than Moses at the Red Sea (ark’s presence).
- **The long day (Josh 10:12–14)**: Astronomical miracle or poetic hyperbole? Traditional: miraculous extension of daylight for battle. Critical: poetic or local atmospheric phenomenon.
- **Incomplete conquest**: The book itself notes that not all the land was taken (Josh 13:1–7; 23:4–5), leading to ongoing conflict in Judges. Theologically significant: the land is both gift and task. Woudstra: complete and incomplete lines run side by side and are equally valid (23:4–5), with emphasis on completeness predominant; driving out intended to take long (Exod 23:29–30; Deut 7:22).
- **Gibeon treaty (Josh 9)**: Joshua’s failure to inquire of the Lord vs. subsequent oath-keeping—tension between wisdom failure and covenant fidelity (Woudstra).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

- **Woudstra (NICOT Joshua)**: Central modern evangelical frame for this figure in the wiki’s sources. Theocentric “prophetical” historiography; land-as-gift with already–not yet; Moses//Joshua continuity culminating in “servant of the LORD” only at death; Torah meditation and covenant statute of grace; liturgical Jericho and ḥerem theology; Ebal possession-tied-to-covenant; Caleb–Joshua structural bracket of allotment; rest motif to Heb 4; Shechem covenant as hopeful positive message; three graves as fulfilment symbol. See [[woudstra-joshua-nicot|Woudstra Joshua NICOT]] and [[marten-woudstra|Marten Woudstra]].
- **Craigie (Deuteronomy)**: Joshua as the faithful executor of the covenant, with the treaty form providing the theological framework for the conquest as covenant enforcement and fulfillment of patriarchal promises.
- **Jewish tradition**: Joshua as the ideal military and spiritual leader who completes the exodus; the book read in synagogue to recall the entry into the land and the centrality of Torah. Emphasis on his humility and the covenant renewal at Shechem.
- **Christian tradition**: Typological (Joshua = Jesus; conquest = Christian life/spiritual warfare; crossing Jordan = baptism or death to sin; walls of Jericho fall by faith). In Hebrews, the "rest" is fulfilled in Christ (Heb 4). Used in discussions of holy war, just war theory, and the relationship between promise and fulfillment. The charge "be strong and courageous" is a favorite for Christian leadership.
- **Modern scholarship**: Source criticism (Deuteronomistic redaction linking to Joshua–Kings); form-critical (etiological legends for place names, boundaries, and customs); canonical (the book as the "fulfillment" section of the Pentateuchal narrative, with warnings about partial obedience). Feminist readings note the roles of Rahab and the daughters of Zelophehad. Liberation and postcolonial readings critique the violence and land dispossession; evangelical responses emphasize judgment on sin and the book's internal critique of incomplete obedience.
- **Cross-tradition**: In Islamic sources, Yusha' is a prophet and military leader who completes Musa's mission; some traditions link him to the servant in Surah 18.
