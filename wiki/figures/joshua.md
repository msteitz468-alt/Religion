# Joshua

Joshua (originally Hoshea son of Nun of the tribe of Ephraim) is the divinely appointed successor to Moses, the military leader of the conquest of Canaan, and a model of faithful obedience and covenant leadership. He first appears as Moses' aide and commander in the battle against Amalek (Exod 17), as one of the two faithful spies (Num 13–14), and is publicly commissioned by Moses before the people (Num 27; Deut 31, 34). His story culminates in the book of Joshua, where he leads Israel across the Jordan, conquers the land, divides it among the tribes, and renews the covenant at Shechem.

## Biographical Overview

Born in Egypt during the oppression, Joshua (whose name means "YHWH saves" or "YHWH is salvation") rises as a trusted lieutenant to Moses. He ascends Sinai with Moses (Exod 24), guards the tent of meeting (Exod 33), and commands the army against Amalek (Exod 17:8–16). As one of the twelve spies sent from Kadesh-barnea, he and Caleb alone give a positive report and urge immediate conquest, earning God's promise that they alone of their generation will enter the land (Num 13–14; Deut 1:36–38). Moses publicly commissions him as leader in the sight of the people (Num 27:18–23; Deut 31:7–8, 14–15, 23), laying hands on him and charging him to be strong and courageous. After Moses' death, God confirms the commission directly (Josh 1:1–9), promising presence and success if Joshua meditates on the law day and night.

Joshua leads the crossing of the Jordan (Josh 3–4, with the ark and memorial stones), the conquest of Jericho (ch. 6, with the famous "walls came tumbling down" after seven days of marching and trumpet blasts — liturgical procession rather than conventional siege), the initial defeat and subsequent victory at Ai (chs. 7–8, after Achan's sin), the southern campaign (ch. 10, including the long day at Gibeon and the defeat of the five Amorite kings), and the northern campaign (ch. 11, defeating Jabin of Hazor and allies). Immediately after crossing he performs the cultic acts of mass circumcision and Passover (5:1–12), encounters the captain of the LORD's army (5:13–15), and later proclaims blessings and curses on Ebal and Gerizim (8:30–35). He divides the land among the tribes (chs. 13–21), with special allotments for Caleb (Hebron) and the Levites (cities of refuge and pasturelands); Eleazar the priest participates in the distribution (19:51). At the end of his life, he renews the covenant at Shechem (ch. 24), setting up a stone as witness and charging the people to "choose this day whom you will serve" (24:15). "Be strong and courageous" is a recurring keynote (Josh 1:6–9, 18; 10:25; echoing Deut 31 and Moses' charge). He dies at 110 and is buried in his inheritance at Timnath-serah (24:29–30). Woudstra (NICOT) stresses that the "prophetical" narrator presents history theocentrically — from God's perspective — with deliberate literary design supporting the theme of promise fulfillment and covenant responsibility.

From [[peter-craigie|Craigie]] (Deuteronomy): Joshua is the faithful executor of the covenant, embodying the transition from Moses' prophetic/legislative role to military and administrative leadership. Craigie notes the transfer of authority in Deut 31 and 34 as a model of orderly succession under divine appointment, with Joshua charged to "be strong and courageous" (Deut 31:7, 23; Josh 1:6–9), echoing the treaty stipulations and the call to exclusive loyalty to YHWH. The conquest is framed as covenant enforcement, fulfilling the promises to the patriarchs while warning of the consequences of disobedience.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Exodus**: Moses' aide and military commander against Amalek (17:8–16); ascends Sinai with Moses (24:13); guards the tent of meeting (33:11).
- **Numbers**: One of the twelve spies sent from Kadesh (13–14); intercedes with Moses and Caleb against the people's rebellion; granted entry to the land (14:6–9, 30, 38); publicly commissioned as Moses' successor (27:18–23).
- **Deuteronomy**: Charged by Moses before the people (31:7–8); witness to the song of Moses and the covenant renewal (31:14–15, 23; 32:44); present at Moses' death and burial (34:9); the people "listen to him" as they did to Moses.
- **Joshua**: Central figure throughout — crossing the Jordan (3–4), conquest of Jericho and Ai (6–8), southern and northern campaigns (10–11), division of the land (13–21), covenant renewal at Shechem (24), death and burial (24:29–30).
- **Judges**: Mentioned in the context of the incomplete conquest and the faithfulness of his generation (Judg 1–2:7).
- **1 Kings**: Reference in the context of covenant faithfulness and the "book of the law of Moses" (context of 1 Kgs 2 or later reflections).
- **Acts**: Stephen's speech references the conquest under Joshua (Acts 7:45).
- **Hebrews**: Joshua's rest is contrasted with the Sabbath rest that remains for God's people (Heb 4:8–10); listed among the heroes of faith (Heb 11:30, walls of Jericho fell by faith).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Judaism**: Joshua is the faithful servant who completes Moses' work and leads Israel into the promised land. The book of Joshua is read in synagogue on certain festivals. Rabbinic tradition emphasizes his humility (he never sought leadership), his role in the circumcision at Gilgal and the first Passover in the land, and his covenant renewal at Shechem as a model for later generations. In the Passover Haggadah and midrash, he is celebrated as the one who fulfills the promise to the patriarchs. Samaritan tradition holds him in high regard as a prophet and leader who established worship on Mount Gerizim.
- **Christianity (evangelical, per Craigie and sources)**: Joshua is a type of Christ — the "captain of the Lord's host" (Josh 5:13–15), leading God's people into their inheritance. Craigie (Deuteronomy) and other commentators see the conquest as fulfillment of the Abrahamic promise and a paradigm of spiritual warfare, rest (Heb 4), and covenant faithfulness. His charge "be strong and courageous" (Josh 1:6–9) is applied to Christian leadership and perseverance. The book illustrates both the gift of the land and the responsibility of obedience; incomplete conquest (remaining Canaanites) leads to later oppression in Judges.
- **Other Christian**: In patristic and medieval exegesis, Joshua prefigures Jesus (the name "Joshua" = "Jesus" in Greek); the crossing of Jordan is baptism or death to sin, the conquest is the Christian life, Rahab is the Church of the Gentiles, the walls of Jericho fall by faith (Heb 11). In liberation theology, Joshua is sometimes critiqued for the violence of conquest; evangelical responses emphasize it as judgment on Canaanite sin (Gen 15:16) and fulfillment of divine promise, while noting the book's own warnings about partial obedience.
- **Islamic**: Yusha' ibn Nun (Joshua son of Nun), companion and successor to Musa (Moses). Mentioned in Islamic tradition as the one who leads the Israelites into the land; some sources identify him with the "servant of God" in the Quranic story of Khidr (Surah 18). Venerated as a righteous military leader and prophet.
- **Critical scholarship**: The historicity of the conquest is heavily debated (archaeological evidence for destruction layers at Jericho, Ai, etc., is sparse or contradictory; many see the book as etiological or ideological, written to justify later Israelite claims to the land). Craigie and conservative scholars defend a core historical nucleus consistent with Late Bronze Age conditions, while acknowledging literary shaping and the book's theological agenda. The "ban" (herem) raises ethical questions about violence and genocide in modern readings.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Joshua completes the Pentateuchal arc: the promises to the patriarchs (Genesis), deliverance and covenant at Sinai (Exodus–Deuteronomy) are realized in the conquest and settlement. He is the model of obedient leadership — "not one word of all the good promises that the Lord had made to the house of Israel had failed" (Josh 21:45; 23:14). Theologically, the book explores the tension between divine gift (the land is given) and human responsibility (it must be taken by faith and obedience). The "rest" motif (Josh 11:23; 21:44; cf. Heb 4) points beyond the temporal land to eschatological rest in Christ. Joshua's covenant renewal at Shechem (ch. 24) echoes Deuteronomy and calls for exclusive loyalty: "Choose this day whom you will serve" (24:15). His story warns against the dangers of incomplete obedience (the Gibeonites, remaining Canaanites) and models intercession, covenant fidelity, and the centrality of the law ("this Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth," Josh 1:8).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Conquest historicity**: Archaeological debates (Jericho's walls fell centuries earlier than the traditional date; no destruction at Ai in the right period; Hazor has a destruction layer but dating disputed). Traditional view: the biblical account is substantially historical, with possible later editing for theological emphasis. Critical view: largely legendary or ideological, composed in the monarchic or exilic period to legitimize claims to the land and explain the presence of Canaanite populations.
- **The "ban" (herem)**: Total destruction of certain Canaanite cities raises ethical questions (genocide? divine command to violence?). Craigie and others argue it was limited, applied only to certain cities as judgment on Canaanite sin (Deut 9:4–5; 20:16–18), and not practiced uniformly (Rahab and Gibeonites spared by covenant or oath). Modern interpreters debate whether it is prescriptive for all time or descriptive of a specific historical-theological moment.
- **Succession from Moses**: The transfer of authority (Deut 31, 34; Josh 1) is seen as a model of orderly leadership transition under divine appointment. Some critical scholars see it as a literary device to link the Pentateuch to the Former Prophets (Deuteronomistic History).
- **The long day (Josh 10:12–14)**: Astronomical miracle or poetic hyperbole? Traditional: miraculous extension of daylight for battle. Critical: poetic or local atmospheric phenomenon.
- **Incomplete conquest**: The book itself notes that not all the land was taken (Josh 13:1–7; 23:4–5), leading to ongoing conflict in Judges. This is theologically significant: the land is both gift and task.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

- **Craigie (Deuteronomy)**: Joshua as the faithful executor of the covenant, with the treaty form providing the theological framework for the conquest as covenant enforcement and fulfillment of patriarchal promises.
- **Jewish tradition**: Joshua as the ideal military and spiritual leader who completes the exodus; the book read in synagogue to recall the entry into the land and the centrality of Torah. Emphasis on his humility and the covenant renewal at Shechem.
- **Christian tradition**: Typological (Joshua = Jesus; conquest = Christian life/spiritual warfare; crossing Jordan = baptism or death to sin; walls of Jericho fall by faith). In Hebrews, the "rest" is fulfilled in Christ (Heb 4). Used in discussions of holy war, just war theory, and the relationship between promise and fulfillment. The charge "be strong and courageous" is a favorite for Christian leadership.
- **Modern scholarship**: Source criticism (Deuteronomistic redaction linking to Joshua–Kings); form-critical (etiological legends for place names, boundaries, and customs); canonical (the book as the "fulfillment" section of the Pentateuchal narrative, with warnings about partial obedience). Feminist readings note the roles of Rahab and the daughters of Zelophehad. Liberation and postcolonial readings critique the violence and land dispossession; evangelical responses emphasize judgment on sin and the book's internal critique of incomplete obedience.
- **Cross-tradition**: In Islamic sources, Yusha' is a prophet and military leader who completes Musa's mission; some traditions link him to the servant in Surah 18.
