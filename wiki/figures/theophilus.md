**Biographical Overview**:
Theophilus is the individual to whom both the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles are explicitly addressed ("most excellent Theophilus," κράτιστε Θεόφιλε). Nothing is known of him outside the prologues of these two works. Green and Bock treat him as a real historical person of high social status who served as literary patron.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Addressed in the formal Greek prologue (1:1-4). Luke presents an "orderly account" (διήγησις) based on eyewitnesses and ministers of the word, so that Theophilus "may know the certainty (ἀσφάλεια) of the things about which you have been instructed."
- **Acts of the Apostles**: Addressed in the prologue (1:1-2) as "Theophilus," with explicit reference to "the first book" (the Gospel) concerning all that Jesus began to do and teach until his ascension.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green argues Theophilus was a real person, not symbolic (despite the name's etymology "lover of God" or "dear to God"). The honorific "most excellent" (κράτιστε) indicates high social status—possibly a Roman official, equestrian, or patron of letters. As patron, he would have underwritten publication and circulation of the two-volume work. The prologue shows he had received some prior instruction (κατηχήθης) about the Christian movement but needed a more robust, persuasive foundation for "certainty." Green reads the address as both personal and representative of an educated, high-status audience grappling with the implications of the Jesus movement.
- **Bock (Acts)**: Reinforces the patron relationship and the purpose of "certainty." The two prologues frame Luke-Acts as a single, continuous work addressed to the same person, underscoring unity and the need for historical-theological grounding.
- **Early Church / Patristic**: Sometimes allegorized as any "God-lover" (generic Christian reader); modern consensus (following Green/Bock) rejects pure allegory in favor of historical patron with representative function.
- **Protestant / Evangelical**: Emphasizes the historical reliability and apologetic purpose—Luke writes to give certainty to inquirers or new believers of status.
- **Critical scholarship**: Debates the extent to which Theophilus is real vs. literary device; most accept a real dedicatee while noting the conventional form of ancient prefaces.
- **No significant Jewish or Islamic reception** (internal Christian literary convention).

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
As explicit narratee, Theophilus represents Luke's intended audience: educated, possibly high-status persons who have heard about Jesus and the church but need an "orderly" (καθεξῆς), historically grounded, theologically coherent account to achieve certainty. The dual prologues (Luke 1:1-4; Acts 1:1-2) bind the two volumes as one story of God's redemptive purpose—from Jesus' ministry through the Spirit-empowered expansion to "the ends of the earth." The address signals that the narrative is proclamation with persuasive intent: to confirm that these events are not obscure or seditious but the fulfillment of God's plan, with universal implications. Green notes the preface itself is "proclamation"; Bock stresses the historical monograph character aimed at thoughtful readers like Theophilus.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Real person vs. symbol**: Older views allegorized the name; Green and Bock (with most modern commentators) affirm a real high-status patron while recognizing the conventional literary form. The "most excellent" title is not used for ordinary Christians.
- **Social status and purpose**: Debate over whether Theophilus was already a Christian, a sympathetic inquirer, or an official needing reassurance about the movement's loyalty. The "certainty" language suggests strengthening existing (but incomplete) knowledge.
- **Patronage implications**: The dedication implies Theophilus' support for dissemination; this affects how we understand the work's intended circulation (not purely private).

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Theophilus as real patron of high status who has been "instructed" but needs certainty. The prologue frames the entire two-volume work as an orderly, eyewitness-based narrative whose purpose is assurance. The address is both personal and representative of an audience navigating the social/political implications of the Jesus movement. Fidelity re-ingest ([[green-luke-nicnt]], 2026-07-18): Green stresses that for Theophilus the primary ingredient is *order* of narrative; concern is less “Did it happen?” than “What happened?” and “What does it mean?” — narration is proclamation (ἀσφάλεια via persuasive sequence). Preface-form also signals release for wider public dissemination beyond a private dedicatee.
- **Bock (Acts)**: Reinforces the patron relationship and the unified purpose across Luke-Acts. The "first book" reference and the historical monograph genre serve readers like Theophilus who need reliable grounding in the origins of the new community.
- Broader: Influences understandings of Luke-Acts as apologetic historiography aimed at educated readers; model for dedications in early Christian literature. Cross-tradition: highlights the conventional yet purposeful address in Greco-Roman literary culture.
