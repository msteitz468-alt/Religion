## Biographical Overview

Shakuni is the brother of [[gandhari]] and thus the maternal uncle of Duryodhana and the Kauravas. He is the master dice player who engineers the game through which the Pandavas are stripped of their kingdom, their freedom, and nearly their wife. Rajagopalachari consistently designates him as "wicked" and his acts as "wicked." He is the central instrument of the Kaurava plot against the Pandavas, though he operates entirely in Duryodhana's service.

## Primary Source Appearances

### The Dice Game (Ch. XXIV–XXV)

Shakuni's role in the dice game is that of the expert who actually throws on behalf of the Kauravas. Yudhishthira was the one gambling, but it was Shakuni who cast: "Sakuni answered: 'I cast the dice'" — and consistently won each round. He pressed Yudhishthira at every step, asking "Is there anything else you can offer?" after each loss, until Yudhishthira had staked and lost his wealth, his brothers one by one, himself, and finally Draupadi.

When Vikarna (a son of Dhritarashtra) rose to argue that the wager for Draupadi was legally void on three grounds — she belonged to all five Pandavas, Yudhishthira had already lost his freedom, and Shakuni had violated game rules by suggesting the specific stake — Karna dismissed him and ordered Duhsasana to seize the garments. The rules Vikarna cited bear noting: Shakuni suggested Draupadi as the wager, which Vikarna argued was against "the rules of the game, under which neither player may demand a specific bet" (line 3223).

After [[dhritarashtra]] restored the Pandavas' freedom, Shakuni joined Duryodhana and Duhsasana in upbraiding the king for having frustrated their plans "on the very threshold of success." Duryodhana secured Dhritarashtra's "reluctant and bodeful approval" for a second game. Shakuni again cast the dice; again the Pandavas lost. The terms of this second loss sent the Pandavas to twelve years of forest exile plus a thirteenth year incognito.

### During the Forest Exile (Chs. XXIX, XXXVIII–XXXIX)

In the forest, Bhima explicitly names Shakuni alongside Karna and Duryodhana as the three enemies he is burning to kill: "I shall be satisfied only when the wicked Sakuni, Karna and Duryodhana are slain" (Ch. XXIX). Shakuni's name thus functions throughout the exile as one of the defining objects of Pandava grievance.

When Duryodhana wanted permission to visit the Pandavas in the forest (ostensibly for cattle stock-taking), it was Shakuni who talked [[dhritarashtra]] around after the king refused: "O king, Yudhishthira knows and follows the path of dharma. He has given his promise in the open assembly and the Pandavas will follow his bidding. The sons of Kunti will not show any enmity towards us. Do not oppose Duryodhana... I shall also accompany him and see to it that none of us go anywhere near the Pandavas." Dhritarashtra, "overpersuaded as usual," relented (Ch. XXXVIII).

When Duryodhana, captured by the gandharvas and released by the Pandavas, announced he would fast unto death in shame, it was Shakuni who urged him to desist: "Why do you say that you would give up your life when the kingdom seized from the Pandavas is yours to enjoy? Fasting serves no purpose; if you really repent of what you have done till now, you should make friends with the Pandavas and give them back their kingdom." This last remark snapped Duryodhana out of his despair — but by provoking his pride rather than reforming it (Ch. XXXIX).

### At the Council After the Exile (Ch. XLVIII)

When allied kings gathered at Upaplavya after the thirteenth year ended, Balarama proposed sending an emissary to Duryodhana for a peaceful settlement and mentioned that the envoy should "get the co-operation of Bhishma, Dhritarashtra, Drona and Vidura, Kripa and Aswatthama and even of Karna and Sakuni if possible." Shakuni is thus included among the Kaurava principals whose agreement would be necessary for any peace — a measure of his perceived influence.

During the battle at Matsya (Ch. XLVI), Aswatthama rebuked Karna and Duryodhana with pointed irony: "Can Sakuni, by mere cheating, sneak a victory in battle for you? A fight with Arjuna, you will find is a very different thing from a throw of the dice. The Gandiva will send forth sharp arrows and not fours and twos as in the game of dice" — confirming that Shakuni's method (cheating at dice) was broadly understood as his only instrument of power.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Shakuni is the human instrument of what Rajagopalachari treats as a metaphysical catastrophe. The narrator at several points employs the language of cosmic fate — Yudhishthira was "a mere pawn moved by Kali to relieve the burden of the world" — which situates Shakuni as the agent of a darkly determined mechanism rather than merely a scheming individual. Yet Rajagopalachari's own editorial voice also attributes the disaster to moral failure: Dhritarashtra's weakness, Duryodhana's covetousness, and the specific mechanism of the dice game which Shakuni made possible by his skill. The two explanations coexist without resolution.

Shakuni's distinction from Duryodhana is that he is a craftsman rather than a principal: he schemes on Duryodhana's behalf and for Duryodhana's goals. His personal motivations — whether hatred of the Pandavas, loyalty to the Kaurava cause, or the familial bond with his sister [[gandhari]] and her sons — are not explored in Rajagopalachari's text within this range.

He is consistently described as "wicked" ("the wicked Sakuni") in Rajagopalachari's narrator voice, without the complexity or explanatory sympathy shown to characters like [[dhritarashtra]].
