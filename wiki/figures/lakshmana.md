# Lakṣmaṇa

**Lakṣmaṇa** (Lakshmana) is the third of Daśaratha's four sons, born of Queen Sumitrā, and the most devoted companion of his half-brother [[rama|Rāma]]. He accompanies Rāma and [[sita|Sītā]] into fourteen years of forest exile, serves as Rāma's warrior shield throughout the war against [[ravana|Rāvaṇa]], and represents an ideal of fraternal loyalty and martial duty. In the Rāmāyaṇa he is understood as an avatar of Śeṣa (the cosmic serpent on whom Viṣṇu rests).

## The Exile Departure (Buck's Retelling)

At the moment of departure from Ayodhyā, Lakṣmaṇa is characterized primarily by complete, voluntary self-subordination to Rāma. When Rāma tries to dissuade him from coming to the forest, Lakṣmaṇa's vow is unequivocal: "I'll stay with you right through the gates of blazing Hell as long as you want me!" (lines 4464–4465). Vasishtha, blessing the three departing exiles, singles out Lakṣmaṇa: "This design of yours to serve Rama is already a great blessing to you, a great good fortune, your high wide way to heaven. You follow him, I salute you, I bless you" (lines 3901–3904).

Lakṣmaṇa preserves Rāma's sacred fire in a bowl in a corner of Sumantra's chariot when they depart (line 3913) — a detail that encodes his role as the practical keeper of Rāma's household and spiritual continuity.

## Character Portrait: The Night Watch at Tamasa

The most focused character portrait in this range comes during the first night of exile. The Kosalas who followed Rāma fall asleep by the Tamasa River. Lakṣmaṇa and Guha the Hunter King sit up all night by the shore. When Guha offers Lakṣmaṇa a soft bed, Lakṣmaṇa refuses: "King of the Wilderness, with Rama asleep on the ground and in misfortune what use would be my comfort?" (lines 4133–4134). They hold an all-night drinking contest — Guha telling the story of his death and rescue by Shiva — that ends at dawn, a draw (line 4382–4383).

Buck's narrator provides an explicit character statement about Lakṣmaṇa standing guard after Rāma and Sītā fall asleep:

> "Truly, he stood guard naturally; he had no fear and no violence in his heart; he had no envy and once he had sworn by Dharma he obeyed Rama. He never doubted Rama was right, or that he was right to obey him." (lines 4468–4472)

This formulation is precise: Lakṣmaṇa's obedience is not servile or unreflective — it is grounded in a sworn dharmic commitment. He is characterized by the *absence* of negative qualities (fear, violence, envy, doubt) as much as by positive ones.

His **blue eyes** are noted when he stands guard: "His blue eyes looked into the black night" (line 4468) — a detail that marks him as distinctive within the Rāmāyaṇa's visual iconography.

## The Warrior Role: Forest Provider and Builder

In the forest, Lakṣmaṇa is the practical arm of the exile. He goes first on the forest path: "First went Lakshmana holding his bow and carrying their fire and few possessions. Two quivers were on his back and he kept watch for animals and pitfalls and trod down the thorns and sharp grass. Sita followed him and Rama came last" (lines 4438–4442). Rāma walks last with his bow "not even strung, just a few arrows tied carelessly at his belt and in his mind the mantras that could make those arrows countless" (lines 4442–4444) — a contrast that establishes Lakṣmaṇa as the active warrior protector and Rāma as the latent cosmic power.

Lakṣmaṇa hunts to provide food throughout:
- He kills a wild sow on their first evening in the forest (line 4448)
- At Chitrakuta he kills a black deer, makes a household offering, and is described as bringing a black deer home from the hunt (lines 4543, 4473)

He has also acquired forest skills through prior contact with Guha: Guha tells Sita, "I taught them forest lore and hunting. Lakshmana learned well" (lines 4103–4104), noting with a smile that Rāma he could never teach to hunt.

From the forest's plants Lakṣmaṇa extracts practical supplies: "to Lakshmana all these plants surrendered the supplies of a rich caravan. He got from them needles and thread and cloth, food and cords and soap, cups and jars and medicine" (lines 5402–5404).

At Chitrakuta, Lakṣmaṇa builds the forest house:

> "Near a cave, by a clear sweet mountain spring Lakshmana built on pillars a strong house framed with long bamboos and floored with grass, whose walls were of woven wood tightly lashed by cords to keep out wind, whose roof was leaves. It had one door and many rooms and fair windows." (lines 4535–4539)

When the house is finished and the household offering made, Rāma says in admiration: "Surely we shall live here. How did you learn to build so well and do all this for us?" (lines 4540–4541).

## Key Speeches and Exchanges

**Against despair**: When Rāma, exhausted after the first day's walk, says Lakshmana should return to Ayodhyā or Kaikeyi will harm their mothers, Lakṣmaṇa does not argue but redirects Rāma from self-pity: "Forget it. You're tired, go to sleep with Sita. Don't tell me about inevitable things that displease me." Rāma responds: "You are right, despair is a bad enemy" (lines 4458–4461). Lakṣmaṇa then receives Rāma's direct commission: "Alone or with me, Lakshmana, in our exile always guard Sita" (lines 4462–4463).

**On Dasaratha's culpability**: When Guha says the banishment "will kill your father" and it is simply time for him to die, Lakṣmaṇa protests: "our father did wrong" and "It is the fault of those promises" (lines 4280–4285). Guha gently corrects him — the wishes are "but the blind instruments of Fate," and Dasaratha is dying from natural exhaustion, not moral failure. This exchange shows Lakṣmaṇa's moral clarity about justice alongside his emotional investment in Rāma's vindication.

**On Bharata**: When the news comes that Dasaratha has died, Lakṣmaṇa's first response after the offerings to Dasaratha is silence. But later, when the question of Bharata's conduct is raised, Satrughna says "Lakshmana did right to go beside him," and Bharata agrees: "True enough" (lines 4990–4991). The parallel between Lakṣmaṇa's following of Rāma and Satrughna's following of Bharata is noted by Sumitra herself: "My son Lakshmana is with Rama; my son Satrughna is with you, we are friends" (lines 5002–5004).

## Theological Significance

In Buck's retelling the blessing of Vasishtha frames Lakṣmaṇa's role explicitly: following Rāma is itself "a great good fortune" and "your high wide way to heaven" — not a sacrifice but an ascent. The night watch at Tamasa, the house-building at Chitrakuta, the hunting and provisioning all enact a form of karma yoga avant la lettre: selfless action in service without personal desire. Lakṣmaṇa explicitly refuses comfort that Rāma lacks (lines 4133–4134); he walks first on the dangerous path (lines 4438–4442); he stays awake when Rāma sleeps.

The narrator's statement that he "had no fear and no violence in his heart; he had no envy" (lines 4469–4470) situates Lakṣmaṇa as a purified instrument — the dharmic warrior whose qualities are defined by absence of the passions that corrupt warriors, not by their presence.

## During the Search for Sītā and the Assault on Laṅkā (Lines 8501–11500)

While [[hanuman|Hanumān]] searches Laṅkā, Lakṣmaṇa remains with Rāma at Kishkindhyā awaiting word: "Sugrīva was sitting outside Kishkindhya cave, with Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa on their hill, awaiting some word of Hanumān" (line 10384–10385). He is present but does not speak during Hanumān's report — his role here is loyal attendance rather than initiative.

In the prophetic dream reported by the Rakshasi Trijata to Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa appears as a sign of imminent rescue: "I saw Lakṣmaṇa sitting on a pile of white bones, drinking milk and eating rice" (lines 9669–9670) — an image of the auspicious warrior at rest before triumph, which Trijata reads as presaging Laṅkā's fall.

Rāvaṇa's subsequent lie to Sītā — claiming Prahasta killed Rāma and his army — specifically claims "Lakṣmaṇa alone ran away and fled to safety" (line 11060–11061). The fiction of Lakṣmaṇa's survival while Rāma perishes isolates Sītā's grief; using Lakṣmaṇa's survival as the one plausible detail in an otherwise false story indicates his reputation for hardy survival.

In the march to the sea, Lakṣmaṇa is carried on Angada's shoulders (line 10616 — "Angada carried Lakshmana on his shoulders and Hanumān carried Rāma"), a mark of honor.

Before the battle, Lakṣmaṇa speaks first in the war council: "Who approaches close-guarded Laṅkā of the Waves and returns from the demons? We cannot cross the sea . . ." (lines 10530–10534) — reflecting practical uncertainty rather than cowardice — then falls silent as the bear king Jambavan urges pure attack. Outside Laṅkā's walls he embodies the army's war readiness: "Lakṣmaṇa walked back and forth in front of the animals, his skin like pure gold, his lips swollen with wrath, not easy to pacify" (lines 11339–11341). He and Rāvaṇa exchange the simultaneous signal that opens the first engagement: "Lakṣmaṇa pointed at Laṅkā with his bow and Rāvaṇa raised a mace over his head" (lines 11358–11359).

## Against Kumbhakarna and Indrajit's First Assault (Lines 11501–13565)

When Kumbhakarna steps over Laṅkā's wall, virtually all the animal army flees his war cry. The text records: "between Rāma and Kumbhakarna there was only Lakṣmaṇa" (ll. 11781–11782). He stands his ground — though his arrows "could not penetrate the stiff coily hair that covered Kumbhakarna's body wherever his armor did not" (ll. 11794–11796). Kumbhakarna looks down at him and says, "You also are dead, when Rāma dies!" — then steps over him and advances on Rāma (ll. 11797–11799). The passage captures Lakṣmaṇa's characteristic function: he is always in the gap between his brother and death, regardless of tactical effectiveness.

Indrajit's first invisible assault kills Rāma and then Lakṣmaṇa: "more arrows came through his heart and killed him before he could see again" (ll. 12011–12013). He is revived by Hanumān's healing herbs alongside the rest of the army.

## The Killing of Indrajit

Lakṣmaṇa's defining act in the war of Laṅkā is the interruption and defeat of [[ravana|Rāvaṇa]]'s son Indrajit — "the Invisible Warrior." Vibhīṣaṇa reveals Indrajit's secret grove and explains: "Without it he has no divine protection in war, and cannot stay invisible if I reveal him" (ll. 12339–12340). The grove is made visible by Vibhīṣaṇa's mantra to Night. They see Indrajit kneeling at a fire altar in a crimson robe, making the final butter offering that would summon his invincible tiger-chariot.

With perfect timing Lakṣmaṇa shoots one arrow: "Quick as a lightning flash Lakshmana shot one arrow. He broke the iron ladle out of Indrajit's hand" (ll. 12382–12384). The sacrificial offering breaks; Agni the Fire Lord rises from the flame, sees the disruption, and fades away with a smile; Indrajit's tigers and war-car burn away (ll. 12388–12395).

In the single combat that follows, Lakṣmaṇa and Indrajit are evenly matched in speed: "So fleet and swift were both warriors' hands that no one could see them notch an arrow or draw their bows" (ll. 12422–12424). Lakṣmaṇa cuts all of Indrajit's seven armor strings so "the silver mail fell down like a cluster of stars spilled out from heaven at night" (ll. 12419–12421); he hits Indrajit three times in the face and pierces his body with five arrows that "went right through the marrow of his bones" (ll. 12437–12440); he breaks Indrajit's sword in mid-swing (ll. 12443–12444). Then Lakṣmaṇa "carefully withdrew Agastya's arrow from its pocket in his quiver, a long grass arrow with a silver blade. He put it on his bowstring and pulled it back to his ear and said — Kill him! He released the bowstring and beheaded Indrajit" (ll. 12445–12449).

Rāma tends to Lakṣmaṇa's wounds: "using herbs and moss Rāma stopped his bleeding, and did not let his brother's life flow out" (ll. 12463–12465). Rāma says: "That was a deed hard to do. We could never win this war while Indrajit lived; all Earth is grateful to you" (ll. 12461–12462).

## The Final Night

On the last night of the war, Lakṣmaṇa stands with the animals at the bridgehead watching Suka the Rākṣasa messenger, who holds Rāvaṇa's stone letter and waits for dawn (ll. 13413–13414). He plays no active role in Rāma's single combat with Rāvaṇa, having been sent to the rear ("take cover, and you with them" — l. 13118) while Rāma fights alone on Indra's chariot. After the battle, it is revealed that Lakṣmaṇa had kept Rāma's clothes from Ayodhyā through all their wanderings: Vibhīṣaṇa brings "clothes Lakṣmaṇa sends to you now . . . They're from Ayodhyā. I don't know how he did it" (ll. 13585–13590).
