# Lucretia

The legendary Roman matron whose rape by Sextus Tarquinius and subsequent suicide traditionally
precipitated the overthrow of the monarchy and founding of the Republic (509 BCE). She is the central
moral test case in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Book I**, where [[augustine|Augustine]] uses her to
argue against suicide and to vindicate the violated Christian women of the 410 sack of Rome. (She recurs
in Book III as the trigger for the expulsion of the Tarquins.)

## Augustine's Argument

Augustine accepts that Lucretia did not consent — "There were two, but the adultery was committed only by
one" — and so insists that her **chastity, a virtue of the soul, was never lost**, since "while the will
remains firm and unshaken, nothing that another person does with the body… is any fault of the person who
suffers it." This is the principle by which he comforts and defends the raped Christian women: purity is
not destroyed by violence the will does not consent to.

But this very innocence makes her suicide a problem. Augustine presses the **dilemma**: "If she was
adulterous, why praise her? if chaste, why slay her?" If innocent, she killed an innocent person —
homicide forbidden by "Thou shalt not kill," which (omitting "thy neighbor") prohibits self-killing too.
He reads her motive not as love of purity but as Roman shame and "proud dread" of others' opinion — the
distorted glory-seeking of the [[two-cities|earthly city]]. The Christian women, who chose to live,
"gained humility" where she sought reputation.

## Significance

Lucretia anchors Augustine's twin claims that (1) chastity is inward and inviolable, and (2) suicide is
never an act of true virtue but of weakness — claims that became foundational for Western Christian ethics
of martyrdom, rape, and self-killing. She stands as a foil contrasting Roman *gloria*
([[gloria-love-of-praise]]) with Christian humility.
