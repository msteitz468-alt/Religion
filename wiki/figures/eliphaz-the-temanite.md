# Eliphaz the Temanite

The first and most prominent of the three friends who come to comfort [[job|Job]] in the
[[book-of-job|book of Job]]. He speaks three times, opens each cycle of the dialogue, and is the
friend Yahweh addresses directly in the epilogue's rebuke (42:7).

## Biographical Overview

Eliphaz is introduced at Job 2:11 as one of three comforters who come to Job on hearing of his
misfortune. He is identified by his home rather than by genealogy.

[[john-hartley|Hartley]] glosses the name and the place together: *"Eliphaz, meaning 'God is fine
gold,' came from Teman, 'the south land,' a principal site in the northern region of Edom (Ezek.
25:13). His identification with Edom fits his role in the speeches as a wise man, for Edom was well
known for its excellence in wisdom (cf. Jer. 49:7; Obad. 8)"* ([p. 85–86], on 2:11). The Edomite
placement is therefore, on Hartley's reading, characterization rather than incidental geography —
it certifies Eliphaz as a sage before he opens his mouth. See [[uz|Uz]] for the wider geographical
frame Hartley reconstructs for the book.

Hartley resists the common schema that ranks the friends by age. He judges the friends to be
contemporaries: *"more likely all the comforters are similar in age, mature and responsible elders
with whom a few years makes little difference. The order of speaking is based on their status and
their eloquence rather than their age"* ([p. 165]). On that reading Eliphaz's primacy is a matter of
standing, not seniority: *"Being the first to speak, Eliphaz is definitely the most prominent and
eloquent statesman of the three comforters."*

## Primary Source Appearances

Eliphaz speaks three times, and his speeches shorten across the book — Hartley records 98, 68, and
58 lines respectively.

- **First speech (4:1–5:27).** A speech of consolation containing the night vision (4:12–21), a
  statement of retribution (4:7–21), and a hymnic praise of God's deliverance (5:9–26), closing in
  exhortation. Hartley describes the rhetorical range: *"proverbs, parables, analogies, precepts,
  exhortations, wisdom sayings, hymns, and a vision report."*
- **Second speech (15:1–35).** Two divisions on Hartley's analysis: a disputation rejecting Job's
  claim to wisdom (vv. 2–16), then an instruction on the woes of the wicked (vv. 17–35). The first
  is built of alternating rhetorical questions and flat assertions — *"Eliphaz adopts this style to
  ridicule Job's self-defense"* ([p. 242]). The whole functions as an implied threat: *"By
  implication he is warning Job of the consequences that will befall him if he does not repent from
  his stubborn way."*
- **Third speech (22:1–30).** Accusations (vv. 1–11), a disputation on God's activity (vv. 12–20),
  and a call to repentance (vv. 21–30).
- **Epilogue (42:7–9).** Yahweh addresses Eliphaz as the friends' leader; Eliphaz and his companions
  offer sacrifice and submit to Job's intercession.

### The night vision (4:12–21)

Hartley treats this as one of two items unique in the Hebrew Bible that the book of Job contains:
*"the most detailed account from ancient Israel of a nonprophetic encounter with a numinous being."*
On its function he is explicit: *"Within the framework of a vision report Eliphaz states the central
tenet of his theology: no human or heavenly creature is just in relationship to God. He recounts his
vision to lend irrefutable authority to his position"* ([p. 111]).

Hartley compares it with prophetic call-reports and finds it both like and unlike them. Like:
*"As with a prophet, the initiative for this vision originated outside of Eliphaz himself. There is
no evidence that he followed any incubation rites or induced the vision in any way. Hearing dominates
over seeing."* Unlike: *"no prophet ever mentions hearing a word from 'a spirit.' … In contrast,
Eliphaz reports hearing a general truth, the kind of precept valued by the teachers of wisdom."* He
adds a caution about the limits of interpretation: *"Since this is the only account of such an
auditory vision in the OT, our understanding of it is limited."*

On the *tardēmā* of 4:13 Hartley reads a God-induced revelatory stupor of the kind that fell on
Abraham (Gen. 15:12–21), and judges that *"the piling up of rare and technical words is designed to
heighten the numinous quality of Eliphaz's spiritual encounter."*

Hartley's most consequential observation is doctrinal. *"Whereas the usual basis in the OT for this
thought of human unworthiness is humanity's sinful disposition, Eliphaz grounds the doctrine of
human insignificance on humanity's inferiority before God. His approach allows him to reject Job's
defense of his innocence from the start without directly disputing whether Job is a sinner"*
([p. 113]). Hartley also notes that Eliphaz later revises his own inspired message: *"In contrast to
his presentation of the inspired message in 4:17, which speaks of a person never being purer than
God, Eliphaz now posits that no person may achieve moral virtue because of the limitations of human
nature"* ([p. 247], on 15:14–16).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

The ingested source is a modern evangelical critical commentary in the NICOT series, and it carries
no survey of Jewish, Islamic, or patristic reception of Eliphaz. This wiki's ingested sources
therefore carry no material on rabbinic, Qur'anic, or Christian-patristic treatments of Eliphaz as a
figure in his own right, beyond one passing datum: Hartley reports that *"some of the early church
fathers"* judged [[elihu|Elihu]] overconfident, but names no comparable patristic tradition on
Eliphaz.

What the source does supply is a *canonical* reception internal to the book. In the epilogue Yahweh
condemns the friends' speech and singles Eliphaz out as their head. Hartley: *"Yahweh holds all of
them responsible for the implications of their teachings and exhortations to Job"* ([p. 538]). The
charge, on Hartley's reading, is graver than the rebuke Job receives: *"Whereas Yahweh has accused
Job of darkening knowledge (38:2), his charge against the friends is much stronger. Job has been
genuinely groping for the truth, but the friends have spoken falsely in their attempt to defend
God"* ([p. 539]). Eliphaz's final act in the book is obedience: Hartley credits that *"the comforters
showed their strong commitment to God by obeying him in humbly letting Job, against whom they had
spoken so harshly, offer up sacrifices for them"* ([p. 540]).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The character's own doctrine

In the text Eliphaz argues that everyone is guilty of error, that the righteous prosper and the
wicked suffer and die early, and that God delivers his own from sorrow. Suffering is punishment:
Hartley summarizes the position as *"punishment is not a part of the natural processes, but is an
inevitable outworking of a person's sinful nature… Suffering and misfortune, then, are direct
punishments for wrongdoing"* (on 5:6–7). Eliphaz refines this with a doctrine of discipline as an
intermediate stage — *"One who needs correcting is reproved in order that he might repent and find
forgiveness lest he persist in his error and experience the full punishment his error merits… Pain
is the instrument of discipline long before it becomes the fulfillment of a curse"* ([p. 125], on
5:17). See [[doctrine-of-retribution|the doctrine of retribution]].

Two further positions belong to the character, not to the commentator. At 5:1 Eliphaz raises and
then dismisses the idea of an angelic intercessor: *"Eliphaz resoundingly rejects this idea…, for he
believes that the angels are so tainted (4:18) that they do not have the standing to represent a
person before God"* ([p. 117]) — a rejection that stands in direct opposition to the mediator Elihu
will later announce at 33:23–28. And at 15:11 Eliphaz identifies the friends' own counsel with
God's: *"in Eliphaz's opinion, to reject their consoling advice is tantamount to rejecting God's
consolations."*

### Hartley's verdict

Hartley does not endorse any of this. His verdict on the first speech is the sharpest register
marker on the page:

> *"Unfortunately, and obviously without realizing it, Eliphaz sides with the Satan against God in
> offering this counsel, for he seeks to motivate Job to serve God for the benefits that piety
> brings. **His error is not in his doctrine, but in his inability to counsel Job rightly.** Failing
> to discern that Job is sorely troubled by bearing suffering for no reason at all (cf. 2:3),
> Eliphaz by his counsel tempts Job to seek God for personal gain, not for God himself."*
> ([p. 129])

He adds: *"should Job follow his advice he would fall into the trap the Satan has set."* On
Hartley's reading Eliphaz — with no such intention — argues [[satan|the Satan]]'s side of the
prologue wager. The same verdict is passed on [[zophar-the-naamathite|Zophar]].

Hartley tracks a hardening across the three speeches. Of the first he says Eliphaz's motive is
pastoral fear: *"he feels compelled to speak lest Job's bitter words alienate God."* He denies that
4:6 is sarcasm — *"a mild reproof"* — though he also reads condescension into the courtesy: *"On the
surface it appears that Eliphaz is respectfully sensitive to Job, but Job's response will indicate
that he detects a condescending tone in Eliphaz's manner."* By the third speech the courtesy is
gone: *"A complete turnabout has taken place in Eliphaz's attitude toward Job… Since Job appears to
be as recalcitrant as a hardened sinner, Eliphaz has reached the end of his patience"* ([p. 325–326]).

The specific charges of ch. 22 are, in Hartley's judgment, deduced rather than evidenced: *"Without
any empirical evidence that Job has sinned, Eliphaz arrives at this position by deduction, reasoning
that since Job is being severely reproved, he must be guilty of breaches of hospitality and kindness
to the unfortunate."* The catalogue: *"economic abuse of the poor, refusal of help to the afflicted,
and lack of compassion for the bereaved"* (22:6–9). Hartley notes an innuendo at 22:24 that Job's
wealth itself was extorted, and that the word *ḥinnām* at 22:6 — pledges taken *"without any
basis"* — is the prologue's own key term (cf. 1:9).

Hartley also holds that Eliphaz misreads Job's complaint in ch. 22. Eliphaz charges Job with
something *"close to what is classified today as modern deism or practical atheism,"* and Hartley
corrects him: *"But Eliphaz has not correctly heard Job's complaints… it is not Job's concern that
God is not active in affairs on earth, but that it seems from appearances that God judges the wicked
erratically and capriciously. Sadly, Eliphaz is charging Job with heretical thoughts by
overinterpreting his sincere complaints against God"* ([p. 330]).

### The "Aim" on the third speech

Hartley's closing assessment is homiletically loaded and is his own, not the text's:

> *"At this point Eliphaz makes his greatest mistake. Pressed to defend God's honor, he condemns
> Job… **But a genuine defense of God must not downgrade another human being. Eliphaz fails to
> realize that in condemning Job he is also casting reproach on God, Job's creator.** In defending
> God Eliphaz stumbles over the stone of futility, the 'no reason or purpose' (ḥinnām) of the
> prologue, which has been put in Job's path. Furthermore, he fails to apply his own conclusion —
> that the righteous can intercede for the guilty — to his relationship with Job. Rather than
> condemning Job, he needs to plead for Job's restoration."* ([p. 335])

The irony Hartley presses is exact: Eliphaz himself teaches at 22:29–30 that the righteous *"does
have power to stand in the gap for another,"* and it is Job who ends up interceding for Eliphaz.
Hartley draws a pastoral rule from it — *"a call to repentance loses its power when it is offered
from inaccurate perceptions and wrong motivations"* — quoting Delitzsch in support: *"the most
brilliant sermon that exhorts to penitence remains without effect when it is prompted by pharisaic
uncharitableness."* His closing verdict on the figure:

> *"A study of the movement of Eliphaz's rhetoric in his three speeches reveals the tension that
> exists between what one believes and the course of earthly affairs. Unfortunately Eliphaz is
> unable to hold this tension in balance. His care for Job hardens into condemnation because he
> feels he has to protect his cherished beliefs from Job's charges."* ([p. 336])

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Hartley treats Eliphaz as a character in a literary work set in the patriarchal period, and offers
no argument for or against his historicity. He notes that the author *"set his work in the
patriarchal period,"* evidenced by archaic divine names and a patriarchal ethical standard — which
places Eliphaz inside an antiquarian literary frame rather than a datable one.

Two interpretive cruxes attach to Eliphaz in the ingested material:

1. **The status of the night vision (4:12–21).** Hartley accepts it as a genuine, unsought, uninduced
   auditory revelation and declines to explain it away, while insisting that its *content* is a
   wisdom precept rather than a prophetic commission, and that its doctrinal basis diverges from the
   Hebrew Bible's usual grounding of human unworthiness. He records that its singularity limits
   confident interpretation.
2. **Whether 22:18b is a transmission doublet.** The line *"but the counsel of the wicked is far
   from me"* stands verbatim at both 21:16b (Job's mouth) and 22:18b (Eliphaz's). Hartley treats
   each in place — the second as *"Eliphaz interject[ing] his personal rejection of the counsel of
   the wicked. Such personal statements are typical of his style"* — and does not discuss the
   doublet as a textual problem. This is a gap in Hartley's treatment, not a position of his.

Hartley also reads 15:7 as alluding to a myth beyond Genesis: *"he seems to have in mind the myth of
the heavenly Adam, the primordial man who served as the model for the earthly Adam,"* who was wise
*"since he sat in the heavenly council (cf. 1:6)."* He notes this as the sole Hebrew Bible allusion
to that myth.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Within the ingested source, Eliphaz's importance for commentary is structural rather than
doxographic: his positions are the ones the rest of the book must answer. Hartley traces Job
restating Eliphaz's thesis forensically at 9:2 (*"Job… moves away from the moral dimension of
Eliphaz's thesis to the forensic… He ponders how a person can be acquitted when it is God who is his
accuser"*), reusing Eliphaz's own hymn-lines at 9:10 against him, and countering 5:19–22 at 9:22–24.
[[bildad-the-shuhite|Bildad]]'s reconstructed third speech at 25:4 *"quot[es] Eliphaz's great insight
presented in 15:14–15"* — see [[job-third-cycle-integrity|the third-cycle problem]]. And Hartley
reads [[elihu|Elihu]] as answering Eliphaz specifically: *"Elihu is also countering Eliphaz's
position that there is no holy one to whom Job may turn for help"* ([p. 446]), and as differing from
him precisely by refusing to compose *"a theoretical list of sins Job has committed"* (contrast
22:5–9).
