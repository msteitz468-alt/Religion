# L. Ron Hubbard

**Lafayette Ronald Hubbard** (1911–1986) is the founder of [[scientology|Scientology]] and the sole author
of its scripture — known within the movement simply as **"Source."**

## Biographical Overview

### Early Life and Pulp Fiction Career

Born in **Tilden, Nebraska**, son of navy officer Harry Ross Hubbard. The Church promotes an elaborate **hagiographic mythology** (Dorthe Refslund Christensen's term): war hero, nuclear physicist, explorer, philosopher. Critics and documentary evidence sharply contest this portrait.

Hubbard was primarily a **prolific pulp-fiction writer** — reportedly producing 70,000–100,000 words per month, using pseudonyms including "Rene Lafayette." His science fiction was not merely commercial: it was a laboratory for ideas he would later systematize as Dianetics and Scientology.

Key prefigurations in the fiction:
- **"Excalibur" manuscript** (1938): near-death experience under anesthetic → "SURVIVE" as the fundamental command of life; never published but circulated among followers
- **"Typewriter in the Sky"** (1940): a god-like author creates and destroys universes → foreshadows the thetan's creative power
- **"Ole Doc Methuselah" series** (1947–50): Soldiers of Light with a crossed-rods symbol → the visual precursor of the Sea Org uniform and Scientology cross

### Academic and Military Record

The church claims Hubbard was a **nuclear physicist and war hero**. The documentary record:
- **GWU**: failed nuclear physics; eventually dropped out
- PhD from **Sequoia University** (unaccredited correspondence school)
- Navy record: investigated for firing on Mexican territorial waters; assessment: "not qualified for command"

### The Parsons / Crowley Connection (1946)

One of the most contested episodes. **Jack Parsons** (1914–1952) — JPL rocket scientist and member of Aleister Crowley's **Ordo Templi Orientis** — invited Hubbard to participate in the **"Babalon Working,"** a magical ritual intended to summon an elemental and produce a "moonchild." Hubbard served as Parsons's "Scribe," allegedly channeling Babalon.

**Aleister Crowley** (after reading Parsons's enthusiastic letters) wrote: "It seems to me that you are in the hands of a criminal confidence trickster." Crowley later commented that he "suspects Ron playing confidence trick." Hubbard allegedly absconded with Parsons's girlfriend Betty Northrup and **$20,970.80** of Parsons's life savings. The Church has claimed Hubbard was on a "military mission to break up black magic."

Hubbard himself later called Crowley "my very good friend" in a 1952 lecture (despite never having met him), and his son L. Ron Jr. claimed Crowley's *Book of the Law* was foundational: "The one super-secret sentence that Scientology is built on is: 'Do as thou wilt.'"

Urban notes: the occult connections are real but their precise weight in shaping Scientology remains debated.

### Dianetics (1950)

In **May 1950**, Hubbard published "Dianetics: The Evolution of a Science" in *Astounding Science Fiction*, followed by *Dianetics: The Modern Science of Mental Health* (book form). The book spent **28 weeks on the NYT bestseller list**.

The system: **analytical mind** vs. **reactive mind**; [[engram|engrams]] as recordings of pain and unconsciousness; [[auditing|auditing]] to locate and erase them; **Clear** as the target state. Dianetics positioned itself explicitly as a *science*, not a religion — a distinction Hubbard later reversed strategically.

**Aldous Huxley** was personally audited by Hubbard. **William S. Burroughs** became Clear #1163. Prominent intellectuals engaged seriously with it before professional ridicule set in.

The **E-meter** was adapted from Volney Mathison's device; after a dispute, Hubbard-Mathison split in 1954.

### The "Religion Angle" and Church Founding (1952–54)

When Dianetics faced **financial collapse** (Don Purcell bankruptcy, 1952), **FDA pressure** on medical claims, and the controversy over past-lives auditing, Hubbard reorganized the movement as a *religion*. The pivotal document:

> **April 10, 1953 letter to Helen O'Brien**: "Perhaps we could better our income by . . . the creation of a religious fellowship . . . . It is not necessary to prove any specific doctrine. It is not necessary to prove whether we are right or wrong. It is necessary to make money."

Three "churches" were incorporated in Camden, NJ, in **December 1953**: Church of American Science, Church of Scientology, Church of Spiritual Engineering. The first California church followed February 18, 1954.

### Scientology's Doctrine (1952–66)

- **Thetan** as the immortal true self, once godlike, now trapped in the MEST universe (Matter, Energy, Space, Time)
- **Space opera cosmology**: Galactic Confederacy; Invader Forces; Marcab Confederacy; "Earth is a sort of prison"
- **Exteriorization**: "Be three feet back of your head"; Grand Tour of the Universe — parallels to astral projection (Sylvan Muldoon; Crowley's *Magick*)
- **Eastern religions** claimed as predecessors: Veda as "earliest ancestor"; dhyana = Scientology. Hubbard's "Am I Metteyya?" poem (1955–56 Buddhist convention); claimed Hinayana Buddhist lineage. Stephen Kent notes these claims were deployed precisely when Hubbard faced FDA/medical scrutiny.
- **OT levels** (from July 1966): increasingly confidential; Hubbard claimed the upper materials were "fatal to the unprepared"

### The Sea Organization and Cold War Period (1966–77)

Hubbard formed the **Sea Org** (1968), a paramilitary religious order operating at sea; he became "the Commodore." This period saw:
- **Fair Game policy** (1965): enemies "may be deprived of property or injured by any means … tricked, sued, lied to, or destroyed." Officially canceled 1968 (for PR reasons); practice continued.
- **Security Checks**: interrogations including "Have you ever enslaved a population?" and "Did you come to Earth for evil purposes?"
- **Guardian's Office** (1966): "private CIA"; Mary Sue Hubbard director; covert operations against critics
- **Operation Freakout** (vs. journalist Paulette Cooper, *The Scandal of Scientology*, 1971): framed her for terrorism, threatened Henry Kissinger
- **Operation Snow White** (1973): systematic infiltration of IRS and other government agencies — ultimately resulting in the **July 7, 1977 FBI raids** (134 agents; Mary Sue Hubbard and Jane Kember convicted; Hubbard himself named unindicted co-conspirator)

### Final Years

Hubbard spent the years after the FBI raids in **hiding in the California desert**. He died on **January 24, 1986**, in a Blue Bird motor home near Creston, CA — unkempt, with long hair and fingernails; hydroxyzine (an anti-anxiety sedative) was found in his system. The Church announced he had chosen to leave his body to "research the next level"; a luxurious mansion at Gold Base was maintained for his return.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within Scientology, Hubbard functions as **sole prophet, sole revelation, and sole authority**. His exact words are "Source" — to be applied verbatim, with no deviation ("standard tech"). Unlike most founders, Hubbard's authority is not posthumously canonized but was asserted by himself in the strongest possible terms during his lifetime: he explicitly demanded that no one alter his works, ever.

This gives Scientology one of the most **anti-interpretive hermeneutics** in the wiki: the opposite of the commentary traditions in Judaism (PaRDeS), Christianity (Quadriga), or Islam (tafsir). There is no room for commentary; there is only correct or incorrect *application*.

## Historicity and Controversies

The **gap between the official hagiography and the documentary record** is itself one of the defining features of Scientology scholarship. Key disputed points:
- Military and academic credentials (fabricated per documentary evidence)
- Nature of the religion angle (cynical strategy per Armstrong and documentary evidence; genuine development per apologists)
- Parsons/Crowley connection (significance disputed)
- Role of the OT materials (Hubbard's own composition vs. psychotic episode vs. deliberate mythology)
- Death circumstances and succession (natural death vs. suspicious; Miscavige's "coup" vs. legitimate succession)

**Contradiction Protocol**: All positions recorded without adjudication.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Because Hubbard explicitly **forbade commentary and interpretation** of his works, there is no Scientology commentary tradition in the wiki's sense. His significance in this wiki is primarily as a **figure page** — the man whose life, claims, and texts generated the movement and its controversies.

## Wright's Additional Evidence (Going Clear, 2013)

Lawrence Wright (*Going Clear*) adds significant primary documentation:

- **"Admissions" / "Affirmations"**: A private document (church disputes authenticity) in which Hubbard instructs himself to overcome personal weaknesses including sexual dysfunction and insecurities. Authenticated by content overlaps with correspondence. The church filed it in the Armstrong case; Armstrong testified to its authenticity.
- **Military forgery confirmed**: Two St. Louis archivists independently found the Church's "Notice of Separation" document to be a forgery — phantom signatory "Howard D. Thompson," font inconsistencies, medals that did not exist until after Hubbard left service. See [[hubbard-military-records-forgery|Hubbard Military Records Forgery]].
- **Between-lives doctrine**: Hubbard described "Heaven" as a 43-trillion-year-old electronic implant station — one reason, he argued, that Scientology is stronger than Christianity: "So there was a Heaven after all — which is why you are on this planet and were condemned never to be free again — until Scientology."
- **Tesla-coil suicide machine**: Six weeks before his death, Hubbard asked "Sarge" Pfauth to build an apparatus to "blow" a body thetan — "up the voltage and basically kill the body." Pfauth built a Tesla coil hooked to an E-Meter. Hubbard tried it; it blew up the E-Meter. Hubbard died of a stroke weeks later.
- **Hubbard's self-assessment**: Told Pfauth six weeks before death that he had "failed" and was "leaving." "He said he's not coming back here to Earth. He didn't know where he'd wind up."
- **Quentin Hubbard's death** (1976): Found unconscious in Las Vegas, died in coma; semen in rectum; ruled accidental. Hubbard's response per witnesses: "That little shit — he's done it to me again."
- **Homophobia**: Wright documents Hubbard's explicit anti-gay writing across multiple texts: Science of Survival describes homosexuals at "2.0 on the Tone Scale" and suggests they should be dealt with as "social menaces." These passages were removed from later editions. Davis claimed they were inserted by "a bigot," which Wright characterized as implausible.

## Atack's Account (*A Piece of Blue Sky*, 1990)

Jon Atack — seven years a Scientologist (OT5), six years researching — adds granular primary-source documentation:

### Financial and Corporate Structure
- **OTC (Operation & Transport Company)**: incorporated Panama, January 1, 1968; 98 of 100 shares owned by Hubbard — the corporate vehicle for personal control of Sea Org assets.
- **Religious Research Foundation (RRF)**: incorporated Liberia, August 1973; mechanism for siphoning Church funds to Hubbard tax-free.
- **"Governing Policy" for finance (March 1972)**: every point says "MAKE MONEY."

### The Guardian's Office and Snow White
- **Guardian Order 732, April 1973**: Atack argues (based on the Armstrong archive and court testimony) that Hubbard personally authored Snow White — contradicting the Church's defense that it was an independent GO initiative.
- **Motorcycle accident, Tenerife, November 1973**: broken arm and ribs; Hubbard refused a doctor; he ordered the **RPF** introduced while recovering.

### Personal Documents
- **Letters to wives**: confirm drug use during OT3 research period (North Africa, 1966) — at odds with the Church's presentation of OT3 as the product of "incredible research."
- **1938 letter to first wife Polly**: expresses ambition to equal Napoleon, Genghis Khan, and Alexander the Great in historical legacy.

### Later Years
- **Flight to Creston**: February/March 1980, with Pat and Annie Broeker; the "All Clear Unit" (Miscavige) was tasked with clearing legal obstacles to his return — which never came.
- **Death**: January 24, 1986, "Whispering Winds" ranch, Creston, California; anti-stroke medication (hydroxyzine) in blood; coroner sought autopsy but a religious-objection document signed only four days before death was presented; no inquest held.

### Psychological Profile
- **Frank Gerbode** (psychiatrist): assessed Hubbard as "manic with paranoid tendencies."
- Atack applies **Cleckley's** psychopath checklist extensively and finds Hubbard matches it closely.
- Favorite book since 1952: Bolitho's *Twelve Against the Gods* (adventurers who overreached) — Atack argues Hubbard consciously modeled himself on these figures.

### OT Materials and Cosmology
- **Revolt in the Stars** screenplay: reveals OT3 content in science-fiction form — Xenu, Chi (Galactic Minister of Police), Chu (Executive President of the Galactic Interplanetary Bank), Rawl (the hero, Hubbard's self-insert character).
- **Battlefield Earth**: written in six weeks (van Vogt letter confirms); Hubbard's literary productivity driven by the same manic pace as his Scientology output.
- Within the fully-indoctrinated inner circle: Hubbard was identified as Rawl (the imprisoner of Xenu), the Buddha, and Cecil Rhodes — a "great spirit responsible through the millennia for most real achievements of history."

### Church of Spiritual Technology (CST)
- CST assets: **over $500 million** (Washington DC tax case records).
- Dug a **350-foot tunnel** into a New Mexico mesa for nuclear-war-proof storage of Hubbard's writings; additional facilities near Los Angeles and northern California.

## Headley's Account (*Blown for Good*, 2009)

Marc Headley's memoir adds two specific details about Hubbard's presence and legacy at Gold Base that are not foregrounded in other ingested sources:

### The "Advices"

Hubbard issued management directives to Gold Base management during his years in hiding (post-1977) in the form of **"Advices"** — documents that were deliberately left **unsigned** so that Hubbard could maintain legal deniability about his continued management role. The Church's legal strategy in various proceedings required that Hubbard not be shown to be actively directing Church operations; the Advices were the mechanism by which he did so while preserving that deniability. ([[atack-a-piece-of-blue-sky|Atack]] confirms the hiding-period management context; Headley adds the unsigned-Advice detail from Gold Base experience.)

### Boots in the Stove — Apollo Inspection Story

Headley recounts a story circulating among Gold Base staff about a Hubbard inspection aboard the *Apollo* (the Sea Org flagship during the ship period): Hubbard discovered a crew member's **boots in the stove** — apparently stored there during warm weather. This was cited internally as a legendary example of Hubbard's exacting standards for ship order and cleanliness, and as context for why management at Gold Base maintained such detailed physical-standards requirements.

### Bonnie View — Seasonal Maintenance

Headley confirms from direct observation that **Bonnie View** (Hubbard's mansion at Gold Base) was maintained with seasonal furnishing rotation — furniture and decorations rotated on a seasonal cycle, ready for Hubbard's return long after his 1986 death. This is consistent with Wright's account of the maintained parking spot and vehicles.
