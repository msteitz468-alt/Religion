Gideon (also called Jerubbaal) is the longest narrative arc in Judges and its theological turning point: a reluctant, doubt-filled deliverer who begins like Moses and ends making an ephod that becomes a snare, whose son [[abimelech|Abimelech]] embodies the worst of kingship.

## Biographical Overview

Son of Joash of the clan of Abiezer in Manasseh, Gideon is introduced as a man threshing wheat in a winepress to hide from Midianite raiders (seven years of oppression, 6:1–6). The angel of the LORD appears and greets him "O mighty man of valor," commissioning him to save Israel. Gideon protests his weakness and low clan status; he requests signs (the fleece wet/dry, the dew on the fleece only). He destroys his father's Baal altar by night, earning the name Jerubbaal ("let Baal contend"). The Spirit of the LORD clothes him; he summons tribes but is reduced by divine command from 32,000 to 300 men (those who lap water like dogs vs. those who kneel). The famous night attack with torches, jars, and trumpets routs the Midianites. In pursuit he requests bread from Succoth and Penuel and later threshes the elders with thorns and tears down the tower. He captures and executes the Midianite kings Zebah and Zalmunna after his young son refuses. The people offer him kingship ("Rule over us, you and your son and your grandson also"); he refuses in words ("I will not rule over you, and my son will not rule over you; the LORD will rule over you") but then makes an ephod from the gold earrings that becomes an idol and "a snare to Gideon and to his house" (8:27). He has many wives and seventy sons; one son by a concubine in Shechem is Abimelech. Gideon dies "in a good old age" and is buried in his father's tomb; "as soon as Gideon died, the people of Israel turned again and went after the Baals" (8:33).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 6–8**: The call, signs, destruction of Baal altar, reduction of the army, night victory, pursuit, refusal of kingship, ephod, death.
- **Judges 9**: Abimelech's story (Gideon's legacy of violence and syncretism).
- **Hebrews 11:32**: Listed (with Barak, Samson, Jephthah) among those who "through faith conquered kingdoms, enforced justice, obtained promises."

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Gideon is remembered for the fleece (a paradigm for seeking confirmation) and for the reduction to 300 as proof that "the battle is the LORD's." Rabbinic sources sometimes critique the ephod and the later apostasy after his death. His refusal of kingship is cited positively in anti-monarchical readings.

**Christianity**: Classic example of reluctant calling (like Moses), faith tested by signs, and the danger of syncretism (ephod). The 300 and the unconventional weapons are favorite preaching texts for "less is more" when God is at work. The "LORD will rule over you" is used for theocracy vs. human kingship. Abimelech's rise is a cautionary tale of what happens when a judge's family turns to power politics. Hebrews 11 lists him among the faithful despite his flaws. Patristic writers sometimes allegorize the fleece (Gentiles/Jews or dew of grace). Modern evangelical readings stress Gideon's weakness as the point (God uses the unlikely) and the ephod as the tragic turn from dependence to object.

**Critical**: The Gideon cycle is seen as a composite of hero stories with Deuteronomistic framing. The ephod and the Abimelech sequel are often treated as later additions showing the dark side of charismatic leadership. Some read the "refusal of kingship" as ironic given the ephod and the later monarchy.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Gideon is the hinge of the book. He begins as the ideal: diffident, radically dependent, empowered by the Spirit, achieving victory with absurdly small numbers and non-military means (torches and trumpets). He ends compromised: the ephod becomes an idol, his household is ensnared, and his son Abimelech (whose name means "my father is king") kills seventy brothers on one stone, buys kingship with temple money, and dies grotesquely when a woman drops a millstone on his head — a deliberate parody of everything Gideon might have been. The narrative thus illustrates both the power of YHWH working through a weak vessel and the speed with which even a deliverer can slide into the Canaanization the book diagnoses. The refusal of kingship is the high point theologically ("the LORD will rule over you"); the ephod and Abimelech are the narrative's judgment on what happens when that principle is abandoned. In the downward spiral, Gideon marks the point after which "the land had rest" is never again said.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Fleece and signs**: Historical or paradigmatic? Traditional readings take the events at face value; critical see legendary development.
- **Ephod**: Was it an image, a priestly garment, or something else? The text says the people "played the harlot after it" and it became a snare.
- **Abimelech sequel**: Is it integral or a later anti-monarchical or anti-Shechemite addition? The text presents it as the direct consequence of Gideon's legacy.
- **Kingship refusal**: Sincere theocratic ideal or ironic given Gideon's later actions and the book's later pro-Davidic hints?

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Webb (NICOT) treats the Gideon-Abimelech arc as the theological turning point of the whole book and a masterpiece of narrative art (irony, parody, the millstone death as poetic justice). The story is central to debates about charismatic leadership, the dangers of syncretism, and the relationship between Judges and the rise of the monarchy. It is frequently preached on the themes of weakness, signs vs. trust, and "the LORD will rule." In canonical context it stands between the model Othniel and the increasingly flawed later judges, and it anticipates the monarchy's own temptations (Abimelech as a dark prefiguration of later kings). The fleece remains a devotional touchstone for "putting out a fleece" while also a warning against over-reliance on signs.
