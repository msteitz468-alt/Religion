# Isaac

## Biographical Overview

**Isaac** (Hebrew *Yitzhak*, "he laughs") is the **second of the three patriarchs**, the
**son of the promise** born to [[abraham|Abraham]] and Sarah in their old age (Gen. 21). His
narrative is the briefest of the three — he is largely a figure of **continuity**, receiving
and transmitting the covenant between the towering figures of Abraham and Jacob. He is bound
on the altar (the **Aqedah**, Gen. 22), marries **Rebekah**, fathers the twins **[[edomites|
Esau]]** and **[[abraham|Jacob]]**, sojourns in **[[gerar|Gerar]]**, digs wells in the Negev,
and in blindness bestows the decisive blessing.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Genesis 21**: birth and the naming ("laughter"); the expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael.
- **Genesis 22 — the Aqedah ("binding")**: Abraham is commanded to sacrifice Isaac on a
  mountain in **Moriah**; the ram is substituted. The single most commented-upon Isaac text.
- **Genesis 24**: the servant fetches **Rebekah** from Aram as Isaac's wife.
- **Genesis 26**: Isaac in **[[gerar|Gerar]]** — the wife-sister episode with Abimelech, the
  hundredfold harvest, the well-disputes, and the Beersheba treaty (see [[beersheba]]).
- **Genesis 27**: the aged, blind Isaac is deceived into **blessing Jacob over Esau**.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: the Aqedah (**Akedat Yitzhak**) is central — read as the supreme test of
  Abraham and, in some midrash, as Isaac's own willing self-offering; it is invoked liturgically
  (Rosh Hashanah) as a merit for Israel. Isaac is the patriarch of *gevurah* (disciplined
  strength) in later mystical schemes.
- **Christian**: Isaac is a **type of Christ** — the beloved son carrying the wood of his own
  sacrifice up the mountain (Gen. 22 → the cross); Paul makes Isaac the child "of promise" and
  "of the free woman," over against Ishmael, in his covenant allegory (Rom. 9.7–9; Gal.
  4.21–31). Hebrews 11 praises Abraham's faith that God could raise Isaac.
- **Islamic**: **Ishaq** is a prophet and the son of Ibrahim by Sarah; the near-sacrifice is
  most often (though not unanimously) identified in Islamic tradition with **Ishmael** rather
  than Isaac — a notable inter-tradition divergence.
- **Kabbalistic (the Zohar)**: At the **Sod** level, Isaac is the human embodiment of the
  fifth [[sefirot|sefirah]], **Gevurah / Din** (Power / Judgment) — the left arm of the divine
  body, the "north," the limiting and measuring force that balances [[abraham|Abraham]]'s
  unbounded Ḥesed. The Zohar grounds this in the biblical epithet **"the Fear of Isaac"**
  (*Paḥad Yitzhak*, Gen. 31:42), read not as Isaac's fear but as the divine attribute of
  awe-inspiring power *named after him*. The **Aqedah** becomes the supreme moment of this
  identification: bound on the altar, Isaac faces the "face" of Gevurah — the God of
  overwhelming, consuming judgment — and is the only patriarch to have, in a sense, "tasted
  death" and been sanctified by the severe side. Critically, the Zohar warns that Gevurah
  *untempered* by Ḥesed tips into the demonic: the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] is born from the
  blazing of the unbalanced left. Isaac, harmonized within the patriarchal triad (Ḥesed–
  Gevurah–Tif'eret = Abraham–Isaac–Jacob), represents Gevurah held in holy balance. His
  partial blindness in old age (Gen. 27) is read as the dimming proper to the side of stern
  judgment. See [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]], [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]], [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]].
  - *The Aqedah in Zohar Vol II (Va-Yera)*: the binding stages the cosmic emergence of **"water
  versus fire"** — [[abraham|Abraham]]'s Ḥesed (water) against Isaac's Gevurah/Din (fire) — a division
  that "would manifest… until [[jacob|Jacob]] appeared and everything harmonized; the triad of patriarchs
  completed." The Zohar stresses that Isaac was **thirty-seven and willingly let himself be bound** "like a
  lamb," honoring his father — Gevurah consenting to its own severity. His wife **[[rebekah|Rebekah]]** then
  operates the discernment (*din*) that secures the blessing for [[jacob|Jacob]] over [[esau|Esau]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Isaac embodies the **transmission of the covenant promise** across generations and, through
the **Aqedah**, the themes of testing, substitutionary sacrifice, and the God who provides.
His passivity relative to Abraham and Jacob is itself theologically meaningful: the promise
advances not by his initiative but by divine faithfulness.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Like the other patriarchs, Isaac is **not independently attested** and belongs to the
legendary patriarchal horizon; critical scholarship treats the Genesis ancestor narratives as
later compositions (J, E, P) projecting Israel's self-understanding. The **anachronistic
"Philistines" of Gerar** (Gen. 26) are a stock example (see [[gerar]], [[philistines]]). The
identity of the near-sacrificed son (Isaac vs. Ishmael) is a standing Jewish/Christian–Islamic
dispute.
