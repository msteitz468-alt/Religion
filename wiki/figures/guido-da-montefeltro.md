# Guido da Montefeltro

Ghibelline warlord turned Franciscan friar (1223–1298), met by [[dante-alighieri|Dante]]
among the **false counsellors** wrapped in flame in [[inferno|Inferno]] 27 — the
historical, Christian counterpart to the legendary [[ulysses-dante|Ulysses]] of the
preceding canto.

## In the Divine Comedy

Believing (wrongly) that no living man could carry word of him back, Guido tells how,
having repented his "fox-like" cunning and taken the cord of St Francis, he was drawn
back to "former sins" by "that sovereign priest" — **Pope Boniface VIII** — who
sought his counsel to destroy the Colonna stronghold at Penestrina and promised
absolution in advance. Guido's fatal advice: "promise much and keep little." At his
death St Francis came for him, but a devil claimed him with an unanswerable
syllogism: "Repentance fails? There can't be absolution… one cannot will a sin and
not will it at once" (27:118–120) — absolution cannot precede an unrepented sin.
Minos coiled his tail and consigned him to the thieving fire.

## Significance

The episode is Dante's sharpest dramatization of the **logic of sin and grace**:
sacramental absolution is not a transaction that can be pre-purchased, because
contrition and the persisting will to sin are contradictory. It also belongs to
Dante's sustained indictment of Boniface VIII, the pope he condemns more than any
other. Kirkpatrick frames both Guido and Ulysses as men who kept acting "youthful
and useful" when their life's season called them to contemplation.
