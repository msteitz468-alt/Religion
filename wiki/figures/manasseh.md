Manasseh son of [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] and father of Amon, grandfather of [[josiah|Josiah]], was
king of Judah for fifty-five years — the longest reign in either kingdom. **This page concerns the
king; the tribe and half-tribe of Manasseh son of Joseph, with its Cisjordan and Transjordan
allotments in [[book-of-joshua|Joshua]], is a different referent entirely.** Manasseh the king is
the pivot of a major divergence between Kings and Chronicles: for Kings he is the arch-apostate
whose sin makes the exile irrevocable; for Chronicles he is captured, repents in Babylon, and is
restored to his throne. `[CONTRADICTION]` — both accounts are recorded below in their strongest
form and neither is adjudicated here.

## Biographical Overview

Manasseh came to the throne as a boy of twelve and reigned fifty-five years. Both accounts agree
on the character of his cultic policy: he rebuilt the high places his father [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]]
had destroyed, erected altars for Baal and an Asherah, built altars for "all the host of heaven"
in the two courts of the temple of YHWH in [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]], burned his own son as an
offering, practiced soothsaying, augury and sorcery, and dealt with mediums and spiritists
(2 Kgs 21:1–9 // 2 Chr 33:1–9). Kings adds that he "shed very much innocent blood, till he had
filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (2 Kgs 21:16).

The accounts then diverge sharply on how the reign ends. Kings gives no repentance and no reversal
of judgment; Chronicles narrates a Babylonian captivity, a prayer, a restoration, and a partial
cultic reform in Manasseh's last years.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Kings 21:1–18**: the fullest indictment of any king in the book. Manasseh does "what was
  evil… according to the abominable practices of the nations whom YHWH drove out"; the prophets
  announce that YHWH will bring such evil on [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] that "both ears of everyone
  who hears of it will tingle," will wipe Jerusalem "as one wipes a dish," and will cast off the
  remnant of his heritage. He dies and is buried in the garden of Uzza; Amon succeeds him.
- **2 Kings 23:26–27 and 24:3–4**: even after [[josiah|Josiah]]'s reform, YHWH does not turn from
  his wrath "because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him"; the removal of
  Judah is "because of the sins of Manasseh… and also for the innocent blood that he had shed."
- **2 Chronicles 33:1–20**: the same catalogue of abominations, followed by material with no
  parallel in Kings — YHWH brings the commanders of the Assyrian army, who take Manasseh with
  hooks, bind him in bronze fetters and carry him to [[babylon|Babylon]]; there "he humbled
  himself greatly before the God of his fathers" and prayed; God "was moved by his entreaty" and
  brought him back to Jerusalem; he then removed the foreign gods and the idol from the temple,
  restored the altar of YHWH, and commanded Judah to serve YHWH — though the people still
  sacrificed at the high places. His prayer is said to be written in the Chronicles of the kings
  of Israel and in the records of the seers.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **[[iain-provan|Provan]] (NIBC Kings, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]])**: Manasseh is
  "the worst king"; with him "judgment becomes irrevocable" — the wiki's Kings page places him in
  Provan's endgame sequence as **the point of no return**, against which even
  [[josiah|Josiah]]'s reform arrives "too late" and Huldah's word stands.
- **[[peter-leithart|Leithart]] (BTCB Chronicles, as recorded on
  [[1-2-chronicles|1–2 Chronicles]])**: Manasseh's repentance is "unprecedented in Chronicles,
  without parallel in the Old Testament." The worst king — "a man of sixes, perhaps even a 666" —
  is exiled in "thorns" to Babylon, humbles himself according to 2 Chr 7:14, and is restored. His
  exile-and-return "anticipates what later happens to the whole nation," a proto-exile-and-return
  grounding the book's final hope that "even *maʿal* can be forgiven." Leithart also places
  Manasseh in the *maʿal* chain running from Achan through Saul, Asa, Uzziah and Ahaz to the exile
  itself (2 Chr 36:14).
- **The Prayer of Manasseh**: a short penitential prayer, composed later and attached to this
  tradition, supplying the text that 2 Chr 33:18–19 says was recorded elsewhere. It is not part of
  the Hebrew canon; the wiki's [[eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern Orthodox]] canon page lists it among
  the books of the broad Orthodox Old Testament canon. It should not be presented as
  Manasseh's own composition, nor as canonical across traditions.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Manasseh carries the theological weight of the exile itself. In Kings he is the causal hinge: the
fall of [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] to the [[babylonians|Babylonians]] is retroactively grounded in
his reign, so that reform under a later righteous king cannot avert it. In Chronicles the same
figure carries the opposite weight — he becomes the model penitent, the proof that humbling
oneself brings restoration, and a personal rehearsal of the nation's exile and return.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**`[CONTRADICTION]` — the Kings/Chronicles divergence.** Recorded as two positions, each in its
strongest form:

- **Kings' position**: Manasseh does not repent. His sin is the stated and sufficient reason for
  the exile, reasserted twice after his death (2 Kgs 23:26–27; 24:3–4). There is no Assyrian
  captivity, no prayer, no restoration, and no reform. The theological point requires the absence
  of repentance: judgment on Judah is irrevocable, and even [[josiah|Josiah]] cannot undo it.
- **Chronicles' position**: Manasseh is captured by Assyrian commanders, taken to
  [[babylon|Babylon]] in fetters, humbles himself, is heard, and is restored to his kingdom, after
  which he reforms the cult. The theological point requires the repentance: the retribution logic
  of Chronicles must account for the longest reign in Judah, and the episode establishes that no
  sacrilege is beyond forgiveness.

The wiki does not adjudicate between them. Related disputes recorded here without resolution:
whether the Chronicles episode reflects an otherwise unattested historical deportation; whether
it is the Chronicler's theological construction; and how the two accounts should be read
together, if at all. The [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] page states the wiki's editorial stance
explicitly for this case: the accounts are recorded "without harmonization."

Also disputed: Manasseh's dates and the extent of any coregency with [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]], and
whether his cultic policy is best read as apostasy or as Assyrian-vassal accommodation — this
wiki has ingested no source supporting the latter reading, so it is noted only as a live question,
not asserted.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Manasseh is one of the standard proving-grounds for the relationship between the Deuteronomistic
History and Chronicles, and for the question of whether Chronicles corrects, supplements, or
theologically reinterprets its source. Within this wiki, he anchors Provan's Kings argument about
irrevocable judgment and Leithart's Chronicles argument that *maʿal* can be forgiven — two
readings of one reign that cannot be merged.
