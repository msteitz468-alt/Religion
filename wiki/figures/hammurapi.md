# Hammurapi

## Biographical Overview

**Hammurapi** (Hammurabi) was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of **[[babylon|Babylon]]**
(r. **c. 1792–1750 BCE**), the **[[amorites|Amorite]]** ruler who raised an obscure city-state
into the dominant power of Mesopotamia. For most of his reign Babylon was one player among
several — Larsa, Eshnunna, Assyria under Shamshi-Adad I, and the middle-Euphrates kingdom of
**[[mari|Mari]]** under **Zimri-Lim**, long Hammurapi's ally. In his final decades he turned
on his rivals, conquered Lower Mesopotamia, and **c. 1760 BCE overthrew and destroyed Mari**,
bringing "all of Mesopotamia under his sway" (Pitard, Coogan Ch. 1). He is **not mentioned in
the Bible**, but his era and his law collection are central to understanding the world and
the legal traditions behind it.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **The Mari archives**: the ~25,000 tablets from Zimri-Lim's palace at [[mari|Mari]] document
  Hammurapi's slow rise and his betrayal of his ally — "inside information, not royal
  propaganda" (Coogan Ch. 1).
- **The Code of Hammurabi**: the famous **diorite stela** (now in the Louvre), topped by a
  relief of the king before the sun-god **Shamash**, inscribed with a prologue, ~282 case
  laws, and an epilogue — the fullest of the Mesopotamian law collections.
- **Royal inscriptions and year-names**: chronicling his conquests and building.

## Significance for Biblical Studies

Hammurapi matters to the wiki chiefly through the **Code of Hammurabi**, the prime comparison
for **biblical law** — especially the **Covenant Code** (Exodus 21–23). Both share the
**casuistic ("if… then") form**, overlapping subject matter (goring oxen, the *lex talionis*
"eye for eye," debt slavery, deposit and injury law), and the framing of law as **divinely
sanctioned royal justice**. The parallels demonstrate Israel's participation in a **common
ancient Near Eastern legal tradition**, while the differences — Israel's laws set in a
covenant between God and people rather than promulgated by a king, and its distinctive
valuation of human life over property — are equally instructive. (No literary dependence is
implied; both draw on a shared Mesopotamian legal stream.)

## Historicity and Controversies

Hammurapi is a securely historical figure, his chronology debated only within the standard
**"high/middle/low" Mesopotamian chronology** options (the middle chronology, used here,
places his reign 1792–1750 BCE). Whether the Code functioned as **enforced statute or as
royal apologetic/idealized justice** is debated, as is the precise nature of the
**Covenant-Code–Hammurabi relationship** (shared tradition vs. typological echo).
