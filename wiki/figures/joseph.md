# Joseph

**Joseph** (Hebrew *Yosef*), the eleventh son of [[jacob|Jacob]] and the firstborn of Rachel, is the protagonist of the longest sustained narrative in Genesis (chs. 37, 39–50) — the dreamer sold into Egyptian slavery by his brothers who rises to become vizier of Egypt and, through forgiveness, the preserver of his family. In Jewish tradition he is uniquely titled **Joseph the Righteous** (*Yosef ha-Tsaddiq*), and in the [[zohar|Zohar]] this title is given its deepest meaning: Joseph is the human embodiment of **Yesod** (Foundation), the [[sefirot|sefirah]] of the covenant and the channel of all blessing — **"the Righteous One, the foundation of the world"** ([[tsaddiq|Tsaddiq]]).

---

## Biographical Overview

- **The favored son and the dreams** (Gen 37): the coat of many colors; the dreams of the sheaves and the stars bowing; the brothers' hatred; sold to Ishmaelite/Midianite traders and brought to Egypt.
- **Potiphar's house and prison** (Gen 39): serves Potiphar; **resists the advances of Potiphar's wife**, is falsely accused, and imprisoned — the episode on which his title "the Righteous" turns.
- **The interpreter of dreams** (Gen 40–41): interprets the dreams of the cupbearer and baker, then Pharaoh's dreams of the seven years; appointed vizier over Egypt; renamed Zaphenath-paneah; marries Asenath; fathers Manasseh and Ephraim.
- **The reunion and forgiveness** (Gen 42–45): the brothers come for grain; the testing; the disclosure ("I am Joseph"); "you intended evil against me, but God intended it for good" (50:20).
- **The descent and the death** (Gen 46–50): Jacob's family settles in Goshen; Joseph's death at 110; his bones to be carried up at the Exodus.

---

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Genesis 37, 39–50** — the "Joseph novella," widely regarded as a literary masterpiece and the climax of Genesis.
- **Exodus 13:19; Joshua 24:32** — Joseph's bones carried out of Egypt and buried at Shechem.
- **Qur'an, Sura Yusuf (Q 12)** — "the most beautiful of stories"; a complete, independent retelling emphasizing Yusuf's chastity, the women of the city, and divine providence.
- **Acts 7:9–16; Hebrews 11:22** — Joseph in the NT as a model of faith and a figure within the salvation-historical retrospect.

---

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: *Yosef ha-Tsaddiq*, "the Righteous," a title earned by resisting Potiphar's wife — the paradigm of sexual self-mastery and covenant fidelity. Rabbinic Midrash dwells on his beauty, his temptation, and his bones. He is associated with the covenant of circumcision and with the messianic figure *Mashiaḥ ben Yosef* (the suffering, warring Messiah who precedes Messiah ben David).
- **Christian**: a major **type of Christ** — the beloved son rejected by his brothers, sold for silver, suffering unjustly, exalted, and becoming the savior of those who wronged him; his forgiveness prefigures Christ's. Patristic and medieval exegesis develops the typology extensively.
- **Islamic**: Yusuf, a prophet; Sura 12 is the Qur'an's most sustained single narrative, a story of trial, chastity (the famous scene of the women cutting their hands), patience, and the reunion of the family; a touchstone of Sufi devotional and poetic tradition (the beauty of Yusuf as the beauty of the divine Beloved).
- **Kabbalistic (the Zohar)** — see below; Joseph = Yesod, the Righteous Foundation.

## Kabbalistic Reception (the Zohar)

Joseph is the Zohar's premier human embodiment of **Yesod** — the ninth [[sefirot|sefirah]], the Foundation, the channel that gathers all the upper energies and transmits them to the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] (Malkhut). Yesod is symbolized by the **covenant of circumcision** and the generative organ; its human exemplar must therefore be the master of sexual holiness. Key Zoharic readings:

- **"The righteous one is the foundation of the world"** (Prov 10:25): the Zohar makes this verse the definition of Yesod and of Joseph. "All those pillars stand upon one single pillar… called Righteous"; Yesod is "the foundation of the world and its perfection, single point of the world, center of all," identified with **Zion**. See [[tsaddiq|Tsaddiq (the Righteous One, Foundation of the World)]].
- **Guarding the sign of the covenant**: Joseph earns the title *Tsaddiq* by resisting Potiphar's wife. At the decisive moment "**the image of his father Jacob** appeared to him" in the window, and he "withdrew" — because to have sinned would have defiled Yesod, the very sefirah he embodies. "This covenant is the mystery of Joseph." The verse "there was no man of the men of the house" (Gen 39:11) is read to *include* the image of Jacob ([[jacob|Tif'eret]]) present in the room, of which Joseph (Yesod) is the direct extension.
- **Joseph's coffin and the Shekhinah**: because Joseph guarded the covenant, "**Joseph's coffin accompanied Her**" — his bier travels together with the Shekhinah through the wilderness; the one who keeps Yesod is never separated from Malkhut.
- **Joseph and Benjamin / Mashiaḥ ben Yosef**: the Zohar links Joseph to the warring, suffering messianic figure and to the eschatological role of the tribe of his line.

The contrast with **Er and Onan** (Judah's sons, Gen 38), who "wasted seed" and so fed the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]], is pointed: Joseph is the positive pole of the same sefirah whose misuse is the gravest sin. See [[tsaddiq|Tsaddiq]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Joseph is the bridge from the patriarchal age to the national story: his descent into Egypt sets up the Exodus, and his providential reading of his own suffering ("God intended it for good," 50:20) is Genesis's clearest statement of how divine sovereignty works through, not against, human wrongdoing. In the Zohar this providential, world-sustaining role is given metaphysical depth: as Yesod, the Tsaddiq, Joseph is *literally* the channel through which the world is "nourished" and held in being — the righteous human on whom, the Zohar says (with Prov 10:25), the world stands.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- The Joseph narrative is **not independently attested**; critical scholarship treats it as a novella (a "diaspora tale" akin to Esther and Daniel) shaped in the monarchic or post-exilic period, reflecting Egyptian local color of uncertain date. Source criticism finds J and E strands (the Ishmaelite/Midianite traders doublet, the Reuben/Judah doublet).
- The **two-Messiah tradition** (Mashiaḥ ben Yosef vs. ben David) is a later development the Zohar inherits and elaborates.
- Joseph/Yusuf's near-identical role across Genesis and the Qur'an makes him a frequent locus of comparative Jewish–Christian–Islamic study.
