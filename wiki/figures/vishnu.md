# Viṣṇu

*Shell page — to be populated and expanded from ongoing ingests.*

**Viṣṇu** (also Nārāyaṇa, "he who moves on the waters") is the Preserver of the universe and one of the principal deities of Hinduism, forming the **Trimurti** with Brahmā (Creator) and Śiva (Destroyer). He is the supreme deity of Vaishnavism — the majority tradition within Hinduism — and is worshipped in iconic form as a four-armed god holding a discus (*chakra*), mace (*gada*), conchshell (*shankha*), and lotus (*padma*), often resting on the cosmic serpent Śeṣa.

The **avatar doctrine** (*avatāra*, "descent") is inseparable from Viṣṇu: when *dharma* (righteous order) is threatened, Viṣṇu descends to earth in bodily form to restore it. The ten principal avatars (*Daśāvatāra*) include [[rama|Rāma]] (seventh avatar) and [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]] (eighth avatar), making the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]] and [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] among the primary theological narratives of Viṣṇu's presence in the world. The Rāmāyaṇa's avatar logic: Rāvaṇa's boon excludes gods from killing him but not humans, so Viṣṇu must incarnate as a fully mortal man (Rāma) who is largely unaware of his own divinity.

## Primary Sources
- **[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]**: Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa is the divine identity of Rāma; gods repeatedly recognize this while Rāma does not; the revelation comes at the war's end
- **[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad-Gītā]]**: Kṛṣṇa reveals his cosmic identity as Viṣṇu in the *vishvarūpa* (cosmic form) episode
- **[[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]]**: Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa active through Kṛṣṇa throughout

## In the Rāmāyaṇa (Buck's Retelling)

### The Avatar Theology: Why Viṣṇu Had to Become Human

Buck's text presents the avatar doctrine through a scene in which **Indra petitions Nārāyaṇa** after being defeated and humiliated by Rāvaṇa (lines 1404–1461). The theological crux is explicit: Brahmā had granted Rāvaṇa invulnerability to all divine and demonic beings, but Rāvaṇa in his "disdain" (line 1440) had not thought to ask for protection against **men or animals**. As Indra explains: "He eats men; they are his food and why should he fear them?" (lines 1441–1443). Therefore, Viṣṇu must incarnate as a mortal human — not merely disguise himself, but actually "accept birth as a man" (line 1446).

Nārāyaṇa's response is striking: "I already have" (line 1447). The decision precedes Indra's request. The descent is already in motion at the moment the divine council seeks it.

### Iconography Described in the Text

The text describes Nārāyaṇa sitting as Indra approaches: "Indra pressed together his hands, touched his brow, and bent his head low to Narayana's feet. 'Searcher of Hearts, I bow to you, namas. I have still faith in the Good Law of Dharma.' Indra looked up past **Narayana's wide dark chest crossed by a neckchain of sapphires**, up into the great god's **joyful black eyes wide as lotus petals**" (lines 1410–1414). Nārāyaṇa wears "yellow saffron summer robes" (line 1415). He is addressed in Indra's invocation as "Dark blue Narayana clad in yellow" (line 1448) — the standard iconographic colors (dark blue/black skin, yellow garments).

The four divine attributes that Indra tells Nārāyaṇa to "put aside" on descent are: "**the shell trumpet, the razor-edged chakra, the lotus and mace**" held in his four hands (lines 1449–1451). These are the *pañcāyudha* (five weapons) in their four-handed form: conch (*Pañcajanya*), discus (*Sudarśana chakra*), lotus, and mace (*Kaumodakī*). The text's descent scene dramatizes Nārāyaṇa emptying his divine hands.

### The Descent: Becoming Four

Indra's formulation of the descent is theologically unusual: Nārāyaṇa is to "become four" — that is, to become all four of Daśaratha's sons simultaneously: "Become Dasaratha's four sons born of blood and seed. Take your Goddess Lakshmi and let her be your mortal wife" (lines 1453–1454). [[sita|Sītā]] (who is [[dasaratha|Daśaratha]]'s daughter-in-law, daughter of Earth) is here identified with Lakṣmī, Viṣṇu's eternal consort. Nārāyaṇa confirms: "We will go down" (line 1455).

The actual ritual mechanism of descent occurs in Vasiṣṭha's sacrifice by the Sarayu. Vasishtha sings to Nārāyaṇa, flowers are thrown in the fire — and then the four divine weapons drop sequentially from the sky: "The sharp discus drops" (line 1479), then "the huge mace is fallen" (line 1481), then "the shell is down" (line 1481), then "they caught the sudden scent of lotuses" (line 1482). A giant black man then rises from the fire — bearing a golden bowl of sacred rice that will produce the four sons when fed to the queens. This figure is Nārāyaṇa's agent of descent.

### Nārāyaṇa as Cosmic Ground

The text also refers to Nārāyaṇa in his cosmic-resting form: "Brahma the Creator of the Worlds was reborn from a lotus. That flower grew from **the navel of Lord Narayana as he slept afloat on the waters, lying on the white coils of the endless serpent Sesha**" (lines 1637–1639). This is the standard Viṣṇu cosmological image (*Nārāyaṇa on Śeṣa*), here embedded in Viśvāmitra's mythological account of the origin of the Rākṣasas.

Brahmā explicitly defers to Nārāyaṇa's authority when Indra demands answers: "Go to Narayana. Only Narayana knows, or maybe he doesn't. But for myself, where can I find the end, where the beginning, where the middle of all this? Ask Narayana" (lines 1399–1403). Nārāyaṇa is thus the limit-point of Brahmā's own knowledge — a theological statement of Vaiṣṇava supremacy.

### Viṣṇu's Earlier Intervention: The War Against the Rākṣasas

Before the incarnation as Rāma, Nārāyaṇa intervened directly in an earlier war against the Rākṣasas. When the sage Nārada was swallowed by a demon and spat out, he sang a war-song, and: "Narayana came on the bird Garuda in armor bright as a thousand suns" (line 1813). Nārāyaṇa beheaded Māli and drove the Rākṣasas from Laṅkā to the underworld; "no living Rakshasa remained on Earth. Lanka City was empty" (lines 1847–1848). This earlier intervention failed to produce lasting peace — Rāvaṇa's later penance and boon restored Rākṣasa power — which is why the avatar incarnation is necessary.

### Rāvaṇa's Posthumous Letter: Theology of the Incarnation (Lines 11501–13565)

The theological climax of the war of Laṅkā is Rāvaṇa's sealed stone letter, delivered by Suka the morning after the battle. Rāvaṇa addresses Rāma as "Lord Nārāyaṇa, you are the witness, you make the Moon walk in brightness" (l. 13449) and "Dear Rāma, Lord of the Worlds" (l. 13451). He declares:

> *"You do not know who you are again. I knew it all along, but even still you do not know. Nothing you do ever fails, one glance of yours and people sing again the good old songs."* (ll. 13457–13459)

Rāvaṇa claims he deliberately stole Sītā ("I kept Lakṣmī to lure you here") to force his death at Rāma's hands as the only path to divine union: "Except dying at your hand, how else could I make you take me into your own Self?" (ll. 13452–13454). He frames the entire war as a *līlā* he understood and enacted. The letter then expands to a vision of non-dual all-pervasive identity:

> *"You are Nārāyaṇa who moves on the waters. You flow through us all. You are Rāma and Sītā born out of Earth and Rāvaṇa the Demon King, you are Hanumān like the wind, you are Lakṣmaṇa like a mirror, you are Indrajit and Indra, you are the Poet and the Players and the Play. And born as a man you forget this, you lose the memory, and take on man's ignorance again, as you will, every time."* (ll. 13476–13483)

This statement anticipates and explains what will appear later in Agni's and Indra's speeches to Rāma after the reunion: "Again and again you have forgotten your secret" (l. 13888 — from the later ingest). The forgetting is structural: "as you will, every time." Rāma's response to the letter: "Go, go, I do not care what all this means" (ll. 13489–13491) — neither affirming nor denying.

Before this letter, during the final battle, other figures recognize Rāma's identity: Kumbhakarna names the monkey army "the animals of Lord Nārāyaṇa" (ll. 11614–11615); Malyavan recalls from youth the vision of "Lord Nārāyaṇa tall and dark-blue, wearing saffron robes, a soft round jewel on his breast" riding Garuḍa (ll. 12553–12562) — now seen again, incarnated.

### Rama's Persistent Refusal of Divinity

Even as the gods address Rāma directly as Nārāyaṇa, Rāma consistently declines the identification.
At the reunion after Lanka (l. 13865–13937), Agni and Indra both call him "Narayana" and "Searcher of
Hearts." Agni says: "Again and again you have forgotten your secret" (l. 13888). Indra says: "The
closing of your eyes is the Night of the World's End, and your awakening from sleep is Time's beginning.
To favor men you are born as Rama, so let no one think men are weak and alone" (l. 13914–13917). Rāma
replies each time simply: "I am Rama, a mortal man" (l. 13883).

This theological posture — of an avatar unaware of or refusing his own divinity — is fundamental to
Buck's Rāma. The gods can see through the veil; Rāma cannot, or will not. The universe requires his
mortality to be genuine.

### Brahmā's Message and the Return to Heaven

After Rāma has ruled for nearly eleven thousand years, Brahmā sends Time disguised as a hermit with
the message: "You killed Ravana. Your promised time is full. Oh Narayana, Lakshmi awaits you, so
have I heard. I send Time to remind you. Return to yourself, my friend, we have not seen you for a
moment in heaven" (l. 15924–15927). Rāma's reply is characteristic: "Those are good words, I am
pleased with your coming. I've not forgotten. I remember all of you" (l. 15929–15930).

### The Final Return to Heaven

After Lakshmana's death and Rāma's departure into the Sarayu River, the restoration to divine status
is instantaneous and complete: "Narayana was back in his heaven, with Lakshmi an instant later. The
four dark hands picked up again the shell and the lotus, the discus and the mace" (l. 16272–16279).
This mirrors exactly the descent scene: Nārāyaṇa set down the four divine weapons when entering the
mortal world; he picks them up again at the moment of return. The avatar's life is framed as a
parenthesis in eternity — "I'm going back around by going forward," Rāma says (l. 16043–16045).

Buck emphasizes that Rāma walks to the Sarayu flanked by Lakṣmī on his right and Earth (Sītā's
mother) on his left (l. 16096–16097) — a final image in which Viṣṇu's consort and the earth-goddess
who reclaimed Sītā accompany the avatar's return, implying that the divine reunion is with both.

### Shiva's Word

The text's penultimate cosmic statement belongs to Śiva: "Shiva the great god divided this romance
into three parts, one for heaven, one for Earth, and one for the underworlds. He had one section left,
and divided that into three. He had one verse left, and divided that as well. Shiva had one word left,
that he kept for himself — the word Rama — as being the essence and heart of the whole thing"
(l. 16016–16021). This theological move — Śiva keeping "Rāma" as the distilled essence of the epic —
reflects the Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava synthesis in devotional Hinduism, in which the name of Rāma functions
as a mantra for both traditions.
