**Biographical Overview**: Born in Bethlehem to Mary, raised in Nazareth of Galilee. Baptized by John the Baptist. Conducted a traveling ministry of teaching, healing, and exorcisms primarily in Galilee, before journeying to Jerusalem where he was crucified under Pontius Pilate. His followers proclaimed his resurrection on the third day.

**Primary Source Appearances**: 
- *Gospel of Mark*: The suffering Son of Man, misunderstood by his disciples, whose messianic identity is paradoxically revealed through the cross.
- *Gospel of Matthew*: The fulfillment of the Old Testament, the Davidic Messiah, the new Moses who delivers the definitive Torah (Sermon on the Mount).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**: 
- *Christianity*: The incarnate Word of God, second person of the Trinity, whose death and resurrection provide salvation for humanity.
- *Judaism*: Historically a Galilean preacher and wonder-worker; not accepted as the Messiah.
- *Islam*: Isa al-Masih, a major prophet and messenger, but not divine.
- *Greco-Roman pagan critics* (via [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|Wilken]]): the pagan portraits diverge sharply and are recorded here as the *external* reading, not endorsed. [[celsus|Celsus]] (c. 180) demythologized the Gospel narrative — alleging illegitimate birth (the soldier "Panthera" legend), magic learned in Egypt, and a resurrection "witnessed" only by "a hysterical female." [[porphyry|Porphyry]] (c. 234–305), by contrast, **refused the magic charge** and praised Jesus as "a man of outstanding piety," a sage elevated among the heroes — laying the blame for "error" on the disciples who turned him into an object of worship. [[julian-the-apostate|Julian]] (r. 361–363) called him "the Galilaean" and argued only John, and even he unclearly, called Jesus God. A recurring pagan thesis (Porphyry, Julian) is that **Jesus taught worship of the one God and Law-observance**, and it was his followers who innovated.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**: 
In Mark, Jesus is the paradoxical king whose kingdom comes through suffering, teaching his disciples the way of the cross. In Matthew, he is the climax of Israel's history, bringing the law and the prophets to their intended completion.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**: 
His historical existence is virtually universally accepted by critical scholars. Interpretive controversies focus on his self-understanding (did he see himself as divine?), the nature of his miracles, and the historicity of his resurrection.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**: 
The central figure of the entire Christian commentary tradition. Interpreted both historically and allegorically (e.g., as the true fulfillment of all Old Testament types).

## Historical-Critical Reconstruction ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

[[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]] (2014) presents the mainstream
critical reconstruction: the historical Jesus was a **Jewish apocalyptic prophet** who did
**not** call himself God. Working from independent attestation (Mark, Q, M, L, John),
dissimilarity, and contextual credibility, Ehrman argues:

- **Apocalyptic message.** At the core of Jesus's preaching was the imminent intervention of
  God and the coming kingdom, brought by the [[son-of-man|Son of Man]] — a worldview marked
  by dualism, pessimism, judgment, and imminence (Mark 9:1; 13:30). The "beginning and end"
  argument anchors this: Jesus began under the apocalyptic preacher [[john-the-baptist|John
  the Baptist]] and gave rise to apocalyptic communities ([[paul-apostle|Paul]] expecting the
  end in his own lifetime).
- **Messiah, not God.** Jesus likely understood himself as the future **king** of that
  kingdom — the [[messianic-secret|messiah]] in an apocalyptic sense — but proclaimed it only
  privately (what Judas betrayed; what the "king of the Jews" charge and placard, passing
  dissimilarity, attest). A *crucified* messiah was the opposite of Jewish expectation, so the
  disciples must have called him messiah already in his lifetime.
- **Son of Man as another figure.** In the earliest sayings Jesus speaks of the Son of Man as
  a coming judge other than himself (Mark 8:38); the identification is read *into* the texts.
- **John vs. the Synoptics.** The divine self-claims of John ("Before Abraham was, I am";
  "I and the Father are one") "cannot be ascribed to the historical Jesus" — failing all three
  criteria — and represent the evangelist's voice placed on Jesus's lips. Miracles, forgiveness,
  and veneration in the Synoptics do not amount to a divine self-claim (prophets did miracles;
  "your sins are forgiven" is passive; kings received obeisance).

**Burial and resurrection.** Ehrman doubts a known, decent burial occurred (see
[[burial-of-jesus|the burial controversy]]) and makes the disciples' **belief** in the
resurrection — grounded in [[resurrection-appearances-visions|visionary experiences]] — the
historical origin of all later Christology: the human Jesus came to be understood as
**[[exaltation-christology|exalted]]** to divine status at the resurrection, then progressively
as a pre-existent, [[incarnation|incarnate]] divine being. (Ehrman's reconstruction is a
historical-critical position, contested by confessional and conservative scholarship; recorded
here without adjudication. See also the fuller, tradition-centered profile at [[jesus|Jesus]].)
