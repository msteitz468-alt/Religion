# 'Abdu'l-Bahá

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

'Abbás Effendi ('Abdu'l-Bahá) was born in 1844 — "according to tradition on the very night of the Báb's declaration" (line 1832–1833). He was Bahá'u'lláh's eldest surviving son. His mother was Ásíyih Khánum (d. 1886), the first of Bahá'u'lláh's three wives. Only his sister, Bahíyyih (1846–1932), outlived their father and became one of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's most important aides.

He attended no school but evidently read widely (line 1843–1844). During the Baghdad exile in his teens he began to assist his father. In Edirne and 'Akká he took responsibility for the practical management of the large household of Bahá'u'lláh's family and disciples, also acting as one of his father's secretaries. He was "well known and respected amongst Ottoman officials and reformers, including several of the provincial governors in their various places of exile" (lines 1844–1847).

After his father moved out of 'Akká in 1877, 'Abdu'l-Bahá continued to live in the city, "increasingly gaining acceptance as a local notable despite living under the government's order of banishment. Giving alms to the poor and regularly attending the local mosque, he was regarded as a pious, albeit heterodox Muslim leader rather than as the son of the founder of a new religion" (lines 1848–1855).

He married Munjírih Nahri (1847–1938), a member of a prominent Isfahán Bahá'í merchant family, in 1873. They had four daughters who survived to adulthood, in addition to two sons and three daughters who died in childhood. Unlike his father, grandfather, and uncles, he remained monogamous, later interpreting the Bahá'í marriage law so as to prohibit polygamy (lines 1860–1863).

He chose to be known by the title 'Abdu'l-Bahá — "Servant of Bahá" (line 1831). Bahá'u'lláh named him the "Master" (*Aqá*) and the "Most Great Branch" (*Ghusn-i-A'zam*), and in his will appointed him head of the religion after him (lines 1828–1830).

**Writings before succession:** Most of his writings and recorded oral teachings date from after 1892, but before his father's death he had already written:
- **The Secret of Divine Civilization** (1875) — a treatise on what Smith terms the "modernization" of Iran, anonymously circulated within Iranian intellectual circles (lines 1714–1717), and later forming "part of the Bahá'í textual tradition."
- **A Traveller's Narrative** (1886) — a short history of the Babi and Bahá'í religions, "originally published under the translator's name, Edward G. Browne" (lines 1868–1869).

## Succession and Ministry

'Abdu'l-Bahá's leadership period (1892–1921) is divided by Smith into three phases.

**First phase (1892–1908):** He assumed leadership "as 'the centre of Bahá'u'lláh's covenant' and the interpreter of his writings" (lines 1884–1886). Most Bahá'ís readily accepted this appointment. However, many members of Bahá'u'lláh's extended family, led by 'Abdu'l-Bahá's half-brother Muhammad-'Alí, were unwilling to accept his authority. The followers of Muhammad-'Alí "accused 'Abdu'l-Bahá of overreaching the mandate given to him by his father" (lines 1896–1897). Although unable to shake the allegiance of the mass of Bahá'ís, this campaign led to recurrent problems with Turkish authorities, including the reimposition of confinement in 'Akká (1901) and the threat of exile to North Africa.

These conflicts caused 'Abdu'l-Bahá to give great emphasis to "the doctrine that there was a sacred Covenant that ensured the preservation of Bahá'í unity through obedience to the properly appointed leaders of the Faith." Those who broke this Covenant were denounced as "Covenant-breakers" and ultimately excommunicated (lines 1903–1908).

During this period he also began encouraging the formation of locally elected Bahá'í councils and several "national" bodies. He also wrote his Will and Testament, in which he:
- Appointed his eldest grandson, [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] — then still a child — to be the Guardian of the Faith after him.
- Outlined the system for the election of the Universal House of Justice referred to by Bahá'u'lláh.
- Excluded Muhammad-'Alí from succession because of his Covenant-breaking.

The provisions of the will remained secret until after 'Abdu'l-Bahá's death in 1921 (lines 1912–1927).

This phase also saw: the composition of his *Treatise on Politics* (1892–3); the first pilgrimage visit from Western Bahá'ís (1898–9); encouragement of educational, medical, and economic development among Eastern Bahá'ís; and the beginning of construction of the first Bahá'í House of Worship in Ashkhabad in Russian Turkistan.

**Second phase (1908–1913):** The Young Turk revolution of 1908 freed Ottoman political prisoners, ending the dangers that had faced 'Abdu'l-Bahá in 'Akká. He moved across the bay from 'Akká to the newly developing city of **Haifa**, which "henceforth was to remain the headquarters of the Faith" (lines 1940–1941). The spiritual significance of Haifa for Bahá'ís was established when the remains of the Báb — smuggled out of Iran some years previously — were buried there in 1909 in the shrine on Mount Carmel, completed under 'Abdu'l-Bahá's direction at a site selected by Bahá'u'lláh.

Now in his late sixties and in poor health, 'Abdu'l-Bahá traveled to Egypt in 1910, then undertook Western journeys:
- 1911: London, Bristol, and Paris.
- March 1912–June 1913: Fourteen months visiting thirty-eight American and Canadian cities, then Britain, France, Germany, and Austria-Hungary.

His public addresses during these journeys are described by Smith as "an important addition to Bahá'í scripture" (line 1973). The journeys consolidated fledgling Western Bahá'í communities and attracted considerable sympathetic newspaper attention. He met many eminent people including John Dewey, Henri Bergson, Emmeline Pankhurst, Annie Besant, and the author Kahlil Gibran (lines 1979–1985). He also "very deliberately scandalized social convention by insisting that a leading black Bahá'í should sit next to him at a prestigious dinner" in Washington DC (lines 1993–1995).

**Third and final phase (1913–1921):** 'Abdu'l-Bahá returned to Haifa in December 1913. World War I (1914–18) effectively confined him to Palestine; his life was again threatened by the Turkish army chief, Jamal Pasha, but this danger ended with the collapse of Turkish rule. During the war he averted local famine by supplying grain stocks, gaining the respect of the newly established British authorities, who secured him a knighthood in 1920.

During the final three years of his life he was recognized as "the head of an international religious movement" (lines 2013–2014). He communicated with the executive committee of the Central Organization for a Durable Peace at The Hague, indicating that the League of Nations "was not to be equated with Bahá'u'lláh's supreme tribunal, being insufficient to establish universal peace" (lines 2017–2019). He warned that the vanquished powers "would rekindle the flames of conflict" and that the Balkans would remain an area of chronic instability; he also predicted that "'the Movement of the Left' and other movements that would emerge would gain great influence" (lines 2025–2027, citing *'Abdu'l-Bahá, Selections* 249–50, 306).

'Abdu'l-Bahá died on 28 November 1921. His funeral was "marked by the great number and religious diversity of its mourners" (lines 2028–2029). He was buried in a separate part of the Báb's shrine.

## Theological and Institutional Role

As "the centre of Bahá'u'lláh's covenant" and the authorized interpreter of his father's writings, 'Abdu'l-Bahá occupied a unique position in Bahá'í doctrine: he was neither a Manifestation of God (he explicitly discouraged any such identification) nor an ordinary believer, but the authorized mediator of Bahá'u'lláh's revelation. His own writings and authenticated talks "formed a major addition to the Bahá'í 'canon': over 27,000 of his letters have survived" (lines 2049–2050). His writings prior to his succession — notably *The Secret of Divine Civilization* — were "readily accorded the same status as his later writings" (lines 2052–2054).

The institution of the Covenant, which 'Abdu'l-Bahá elaborated in response to the challenges of his half-brother, became a central structural principle of the Bahá'í Faith: unity of the community is secured through obedience to the appointed center, and those who break from this center are excommunicated.

## Writings and Their Content

'Abdu'l-Bahá's Western writings and talks are of "particular significance" (line 2055). His addresses, letters to Western Bahá'ís, and recorded conversations "reflect his responses to Western concerns and indicate the manner in which he thought it appropriate to present the Faith to Westerners" (lines 2059–2061).

In his public talks in the West, 'Abdu'l-Bahá frequently presented a list of Bahá'í principles (ranging from nine to fourteen), which Smith notes "henceforth becoming a common element in Bahá'í literature" (lines 2073–2075). These principles included:

- Independent investigation of truth, setting aside historical prejudices.
- All divine religions are one; the teachings of Bahá'u'lláh best represent the "universal religion" needed at the present time.
- Genuine religion is a powerful support for social stability.
- Material civilization by itself is not sufficient; it must be combined with "divine civilization" and the Holy Spirit.
- Religion must be the cause of love and unity; if it produces hatred and division it should be abandoned.
- Religion must be in conformity with science and reason; if not, it is only ignorant superstition.
- Bahá'u'lláh has come to establish the Most Great Peace; an international tribunal should be instituted.
- The whole human race is one; all human beings are equally the children of God.
- Religious, racial, political, national, and class prejudices are destructive.
- Human progress cannot occur while people struggle for daily existence; extremes of wealth and poverty are to be abolished.
- All individuals are to be equal before the law.
- Women are the equals of men and are to have equality of rights, particularly of educational opportunity.
- All children are to receive an education.
- There should be an international auxiliary language.

Smith notes that 'Abdu'l-Bahá showed "significant differences in emphasis" from his father: "most obvious in the advocacy of the emancipation and equality of women, a principle that appears to have received little more than passing reference from Bahá'u'lláh, but which was discussed at length by 'Abdu'l-Bahá" (lines 2119–2121).

**Tablets of the Divine Plan (1916–17):** A series of letters addressed to the North American Bahá'ís during World War I, calling on them to undertake a global campaign of teaching — "to establish the Bahá'í Faith throughout not only all the American states and Canadian provinces, but in all parts of the world" (lines 2128–2130). Smith notes these "later came to have great significance" (line 2125).

## Reception in East and West

Many non-Bahá'ís referred to 'Abdu'l-Bahá as a powerful personality. E. G. Browne wrote of his "eloquence, wide religious knowledge, and 'majestic' and 'genial' bearing" (lines 2035–2037, citing *Travellers Narrative* vol. II, xxxvi). He was "both a commanding presence and intensely approachable, with a ready sense of humour" (lines 2037–2038). Many Western Bahá'ís "were inclined to see him in Christ-like terms as the personification of love and compassion" (lines 2044–2045).

Despite using interpreters for his Western addresses (mostly Iranians, but also some Western Bahá'ís who had learned Persian), he appears to have had some knowledge of English in addition to his native Persian, Turkish, and Arabic (lines 2066–2070).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### The Covenant and Succession
'Abdu'l-Bahá is the Centre of [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]]'s Covenant, appointed in the [[covenant-bahai|Book of the Covenant]] (Kitáb-i-'Ahd) as the *ghusn-i-a'zam* (Most Great Branch) and Bahá'u'lláh's successor. 'Abdu'l-Bahá described the Covenant doctrine as 'the most great characteristic' of Bahá'u'lláh's religion, stating that through it the unity of the Bahá'í Faith was assured and schism prevented (lines 9979–9982).

He declared his half-brother Mírzá Muḥammad-'Alí (also named in the Book of the Covenant, but as subordinate) and his followers to be [[covenant-breakers|Covenant-breakers]], expelling the larger part of Bahá'u'lláh's family from the Faith (lines 10082–10095). He also expelled Dr Ameen U. Fareed (Amínu'lláh Fáríd) for financial misconduct (lines 10096–10106).

His Will and Testament appointed [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] as Guardian and promised divine guidance for the Universal House of Justice (lines 9965–9974).

### Some Answered Questions
This key Bahá'í text was compiled by [[laura-dreyfus-barney|Laura Clifford Dreyfus-Barney]] from her recorded conversations with 'Abdu'l-Bahá in Akká beginning 1904, published 1908 (lines 11160–11168). See [[some-answered-questions|Some Answered Questions]].

### Commentary on 'I was a Hidden Treasure' (lines 9279–9306)
A work by 'Abdu'l-Bahá, composed in Baghdad, addressing themes in Islamic mystical philosophy: stages of divine love, the unknowability of God, and the question of whether archetypal forms were eternal or created. 'Abdu'l-Bahá held that both viewpoints rested on the mystical state of their respective proponents rather than the characteristics of what they were describing.

### Economic Teachings
'Abdu'l-Bahá saw the struggle for physical existence as 'the fountain-head of all calamities' (line 11270). He taught the necessity of abolishing extremes of both poverty and wealth through legislation and voluntary sharing; advocated communal village storehouses, graduated income taxes, industrial profit sharing, adequate wages, and industrial courts to resolve labour disputes (lines 11325–11402). See also poverty, wealth, and reciprocity themes (lines 11258–11402).
