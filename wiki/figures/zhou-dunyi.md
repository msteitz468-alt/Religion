# Zhou Dunyi (1017–1073)

**Zhou Dunyi** (周敦頤, style name *Maoshu*, known posthumously as *Lianxi xiansheng*, "Master of the
Lian Stream") is the retrospective founding figure of the [[neo-confucianism|Daoxue]] (Learning of the
Way) movement — the stream of Song–Ming Confucian philosophy later called "Neo-Confucianism." His
actual fame in his own time was minimal; his elevation to "first link after Mencius in the
[[daotong|daotong]] succession" was almost entirely the work of [[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]], accomplished nearly
a century after his death.

## Biographical Overview

Born in 1017 in Chongling village, Yingdao county, Hunan province. His father died young; he was
raised partly in the household of his maternal uncle, Zheng Xiang, a government official. Zhou
himself spent his career as a local prefect and minor official — a capable but unremarkable
administrator noted for fairness in applying the law. He married into the household of Lu Shen
(1012–1070), an official from Hangzhou.

Zhou died in 1073. His public life was modest; Pan Xingsi, who wrote his epitaph (the earliest
biographical source), lists his writings as: the Taiji Diagram (*Taijitu*), a "Discussion of the Yi"
(*Yishuo*, now lost), and the *Yitong* (later renamed *Tongshu*). This suggests Zhou was known for
philosophical/cosmological writing, not for a teaching school.

The famous characterization attributed to Cheng Hao — that Zhou "always instructed us to look for the
things that Confucius and Master Yan enjoyed" and that he enjoyed "chanting of the wind and fondling of
the moon" (*yinfeng nongyue*) — suggests an aesthetically contemplative figure, not a charismatic
teacher.

## Primary Source Appearances

Zhou Dunyi authored two texts that survive:

1. **[[taijitu-shuo|Taijitu shuo]]** ("Discussion of the Supreme Polarity Diagram"): A brief
   cosmological account (one printed page) explaining the Taiji Diagram — how the *taiji* (Supreme
   Polarity) generates yin and yang, which produce the Five Phases, which produce the ten thousand
   things including human beings as the "finest and most numinous."
2. **[[tongshu|Tongshu]]** ("Penetrating the Scripture of Change," originally titled *Yitong*,
   "Penetrating the Yi"): 40 short, aphoristic, largely independent sections on sagehood, moral
   cultivation, and the Confucian Way, centered on the concept of *cheng* (being authentic) as the
   foundation of the sage.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Zhu Xi's Construction (Adler's account)

The transformation of Zhou Dunyi from minor official-philosopher to founder of the Daoxue lineage was
accomplished by **Zhu Xi** between approximately 1169 and 1196 through a sustained campaign of 22
written pieces (prefaces, postfaces, inscriptions, stele epitaphs, poems). The key steps:

- **1169**: Zhu Xi wrote his first postface to Zhou's collected works, placing the *Taijitu shuo* at
  the head and arguing it should be read as the framework for the *Tongshu*. He also first connected
  Zhou to the *daotong* succession as the link between Mencius and the Cheng brothers.
- **1179**: Revised postface, deepening the daotong claim while in Nankang (near Zhou's ancestral
  area). Acknowledges but dismisses evidence that the Taiji Diagram was received from Daoist sources.
- **1181**: First extant piece placing Zhou explicitly in the *daotong* by name.

Hu Hong (1106–1161) was a predecessor who first suggested Zhou had transmitted something to the Chengs,
but it was Zhu Xi who systematized this into an explicit lineage claim.

**Why Zhu Xi chose Zhou**: Adler's argument (based on Adler 2014) is twofold:
1. *Cosmological reason* (conventional account): Zhou's *taiji* concept provided the link between
   *qi* (psychophysical stuff) and *li* (principle), which the Cheng brothers' philosophy needed but
   Zhou himself had not fully worked out.
2. *Religious-practical reason* (Adler's addition): Zhou's theory of the **interpenetration of
   activity and stillness** (Tongshu section 16) provided the cosmological verification of Zhu Xi's
   own hard-won solution to his spiritual crisis of the 1160s. Section 16's "That which has no
   activity in activity, and no stillness in stillness, is spirit" was the text Zhu Xi found confirmed
   his 1169 "ji-chou enlightenment."

### Zhu Xi's Daotong Placement

In the full lineage as constructed by Zhu Xi:  
*...Mencius → [1,400-year gap] → **Zhou Dunyi** → Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi → [implicitly] Zhu Xi*

Zhu Xi's term *zheng tong* ("orthodox succession") applied to this philosophical lineage challenges
the imperial use of *zheng tong* for legitimate dynastic succession — a politically significant move.

### The Taiji Diagram Origins Controversy

[[lu-jiuyuan|Lu Jiuyuan]] (1139–1193), Zhu Xi's great opponent, argued:
1. *Wuji* (Nonpolar) was a Daoist term from *Laozi* ch. 28 and had no place in Confucian metaphysics.
2. Zhou received the Taiji Diagram from Daoist circles (Chen Tuan chain).
3. Zhu misinterpreted *taiji* by equating it with *li*.

Modern scholarship largely accepts the Daoist origin: Chen Tuan (d. 989) → Chong Fang (965–1015) →
Mu Xiu (979–1032) → Zhou Dunyi's circles, with the transmission first recorded by Zhu Zhen (1072–1138)
in 1134. Zhu Xi acknowledged this claim in his postfaces but dismissed it, arguing Pan Xingsi's
epitaph implied Zhou composed the diagram himself. This position is now generally rejected.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within the Cheng-Zhu framework, Zhou Dunyi serves three functions:

1. **Cosmological anchor**: The *Taijitu shuo* provides the cosmological sequence (nonpolar → Supreme
   Polarity → yin/yang → Five Phases → ten thousand things → humanity/virtue) that grounds Cheng-Zhu
   metaphysics in an account of Heaven-and-earth.
2. **Lineage legitimacy**: His position in the *daotong* gives the Cheng-Zhu school claim to be the
   authentic transmission of the ancient sages, not merely one philosophical school among many.
3. **Religious-practical model**: His "emphasis on stillness" (*zhu jing*) in Tongshu section 40
   and his portrait of sagehood through *cheng* (being authentic) provide the contemplative-ethical
   framework for cultivation.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

1. **Was Zhou a Confucian or a syncretist?** His texts show significant Daoist influence (the diagram
   itself; *wuji*; "emphasizing stillness"). Zhu Xi systematically re-read these elements as
   fully Confucian. Modern scholars (including Adler) regard Zhou as working at the boundary of
   Confucian-Daoist synthesis, not as a pure Confucian.
2. **Did he transmit anything to the Cheng brothers?** The Chengs' own writings never cite Zhou as a
   significant teacher; the transmission claim depends entirely on later reconstructions. Zhu Xi
   himself says "no one knows where his teaching tradition came from."
3. **Taiji Diagram origin**: Virtually all modern scholars accept the Daoist chain; Zhu Xi's denial
   is editorially motivated.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

- **Zhu Xi's commentary on the *Tongshu*** (1187) is the dominant interpretive lens through which
  Zhou's texts have been read since the Song dynasty.
- **Zhang Boxing** (1651–1725, Qing) compiled *Zhou Lianxi ji* (Complete Works of Zhou Dunyi),
  incorporating Zhu Xi's commentary with additional passages from Zhu Xi's *Yulei* (Classified
  Conversations); this remains a standard edition.
- Zhou's role as "the man who told the Chengs to look for what Confucius and Yan Hui enjoyed" became
  a standard trope in the Daoxue tradition's self-understanding.
