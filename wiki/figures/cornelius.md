# Cornelius

Cornelius is the Roman centurion of Caesarea whose conversion (Acts 10:1–11:18)
[[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats as the theological hinge of Gentile inclusion in
[[acts|Acts]]. On Bock's reading this is "one of the most important units in Acts" and
"the book's turning point," after which the gospel "will fan out in all directions."

## Biographical Overview

Cornelius commanded a company of 100 within the Italian Cohort (σπείρης, a cohort of
~600, probably an auxiliary force) stationed at [[caesarea|Caesarea]], the Roman
provincial capital. Bock notes an Austrian inscription (ILS 9168) documenting the cohort
in Palestine down to AD 69, and, following Fitzmyer, dates the episode "probably still
before AD 41." Bock observes Cornelius was likely a *freedman* — Appian records that more
than 10,000 freed slaves took the name Cornelius when Cornelius Sulla freed them in 82 BC —
and that only his Gentile first name is used (as with Julius in Acts 27:1).

The narrative gives four positive descriptions of him (Acts 10:2): devout (εὐσεβής, rare
in the NT), God-fearing, generous in alms, and praying continually — a description Bock
calls "unique." Bock identifies him as a [[god-fearers|God-fearer]]: on Bock's view "not…
a full Jewish proselyte" but a Gentile responsive to Israel's God without embracing
detailed legal practice, and one who "would not have been circumcised." Bock cautions that
"God-fearer" is "probably not a technical term" for Luke. Witherington notes that a
high-status Gentile convert would make purity-conscious Jews "nervous."

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 10:1–8** — Cornelius's vision at the ninth hour (3 p.m., the time of the temple
  afternoon offering); an angel tells him his prayers and alms have ascended as a
  *memorial* (μνημόσυνον, an NT hapax with OT sacrificial roots). Bock, citing Witherington,
  reads his prayer as "described like a sacrifice" honored "outside the temple."
- **Acts 10:9–33** — Peter's parallel rooftop vision of the sheet and the emissaries'
  journey; Cornelius offers Peter homage (προσεκύνησεν), which Peter refuses ("I too am a
  man").
- **Acts 10:34–43** — [[peter-apostle|Peter]]'s second sermon in Cornelius's household,
  opening with the theme "God shows no partiality."
- **Acts 10:44–48** — the Spirit falls on Cornelius's household while Peter is still
  speaking; they are then baptized.
- **Acts 11:1–18** — Peter defends the episode before the Jerusalem believers.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock frames the whole episode as divinely coordinated: the answer to how Gentiles joined
the church is "that God directed it, not that anyone among the Twelve decided it." He reads
Peter's opening line, "God shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34–35; προσωπολήμπτης, an NT hapax),
as meaning God "makes no distinction in how he reacts to people," rooted in the LXX (Lev.
19:15; Deut. 10:17; 2 Chron. 19:7). The two marks of the acceptable person are that he
fears God and "performs righteousness" — but Bock stresses this is "not that Cornelius
earned righteousness" (Rom. 4:5); rather his responsiveness leads God to reveal more.

Bock reads the decisive sign — the Spirit "falling" (ἐπέπεσεν) on the Gentiles *before*
baptism and without laying on of hands — with Bruce as "the 'Pentecost of the Gentile
world.'" The circumcised believers' astonishment (Acts 10:45) matches the Acts 2:12
reaction. On Bock's synthesis, "God directs an epoch-making event": Gentiles are accepted,
receive the gospel and the gift of the Spirit as "the sign that the new era has arrived,"
"and they are not circumcised and yet table fellowship… ensues." Following Gaventa, Bock
notes the Trinitarian shape — "God takes the initiative. Jesus Christ is at the center…
The Spirit confirms."

Bock draws a contrast with Paul: "Paul went from enemy to believer by a dramatic
appearance, Cornelius went from an open seeker to a believer through the preached gospel."
Following Fernando, Bock holds that Cornelius's devout pursuit "does not exempt… him from
needing the forgiveness Jesus has obtained." Bock also speculates that [[theophilus|
Theophilus]], the addressee of Luke-Acts, "may well have been a 'God-fearer'" for whom the
Cornelius account was especially pertinent.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

For Bock the episode is the strongest passage in Acts for removing "food laws that have
social implications": the food vision ("What God has cleansed, you shall not regard as
common," Acts 10:15) and table fellowship "are related," so that "God uses the picture of
unclean food now made clean to portray unclean Gentiles now made clean" (with Polhill:
"purity distinctions and human discrimination are of a single piece"). Cornelius thus
becomes the test case establishing that [[gentiles|Gentiles]] enter the people of God
without [[circumcision|circumcision]], a point Peter must then defend in Jerusalem
(Acts 11) and which Bock links forward to the [[holy-spirit|Spirit]]'s equal gift
("just as on us in the beginning," Acts 11:15).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock records a spectrum of positions without adjudicating in wiki voice. Hengel and
Schwemer posit a Jerusalem source and regard "a Lukan exaggeration that Cornelius is the
first converted non-Jew," reasoning that Philip-type disciples would already have preached
to non-Jews. Skeptics — Dibelius, Haenchen, Conzelmann ("only a historical kernel"), and
Barrett — variously split Cornelius's conversion from the vision or see tension with
Mark 7 and Acts 15. Bock rejects these objections: with Marshall he argues for essential
historicity, and with Witherington treats the vision as central and inseparable. On the
Mark 7 tension Bock replies that the implications "were slow to penetrate the church"; on
Acts 15, that the later issue "was not whether Gentiles should be included but how."
