Rehoboam, son of [[solomon|Solomon]] and first king of the reduced southern kingdom of Judah,
remembered for the harsh answer at [[shechem|Shechem]] that cost the house of David the ten
northern tribes, and for the stripping of [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] by Shishak of Egypt in his fifth
year.

## Biographical Overview

1 Kings 12 places Rehoboam at Shechem, where all Israel had come to make him king. The assembly,
with [[jeroboam|Jeroboam]] at its head, asked him to lighten the heavy yoke of forced labor his
father had imposed. He took three days, heard the elders who had served Solomon counsel
conciliation, rejected them for the young men who had grown up with him, and answered: "My little
finger is thicker than my father's loins… my father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise
you with scorpions." The northern tribes seceded with the cry "What portion have we in David?"
Adoram, the officer over forced labor, was sent out and stoned to death; Rehoboam fled by chariot
to Jerusalem. He mustered Judah and Benjamin for war, but the prophet Shemaiah forbade it: "this
thing is from me."

The text says he reigned seventeen years, was forty-one at accession, and that his mother was
Naamah the Ammonitess. In his fifth year Shishak, king of Egypt, came up against Jerusalem and
carried off the treasures of the temple and the palace, including the gold shields Solomon had
made, which Rehoboam replaced with bronze ones kept by the guard. Kings summarizes his reign as
one in which Judah built high places, pillars, and Asherim, and reports continual war between
Rehoboam and Jeroboam. He was succeeded by his son Abijam (Abijah in Chronicles).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 11:43 – 12:24** — accession; the Shechem assembly; the harsh answer; the secession;
  the stoning of Adoram; Shemaiah's prohibition of civil war.
- **1 Kings 14:21–31** — regnal summary; Judah's high places and cult objects; Shishak's campaign
  and the bronze shields; ongoing war with Jeroboam.
- **2 Chronicles 10–12** — the fuller parallel: the same Shechem scene, then material absent from
  Kings — the fortification of cities, the migration of priests and [[levites|Levites]] southward,
  his wives and sons, and an account of Shishak's invasion in which Shemaiah interprets it as
  judgment for abandoning the law, the princes humble themselves, and destruction is mitigated to
  servitude.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation in Kings** — Rehoboam's reign is judged by cultic criteria: "Judah
  did what was evil in the sight of the LORD." The verdict is the narrator's theological
  assessment and is recorded here as such.
- **The Chronicler** — presents a more graded portrait, with a period of strength ("he walked in
  the way of David and Solomon for three years"), an explicit humbling under Shemaiah's word, and
  the concluding note that "he did evil, for he did not set his heart to seek the LORD."
- **[[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] (NIBC, 1995)**, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|the Kings page]]:
  the division is presented as **deferred and non-final** rather than as a simple political
  accident, with the schism narrative flowing from the Solomonic apostasy of 1 Kings 11.
- No rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic source on Rehoboam has been ingested into this wiki; no
  reception from those traditions is recorded here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Rehoboam is the point at which the Davidic promise contracts to a single tribe and a single city.
The narrative holds two explanations of the schism in the same frame: it is Rehoboam's folly —
counsel taken from the wrong advisers — and it is the fulfillment of Ahijah's oracle against
Solomon's house. Shemaiah's word both forbids reconquest and legitimates the loss. Because the
Davidic line survives in Jerusalem, Rehoboam also marks the beginning of the two-kingdom structure
that governs the rest of Kings, with its alternating regnal formulae.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Kings vs Chronicles [recorded tension].** Chronicles supplies a defense network, a priestly
  migration from the north, a humbling narrative around Shishak, and a partial rehabilitation that
  Kings does not contain; Kings gives a flatter negative summary. Both are preserved; neither is
  adjudicated here.
- **Shishak's campaign.** Egyptian royal inscription commemorating a Levantine campaign under a
  pharaoh conventionally identified with the biblical Shishak is the usual external comparison
  point in discussion of this reign. The identification and the relation between the campaign
  record and the biblical account of a Jerusalem tribute are debated, and this page records the
  relevance of the material without asserting a particular correlation or citing figures.
- **The scale of the united monarchy.** How large a kingdom Rehoboam could have lost is bound up
  with wider dispute over the extent of the Solomonic state; positions vary widely and none is
  endorsed here.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Rehoboam's Shechem scene became a standard locus for reflection on counsel, kingship, and the
limits of royal authority, and — in political readings — on taxation and consent. In critical
scholarship the reign is a key datum in reconstructing the chronology of the divided monarchy,
since Shishak's campaign gives one of the few points where the biblical narrative and Egyptian
material can be discussed together. Cross-links: [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]],
