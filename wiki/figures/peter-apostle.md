# Simon Peter (the Apostle)

Simon Peter is the most prominent of Jesus' twelve apostles, the spokesman and leader of the
apostolic band, a foundational figure of the early church, and the traditional author of the
[[1-peter|First]] and Second Letters of Peter. He is named more often than any other disciple in the
Gospels. This is the wiki's second **figure page** (after [[james-the-just|James the Just]]); like
James, Peter is named in primary texts, is the subject of vast biographical and interpretive
tradition, and is cross-linked to the text he traditionally authored. ([[karen-jobes|Jobes]]'s BECNT
*1 Peter* is the source ingest that occasions this page.)

## Names

He bears an unusual cluster of names. Born **Simon** (Greek *Simōn*; the Semitic *Symeōn*, "Simeon,"
appears in Acts 15:14 and 2 Pet. 1:1). Jesus gave him the Aramaic nickname **Cephas** (*kêphāʾ*,
"rock"), rendered in Greek as **Petros**, "Peter" (Matt. 16:18; John 1:42; the Aramaic survives in
Paul's *Kēphas*, 1 Cor. 1:12; Gal. 2:9). He is also "Simon son of Jonah/John" (Matt. 16:17; John
21:15).

## Biographical Overview

- **Origins**: a fisherman from Bethsaida, later resident in Capernaum (Mark 1:21, 29; John 1:44),
  brother of Andrew, partner of the sons of Zebedee. Married (his mother-in-law is healed, Mark 1:30;
  he later traveled with a believing wife, 1 Cor. 9:5).
- **Call and inner circle**: among the first called (Mark 1:16–18); always listed first among the
  Twelve; with James and John he forms Jesus' "inner three" (the raising of Jairus's daughter, the
  Transfiguration, Gethsemane).
- **Confession and rebuke**: confesses Jesus as "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matt.
  16:16), receiving the "rock"/keys saying (16:17–19); moments later is rebuked as "Satan" (16:23) —
  the characteristic Petrine pattern of bold insight and impulsive failure.
- **Denial and restoration**: despite vowing loyalty, denies Jesus three times (Mark 14:66–72); is
  restored by the risen Christ with a threefold commission to "feed my sheep" (John 21:15–19). He
  receives an early resurrection appearance (1 Cor. 15:5; Luke 24:34).
- **Leader of the early church**: preaches at Pentecost (Acts 2); leads the Jerusalem community;
  opens the gospel to the Gentiles (Cornelius, Acts 10–11); is miraculously freed from Herod
  Agrippa's prison and departs "to another place" (Acts 12:17).
- **Apostle to the circumcised**: at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) he supports the Gentile mission;
  Paul names him a "pillar" entrusted with "the gospel to the circumcised" (Gal. 2:7–9) but rebukes
  him at Antioch for withdrawing from table-fellowship with Gentiles (Gal. 2:11–14).
- **Rome and martyrdom**: strong, early tradition places his death in Rome under Nero (c. AD 64–67),
  crucified (John 21:18–19 is read as a prophecy of this; later tradition adds head-downward).
  [[karen-jobes|Jobes]] revives the (minority) argument that Peter's "other place" (Acts 12:17) was
  an *early* arrival in Rome (c. AD 42) under Claudius — relevant to her dating of 1 Peter and to the
  later tradition of a twenty-five-year Roman episcopate. See [[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter:
  Authorship, Date, and the Colonization Theory]].

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Gospels** — spokesman of the Twelve; the confession at Caesarea Philippi; walking on water;
  the Transfiguration; the denial; the empty-tomb and resurrection narratives; the Johannine
  restoration (John 21).
- **Acts** — the dominant figure of chs. 1–12: Pentecost sermon; healing at the temple gate;
  confrontations with the Sanhedrin; Ananias and Sapphira; Samaria; Cornelius (the Gentile
  breakthrough); the Council (ch. 15).
- **Paul's letters** — Cephas/Peter as a resurrection witness (1 Cor. 15:5), a "pillar" (Gal. 2:9),
  the apostle to the circumcised, and the subject of the Antioch incident (Gal. 2:11–14); a Cephas
  "party" at Corinth (1 Cor. 1:12).
- **1–2 Peter** — the two letters bearing his name; 1 Peter writes from "Babylon" (= Rome, 5:13)
  with Silvanus and Mark; [[2-peter|2 Peter]] presents itself as his second, testamentary letter
  (1:12–15), recalling the transfiguration as eyewitness (1:16–18) and foretelling his imminent death
  (1:14). Its Petrine authorship is the most disputed in the NT — see [[2-peter-authorship|2 Peter:
  Authorship]].

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Roman Catholic**: Peter is the first **pope** and bishop of Rome; Matt. 16:18–19 ("you are Peter,
  and on this rock I will build my church… I will give you the keys") is the scriptural charter of
  papal primacy and apostolic succession. The traditional 25-year Roman episcopate and martyrdom
  under Nero ground the See of Rome.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: honors Peter as chief (*coryphaeus*) of the apostles and co-founder, with
  Paul, of the Roman church, but reads the "rock" as Peter's *faith/confession* rather than as a
  transferable jurisdictional office — rejecting universal papal supremacy.
- **Protestant**: typically reads the "rock" as Peter's confession (or Christ himself), or as Peter
  representatively, denying that Matt. 16 establishes a perpetual papal office. Since the Reformation,
  Protestants have been skeptical of the 25-year-Roman-episcopate tradition precisely because of its
  use to validate the papacy.
- **Critical scholarship**: debates the historicity of the Roman residence/martyrdom (strong but
  late literary attestation; contested archaeology under St. Peter's) and treats 1–2 Peter's
  authorship as an open question (most regard 2 Peter as pseudonymous, and many so regard 1 Peter).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The "rock" of Matthew 16:18** — Peter himself / Peter's confession / Christ — the central
  exegetical battleground between Catholic and Protestant readings.
- **Authorship of 1–2 Peter** — whether the apostle wrote either letter. Jobes defends 1 Peter as
  authentically Petrine (or very close); see [[1-peter-authorship|the controversy page]]. 2 Peter is
  more widely regarded as pseudonymous.
- **Peter in Rome and the date of arrival** — the historicity of the Roman residence and the
  minority "early arrival under Claudius" thesis (Wenham, Thiede, Botermann; revived by Jobes).
- **The Antioch incident (Gal. 2:11–14)** — its bearing on Peter's relationship to Paul, to James,
  and to the Gentile mission. See [[james-the-just|James the Just]] and [[galatians|Galatians]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Peter is the paradigmatic disciple — first to confess, first to fail, restored to lead. In the
Gospels his arc (call → confession → denial → restoration) embodies the pattern of grace; in Acts he
is the Spirit-empowered apostle who unlocks the gospel to Jew (Pentecost) and Gentile (Cornelius)
alike. 

From the NT sources in this batch:
- **France (Matthew/Mark)**: Peter as representative of the disciples in the narrative christology and the Galilee/Jerusalem polarity. His confession (Mark 8/Matt 16) is the watershed, immediately corrected by the first passion prediction and the "Get behind me, Satan" rebuke. His denial (Mark 14) exemplifies human failure before the paradox of the cross; the restoration hints are present but understated.
- **Green (Luke)**: Peter as model of the disciple who is called, fails, and is restored for leadership. His call stories, the confession, the denial, and the post-resurrection rehabilitation (Luke 24; implied in Acts 1-2) illustrate Luke's theme of God's purpose prevailing through human weakness. In Acts, Peter is the bridge figure who opens the mission to Gentiles (Cornelius) while remaining rooted in Jerusalem.
- **Michaels (John)**: Peter as the impetuous disciple who is restored in the lakeside epilogue (John 21) with the threefold commission to "feed my sheep," balancing his threefold denial. The Beloved Disciple is often paired with him, highlighting different models of discipleship.
- **Bock (Acts)**: Peter dominates the first half (chs. 1-12) as the Spirit-filled leader at Pentecost, healer, and spokesperson at the Jerusalem Council. His speeches interpret the death and resurrection of Jesus as the fulfillment that opens salvation to all. God remains the central actor; Peter is the instrument.

In **1 Peter** (Jobes) his own theology emerges: the apostle who denied under pressure now writes to suffering believers about following the rejected, vindicated Christ in patient endurance — the source of the church's identification of Jesus with the Isaiah 53 Suffering Servant.

## France on Peter in Matthew (NICNT Matthew, 2007)

*Added 2026-07-18. All interpretive claims in this section are [[rt-france|France]]'s own
unless otherwise attributed.*

### Peter as more than spokesman

France grants that historically Peter was probably acting as the disciples' spokesman at
Caesarea Philippi, "but by adding vv. 17-19 Matthew has made it clear that he regards Peter as
more than simply a corporate spokesman; it is his personal role in the foundation of the
emergent 'church' which is highlighted" — even though 18:18 repeats the binding/loosing formula
in the plural. He grounds this in the historical record: Peter was "the leading figure in the
postresurrection church and the initiator of the new direction which made the community of
Jesus' followers distinct from its Jewish heritage (Acts 10-11)."

France stresses that Peter's leading role is particularly emphasized in Matthew's central
section (15:15; 16:17-19; 17:24-27; 18:21, all peculiar to Matthew), yet that "he does not
always appear as a model of true discipleship or an example to be followed" (16:22-23;
26:33-35, 69-75).

### The confession (16:13-20)

- France notes this is the only time Matthew gives "Simon Peter" his full title; the fuller name
  sounds formal, befitting a momentous declaration, and prepares for v. 18's explanation of the
  nickname.
- **Do not over-read the confession.** "We must not read into Peter's declaration here all that
  later Christian theology has found in the term 'Messiah.'" For Peter, France argues, the title
  conveys glory and success, not defeat and execution — as vv. 22-23 show. Among most
  first-century Palestinian Jews its primary connotation would be a son of [[david|David]]
  restoring national glory: "a nationalistic term, and one which was hard to separate from the
  political aspirations of a subject people."
- **But it is still the breakthrough.** France credits Peter with moving beyond acclaiming Jesus
  a prophet, even the greatest, to recognizing him as "the one climactic figure in whom God's
  purpose is finally being accomplished. In that he has made the crucial breakthrough."
- On "Son of the living God," France notes it is debated whether this is a distinct addition or
  a synonym of messianic status. His own verdict, given Matthew's emphasis on Son of God
  elsewhere and its reaffirmation at 17:5: "it is more likely that he expected his readers to
  hear it as adding a further dimension to Peter's declaration."
- v. 17 is a beatitude congratulating Peter on insight that was **revealed, not deduced** — "not
  in the sense that Peter has himself puzzled out the truth but that, like all knowledge about
  God and his Son (11:27), it has been revealed to him by God himself." France adds the caution
  that a similar corporate beatitude was already addressed to all the disciples at 13:16-17. He
  identifies Gal 1:16 as a particularly relevant "flesh and blood" parallel.
- On "Bar-Jona," France rejects any deliberate association of Peter with the biblical Jonah
  ("it is Jesus, not Peter, who is the 'greater than Jonah'"), preferring that *Iōna* and
  *Iōannēs* are variant Greek forms of the same Semitic name.
- **On authenticity**, France rejects the view that Matthew simply composed vv. 17-19 as a
  counterbalance to Mark. He allows that vv. 18-19 might have circulated as a freestanding
  tradition, but notes v. 17 requires a prior declaration. His conclusion: "it seems more likely
  that Matthew had a tradition (or memory?) that Jesus did first welcome Peter's statement and
  comment on his special role." He supports this by noting it is typical of Matthew's method to
  insert traditional material within an existing pericope to draw out a particular angle.

### The rock: France holds that the rock is Peter

This is France's clearest and most emphatic position on the passage, and it cuts against the
Protestant readings recorded above under **Tradition-Specific Reception**.

France reads v. 18 as deliberately mirroring v. 16 — "Simon has declared, 'You are the Messiah,'
to which Jesus now responds, 'And I in my turn have a declaration for you: You are Peter'" —
each naming also mentioning the father ("Son of the living God" / "son of Jonah"). Jesus gives
Simon a nickname which, like the OT renamings (Abram/Abraham, Sarai/Sarah, Jacob/Israel),
speaks of his future role. *Petros*, representing Aramaic *Kēphâ*, is "otherwise virtually
unknown as a personal name in the ancient world, which makes it the more probable that Jesus
chose it for Simon with a view to its literal meaning."

He dismantles the **masculine/feminine escape route** (*petros* = small stone vs. *petra* =
bedrock): masculine *petros* occurs infrequently in classical poetic Greek for a stone, the
distinction from *petra* is not consistently observed, and *petros* as a common noun would be
unfamiliar to Matthew's readers, being absent from the LXX (except twice in 2 Maccabees) and
from the NT, where the word for stone is *lithos*. "The Greek reader would therefore see here a
difference in form but not in meaning." And "if Jesus was speaking in Aramaic, there would be
no difference at all, with *kēphâ* occurring in both places"; the different Greek form arises
simply because Peter, as a man, needs a masculine name.

His verdict: "**the flow of the sentence makes it clear that the wordplay is intended to
identify Peter as the rock.**"

He then names and rejects both alternatives:

| Reading | France's response |
|---|---|
| The rock is Peter's **confession**, not Peter | Rejected. France explicitly identifies its motivation — it is "beloved especially by those who wish to refute the claims of the Roman Catholic Church based on the primacy of Peter as the first pope." His verdict: "If that was what Jesus intended, he has chosen his words badly, as the wordplay points decisively toward Peter." |
| The rock is **Jesus himself** | Rejected more sharply as "even more bizarre." He grants it is consonant with other NT foundation-stone language, but "in regard to this passage it is the exegesis of desperation," since so abrupt a change of subject would require a "but" rather than an "and," and some stage direction. |

### France on the papacy

France engages the question briefly and **symmetrically** — he does not use his exegesis to
support papal claims, nor to attack them. He calls all such apologetic rewritings "beside the
point, since there is nothing in this passage about any successors to Peter. It is Simon Peter
himself, in his historical role, who is the foundation rock. **Any link between the personal
role of Peter and the subsequent papacy is a matter of later ecclesiology, not of exegesis of
this passage.**"

He resolves the tension with the NT's other foundation imagery — where the foundation stone is
Jesus (1 Cor 3:10-11; 1 Pet 2:4-8, "if written by Peter himself, particularly telling!"), or
corporately the apostles and prophets with Christ as cornerstone (Eph 2:20), or the twelve
apostles on twelve foundations (Rev 21:14) — as follows: "In principle all the apostles
constituted the foundation, with Jesus as the cornerstone, but as a matter of historical fact
it was on Peter's leadership that the earliest phase of the church's development would depend."
He cites Peter's initiative in bringing in Samaritans (Acts 8:14-25) and Gentiles (Acts
10:1-11:18; 15:7-11), concluding: "By the time James takes over as president of the Jerusalem
church, the foundation has been laid."

### The keys, the gates, and binding/loosing

France's readings here are counterintuitive and load-bearing:

- **The gates of Hades = death**, not demonic assault. "'The gates of Hades' is a metaphor for
  death, which here contrasts strikingly with the phrase 'the living God' in v. 16." He grounds
  this in the OT "gates of death" (Job 38:17; Pss 9:13; 107:18), "gates of Sheol" (Isa 38:10),
  and "bars of Sheol" (Job 17:16), with Hades the NT equivalent of Sheol. The gates represent
  "the imprisoning power of death: death will not be able to imprison and hold the church of
  the living God." He rejects the reading of "some interpreters" that Hades means the demonic
  powers of the underworld, and still more firmly rejects "the romantic imagery, sometimes
  derived from the traditional but incorrect translation 'gates of hell'," of the church as an
  army storming the devil's citadel.
- **The keys are storehouse keys, not gate keys.** Taking up Isa 22:20-22, "Jesus declares Peter
  to be the steward (the chief administrative officer) in the kingdom of heaven," as Eliakim was
  the new steward in the kingdom of David. France stresses the limit built into the image: "The
  steward is not the owner." And he specifies which keys: "The keys are those of the
  storehouses, to enable him to make appropriate provision for the household, not those of the
  outer gate, to control admission." The traditional portrayal of Peter as porter at the pearly
  gates, he argues, depends on misunderstanding "the kingdom of heaven" as the afterlife rather
  than God's rule among his people on earth.
- **Binding and loosing concern things, not people.** France defines the terms from rabbinic
  usage as "declaring what is and is not permitted." At 18:18 the same commission goes to the
  whole disciple group in the context of sin within the community, but in both places "the
  objects of the verbs are neuter — it is things, issues, which are being tied or untied, not
  people as such."
- **The future perfects reverse the sequence.** France argues the future perfect passives ("will
  have been tied up / untied"), used twice in each of 16:19 and 18:18, are "sufficiently unusual
  and indeed awkward in Greek to draw attention" — a simple future passive was available. His
  conclusion: not Peter initiating and heaven ratifying, but Peter's decision being found
  already made in heaven, "making him not the initiator of new directions for the church, but
  the faithful steward of God's prior decisions." The saying is thus "a promise not of divine
  endorsement, but of divine guidance."
- **First among equals.** "Peter's personal authority remained, however, that of the first among
  equals, and the extension of this commission to the rest of the disciples in 18:18 will ensure
  that he is kept in his place."

### Peter on the water (14:22-33)

France notes Peter's attempt occurs **only in Matthew**, and that the symbolism attaches here
rather than to Jesus' own miracle, since Peter is chided for lack of faith. He reads the episode
as "an illustration of the vulnerability of the disciple who allows doubt, the natural human
perspective, to displace the faith which relies on the supernatural power of God" — defining the
verb "doubt" (which recurs only at 28:17) as "not so much a theological uncertainty or unbelief
as a practical hesitation, wavering, being in two minds." Weighing the textual variant and the
"inceptive aorist" proposal that Peter failed from the start, France rejects it and concludes
with most interpreters that Peter's attempt was **initially successful**.

He then **leaves the point of the episode explicitly open**: "It is not so clear, however,
whether Matthew intends us to see Peter in this incident as an example of valid faith which went
wrong, or as from the beginning taking a foolhardy risk." Jesus endorses the request, yet the
failure suggests a cautionary tale rather than a model, and France notes Peter's proposal "might
be regarded as coming rather too close to the 'testing' of God" forbidden in 4:5-7.

### 14:33 and 16:16

France flags a real problem: the disciples' "Truly you are the Son of God" at 14:33 seems
premature and "seems to steal the thunder" of Peter's declaration at 16:16 and its confirmation
at 17:5. His resolution: 14:33 is **instinctive recognition** of Jesus' more-than-human nature
under an overwhelming miracle (cf. the centurion at 27:54), whereas 16:16 is a **deliberate
christological formulation** balancing "Son of God" with the functional identification
"Messiah." He grants it remains remarkable that Son of God language, hitherto confined to God
(3:17), demons (4:3, 6; 8:29), and editorial explanation (2:15), should now be used by the
disciples unprompted — possibly triggered by memory of 11:25-27. He quotes [REPORTED: Davies and
Allison]: "The disciples are beginning to catch up with the readers of the gospel."

### Other Matthean notes

- France observes that Peter, "as distinctive a northerner as his master," is unmasked in the
  high priest's courtyard as a companion of "Jesus the Galilean" (26:69) — part of his
  [[galilee|Galilee]]/[[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] sociopolitical reading.
- On why blasphemy against the Son of Man is forgivable, France offers Peter's denial as the
  illustrative case: "Even Peter, in the heat of the moment, would 'speak against the Son of
  Man' (26:69-75) and still be forgiven."
- France suggests [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]] "was perhaps a prototype of the many"
  — Peter included (16:21-23) — "who would find it hard to accept Jesus' concept of
  Messiahship."
- He calls 16:13-23 "the central turning point in Matthew's narrative, as it is in Mark's."

## France on Peter in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], [[france-mark-nigtc|*The Gospel
of Mark* (NIGTC)]]. All interpretive claims are France's unless another scholar is named.*

### The Petrine connection to Mark's Gospel

France quotes Papias (via Eusebius, *H.E.* 3.39.15) that Mark became Peter's interpreter and
"wrote accurately, though not in order," what he remembered of Peter's teaching, and reports the
consistent early tradition (Papias, Irenaeus, Clement, Tertullian, Origen, Eusebius, Jerome and
others) that Mark derived his material from Peter's preaching in Rome. France judges the strand
of that tradition holding that **Peter was still alive when Mark wrote** (Clement, Origen,
Epiphanius, Jerome, arguably Papias) to have "decidedly the better of it" over Irenaeus's
post-mortem reading — yielding a date "not later than the early sixties" (Hengel, he notes,
prefers Irenaeus and A.D. 69). He endorses Vincent Taylor's judgment that many of Mark's ~200
elements peculiar to Mark in chs. 1–6 look like eyewitness details from Peter, and suggests the
vivid Marcan style "may well derive as much from the way Peter used to tell them as from Mark's
own skill as a raconteur." He also notes Mark's early neglect (thin patristic citation) and that
"had it not been for its prestige as the gospel deriving from Peter it might well have fallen
out of use altogether." His own position remains cautious: the Petrine connection is the only
origin question likely to bear on exegesis, "and even that only occasionally." On [[mark|Mark]]'s
double cockcrow (unique to Mark), France's simplest explanation is Petrine reminiscence: "Mark
preserves the account in its fullest and most detailed form (as Peter himself would have
remembered and repeated it)," harmonised away in the other gospels.

### Insight and obtuseness

France portrays Mark's Peter as "a tantalising mixture of special insight (8:29) and
extraordinary obtuseness (8:32-33; 9:5-6)." Key readings:

- **The confession (8:29)** creates the widely accepted narrative watershed, but in Mark
  (contrast Mt 16:17-19) "we are to understand Peter to be more a spokesman for the group than
  the originator of a purely personal insight"; Peter recurs as group representative at 9:5;
  10:28; 11:21; 14:37. France also holds the two-stage healing at Bethsaida (8:22-26)
  symbolises the disciples' gradual cure of spiritual blindness — Peter has "'seen', but not
  yet clearly."
- **"Get behind me, Satan" (8:33)** is more than abuse: Peter's "human thoughts," opposing
  God's revealed purpose, mean "Peter and those who agree with him are acting as spokesmen of
  God's ultimate enemy" — the triple ἐπιτιμάω (vv. 30, 32, 33) marking "a serious confrontation
  of incompatible ideologies." France links this back to the prologue's supernatural-conflict
  tableau (1:12-13), where Satan's opposition resurfaces at 8:33.
- **The transfiguration proposal (9:5-6)** is read sympathetically: "Peter is simply doing the
  best he can to rise to the occasion," not an attempt to institutionalise the moment; the
  divine voice exposes the inappropriateness of ranking Elijah and Moses with Jesus.
- **10:31** ("first last, last first") is partly "a gentle rebuke to Peter's implied smugness"
  in 10:28.

### The denial (14:66-72) — including the cursing of Jesus

France reads Mark's spotlight-alternation between the hall (Jesus) and the courtyard (Peter) in
14:53–15:1 as "a study in witnessing under pressure, in how to do it and how not to do it":
"whereas Jesus both in his silence and in his final dramatic utterance will stand firm, Peter
will crumble" — escaping "at the cost of his integrity as a disciple." The predicted threefold
denial is "not merely weakness but apostasy" — deliberate dissociation. The three denials are
incremental (private contradiction, public denial, then oath), with the accusers pressing
Peter's Galilean identity amid Judaean contempt for Galileans.

France's most striking claim concerns ἀναθεματίζειν in 14:71: being transitive, with Jesus as
the natural unstated object, it most probably means **Peter voluntarily cursed Jesus** — "Mark
portrays Peter as voluntarily doing what Pliny was later informed that 'real Christians' could
not be compelled to do (Pliny, *Ep.* 10.96.5), cursing Jesus" — a sense France notes Christian
translators (RSV, NIV) have found unwelcome. The puzzling ἐπιβαλὼν ἔκλαιεν (14:72) he leaves
lexically unresolved ("Mark's choice of idiom must remain obscure") while affirming the sense of
remorse. Yet he distinguishes Peter's failure from Judas's: the weeping marks "failure under
pressure rather than a deliberate change of allegiance," leaving "the prospect of forgiveness
and rehabilitation."

### "And Peter" (16:7)

France reads the specific inclusion of "and Peter" in the angel's message as answering Peter's
public failure: "even after the curse at the second cockcrow, Peter has not been written off" —
the message implying "an assurance of forgiveness and restoration, the more impressive for
being left unsaid."

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are
Bock's or those of scholars he names. Bock reads Acts through grammatical-historical evangelical
exegesis and Luke-Acts unity, and repeatedly defends the historicity of Peter's ministry against
the skepticism of Dibelius, Haenchen, and Conzelmann.*

Bock makes Peter the dominant human figure of Acts 1–12 and the model witness — though he insists
throughout that "the key character in Acts is God," with Peter as the Spirit-empowered instrument.
Peter leads the reconstitution of the Twelve, proposing Matthias's selection by lot as the last
casting of lots before the Spirit comes (1:15–26); Bock reads the restored Twelve as leaders of
"eschatological Israel." At **Pentecost** Peter preaches the first missionary speech (2:14–36),
which Bock calls "one of the most important theological declarations in the NT": stringing Joel
2:28–32, Ps. 16, and Ps. 110:1 by *gezerah shewa* link-words and "this-is-that" pesher form, Peter
argues a *high* Christology — Jesus made "both Lord and Christ" (2:36) — that Bock insists is early
and resurrection-based, **not** a late Hellenistic or adoptionist development (the resurrection
"made evident what Jesus always was").

Peter's ministry then unfolds as a series of confrontations Bock treats as escalating persecution.
He heals the lame man at the Beautiful Gate and preaches the christologically rich temple sermon
(3:11–26; Servant, Holy and Righteous One, Author/Prince of life, prophet-like-Moses); before the
Sanhedrin, "filled with the Holy Spirit," he declares the exclusivity of salvation — "there is
salvation in no one else… no other name under heaven" (4:12) — which Bock (with Witherington) reads
as no endorsement of religious pluralism. In the **Ananias and Sapphira** episode (5:1–11) Peter
functions as a prophet reading the heart; Bock stresses that God, not Peter, is the actor and that
"lying to the Holy Spirit" is lying to God, so the passage is no "magical" power display. Peter's
Sanhedrin defense (5:29–32, "we must obey God rather than men"; Jesus exalted as Leader/*archēgos*
and Savior/*sōtēr*) and his miraculous prison escape by an angel while the church prays (12:6–17)
round out his Jerusalem career.

The **Cornelius** episode (10:1–11:18) is, for Bock, "the book's turning point": God — not any
decision of the Twelve — directs the gospel to a Gentile household, and the sheet-vision means that
"what God has cleansed" (food, and so table fellowship with Gentiles) is clean, so "God shows no
partiality" (10:34). Peter's insight into the food/fellowship link is, Bock argues, the only way to
make sense of his later "lapse" at Antioch (Gal. 2:11–14), which "he recognized when it was pointed
out to him." At the **Jerusalem Consultation** Peter delivers the first speech (15:7–11), recounting
Cornelius a third time and confessing salvation "through the grace of the Lord Jesus" apart from the
"yoke" of the law — which Bock notes is **his last appearance in Acts**, after which the narrative
turns to Paul. Against Barrett's Tübingen-flavored claim that this "sounds too Pauline" to be
Peter's real view, Bock argues it reflects an inability the law fosters (cf. Rom. 7), not a
repudiation of the law as law.
