# Muhammad

**Muhammad ibn Abdallah** (c. 570 – 632 CE) is the prophet of [[islam|Islam]], believed by Muslims to
be the final messenger (*rasul*) of God and the **Seal of the Prophets** (*khatam al-nabiyyin*),
through whom the [[quran|Qur'an]] was revealed. He is at once the recipient of revelation and — through
his *sunna* (normative example) recorded in **hadith** — the second source of Islamic law and the most
imitated human being in the tradition. Islamic piety distinguishes sharply between the Qur'an (God's
verbatim word) and Muhammad's own words and deeds: he is venerated as a man, not divinized.

## Biographical Overview (Traditional / Sira)

The biographical portrait derives chiefly from the Qur'an, the *hadith*, and the *sira* literature
(notably Ibn Ishaq, via Ibn Hisham, and al-Waqidi) — sources composed generations after his death,
which historical-critical scholarship treats cautiously (see below).

- **Origins**: born in **Mecca** c. 570 into the **Banu Hashim** clan of the **Quraysh** —
  a clan of declining fortune, eclipsed by the Banu Umayya. Orphaned young (a posthumous child whose
  grandfather then died), he was raised by his uncle **Abu Talib** and excluded by custom from
  paternal inheritance.
- **Merchant and marriage**: he traded on Syrian caravan routes and entered the employ of the widow
  merchant **Khadija**, who proposed marriage to him (he c. 25, she c. 40). The marriage was
  monogamous until her death and central to his early life.
- **First revelation (610)**: while meditating in a cave on **Mount Hira** during Ramadan, he
  received the command *Iqra!* ("Recite!") and the first verses of Q 96 ("Recite in the name of your
  Lord who created…"), traditionally delivered by the angel **Gabriel (Jibril)**. **Khadija** became
  the first to believe — "the first Muslim" — followed by his young cousin **Ali**. A period of
  daunting silence (*fatra*) followed before revelations resumed.
- **Meccan mission (610–622)**: a decade of preaching strict monotheism (*tawhid*) and the coming
  Judgment against Quraysh polytheism, met with mounting hostility; the "Year of Sorrow" (c. 619) saw
  the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib. To this period tradition assigns the **Night Journey and
  Ascension** (*Isra* and *Mi'raj*, Q 17:1) — from Mecca to Jerusalem to the heavens and back in a
  single night — later memorialized by the [[jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]].
- **The Hijra (622)**: emigration to **Medina** (Yathrib), the event that begins the
  Islamic lunar calendar (AH) and turns the movement from a persecuted sect into a polity. The
  "Constitution of Medina" organized a community of believers alongside the city's tribes,
  including its Jewish clans.
- **Medinan period (622–632)**: revelations now longer, communal, legal, and military; armed
  conflict with Mecca (Badr 624, Uhud 625, the Trench 627), the expulsion/killing of Medinan Jewish
  groups, treaties (Hudaybiyya 628), and the bloodless conquest of **Mecca (630)**, with the cleansing
  of the **Ka'ba** of idols.
- **Death (632)**: he died in Medina after a final pilgrimage, leaving no surviving son and no
  undisputed succession arrangement — the seed of the [[sunni-islam|Sunni]]/[[shia-islam|Shia]] split.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **The Qur'an** addresses and describes him as warner, herald, and messenger (e.g. Q 17:105–6;
  33:40, the "Seal of the Prophets"); it insists he works no miracle except the Qur'an itself
  (its *i'jaz*, inimitability). The "asbab al-nuzul" (occasions of revelation) tie many passages to
  events in his life.
- **Hadith / [[sahih-al-bukhari|Sahih al-Bukhari]]** and the other collections preserve his *sunna* —
  the basis of law and the most detailed source for his biography.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

- **Seal of the Prophets**: the last and confirming prophet in the Abrahamic line, restoring the pure
  monotheism of **[[abraham|Abraham]]** (whom the Qur'an makes the builder, with Ishmael, of the
  Ka'ba) after the perceived corruptions of earlier communities. [[al-tabari|al-Tabari]] reads
  Muhammad as the successor and confirmation of Abraham.
- **The "beautiful model"** (*uswa hasana*, Q 33:21): his conduct is normative, generating the entire
  edifice of *sunna* and *fiqh*.
- **In Sufism**, his inner reality is elevated to the **Muhammadan Reality** (*al-haqiqa
  al-Muhammadiyya*) and identified with the [[al-insan-al-kamil|Perfect Man]] — see [[ibn-arabi|Ibn
  Arabi]]. Shi'ism reads the "light" verses as joining the Prophet and the Imams in a single divine
  light (see [[jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]]).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Source problem**: the earliest *sira* (Ibn Ishaq) postdates Muhammad by ~120 years and survives
  only in later recension; the hadith corpus was sifted for authenticity by Muslim scholars
  (*isnad* criticism) and is treated skeptically by much Western scholarship. The standard mid-century
  critical biography is **[[william-montgomery-watt|W. Montgomery Watt]]**'s *Muhammad at Mecca* /
  *Muhammad at Medina*, which reads his career in socio-economic terms while accepting his sincerity;
  revisionist scholars (Wansbrough, Crone & Cook's *Hagarism*) have questioned the standard narrative;
  most historians accept a core (a 7th-century Arabian prophet, the Qur'an, the move to Medina) while
  disputing details.
- **Western/Christian reception**: medieval Christendom cast him as impostor or heresiarch — the
  "pseudo-prophet" of **[[robert-of-ketton|Robert of Ketton]]**'s Latin Qur'an title; Dante placed
  him among the schismatics. This polemical portrait is itself a major theme of Qur'an reception
  history (see [[lawrence-quran-a-biography|Lawrence]]).
- **The "Satanic Verses"**: the disputed tradition that Muhammad briefly conceded the intercession of
  three Meccan goddesses before the verses were abrogated (Q 53; cf. 22:52) — accepted by some early
  Muslim historians, rejected by most later tradition.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

As the human locus of revelation, Muhammad anchors all Islamic exegesis: *tafsir bi'l-ma'thur*
proceeds from the Qur'an to *his* explanatory *sunna* ([[al-tabari|al-Tabari]]); the occasions-of-
revelation genre reconstructs his biography to fix meaning; Sufi and Shi'ite exegesis elevate his
inner reality. He is also the central figure of the textual-history debates that occupy modern
Quranic studies (the collection of the Qur'an after his death; see [[quran|the Qur'an]]).
