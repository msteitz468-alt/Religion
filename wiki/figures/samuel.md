Samuel is the last of the judges, the first great prophet after Moses, the priest who anoints both Saul and David, and the kingmaker whose life spans the transition from charismatic leadership to hereditary monarchy.

## Biographical Overview

Born to the barren Hannah and her husband Elkanah (a Levite or Ephraimite with ties to the shrine at Shiloh) after Hannah's vow and fervent prayer (1 Sam 1). Dedicated as a Nazirite from birth, he is brought to Eli the priest at Shiloh as a child and ministers there. The LORD calls him in the night (three times); Eli recognizes it is the LORD and instructs him to respond "Speak, LORD, for your servant hears" (3:1–10). The first oracle is one of judgment on Eli's house for the sins of his sons Hophni and Phinehas. Samuel grows, and "the LORD was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground" (3:19); all Israel knows he is established as a prophet.

After the ark is captured and returned (the Ark Narrative, 4:1–7:1), Samuel calls Israel to repentance at Mizpah, offers sacrifice, and the LORD thunders against the Philistines, giving Israel victory (7:2–17). He judges Israel all his days, traveling a circuit (Bethel, Gilgal, Mizpah) and building an altar at Ramah.

In old age, his sons Joel and Abijah prove corrupt as judges; the elders demand a king "like all the nations" (8:1–5). Samuel is displeased but is told by YHWH that the people have rejected YHWH as king. He warns them of the ways of kings (the "manner of the king," 8:11–18) but anoints Saul (the tallest man, from the smallest tribe) after a donkey-search leads Saul to him (9–10). Saul is publicly chosen by lot and confirmed by victory over the Ammonites (11). Samuel gives a farewell address at Gilgal, reminding Israel of YHWH's righteous acts and warning that obedience is better than sacrifice (12).

Saul's first rejection (ch. 13: offering sacrifice without waiting for Samuel) and second (ch. 15: sparing Agag and the best livestock of the Amalekites against the *ḥerem* command) lead to Samuel's declaration that the kingdom is torn from Saul. Samuel anoints David secretly at Bethlehem (16:1–13), the youngest of Jesse's sons, after the older brothers are passed over ("man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart"). David later flees to Samuel at Naioth in Ramah; Saul's pursuit ends in Saul prophesying among the prophets (19:18–24).

Samuel dies and is buried at Ramah (25:1). Saul, in desperation before the battle of Gilboa, seeks a medium at Endor and has Samuel's spirit raised; Samuel reiterates the rejection and predicts Saul's death and the transfer of the kingdom to David (28:3–25). David later honors Samuel's legacy in his lament for Saul and Jonathan (2 Sam 1) and in the "last words" and administrative lists that close 2 Samuel.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Samuel 1–3**: Birth, dedication, call, first oracle against Eli.
- **1 Samuel 4–7**: Ark Narrative; Samuel's judgeship and victory at Mizpah.
- **1 Samuel 8–12**: Demand for a king; anointing and public confirmation of Saul; Samuel's farewell address.
- **1 Samuel 13, 15**: Saul's rejections; Samuel's verdicts.
- **1 Samuel 16**: Anointing of David.
- **1 Samuel 19, 25, 28**: Samuel as refuge for David; death and burial; spirit raised at Endor.
- **2 Samuel 1**: David's elegy references the era.
- **Acts 3:24; 13:20; Hebrews 11:32**: NT references to Samuel as prophet and in the hall of faith.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Samuel is the great prophet-judge who bridges the periods, the anointer of kings, and the intercessor whose prayers are powerful (cf. Ps 99:6; Jer 15:1). Rabbinic tradition praises his humility, his circuit-riding justice, and his role in establishing the monarchy while subordinating it to the prophetic word. The Endor episode is discussed in debates about necromancy and the reality of the spirit's appearance.

**Christianity**: Samuel is the model prophet who hears and speaks the word of God, the kingmaker who subjects human kings to divine will, and a type of John the Baptist (voice in the wilderness, anointer of the true king). His call ("Speak, LORD") is a paradigm for prophetic obedience and for Christian discernment. The "obedience is better than sacrifice" (15:22) is a key text for prophetic critique of ritual without ethics. In Hebrews 11 he is among those who "conquered kingdoms, enforced justice, obtained promises." The Endor story raises questions about the intermediate state and divine permission. Typologically, Samuel points to Christ as the true prophet-priest-king who perfectly obeys the Father.

**Islamic**: Shamwil (Samuel) is a prophet who anoints Talut (Saul) and Dawud (David); the Qur'an (2:246–251) recounts the demand for a king and the selection of Saul.

**Critical**: Samuel is central to debates about the Deuteronomistic History (Noth saw the anti-monarchic strand as Dtr). Tsumura and others defend substantial early material and Samuel's historical role as a transitional figure. The Endor episode is studied for views of the afterlife in early Israel.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Samuel embodies the principle that in Israel the king is subject to the prophet through whom God conveys his word. His life is the hinge of the Former Prophets: he completes the judges era (circuit, intercession, victory at Mizpah) and inaugurates the monarchy under strict conditions. The two rejections of Saul (ch. 13 and 15) are structured around the prophetic word and the demand for immediate obedience. The anointing of David ("the LORD looks on the heart") enacts the reversal theme from Hannah's Song. Providence works through ordinary events (Hannah's barrenness and vow, Saul's lost donkeys, David's errand to his brothers). Samuel's intercession (even after death in the Endor scene) shows that the prophetic word continues to shape history. In the larger canon he is the one who "begins to save Israel" in a new way, preparing for the Davidic covenant and the Messianic king who will perfectly obey.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Date and sources**: Tsumura defends early (United Monarchy) composition of major blocks, with final editing no later than late 10th century BCE, based on archaic vocabulary, MT priority, and ANE parallels. Critical views see heavy Deuteronomistic redaction in the 6th century.
- **Endor**: Did Samuel's spirit actually appear, or was it a demonic impersonation or psychological phenomenon? Traditional Jewish and Christian readings affirm a real appearance permitted by God; the text says "Samuel said..."
- **Monarchy**: Is Samuel anti-monarchic (the "manner of the king" warning) or pro-Davidic (the grant of a king as accommodation leading to the unconditional covenant)? The text holds tension.
- **Eli's house and Samuel's sons**: The pattern of corrupt sons of good fathers (Eli → Samuel) underscores the need for a different kind of leadership.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Tsumura (NICOT) emphasizes Samuel's role in subordinating kingship to the prophetic word, the reversal pattern, providence in the ordinary, and the defense of the MT against eclectic emendation. The figure is pivotal in all readings of the Deuteronomistic History and in Christian theology of prophecy and kingship (Christ as the true anointed one who obeys). The call narrative and "obedience better than sacrifice" are staples of preaching on hearing God's voice and prophetic ethics. The Endor story influences discussions of the afterlife, spiritualism, and divine sovereignty over the dead. In Jewish tradition Samuel is the model of the righteous judge and intercessor.
