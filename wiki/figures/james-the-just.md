# James the Just (James the Brother of the Lord)

James the Just is the traditionally identified author of the [[james|Letter of James]] and the
preeminent leader of the early Jerusalem church. He is one of at least four men named James
(Greek *Iakōbos*, the OT name Jacob) in the NT, and must be distinguished from James the son of
Zebedee (executed by Herod Agrippa I c. AD 44, Acts 12:2) and James the son of Alphaeus (an
obscure member of the Twelve). [[douglas-moo|Moo]] (PNTC James, 2000) defends the traditional
identification of the author with this James against the now-dominant pseudepigraphal hypothesis.
See [[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]].

This is also the wiki's first **figure page** in the sense defined by the project: James is named
in primary texts, is the subject of substantial interpretive and biographical tradition, and is
distinct enough from purely doctrinal categories to warrant biographical treatment. Because he is
*also* (traditionally) a letter-writer, he is cross-linked between this figure page and his role in
the [[james|text page]] (the wiki has no separate commentator page for him, since the wiki's
commentator pages cover modern interpreters).

## Biographical Overview

**Family**: James is named among the brothers of Jesus (with Joses/Joseph, Judas/Jude, and Simon)
in Matt. 13:55 and Mark 6:3. The exact physical relationship is itself a matter of long
controversy, driven by the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity (see below).

**Estrangement, then conversion**: The Gospels indicate that Jesus' brothers did not believe in
him during his ministry (John 7:5; cf. Mark 3:21, 31–35, where Jesus contrasts his physical
family with his "true family" who do God's will). Moo stresses that James's physical kinship
"never spilled over into a spiritual relationship" during Jesus' lifetime. James's conversion is
linked to a resurrection appearance specifically to him (1 Cor. 15:7).

**Leadership of the Jerusalem church**: After the resurrection James rose rapidly to a position of
authority. Peter sends word to James on his escape from prison (Acts 12:17); James presides at and
delivers the decisive verdict at the Apostolic Council (Acts 15:13–21); Paul names him first among
the "pillars" (Gal. 2:9) and reports James's role in both the Antioch incident (Gal. 2:12) and
Paul's final Jerusalem visit (Acts 21:18). Paul calls him an apostle (Gal. 1:19). His authority
rested, Moo argues, on his spiritual standing and resurrection commission — *not* on his physical
kinship to Jesus, which Acts never mentions.

**Martyrdom (AD 62)**: Both Christian tradition (Hegesippus, preserved in Eusebius, *History* 2.23)
and the Jewish historian **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 20.200–201) record his death. Josephus places
it in AD 62, when the high priest Ananus had James stoned during the interregnum between Roman
governors — a fixed point that constrains the dating of the letter (it must precede 62 if James
wrote it). Hegesippus elaborates a martyrdom narrative (thrown from the temple, stoned, clubbed)
and reports the epithet "the Just/Righteous" (*ho dikaios*) and a reputation for prayer and
temple-piety.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Matt. 13:55 / Mark 6:3** — named as a brother of Jesus; basis for the kinship question.
- **John 7:5** — the brothers' unbelief during the ministry.
- **1 Cor. 15:7** — the resurrection appearance "to James," tied to his conversion and apostleship.
- **Acts 12:17; 15:13–21; 21:18** — his Jerusalem leadership; the Council speech (citing Amos
  9:11–12 and proposing the "apostolic decree"); the request that Paul fund and join a purification
  vow to demonstrate Torah-loyalty.
- **Gal. 1:19; 2:9, 12** — Paul's witness: James as apostle and "pillar"; the men "from James" whose
  arrival at Antioch prompted Peter's withdrawal from table-fellowship with Gentiles.
- **Jas. 1:1** — the self-identification "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" —
  notably *not* "brother of the Lord," which Moo takes as evidence (against pseudepigraphy) that the
  real James, whose authority was spiritual, would not trade on the kinship.

## The Kinship Question (Three Positions)

How is James physically related to Jesus? The debate is bound up with Mariology:

- **Helvidian** (Helvidius; most Protestants): James is a *younger* full/half-brother, son of
  Joseph and Mary born after Jesus. The natural reading of *adelphos* ("brother") and the close
  association of Mary with the brothers (Mark 3:31; 6:3) favor this. Incompatible with Mary's
  perpetual virginity.
- **Epiphanian** (Epiphanius; Eastern Orthodox): James is an *older* step-brother, son of Joseph by
  a prior marriage. Preserves Mary's perpetual virginity. Bauckham inclines slightly here.
- **Hieronymian** (Jerome; Roman Catholic): *adelphos* means "cousin"; James is Jesus' cousin.
  Moo's objection: there is no NT evidence that *adelphos* can mean "cousin"; the word requires at
  least one shared blood parent.

Moo and Bauckham both note the issue cannot be settled with certainty; Bauckham declines to decide
between Epiphanian and Helvidian.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Pauline / NT**: James as a "pillar" of the Jerusalem church and a partner-and-sometimes-foil to
  Paul (the Antioch incident; the Council compromise). The relationship is the historical hinge of
  the James/Paul justification question. See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on
  Justification]].
- **Jewish-Christian legend**: Later Jewish-Christian (Ebionite) and apocryphal sources (Hegesippus;
  the *Pseudo-Clementines*; the Gospel of the Hebrews) made James the supreme apostle and a
  paragon of Torah-piety — even a Nazirite-like temple ascetic. Moo regards this portrait as
  **tendentious legend**, sources "seeking to capture James for their own radically Jewish-Christian
  agenda," with no reliable basis. The historical James was a devout Jewish Christian loyal to Torah
  and concerned to maintain ties with Judaism — but not the "hidebound" zealot of the legend.
- **Catholic / Orthodox**: venerated as the first bishop of Jerusalem; the kinship question shaped
  by perpetual-virginity dogma (Hieronymian in the West, Epiphanian in the East). The Liturgy of
  St. James is attributed to him.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Did the real James write the letter?** The major modern dispute. Moo argues yes (early date,
  middle 40s); the critical consensus argues for a later pseudepigraphon. See
  [[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]].
- **The "men from James" (Gal. 2:12)** — did James authorize the delegation that triggered the
  Antioch split, or did they merely claim his name? Moo thinks the former is more likely: James,
  situated in Jerusalem amid rising Zealot pressure, wanted Jewish Christians to keep observing
  Torah even in mixed communities, partly to protect the Jewish mission.
- **The legendary "torah-fanatic"** — the reliability of Hegesippus's portrait (see above).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

James embodies the **Jewish-Christian** wing of the apostolic church: continuity with Israel,
Torah-loyalty, and the centrality of practical obedience. In the canonical balance he provides, on
Moo's reading, a necessary "counterweight" to a one-sided reading of Paul — the insistence that
genuine faith is a "faith that works." His Council speech (Acts 15) models the early church's
resolution of the Gentile question, and his letter models a wisdom-inflected, Jesus-saturated
ethic of *spiritual wholeness*.

From the NT sources in this batch (Gospels + Acts): In the Gospels he is one of the brothers who did not believe during the ministry (John 7:5; Mark 3, 6) but is listed among those who receive a resurrection appearance (1 Cor. 15:7, though not narrated in the four Gospels). In Acts he emerges as the leader who presides at the Council and proposes the decree that allows Gentile inclusion while respecting Jewish sensitivities. His authority is spiritual (resurrection commission and Torah piety) rather than merely kinship-based. The "men from James" (Gal. 2:12) show the pressures on the Jerusalem church. 

**Influence from this batch**: The Gospels portray the family's initial unbelief and later presence in the community (Acts 1:14); Acts shows James as the stabilizing pillar who facilitates the church's expansion while maintaining continuity with Judaism. This complements the Letter of James (Moo) and provides the historical context for the James/Paul tension.
