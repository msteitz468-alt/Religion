Joash of Judah — the infant hidden in the temple from [[athaliah|Athaliah]], crowned at seven by the
priest [[jehoiada|Jehoiada]], and remembered for the repair of the temple. **He is not the same
person as Joash (Jehoash) king of Israel**, the northern king son of Jehoahaz and grandson of
[[jehu|Jehu]], who reigned at Samaria in the same general period and appears in the Elisha
death-bed episode and the war with Amaziah; the two share a name and overlap in time, and the
biblical text uses both the long and short forms for each. This page is the **Judahite** Joash, son
of Ahaziah. His reign is the wiki's sharpest Kings / Chronicles divergence: 2 Kings gives a largely
approving account, and 2 Chronicles adds an apostasy after Jehoiada's death culminating in the
stoning of Jehoiada's son Zechariah in the temple court.

## Biographical Overview

When Athaliah destroyed the royal seed of Judah after her son Ahaziah's death, the infant Joash was
taken by Jehosheba and hidden with his nurse in the house of YHWH for six years. In the seventh year
Jehoiada brought him out, crowned and anointed him, and had Athaliah executed at the horses'
entrance to the palace. Joash was seven years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty
years in [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]].

2 Kings 12 evaluates him positively — he did what was right in the eyes of YHWH all his days,
"because Jehoiada the priest instructed him" — with the standard qualification that the high places
were not removed and the people still sacrificed there. The centerpiece of the account is the temple
repair: Joash orders the priests to take the money brought in and repair the breaches of the house.
When by the twenty-third year the repairs are still not done, he calls Jehoiada and the priests to
account, and the arrangement is changed — the priests no longer take money from the people or repair
the house, and a chest with a bored lid is set beside the altar, its contents counted and paid
directly to the workmen, from whom no accounting is required because they deal honestly. Guilt and
sin money is reserved to the priests.

The reign ends badly in both accounts. Hazael of Aram comes up against Gath and turns toward
Jerusalem, and Joash takes the dedicated things of his fathers and his own dedicated things and the
gold of the temple and palace treasuries and sends them to Hazael, who withdraws. Joash is then
killed by his own servants in a conspiracy, struck down at the house of Millo, and buried in the
city of David; his son Amaziah succeeds him.

2 Chronicles 24 covers the same reign and adds the material that makes it a contested case (below).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Kings 11** — the rescue, the six-year concealment, the coronation at seven, the covenant, the
  fall of Athaliah.
- **2 Kings 12** — the positive evaluation "all his days, because Jehoiada the priest instructed
  him"; the high places not removed; the failure and reform of the repair fund; the chest by the
  altar; the tribute to Hazael; the assassination by his servants.
- **2 Chronicles 22:10–12** — the parallel rescue account; Jehoshabeath as Jehoiada's wife.
- **2 Chronicles 23** — the parallel coup, with priests and Levites foregrounded.
- **2 Chronicles 24:1–16** — "Joash did what was right… all the days of Jehoiada the priest"; the
  Levitical repair levy; Jehoiada's death and burial among the kings.
- **2 Chronicles 24:17–27** — the princes of Judah, the abandonment of the temple for Asherim and
  idols, the prophets sent and unheeded, the Spirit clothing Zechariah son of Jehoiada, his stoning
  in the court of the house of YHWH by the king's command, his dying words, the Aramean invasion,
  Joash's severe illness, and his murder in his bed by servants avenging the sons of Jehoiada, with
  burial in the city of David but not in the tombs of the kings.
- **Matthew 23:35 // Luke 11:51** — the blood of "Zechariah son of Berechiah" (Matthew; Luke gives
  no patronymic) murdered between the sanctuary and the altar. See Controversies.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **[[iain-provan|Provan]] (NIBC Kings, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] and the source page)**
  — Joash's survival of Athaliah is one of Provan's instances of **salvation within judgment**,
  recorded on the wiki's Kings page as "David" surviving "Jezebel." Provan is also recorded there as
  reading the exiled Jehoiachin at Babylon's table as a "Joash-like hope that grace may still
  triumph over law" — i.e. the hidden-heir pattern is a template Provan uses for the end of the
  book.
- **[[peter-leithart|Leithart]] (BTCB Chronicles, as recorded on [[1-2-chronicles|1–2 Chronicles]])**
  — Joash is one of the Chronicler's **faithful-early / faithless-late** kings, alongside Asa: he
  "restores the temple under the priest Jehoiada but apostatizes after Jehoiada's death and murders
  the prophet Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the temple court ('May YHWH see and avenge')." Leithart's
  comment on the pattern: "We are back in the age of the judges." He is worked through the
  Chronicler's retribution engine — those who seek find, those who forsake are abandoned.
- No rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic treatment of Joash has been ingested into this wiki; none is
  attributed here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Joash is the point at which the Davidic promise is carried by a single concealed child, and the
point at which a restored king's fidelity is explicitly tied to a priest's tutelage. In the
Chronicler's shape the reign becomes the book's clearest statement that early faithfulness confers
no immunity: the same king who reorganizes the temple funds later strips the temple's dedicated
things and kills a prophet in its court. The murdered Zechariah's dying words — "May YHWH see and
avenge" — are, within the Chronicler's account, the hinge on which the reign's collapse turns: the
Aramean campaign and the king's own murder follow directly.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**[CONTRADICTION] Kings vs Chronicles on the later reign.** Both positions are recorded in their
strongest form, attributed, and not adjudicated here.

- **Position A — 2 Kings 12.** Joash did what was right in the eyes of YHWH *all his days*, because
  Jehoiada the priest instructed him; the only qualification the narrator enters is the standard one
  that the high places were not removed. His reign's failures in this account are the delayed temple
  repairs, corrected by his own intervention, and the tribute paid to Hazael from the temple
  treasury. Kings knows of the conspiracy that kills him but assigns it no stated motive and reports
  no apostasy, no prophet-killing, and no reversal after Jehoiada's death — Jehoiada's death is not
  narrated in Kings at all.
- **Position B — 2 Chronicles 24.** Joash did what was right *all the days of Jehoiada the priest*.
  After Jehoiada's death the princes of Judah paid homage to the king, and he listened to them; they
  abandoned the house of YHWH and served the Asherim and idols, and wrath came on Judah. Prophets
  were sent and not heeded. The Spirit of God clothed Zechariah son of Jehoiada, who stood above the
  people and charged them with forsaking YHWH; they conspired against him and stoned him **at the
  king's command** in the court of the house of YHWH. Joash did not remember the kindness Jehoiada
  had shown him but killed his son, who said as he died, "May YHWH see and avenge." Within the year
  an Aramean force came, executed judgment on Joash, and left him severely wounded and ill, and his
  own servants conspired against him **because of the blood of the sons of Jehoiada the priest** and
  killed him on his bed. He was buried in the city of David but not in the tombs of the kings.

The two accounts are not harmonized here. Recorded points of tension: the scope of "all his days"
versus "all the days of Jehoiada"; the presence or absence of an apostasy phase; the stated motive
for the assassination; and the burial notice, which Chronicles qualifies and Kings does not.
`dispute_type`: this is a source-divergence within the canon touching both interpretive and
historical questions; readers who take Chronicles as supplementing Kings and readers who take the
Chronicler's addition as theological construction both hold live positions.

**The identification of Zechariah in the Gospels [attributed, disputed].** Matthew 23:35 and
Luke 11:51 refer to a Zechariah murdered between the sanctuary and the altar, whose blood closes a
sequence beginning with Abel. Matthew names him "son of Berechiah," which is the patronymic of the
prophet Zechariah of the book bearing that name, not of Jehoiada's son; Luke gives no patronymic.
Some readers identify the Gospel figure with Zechariah son of Jehoiada — commonly on the grounds
that Chronicles stands last in the Jewish canonical order, making the Abel-to-Zechariah span a
first-to-last-murder formula — and others take the Matthean patronymic at face value or treat it as
a textual or scribal matter. This wiki records the reference and the dispute; it does **not** assert
the identification.

**Other controversies.** The chronology of the reign depends on the contested synchronisms of the
divided monarchy. The Kings and Chronicles accounts of the repair fund differ in mechanism (chest by
the altar; Levitical levy). And the relation between the Hazael tribute in Kings and the Aramean
punitive campaign in Chronicles is disputed — whether one event is narrated twice under different
theological frames, or two events are in view.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Joash is the standard example in discussions of the Chronicler's method: the reign where the added
material is most consequential and where the "immediate retribution" pattern is most visible. He is
also central to the question of how far royal fidelity in the biblical histories is presented as
institutionally sustained rather than personally held. The Gospel Zechariah reference makes the
reign a recurring node in Christian discussion of the canonical order of the Hebrew Bible.
