# Satan / the Devil

**Satan** — the Adversary, the Devil — is the cross-tradition personification of the
power opposed to God and the embodiment of evil. He is, however, not a single stable
character drawn from one text but a **composite figure assembled over more than a
millennium**: a relatively minor heavenly functionary in the Hebrew Bible (*ha-satan*,
"the accuser"), elaborated in Second Temple Judaism into a cosmic rebel, fixed by the New
Testament and the church fathers as the fallen prince of demons, recast by Islam as the
disobedient *Iblis*, and developed by Augustine, the medieval church, and Milton into the
grand antagonist of Western Christendom — before, in Andrew Delbanco's argument, beginning
to "die" as a credible category in the modern secular imagination. This page tracks that
arc and its tradition-specific receptions.

## Biographical Overview

Strictly, Satan has no biography: he is a supernatural being, not a historical person, and
the "life" attributed to him is a **theological synthesis** drawn from scattered and
originally unrelated passages. The synthesized arc runs:

1. **Heavenly prosecutor.** In the oldest stratum (the Hebrew Bible) *ha-satan* is **a
   title, not a name** — "the accuser/adversary," a member of the divine council who tests
   and prosecutes human beings *with God's permission*. He is not yet God's enemy.
2. **Cosmic rebel and fallen angel.** Second Temple literature and later Christian and
   Islamic tradition fuse this figure with the myth of an angel who rebelled out of pride
   and was cast down, becoming the ruler of a kingdom of evil spirits and the seducer of
   humanity.
3. **Lord of hell and end-time enemy.** In fully developed Christian eschatology he is the
   dragon of [[revelation-to-john|Revelation]], bound and finally destroyed at the last judgment;
   in folk and literary tradition, the costumed Devil of damnation.

The crucial historical observation, common to critical scholarship, is that **stages 2–3
are read back into stage 1**: the unified "Satan" of later tradition is a retrojection onto
texts that did not originally describe him.

## Primary Source Appearances

**Hebrew Bible (where *ha-satan* is an office, not the Devil):**
- **[[book-of-job|Job]] 1–2** — *ha-satan* ("the Accuser") appears among the "sons of God" in the
  divine court and proposes testing Job's disinterested piety. He acts only by God's leave;
  he is a prosecutor, not a rival deity. This is the wiki's central locus — see
  [[theodicy|Theodicy]].
- **Zechariah 3** — *ha-satan* stands to accuse the high priest Joshua and is rebuked by
  the angel of YHWH.
- **1 Chronicles 21:1 vs. 2 Samuel 24:1** — the later Chronicler attributes David's census
  to *satan* where the earlier text attributed it to YHWH's own anger: a key datum for the
  **gradual externalization** of the impulse to evil away from God onto a distinct figure.
- **Numbers 22:22** — the angel of YHWH stands in Balaam's road "as a *satan*" (adversary):
  here a *good* angel performs the satan-function, confirming that the word names a role.
- **Genesis 3 (the serpent)** and **Isaiah 14:12–15 / Ezekiel 28** ("Day Star, son of
  Dawn"; the king of Tyre's fall) were **not originally about Satan**, but were later read
  as the serpent-Devil and as "Lucifer's" prideful fall — a foundational case of
  retrojective exegesis.

**Second Temple Judaism (the figure expands into a cosmic adversary):**
- **[[1-enoch|1 Enoch]]** — the Watchers and their chief (Semihazah / Azazel) descend,
  corrupt humanity, and are bound; evil acquires an angelic origin story.
- **[[jubilees|Jubilees]]** — **Mastema**, "the prince of the spirits," is the first
  developed Jewish personification of the Devil: he tests, accuses, and is permitted a
  retinue of demons. (Qumran's **Belial / Beliar** is the parallel sectarian name.)
- **Wisdom of Solomon 2:24** — "through the devil's envy death entered the world" — the
  earliest explicit identification of the Eden serpent with the Devil.

**New Testament (Satan as a fixed personal enemy):**
- **The tempter** of Jesus in the wilderness (Matt 4; Luke 4; and Mark 1:12-13 — see the Marcan
  section below); **Beelzebul**, "prince of demons" ([[mark|Mark]] 3); **"I saw Satan fall like lightning"** (Luke 10:18); **"your father the
  devil"** (John 8:44); **"the god of this age"** (2 Cor 4:4) and **"the prince of the
  power of the air"** (Eph 2:2).
- **[[revelation-to-john|Revelation]] 12 and 20** — "the great dragon..., that ancient serpent, who
  is called the Devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world," cast down, then bound a
  thousand years, then thrown into the lake of fire. This is the climactic biblical
  portrait and the seed of the medieval Devil. See [[antichrist|Antichrist]] for the
  related but distinct end-time opponent.

**Qur'an:** **Iblis** refuses God's command to prostrate before [[adam|Adam]] (Q 2:34;
7:11–18; 38:71–85), is expelled for arrogance, and becomes **al-Shayṭān**, the whisperer
(*al-waswās*) who tempts but has no power to compel — see Islamic reception below.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Jewish.** Classical rabbinic Judaism largely **declined to develop a dualist Devil**.
*Ha-satan* is frequently identified with the **yetzer ha-ra** (the evil inclination) and
with the angel of death — an internal, divinely-created prosecutorial function rather than
an independent enemy ("Satan, the evil inclination, and the angel of death are one," b.
Bava Batra 16a). Strict monotheism resists a rival power; evil is held within the divine
economy (cf. Isa 45:7, "I form light and create darkness"). The boldest Jewish
demonology is **Kabbalistic**: the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] ("the Other Side") and its
chief **Samael** locate evil as a parasitic shadow of the divine *Gevurah*, not a second
god — see [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]].

**Christian.** Christianity made Satan a **central cosmic protagonist**: a pre-mundane
fallen angel (the "Lucifer" tradition built from Isa 14), author of the Fall, tempter of
Christ, ruler of a demonic kingdom, and the eschatological enemy defeated at the cross and
finally at the parousia. **Augustine** fixed the orthodox account — Satan is a *created*
and *good* angel who fell by the free, prideful turning of his will from God; evil is
*privatio boni* (privation of good), so the Devil has no independent ontological essence.
This guards monotheism against [[gnosticism|Gnostic]] and Manichaean dualism (where evil is
a co-eternal principle). The medieval and Reformation church elaborated an immense
demonology, witchcraft theory, and iconography; **Milton's *Paradise Lost*** gave the
tradition its definitive literary Satan — "obdurate pride and stedfast hate."

**Islamic.** **Iblis** (from Greek *diabolos*) / **al-Shayṭān** is in most readings a
**jinn** rather than an angel (Q 18:50), made of smokeless fire, who refuses to bow to Adam
out of arrogance ("I am better than he: You created me from fire and him from clay"). He is
granted respite until the Day of Judgment and devotes it to misguiding humanity by
**whispering** (*waswasa*), but he **cannot compel** — human responsibility is preserved,
and on Judgment Day he disowns his followers (Q 14:22). Sufi tradition contains a striking
minority reading (al-Ḥallāj, ʿAṭṭār) in which Iblis's refusal to bow to any but God is a
paradoxical, tragic *monotheist* love.

**Zoroastrian comparandum.** [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]'s **Angra Mainyu
(Ahriman)** is the nearest ancient analogue — but a genuinely *dualist* one: a hostile
spirit co-primordial with (though destined to lose to) the good. The much-debated question
of whether the Jewish-Christian Satan owes his cosmic-enemy promotion to Persian influence
is treated at
[[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|Zoroastrian Influence on Abrahamic
Eschatology]]. The Abrahamic traditions consistently **subordinate** Satan to the one God
(a creature gone wrong), refusing Zoroastrian symmetry.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Satan does theological work that strict monotheism otherwise finds awkward: he **externalizes
the origin of evil** (relocating temptation, accusation, and disorder away from God onto a
created rebel), supplies a **narrative antagonist** for the drama of salvation, and anchors
**theodicy** (evil as the work of a defeated-but-active enemy rather than of God).
Structurally he is a **parody of the divine**: Revelation's "false trinity" (dragon, beast,
false prophet) can only *imitate* what God has done (see [[antichrist|Antichrist]]); the
[[principalities-powers|principalities and powers]] are the cosmic system he heads. The
permanent tension is between **monotheism** (Satan as a leashed creature, evil as privation)
and the **functionally dualist** picture of two warring kingdoms.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Title vs. proper name.** *Ha-satan* (with the article) is an *office* in the Hebrew
  Bible; only later does "Satan" become a personal name. Critical scholarship dates the
  personalization to the Persian/Second Temple period.
- **The "Lucifer" construction.** The identification of Isa 14:12 ("Day Star, son of
  Dawn," Latin *lucifer*) and Ezek 28 with Satan's fall is a **later exegetical
  development**, not the texts' plain sense (which concern the kings of Babylon and Tyre).
- **The Eden serpent.** Genesis 3 does not name the serpent as Satan; the identification is
  intertestamental (Wisdom 2:24) and Christian (Rev 12:9).
- **Monism vs. dualism / Zoroastrian influence.** Whether Satan's elevation reflects Persian
  dualism or inner-biblical theodicy is unresolved — see
  [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|the influence page]].
- **Demythologization.** Modern critical and liberal theologies frequently treat Satan as a
  symbol or mythological projection rather than a literal being — which is the doorway to
  Delbanco's thesis below.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

The figure shaped the entire Western imagination of evil: **Augustine's** privation theory
and free-will account of the angelic fall; the **scholastic** angelology of Aquinas; the
Reformation's polemical Devil (and the application of "Antichrist" to the papacy); the
**Puritan** cosmos in which Satan was an "incandescent presence" in everyday life; and the
literary Devil of **Milton, Goethe (Mephistopheles), and Dostoevsky.** In Judaism the major
elaboration is **Kabbalistic** ([[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] / Samael).

## Satan in the Gospel of Mark (France, NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the fidelity reingest of [[france-mark-nigtc|France's NIGTC Mark]]. All
interpretive claims are [[rt-france|R. T. France]]'s.*

- **The wilderness tableau (1:12-13).** France reads Mark's thirty-word "temptation" account —
  which he retitles "The Context of Jesus' Mission" — as a tableau of the dramatis personae of a
  supernatural conflict behind the earthly scenes of Galilee and Jerusalem: "on the one side,
  supporting Jesus, are the Spirit and the angels; on the other, Satan and the wild animals."
  The conflict resurfaces at 8:33, where Jesus' "Get behind me, Satan" to
  [[peter-apostle|Peter]] is more than abuse: Peter's "human thoughts," by opposing God's
  revealed purpose, make him and those who agree with him "spokesmen of God's ultimate enemy."
  France notes the sequence from divine affirmation (1:11) to Satanic testing recalls Job 1, and
  reads πειραζόμενος positively as "testing" initiated by the Spirit.
- **No decisive defeat in the wilderness.** Against E. Best, France argues 1:12-13 records no
  outcome: the continued demonic encounters show a real ongoing contest, so the verses "present
  dramatis personae, not a decisive victory." Likewise on the "binding the strong man" saying
  (3:27): "Nothing may be seized from the strong man until he is bound, but this does not
  require that he be bound once for all" — each exorcism remains a real "power encounter"
  implementing a victory-in-principle, "as in NT eschatology generally."
- **The kingdom in terminal decline.** France argues the exorcisms' ultimate significance in
  3:22-30 is christological: they reveal the collapse of Satan's kingdom before the incoming
  kingdom of God — "The power of Satan ... has entered terminal decline"; Jesus' ministry is
  "the decisive turning point in the contest between good and evil."
- **Beelzebul.** France holds that Beelzebul in Mark is "simply an alternative name for Satan,"
  of unknown derivation and unattested as a demonic name in pre-Christian Jewish literature: "In
  the end we simply do not know where Mark got it from." The scribes' charge that Jesus' ministry
  is Beelzebul's work is, in Mark's setting, the unforgivable blasphemy against the Spirit (see
  [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]]).
- **Against exorcistic technique.** France cautions against reading exorcistic method — including
  modern charismatic "binding Satan" rituals — out of 3:27: the imagery relates "not to Jesus'
  exorcistic methods, but rather to the eschatological salvation which he now brings," the σκεύη
  plausibly the people rescued from Satan's oppression (echoing Isa 49:24-26).

## The Satan in Job (Hartley, NICOT Job, 1988)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[hartley-job-nicot|Hartley's NICOT Job]]. All
interpretive claims are [[john-hartley|John E. Hartley]]'s.*

Hartley's treatment is notably **restrained**, and its restraint is itself the significant
datum: he develops no demonology from Job 1–2 and makes no identification with the later
Devil.

- **Title, not a name.** "Here the Hebrew word *haśśāṭān* has the article, so it functions as
  a title rather than as a personal name. The Hebrew root *śṭn* means 'to oppose at law'"
  (p. 71). Hartley uses the English "the Satan," with the article, throughout translation and
  comment. He deploys the point against a late date for the book: "The term *the Satan* in the
  prologue functions as a title, not as a proper name as in the late books of Chronicles and
  Zechariah" (p. 18).
- **"Prosecutor" reported but judged inadequate.** Hartley reports that "some scholars
  conjecture that the Satan may be the prosecuting attorney of the heavenly council. If this
  view is correct, his task on earth was to discover human sins and failures and to bring his
  findings before the heavenly assembly" (pp. 71–72) — then declines it for this scene: "But
  his role in this scene deviates from this explanation. Instead of uncovering disruptive
  plans, he acts as **a troublemaker, a disturber of the kingdom**" (p. 71).
- **Membership in the sons of God left open.** Hartley records the dispute without settling
  it: "The majority of scholars assume that he was"; Driver-Gray "understand the preposition
  *among* (Heb. *bəṯôḵ*) to indicate that he had a prominent place in this assembly"; but
  "some recent scholars understand the text to portray the Satan as an intruder," reading
  *gam* as "other than" or *among* as marking an outsider (p. 72). Hartley redirects to
  function rather than adjudicating: "the main function of this assembly here is to provide an
  open forum in which Yahweh permits the testing of Job… the plan to test Job was not hatched
  in a secret meeting" — which matters because "the author has a doubly difficult task
  throughout his work: continually to characterize Job as innocent and not to characterize
  Yahweh as demonic" (pp. 72–73).
- **No identification with the later or NT Satan.** In the extracted range Hartley **nowhere**
  equates *haśśāṭān* with a devil-figure, a fallen angel, or the Satan of the New Testament.
  This is a deliberate restraint worth stating explicitly, given how heavily the rest of this
  page is shaped by exactly that later synthesis.
- **Not a wager.** "Many scholars speak of this transaction between Yahweh and the Satan as a
  wager. But this is inaccurate, for no sum was set to be handed over to the winner. The single
  issue at stake was the motivation for Job's upright behavior and his fear of God. The Satan
  functions as God's servant, solely an instrument in the testing. **The author holds to a pure
  monotheism wherein God is ultimately responsible for all that happens**" (p. 74). Consistent
  with this, Hartley reads Yahweh's *sûṯ* ("incite") at 2:3 as Yahweh accepting "full
  responsibility for Job's plight," conceding no authority to the Satan (pp. 79–80), and
  explains the Satan's disappearance from the epilogue accordingly: "Yahweh himself feels
  obliged to resolve the conflict for Job" (p. 80). Job, for his part, "has no battle with the
  Satan" and at 10:8 "attributes the swallowing to God, with no thought about the Satan"
  (pp. 80, 186).
- **The challenge and its *ḥinnām* reversal.** The Satan "doubted whether any person would
  fear God for nothing or without reason (Heb. *ḥinnām*)… The Satan's question insinuated that
  all good deeds spring from selfish motives" (p. 73); Yahweh's counter-use of the same word
  at 2:3 declares the accusations "groundless" (p. 80). See
  [[doctrine-of-retribution|The Doctrine of Retribution]], where Hartley argues that Job's
  friends inadvertently take the Satan's side by urging repentance for the sake of restored
  blessing.
- **Behemoth and Leviathan as the functional stand-in.** The Satan never reappears, and
  Hartley reads the second Yahweh speech as addressing his challenge obliquely: mastery over
  the "primordial monsters" demonstrates that Yahweh is "Lord of all forces, earthly and
  cosmic, that brought on Job's affliction" (p. 534). See
  [[behemoth-and-leviathan|Behemoth and Leviathan]].

## Modern Reception — "The Death of Satan" (Delbanco)

[[andrew-delbanco|Andrew Delbanco's]] *The Death of Satan: How Americans Have Lost the
Sense of Evil* (1995) traces what happens to this figure in modernity. His thesis: as
American culture secularized — from the Puritan world where the Devil was a vivid daily
presence, through the Enlightenment's "Age of Reason," the Romantic "birth of the self,"
and the modern "age of blame" and "culture of irony" — the **personified Devil was
"unnamed"** and lost his grip on the imagination, leaving (in Henry Murray's phrase) "a
ludicrous ham actor with no greater part to play... than the vermiform appendix." The
problem Delbanco diagnoses is not that people stopped sinning but that a culture which
abolished the *symbolic language* of evil was left "with a sense of evil but no longer the
religious or philosophical language to talk intelligently about evil" (Sontag) — confronting
horror (the Holocaust, modern atrocity, Arendt's "banality of evil") with only "inarticulate
dread." This is developed as a concept at
[[secularization-of-evil|The Death of Satan / Secularization of Evil]]. Delbanco's Satan is
thus the *absent* Satan — the figure defined by his disappearance.

## In Dante's Inferno

At the frozen centre of [[inferno|Hell]] (Inf. 34), Dante's Satan is a deliberate
**anti-climax**: a vast, three-faced figure — a parody of the [[trinity|Trinity]] —
frozen to the breast in ice, weeping from six eyes and mindlessly chewing Judas,
Brutus, and Cassius. Far from a heroic rebel, he is impotent, capable only of
flapping his bat-wings (which freeze the lake) and gnawing. Kirkpatrick reads him as
"a parasitical figure, existing only as a negative image of the ultimate truth": the
embodiment of [[evil-as-privation|evil as privation]]. Per Paradiso 19, his fall
came not through grand rebellion but through impatient refusal to wait for light —
he fell "acerbo," unripe. See [[divine-comedy|The Divine Comedy]].
