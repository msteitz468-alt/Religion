**Biographical Overview**:
Elizabeth was a 1st-century Jewish woman, a descendant of Aaron, and the wife of the priest Zechariah. She lived in the hill country of Judea. Long barren and advanced in age, she miraculously conceived and gave birth to John the Baptist. She was also a relative of Mary, the mother of Jesus (Luke 1:36). Her story is confined to the Lukan birth narrative; no other canonical source mentions her.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Elizabeth appears only in Luke 1. She conceives John after Zechariah's temple vision and goes into seclusion for five months. When visited by her pregnant relative Mary, the unborn John leaps in her womb; Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, utters a prophetic blessing upon Mary and her child (1:39-45): "Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!" She is the first to recognize Mary's child as "my Lord." At the circumcision, she insists the child be named John, overriding family tradition (1:59-60).
- Cross-references in other Gospels are indirect (no name).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green's commentary observes that Elizabeth's barrenness in a first-century Mediterranean agrarian context would have brought severe social disgrace and loss of honor, despite (or because of) her high status as a daughter of Aaron married to a priest serving in the temple. Her miraculous pregnancy represents profound status reversal and restoration of honor ("He has looked favorably on me and taken away the disgrace I have endured among my people," 1:25). Elizabeth acts as a prophet; filled with the Spirit, she is the first person in the narrative to recognize the messianic identity of Mary's unborn child and to bless Mary as "the mother of my Lord."
- **Early Church / Patristic**: Celebrated in the Visitation feast; seen as model of hospitality and prophetic recognition. Parallels to Sarah (Gen 18, 21) and Hannah (1 Sam 1-2) developed typologically.
- **Eastern Orthodox / Catholic**: Venerated as saint; her role in the Visitation iconography emphasizes the meeting of the two miraculous pregnancies and the unborn John's prophetic leap.
- **Protestant**: Emphasizes her as example of faithful, Spirit-filled woman who recognizes God's work; her insistence on the name "John" as obedience to divine revelation over tradition.
- **Jewish**: No direct tradition; her story is internal to Christian narrative as fulfillment of OT barren-matriarch pattern.
- **Islam**: Not named in Quran; John (Yahya) is mentioned, but mother is not developed.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Elizabeth embodies the Lukan theme of God's intervention on behalf of the marginalized and disgraced, turning tables on human expectations (priest Zechariah doubts; his wife Elizabeth believes and prophesies). She parallels OT matriarchs (Sarah, Hannah, Rachel), situating the events within Israel's salvation history and signaling that God's eschatological action is underway. Her Spirit-filled prophetic blessing makes her the first human witness to the identity of Jesus in the narrative. Together with Mary, Zechariah, Simeon, and Anna, she forms the chorus of prophetic voices at the hinge of the ages. Green notes the dramatic irony: the priest in the temple is silenced for doubt; the "disgraced" wife becomes the first prophet of the new era.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Relationship to Mary**: "Relative" (συγγενίς); exact kinship (cousins? ) not specified. Green treats it as narrative device linking the two miraculous births.
- **Historical basis**: The birth narrative is highly stylized (parallels to OT, chiastic structures, songs). Critical scholarship views much as theological construction; conservative readers (Green) affirm core historicity of the events while recognizing literary shaping.
- **Prophetic role of women**: Elizabeth (and Mary, Anna) as Spirit-filled prophets raises questions of women's roles in earliest Christianity and Luke's theology of the Spirit poured out on all flesh (Joel 2 / Acts 2).

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Elizabeth's story turns the tables on Zechariah (the chosen priest) in favor of the "disgraced" wife. Her character reinforces Luke's elevation of women as key prophetic voices and recipients of divine revelation. The social-scientific lens (honor-shame, status reversal in agrarian society) makes her pregnancy a public vindication. The "crescendo" of the opening narrative privileges her faith over her husband's doubt.
- Broader: Typological link to Hannah (Magnificat/Benedictus as new Hannah songs); model for Christian understandings of hospitality (Visitation), prophetic recognition, and God's preference for the lowly. Cross-tradition: highlights Lukan emphasis on women in salvation history (cf. other Gospels' more marginal treatment of female figures).
