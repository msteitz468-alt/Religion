## Biographical Overview

Dhritarashtra is the blind king of Hastinapura and father of Duryodhana and the other Kauravas. His blindness is both literal and, in Rajagopalachari's retelling, a recurring metaphor for his inability or unwillingness to see the consequences of his favouritism toward his sons. He is the uncle of the Pandavas by birth. He is married to [[gandhari]], who blinded herself in solidarity with him.

## Primary Source Appearances

### After the Dice Game (Ch. XXV–XXVI)

Dhritarashtra's most morally significant action in these chapters comes immediately after Draupadi's ordeal: when the assembly stood stunned and ominous portents broke out (jackals howling, donkeys braying, carnivorous birds crying from all directions), Dhritarashtra "for once acted with wisdom and courage." He called Draupadi to his side, comforted her with words of gentleness and affection, then turned to Yudhishthira and restored to the Pandavas their kingdom, their wealth, and their freedom. The Pandavas departed, "bewildered and stunned, and seeing a miracle in this sudden release from calamity."

The reprieve did not last. Duryodhana, Duhsasana, and Shakuni upbraided Dhritarashtra for having frustrated their plans. Duryodhana argued that no device was wrong if it aimed at destroying formidable enemies, and pressed for a second invitation to dice. Dhritarashtra gave "his doting father's reluctant and bodeful approval" — the narrator's characterisation making clear this was weakness rather than wisdom.

### Dhritarashtra's Anxiety (Ch. XXVI)

The chapter titled "Dhritarashtra's Anxiety" is given over entirely to his torment after the Pandavas departed for the forest. Unable to see (literally), he asked [[vidura]] to describe the manner of the Pandavas' departure — Yudhishthira covering his face with a cloth, Bhima walking with eyes downcast on his mighty arms, Arjuna scattering sand, the twins smeared with dust, Draupadi walking with dishevelled hair and streaming eyes. The description intensified his fear.

He asked: "What do the citizens say?" Vidura told him: citizens of all castes blamed "the covetous Dhritarashtra and his sons" for driving the Pandavas away. Then the sage Narada appeared and declared that in fourteen years the Kauravas would become extinct "as the result of the crime committed by Duryodhana" — and vanished.

When Dhritarashtra conversed with Sanjaya about the causes of the disaster, he acknowledged: "I did not follow the wise path of dharma and statesmanship but suffered myself to be misled by my foolish son and, as you say, we are fast hastening towards the abyss."

### The Quarrel with Vidura and Reconciliation (Ch. XXVI)

Vidura repeatedly urged Dhritarashtra to recall Yudhishthira from the forest, restore the Pandavas' kingdom, and restrain Duryodhana by force if necessary. At first Dhritarashtra "listened in sad silence" — he knew Vidura to be wiser and better-intentioned. But gradually his patience wore thin. One day he burst out: "You are always speaking for the Pandavas and against my sons. Duryodhana was born of my loins. How can I give him up?" He dismissed Vidura, who sorrowfully went to the Pandavas in the forest.

Dhritarashtra almost immediately regretted the dismissal: "What have I done? I have only strengthened the Pandavas still further by driving the wise Vidura into their arms." He sent Sanjaya with a repentant message. The soft-hearted Vidura returned. The two embraced and their difference was "washed away in tears of mutual affection."

### The Visit of Sage Maitreya (Ch. XXVI)

When the sage Maitreya came to Dhritarashtra's court, the king received him with great respect and asked whether his "beloved children, the Pandavas" were well and whether "mutual affection would abide in our family." Maitreya then confronted Duryodhana directly, advising him to make peace with the Pandavas — and when Duryodhana laughed and slapped his thighs in derision without answering, Maitreya cursed him: that Bhima's mace would break those thighs. Dhritarashtra "jumped up and fell at the feet of the sage and begged forgiveness." Maitreya replied that the curse would not take effect if Duryodhana made peace — otherwise it would.

### Passive Consent to Duryodhana's Expedition (Ch. XXXVIII)

When Duryodhana wished to visit the forest where the Pandavas were living — ostensibly for cattle stock-taking, actually to gloat — Dhritarashtra refused at first: "It is not advisable for you to go there. I cannot agree to send you to a place near the abode of Bhima and Arjuna while there is still occasion for anger and strife." But Sakuni argued, and Dhritarashtra "overpersuaded as usual" gave in: "Well, please yourselves." The narrator's editorial comment follows: "A heart full of hate can know no contentment."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Dhritarashtra is Rajagopalachari's primary study in the failure of a father. His blindness — literal, inherited, and chosen — becomes the narrative explanation for the entire catastrophe. He is not without conscience: he experiences genuine remorse, he acts well in the single critical moment after Draupadi's ordeal, and he acknowledges his failures plainly in conversation with Sanjaya and Vidura. What undoes him is his inability to sustain any of these moral recoveries against Duryodhana's pressure. The pattern repeats: clarity, acknowledgment, weakness, capitulation. Sanjaya's diagnosis is recorded: "Time, the all-destroyer, does not take a club and break the head of a man but by destroying his judgment, makes him act madly to his own ruin." Dhritarashtra accepts this verdict about his sons but cannot apply it to himself.

Rajagopalachari's narrator ends Ch. XXVI with a moral drawn explicitly for readers: "if we read this chapter and ponder on it when we are inclined to be angry, it may make us wise and save us from foolishness and crime" — pointing to the broader dynamics of resentment and rage in the Kaurava camp rather than to Dhritarashtra's individual failure.

His final condition at the close of this range: haunted by the fear of Bhima and Arjuna's vengeance, unable to find peace after having "injured the Pandavas," aware that "wrong cannot but yield its bitter harvest."
