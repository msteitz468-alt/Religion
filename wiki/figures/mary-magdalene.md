# Mary Magdalene

**Mary Magdalene** is, across all four Gospel resurrection narratives, the first (or among the
first) to announce that [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]] has been raised. [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]
([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) notes that although she is "virtually
absent everywhere else in the Gospels," she is consistently placed at the empty tomb and the
first appearances — and infers that "one plausible explanation is that she too had a vision of
Jesus after he died," making her one of the handful (with Peter, Paul, possibly James) whose
visionary experiences generated resurrection faith (see
[[resurrection-appearances-visions|the appearances controversy]]). In John 20:14–16 she at
first mistakes the risen Jesus for the gardener — an instance of Ehrman's "Doubt Tradition."

## France on Mary Magdalene and the Women in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], [[france-mark-nigtc|*The Gospel
of Mark* (NIGTC)]]. All interpretive claims are France's unless another scholar is named.*

- **A known figure**: France states that "Μαρία ἡ Μαγδαληνή needed no introduction to Mark's
  readers," known chiefly through her resurrection-narrative role in all four gospels (Mk
  15:40, 47; 16:1). Salome is unique to Mark; the second Mary he takes as probably *not* Jesus'
  mother (contra some) but likely the mother of James the son of Alphaeus. He notes that only
  John names the Bethany anointing woman (Mary of Bethany), and that "it was Ephraem in the
  fourth century who first suggested that she was Mary of Magdala" — the identification has no
  earlier basis, and Luke's anointing account is probably a separate tradition.
- **The women as continuous witnesses**: France argues 15:40-41 belongs with the burial/tomb
  sequence rather than the crucifixion: the women bind the closing scenes together — "They saw
  him die, they saw him buried, and they saw that same tomb empty" — ruling out, e.g., a
  wrong-tomb mistake. Only here does Mark reveal that Jesus' entourage had all along included a
  substantial group of women followers from [[galilee|Galilee]], described with ἀκολουθέω and
  διακονέω, "the language of discipleship" — "a remarkable shift" from the apparently all-male
  movement of the narrative so far. (Earlier, France attributes the all-male composition of the
  Twelve to the social climate of the time rather than to theological motives, noting 15:40-41.)
- **Witness against social convention**: quoting Myers approvingly, France notes that in a
  society giving no legal status to women's testimony (Josephus, *Ant.* 4.219; m. Roš HaŠ. 1:8),
  "everything will nevertheless come to depend on their witness" — Mark's most radical
  "narrative subversion of the canons of social orthodoxy."
- **The silence of 16:8**: France finds the women's total silence (οὐδενὶ οὐδὲν εἶπαν) the truly
  "inexplicable" element of Mark's ending — for Mark "to build up so carefully the women's
  unique role as the first witnesses … only to knock it down in his final sentence by insisting
  on their complete silence seems bizarre" — and judges all attempts to give it a deliberate
  literary function unpersuasive as presupposing "an inappropriately 'modern' understanding of
  literary technique." He finds "least unsatisfactory" Gundry's view that the intended (lost or
  unwritten) continuation contained a meeting with Jesus (underlying Mt 28:9-10) in which the
  women's silence was overridden and the message delivered. He also notes the Longer Ending
  awkwardly reintroduces Jesus and Mary Magdalene as if unmentioned — one sign neither added
  ending follows naturally after v. 8. See the [[mark-ending|Mark 16 ending controversy]].
