# Guru Ram Das

## Biographical Overview

Guru Ram Das was born Jetha Sodhi. He was the son-in-law of [[guru-amar-das|Guru Amar Das]], having married Amar Das's daughter **Bibi Bhani**. In 1574, when Amar Das died, Jetha Sodhi became the fourth Guru, taking the name Ram Das (McLeod ~L1743–1748).

"Following one who had lived to a very old age, Ram Das was Master of the Panth for only seven years" — he died in 1581. "In his case also most of the information has been passed down by tradition and very little of it can be positively affirmed" (McLeod ~L1745–1750).

## Significance: Founding of Amritsar

The single most important tradition associated with Guru Ram Das is his **founding of the new centre known as Chak Guru or Ramdaspur**, later renamed [[amritsar|Amritsar]]. "The hostility of the sons of Guru Amar Das is said to have been responsible for the move from Goindval" (McLeod ~L1757–1759). Ram Das established himself at the new location and "began excavating the sacred pool" (McLeod ~L1760).

The site became the principal centre of the Nanak-panth: "The site thus chosen became the principal centre of the Nanak-panth until the sixth Guru, Hargobind, was compelled to abandon it early in the seventeenth century and move to the safety of the Shivalik hills. Amritsar recovered its prominence during the warfare of the eighteenth century and has ever since remained the chief centre of the Sikhs" (McLeod ~L1761–1766).

Note: McLeod attributes the decision to excavate a sacred pool to the period of the *third* Guru (Amar Das) but credits the *founding and excavation at the Amritsar site specifically* to Ram Das (McLeod ~L1658, ~L1754–1764).

## The Masand System

"The other feature commonly associated with the fourth Guru is the appointment of **masands** or vicars who acted on behalf of their Guru in the more dispersed of his sahgats." The word masand means "throne" or "raised platform," indicating the status these deputies were to acquire. "These were men who supervised individual sahgats or clusters of sahgats on behalf of the Guru, perhaps acting as spiritual guides and certainly empowered to collect the tithes or other contributions which a loyal Sikh might be expected to give to his Master" (McLeod ~L1768–1782).

The masand system developed from the **manji system** attributed to Guru Amar Das. It "presumably did its job reasonably well in the first few decades of its existence and lasted until its decline into semi-independence and corruption produced formal abolition by Guru Gobind Singh at the end of the seventeenth century" (McLeod ~L1789–1798).

## As Hymn-Writer

McLeod singles out Guru Ram Das's quality as a poet: "Kirtan was of the essence of the Nanak-panth and the fourth Guru certainly provided his followers with hymns in praise of the divine Name. The Guru's sense of melody and rhythm place him high according to the exalted standards of the Adi Granth, a feature of considerable importance" (McLeod ~L1800–1808).

## Hereditary Succession from Ram Das

From Ram Das onward, all subsequent Gurus descended from him in the Sodhi Khatri sub-caste. He chose his youngest son **Arjan** as the fifth Guru, passing over his oldest son Prithi Chand and his second son Mahadev. This established the convention that "hereafter the succession should be limited to the direct descendants of Guru Ram Das" — intended to minimize succession disputes, though it did not eliminate them. Prithi Chand's opposition became a defining difficulty for Guru Arjan (McLeod ~L1810–1926).
