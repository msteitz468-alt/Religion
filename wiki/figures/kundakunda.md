# Kundakunda

Kundakunda is the most influential early [[digambara|Digambara]] philosopher and mystical theologian in the history of [[jainism|Jainism]]. Dundas describes him as "one of [India's] most intense advocates of the centrality of inward experience and the reorientation of all religious practice to focus upon the self" (lines 827–829).

## Biographical Overview

Almost nothing is known of Kundakunda's life. "Nothing is known of Kundakunda's life. Although scholarship has conventionally located him in the second or third century CE, hagiographical accounts do not appear until the tenth century, a fact which has prompted a recent radical reassessment of his dating which would locate him after 750 CE" (lines 830–838). His original monastic name was **Padmanandi** and he is called Kundakunda because he "supposedly came from a village of that name in south India" (lines 839–841).

"Tradition regards Kundakunda as being the founder of the **Mula Sangha**, the main Digambara ascetic lineage, and his name is invoked along with those of [[mahavira|Mahavira]] and his disciple Gautama in a famous auspicious verse still recited in Karnataka" (lines 842–845).

## Works

Sixteen works are attributed to Kundakunda, though Dundas cautions that "caution must be exercised in connecting him with all of them" (lines 848–849). The group of texts known as the "Eight Treatises" (*Ashtapahuda*) "was shown by Schubring to be later than Kundakunda on metrical and stylistic grounds, while other texts evince clear signs of interpolations. None of these philological considerations has had any impact upon the Digambara community which continues to regard all Kundakunda's writings as genuine and forming a unity" (lines 852–857).

"Kundakunda's most significant works are written in Prakrit of the variety known as Jaina Sauraseni and are deceptively simple in style" (lines 858–860):

| Work | Abbreviation | Content |
|------|-------------|---------|
| *Niyamasara* (*NiyS*) | NiyS | "Essence of Restraint" — obligatory ritual practices for ascetics |
| *Pancastikaya* (*PS*) | PS | "Essence of the Five" (title not fully preserved in slice) — ascetic and spiritual behaviour, probably directed to novices |
| *Pravachanasara* (*PS*) | PS | "Essence of the Scripture" — manual for novices on ascetic and spiritual behaviour |
| *Samayasara* (*SS*) | SS | "Essence of the Doctrine" — most famous work; "largely devoted to a discussion of the real nature of the self" (lines 864–866) |

(Note: The slice's description of the *Pancastikaya* title is partially obscured — line 862 appears broken in the source. The "Essence of the Five" is a common reconstruction (not in Dundas's account of this passage).)

## Hermeneutical Method: The Two Standpoints

Kundakunda's signature contribution is a **two-standpoints** (two-truths) framework applied to Jain doctrine:

- **Nishchaya (*niscaya*) standpoint** — also called *paramartha* (supreme) or *suddha* (pure): the ultimate perspective, in which the *jiva* alone is real and is already liberated. "The soul (*jiva*) is the only true and ultimate category in existence which, as such, provides a particular standpoint (*naya*), the pivotal point, called either 'certain' (*niscaya*), 'supreme' (*paramartha*) or 'pure' (*suddha*), with reference to which all other entities, beliefs and practices can be judged" (lines 873–877).
- **Vyavahara standpoint** — the worldly, transactional perspective, which has provisional but not ultimate validity.

"The Jain ascetic should therefore direct his energies to inner experience" (line 888–889). The *nishchaya* standpoint reveals that "the soul cannot be in any meaningful way modified by contact with karmic matter and therefore … the sole object of omniscience is the soul itself" (*NiyS* 159–66, line 885).

Dundas notes that this is "one of the first examples of the two-truths model of reality, better known through being used by Mahayana Buddhism and the non-dualist brand of Vedanta in Hinduism" (lines 890–893) — if Kundakunda's conventional dating is accepted.

## Doctrinal Positions

From the *nishchaya* standpoint:

1. **The soul's inner nature** — Karma causes the soul to lose "an awareness of its own inner nature as pure knowledge and its status as being already liberated" (lines 880–882).
2. **Karma's real impact** — "on the level of the certain standpoint, the soul experiences only the mental results of karma" (*NiyS* 18 and 42; *PS* 1.77, lines 897–898), not real physical contact.
3. **Collapse of moral polarities** — "good and bad, pleasure and pain, fasting and non-fasting and so on are of significance solely at the worldly level in the flawed realm of transmigration" (*SS* 306–7, lines 903–905).
4. **Scripture's limits** — in a statement "deeply subversive of Jain tradition," Kundakunda "declares that scripture and its injunctions … are incapable of bringing [realisation of the soul] about because they cannot be said to know anything" (*SS* 360, lines 916–919).
5. **The Three Jewels** — right faith, knowledge and practice "are in reality reckoned to be merely another means of describing the soul" (lines 909–911).

Yet a second strand also runs through Kundakunda's writings: "the practice of the correct ascetic and moral behaviour … bringing about the warding off and destruction of karma which then leads the self to a direct understanding of its own nature" (lines 921–924). Dundas suggests this tension is expected in a two-truths framework and is not necessarily evidence of interpolation.

## Influence and Reception

### Digambara Reception

Kundakunda established "a general idiom for Jain mysticism" (line 945). His most significant Digambara successor was **Yogindu** (perhaps tenth century CE), whose Apabhramsha poetry on the soul as *paramatman* "presents an internalised Jainism which at times taps into a vein of institutional subversion" (lines 946–949).

Later Digambara commentators wrestled with the status of the *vyavahara* standpoint. The thirteenth-century layman **Asadhara** described the person wishing to abandon worldly means in pure mystical pursuit as "a fool trying to grow corn without seed" (*AnDh* p. 74, lines 939–940).

The laity-based **Adhyatmika sect** drew its inspiration from Kundakunda and his commentators, de-emphasising the role of ritual and ascetics — a development attacked by the seventeenth-century Svetambara intellectual Yasovijaya (see below).

### Svetambara Reception

The Svetambaras were "generally ill at ease with what they perceived as Kundakunda's reductionist approach to reality" (lines 954–956). **Haribhadra** shows partial Kundakundan influence in stating that "on the worldly level of truth the abode of the liberated souls is a physical place while, on the higher level, it is nothing other than the true nature of each individual soul" (*LV* p. 261, lines 951–953).

**Yasovijaya** (seventeenth century) — described as "perhaps the last truly great intellectual figure in Jainism" — "criticised the inadequacy of Kundakunda's reliance on only one standpoint" (lines 959–961) and strongly attacked the Adhyatmika sect.
