## Biographical Overview

Eusebius of Caesarea (Palestina) was bishop of Caesarea and the foremost theological intellectual among the "Eusebians." Ayres notes that "the usefulness and prominence of his *Ecclesiastical History* has led to his erroneously being considered a 'historian' rather than a 'theologian'" (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*–858). Ayres treats Eusebius primarily as a theologian whose work is essential for understanding the "Eusebian" trajectory. Unlike [[eusebius-of-nicomedia|Eusebius of Nicomedia]] and Arius, Eusebius of Caesarea "was not a 'student' of Lucian". He knew [[origen|Origen]] and "drew on aspects of his thought".

## Role in the Fourth-Century Controversy (per Ayres)

Eusebius of Caesarea is the second major figure in Ayres's "Eusebian" trajectory — "theologians of the 'one Unbegotten'" — but his theology is "subtly different" from that of Arius and from the other Lucianists. He "made common cause with the 'Lucianists' against Alexander", and a letter he wrote to Alexander c. 320 (preserved in the acts of the second council of Nicaea) protests against Alexander's treatment of Arius:

> your letter accuses them as though they were saying that the Son has come into being as one of the created things... they do not say this, but clearly determine that he is 'not as one of the created things'... You censure them for saying that 'he who is' has begotten 'he who is not'. I marvel that someone is able to say otherwise. (Opitz, *Werke*, III/i, U.7)

Ayres reads this letter as demonstrating that "Eusebius here takes as axiomatic that the Son's status is something which comes via the Father's gift and act, and that it is consequently obvious that he was in some sense 'created'".

## Theological Position / Trajectory

Eusebius's account of the Son combines Origen-derived analogies of overflow and light with a clear commitment to the Father's ontological priority. The Son is generated before creation as a kind of "foundation" for all created things:

> He subsists, not like the rest of begotten things, nor does he have life as the things begotten through him do; but he alone was born of the Father himself and is life itself. It was fitting for the God above all, before everything that came to be and before all ages, to bring forth the unique Begotten like a foundation or an unbreakable fundament for the things that would come to be through him. So he begot the Son before all things that were going to be, like a ray of light and a source of life and a treasury of goods, 'in which all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden'. (*Eccl. theol.* 1. 8. 2–4–833)

The Son is simultaneously "life itself" and a product of the Father's will:

> He is not unbegotten, but is offspring of an unbegotten Father. He is only-begotten, Logos, God from God, put forth from the being of the Father, not by a partaking or a cutting or a division, but unspeakably and, for us, unexplainably. He is from time or rather before all time. He has his being from the inexplicable and incomprehensible will and power of the Father. (*Dem. evang.* 4. 3. 13–845)

Ayres notes that "Eusebius' focus on the Father's will is subtly different from that of Arius: he describes the Son as 'life itself' and as the 'ray' of the Father's 'light', expressions that have no place in Arius' surviving texts". Crucially, the notion of will in Eusebius serves both to deny that the Son is generated by a division of the Father's essence and to explain how God gives himself in the creation: "at one point he remarks that the will of God is the original material for all things".

### "Image of the Father's Ousia"

Ayres notes that Hanson called "image of the Father's ousia" Eusebius's "favourite doctrine" (*The Search*, 52). Eusebius uses the analogy of perfume or fragrance extensively to describe how the Son relates to the Father:

> Perhaps one might say that the Son originated like a perfume and a ray of light from the Father's unoriginated nature and ineffable substance infinite ages ago, or rather before all ages.

And more fully:

> [the Son] is the image of God, in a way mysterious and incalculable to us, the living image of the living God and existing in its own right immaterially... but not like an image in our experience, when the form is distinct from the image, but himself wholly the form, and assimilated in his own reality to the Father, and so he is the most lively perfume of the Father, once again in a way mysterious and incalculable to us. (*Dem. evang.* 5. 1. 18–21–893)

This emphasis on the Son as mirroring the Father's unity is distinctive: "as image the Son must mirror the Father's incomposite unity". Ayres suggests this may have "differed significantly from Arius, in whom the Son seems to have been a composite being not sharing the Father's simplicity".

### Two Powers and Hypostases

Eusebius "speaks of two powers in God: the power of God unique to his nature and a second power, the Word, who is the first principle of creation". He "speaks of distinct hypostases but his use of this term is not consistent", sometimes using hypostasis in other senses and "occasionally, early on, speaks also of two οὐσιαι". "Early in his career he is happy to speak of the Son as 'a second God' in a clearly subordinationist sense".

### Quotes from [[asterius-the-sophist|Asterius the Sophist]]

Eusebius cites with approval a fragment of Asterius: "the only begotten Logos (John 1: 18) and first born of all creation, the alone [begetting] the alone, perfect the perfect, King the King, Lord the Lord, God [begetting] God, exact image of the being and will and power and glory" (*C. Marcellus* 1. 4–645). Ayres notes this text reappears at an important council in 341.

## Reception and Later Labelling

Ayres stresses that Eusebius of Caesarea's position within the "Eusebian" bloc is one of relative theological distinctiveness. He retains Origen-derived "overflow" language and the analogy of perfume or ray that has no parallel in Arius's surviving fragments. Ayres notes that "it has been argued that during the years of controversy around Nicaea Eusebius gradually modified his position, increasingly subordinating the Son to the Father, dropping the language of scent and perfume" — but "the evidence is, however, unclear and fragmentary".

## Influence

Eusebius's theological writings — especially his *Demonstratio Evangelica* and *Ecclesiastica Theologia* — were central to the fourth-century debate, providing the most sophisticated articulation of the Eusebian trajectory's Origen-derived resources. Ayres notes that within the broader Eusebian camp, "some emphasize the subordinate nature of the Son with increasing clarity, others discover that struggling to preserve the traditional languages which still figures strongly in Eusebius of Caesarea's theology forces them along a different path and eventually leads them to make a significant contribution to the formation of pro-Nicene orthodoxy".

## Pelikan: Founder of Christian Historiography

[[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 1) presents a complementary side of Eusebius to his role in the Arian debate: his *Praeparatio evangelica* and *Demonstratio evangelica* as "probably the most important apologetic work of the early church." Eusebius criticized his apologist predecessors' "preoccupation with 'dialectical arguments (*logoi*)' at the expense of 'historical facts (*erga*)'," and answered the pagan charge that Christianity was "too recent and too provincial" not by dialectic but by writing universal history — the *Chronicle* and the *Ecclesiastical History* — thereby **laying the foundations for ecclesiastical history** as a genre. His proof text for the Christian reading of history was Genesis 49:10 LXX, "the expectation of the nations." After Constantine's conversion removed the political need for apology to the empire, Eusebius "continued to provide the materials for the apologia to the intellectuals."
