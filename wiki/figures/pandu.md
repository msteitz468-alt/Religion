## Biographical Overview

Pāṇḍu was the son of Vicitravīrya and his queen Ambālikā, and thus a half-brother to Dhritarāṣṭra (born of Ambikā) and to Vidura (born of a servant-maid). He is the biological father of the five Pāṇḍavas. His reign at Hastinapura preceded the events of the main narrative; he came to the throne because Dhritarāṣṭra was born blind and the older convention held a blind man unfit to be king.

At [[kunti|Kuntī]]'s svayamvara, Pāṇḍu's "personality eclipsed the lustre of all the other princes assembled" and Kuntī chose him. He subsequently also married Mādrī, sister of the king of Madra, on the advice of [[bhishma|Bhīṣma]] and in accordance with the custom of ensuring progeny (lines 1341–1353).

**The curse and the forest life (Chapter IX):** One day while hunting, Pāṇḍu shot a sage and his wife who were sporting in the forest in the form of deer. The dying sage cursed him: "You will meet with death the moment you taste the pleasures of the bed." Pāṇḍu was heartbroken and, entrusting his kingdom to Bhīṣma and Vidura, retreated to the forest with his wives and lived in complete sexual abstinence. Seeing his grief at being denied progeny, Kuntī confided to him the story of Durvāsa's mantra. At Pāṇḍu's urging, Kuntī used the mantra to invoke the gods; Mādrī also used it once at Kuntī's permission. The five Pāṇḍavas — Yudhiṣṭhira (son of Dharma), Bhīma (son of Vāyu), [[arjuna|Arjuna]] (son of Indra), Nakula and Sahadeva (twin sons of the Aśvins, born to Mādrī) — were thus born and raised in the forest among ascetics (lines 1355–1369).

**Death:** One spring day, overcome by the season's sensual charge despite Mādrī's earnest protests, Pāṇḍu broke his abstinence. The sage's curse took immediate effect and he fell dead. Mādrī, overwhelmed with remorse for having been the occasion of his death, immolated herself on his funeral pyre; she asked Kuntī to remain and be a mother to her doubly orphaned children. The forest sages brought the bereaved Kuntī and the five young Pāṇḍavas to Hastinapura and entrusted them to Bhīṣma. Yudhiṣṭhira was sixteen at the time (lines 1370–1386).

The whole kingdom mourned Pāṇḍu's death. Vidura, Bhīṣma, Vyāsa, Dhritarāṣṭra and others performed the funeral rites. Vyāsa told Satyavatī that the world had now entered upon sorrow and disillusionment, and advised the three aged queens to retire to the forest — advice they followed (lines 1383–1394).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Pāṇḍu functions structurally as the absent father whose death sets all subsequent events in motion. His moral status in the epic is ambiguous: he is described as righteous and a good king, yet the curse was not unjust (he had shot the sage without proper identification of his target). His death immediately before the main narrative begins means he is known almost entirely through his sons and through others' memories of him. Dhritarāṣṭra tells the newly crowned Yudhiṣṭhira: "My brother Pandu made this kingdom prosperous" (line 2394).

His abdication of the kingdom to live in the forest is presented by Rajagopalachari as an act of genuine renunciation rather than retreat; his desire for progeny under such constraints drives the theological mechanism of the mantra-births, making the divine paternity of the Pāṇḍavas narratively necessary.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari treats Pāṇḍu as a legendary figure. No historical-critical commentary is offered.
