# Bahá'u'lláh

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

Bahá'u'lláh ("The Splendour" or "Glory of God") was the religious title of Mírzá Husayn-'Alí Núrí (1817–92). He was the son of Mírzá 'Abbás, known as Mírzá Buzurg Núrí (d. 1839), a landowning notable, former courtier, and provincial governor. Smith describes him as "one of the most socially prominent of the Babis" (lines 1255–1256). He converted to the Babi religion in 1844 and used his wealth and social position to provide support and protection to his co-religionists — "one of the few non-clerics to occupy a position of importance and authority within the movement" (lines 1257–1259).

**The Black Pit and the visions (1852):** Although Bahá'u'lláh opposed the militant Babi plottings, he was arrested in the aftermath of the attempt on the Shah's life. For four months he was imprisoned in the underground prison in Tehran called the "Black Pit" (Síyáh-Chál). According to his own later account, he experienced initiatory visions there: when he did sleep, he felt "that a torrent flowed down from his head over his body and every limb was set afire. He then 'recited what no man could bear to hear'" (lines 1275–1277). He also saw a sweet-voiced heavenly maiden who informed all in creation that he was the "Beauty of God" and the power of His sovereignty. A voice assured him that God would render him victorious (lines 1278–1281, citing Shoghi Effendi, *God Passes By* 101–2).

**Exile to Baghdad (1853):** Through the efforts of powerful relatives and friends, Bahá'u'lláh's release was secured. Deprived of his property and refusing an offer of asylum in Russia, he was exiled from Iranian territory, arriving in Baghdad in April 1853.

**Kurdistan retreat (1854–56):** Distressed by tensions with his half-brother Subh-i Azal (who resented Bahá'u'lláh's growing prominence but insisted on remaining hidden), Bahá'u'lláh withdrew from Baghdad in April 1854, living "a more or less solitary life as a dervish for two years in the mountains of Kurdistan" (lines 1294–1295), coming into contact with local Sufi leaders. He returned to Baghdad in March 1856 and was quickly recognized as the pre-eminent Babi leader.

**Baghdad period (1856–1863):** During seven years in Baghdad, Bahá'u'lláh worked to reinvigorate the Babi movement, receiving visitors from Iran, maintaining correspondence with Babi groups, and writing several influential works. He "gained increasing sympathy from both residents and visitors in Baghdad, including Ottoman officials, Iranian notables, and even Sunni and Shi'i clerics" (lines 1336–1338).

**Declaration at the Garden of Ridván (1863):** On the eve of his departure from Baghdad, between 22 April and 3 May 1863, in the Garden of "Ridván" (Rezván, "Paradise"), Bahá'u'lláh "announced a claim to spiritual authority to at least some" of his disciples (lines 1377–1378). This event is later commemorated by Bahá'ís as marking the inauguration of his ministry.

**Istanbul and Edirne (1863–68):** The Ottoman authorities invited Bahá'u'lláh to come to Istanbul, where he arrived in August 1863. Refusing to make political contacts and remaining aloof from government ministers, he was exiled to Edirne (Adrianople) in December 1863. It was during the Edirne period that the final break with his half-brother Subh-i Azal occurred; Bahá'u'lláh's claims were made generally known and the great majority of Babis became Bahá'ís.

**'Akká period (1868–1892):** In August 1868 Bahá'u'lláh, his family, and sixty-seven followers were dispatched to 'Akká — "the old crusader city of St Jean d'Acre — in Ottoman Syria" (line 1420). He lived at first in the fortress and then under house confinement. Three of the exiles grew sick and died; one of his sons (Mírzá Mihdí, "the Purest Branch") died in an accident whose sacrificial meaning his father acknowledged. By 1877 the order of confinement was effectively a dead letter and Bahá'u'lláh was allowed to move outside the city, eventually settling in the mansion of **Bahjí**, where he died on 29 May 1892.

## Primary Source Appearances

Smith describes Bahá'u'lláh as having written "at least 15,000 'Tablets' by him, mostly letters, but also including a number of substantial works" (lines 1541–1542). All his writings, including early works composed before his declaration, were accepted by his followers as divine revelation.

**Major early works (Baghdad period):**
- **The Hidden Words** (*Kalimát-i-Maknúnih*, c.1858) — emphasized the importance of the "spiritual path" and outlined its ethical and religious goals (lines 1307–1310).
- **The Book of Certitude** (*Kitáb-i-Íqán*, 1862) — provided the Babis with "a clear account of their religious doctrines, particularly the concept of a succession of Manifestations of God and the fulfilment of prophecy" (lines 1311–1314). Written before Bahá'u'lláh's declaration, in the messenger's role.
- **The Seven Valleys** — addressed themes of Islamic mysticism (Sufism) (line 1320).

**Major later works ('Akká period):**
- **Súriy-i-Mulúk** (Chapter of the Kings, 1868) — notional address to kings and political leaders; part of the proclamation to rulers.
- **Kitáb-i-Aqdas** (The Most Holy Book, c.1873) — the book of holy law, completed while still in 'Akká; supplemented by Bahá'u'lláh's replies to questions about it (lines 1773–1778).
- **Letters to individual rulers:** Sultan Abdulaziz, Nasiri'd-Dín Sháh, Napoleon III, Queen Victoria, Czar Alexander II, and Pope Pius IX; also notional addresses to the Austro-Hungarian Emperor Franz Joseph and the German Kaiser Wilhelm I (lines 1627–1707).
- **The "Fire Tablet"** — a dialogue between the voices of God and Bahá'u'lláh in which Bahá'u'lláh bewails his sufferings and God comforts him (lines 1563–1564).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Bahá'í reception:** Bahá'ís regard Bahá'u'lláh as a Manifestation of God — the promised one foretold by the Báb as "He whom God shall make manifest" and the fulfilment of the eschatological promises of all prior religions. His writings constitute divine revelation. Smith notes that with his claim to be a Manifestation of God, "all his writings assumed the status of divine revelation, including the early work composed prior to his declaration of mission" (lines 1537–1539).

**Contemporary non-Bahá'í reaction:** The English orientalist Edward Granville Browne visited Bahjí in 1890 and described Bahá'u'lláh as "a wondrous and venerable figure" whose face he "could neither describe nor forget" and whose piercing eyes "seemed to read one's very soul." Browne recorded that Bahá'u'lláh was "'the object of a devotion and love' from his followers 'which kings might envy and emperors sigh for in vain'" (lines 1459–1461). Browne's description is the only known account by a Western observer of a meeting with Bahá'u'lláh.

**Context of emergence from Babism:** From the Babi perspective, Bahá'u'lláh was initially one of the most prominent Babi lay leaders. The transition from Babi leader to the founder of a new religion occurred gradually: the declaration in the Garden of Ridván (1863) was made to only some disciples; in Edirne the claims were made generally known; and the great majority of Babis accepted him as the fulfillment of the Báb's prophecy "within a few years" (line 1384).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Smith identifies four major thematic registers in Bahá'u'lláh's writings (lines 1544–1551):

1. **Awareness of the divine presence** — sustained throughout his ministry; his writings give "an impression of both the ineffability and immediacy of the relationship he felt with the Godhead" (lines 1559–1561). Several writings take the form of dialogues between God and Bahá'u'lláh. At times he speaks "with the voice of God"; elsewhere he "compares himself to a leaf stirred by the winds of God's will, and transformed by him — wishing to be silent, but aroused by the Holy Spirit and bidden to speak" (lines 1570–1572, citing Shoghi Effendi, *God Passes By* 102).

2. **Ethics and the spiritual path** — from the Baghdad period onward, Bahá'u'lláh emphasized the requirements of the "true seeker" after God: "complete trustworthiness and honesty in all social dealings; wisdom; righteousness; charity; kindness to all, including animals; forgiveness; steadfastness; patience; humility and avoidance of backbiting" (lines 1583–1586). While employing Sufi phraseology, he was "far more concerned with outlining the ethical and religious requirements of daily life" than with mystical speculation (lines 1578–1579).

3. **Theology and metaphysics** — Bahá'u'lláh's theology "revolves around the Manifestations of God" (line 1607). God is in essence unknowable and can only be approached through his attributes, "most perfectly expressed in the persons of the Manifestations of God" (lines 1608–1610). Each Manifestation "brings a renewal of God's grace and gives his followers the teachings appropriate for that particular age, and on that basis a new divine civilization develops" (lines 1611–1614). Heaven and hell are spiritual states; "there is no bodily resurrection; and creation is eternal" (lines 1623–1624). In addition to the prophets listed in the Quran (Adam, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad), Bahá'u'lláh recognizes Zoroaster as a Manifestation, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá later added Krishna and the Buddha (lines 1614–1618).

4. **Addresses to world rulers and world order** — letters to specific rulers announcing his claims, reproving or praising them, and prescribing a reformed world order. The proclamation: Bahá'u'lláh "declares himself to be the king of kings. The kings are his vassals, but he has not come to seize their kingdoms" (lines 1640–1641). To Western rulers he stated that he was "the fulfilment of Christian prophecy and the return of Christ" (lines 1644–1646). Internationally, he advocated collective security, a world tribunal, and "a single language and script to be taught throughout the world as auxiliaries to the various mother tongues" (lines 1744–1746). He called the peoples of the world to the "Most Great Peace" — "the unification of religions and races and the transformation of the world into a single homeland in which the people would be as the 'leaves of one tree'" (lines 1749–1751).

**Holy Law (Kitáb-i-Aqdas):** The Aqdas lays down "a double obligation on all human beings: recognition of Bahá'u'lláh and obedience to his laws" (lines 1779–1780). Key obligations include individual daily ritual prayer, an annual nineteen-day fast, payment of the "right of God" (*huqúqu'lláh*) religious tax, employment in a trade or profession as a form of worship, education of children, and service to the Bahá'í Cause. In contrast to Islam, "these obligations are made matters of conscience" (lines 1793–1794). Various Islamic and Babi laws are explicitly abrogated, including Islamic restrictions on music, the Shi'i concept of ritual purity, and the Bayanic laws requiring destruction of non-Babi books and prohibiting marriage with non-believers. Bahá'ís are instead bidden "to associate with the followers of all religions in friendship and peace" (lines 1811–1813).

**Political quietism:** A theme already emerging in the Edirne period was Bahá'u'lláh's "strong emphasis on political quietism: the Baha'is were to eschew militancy and be loyal to established governments" (lines 1403–1404). This distinguished the Bahá'ís sharply from the Azalí Babis.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Bahá'u'lláh's writings became the primary authoritative canon of the Bahá'í Faith, treated as divine revelation. The *Kitáb-i-Íqán* established the doctrinal framework of successive Manifestations that revivified the Babi movement in Iran. In his will he appointed [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]] as his successor and "the centre of Bahá'u'lláh's covenant," and indicated that after 'Abdu'l-Bahá the "Universal House of Justice" would guide the Faith. He also stated that "there would be no further Manifestation of God for at least a thousand years" (lines 1473–1474).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2026-06-28)

### Life — Detailed Narrative

**Family and early life:** Bahá'u'lláh was born on 12 November 1817 in Tehran, the son of Mírzá 'Abbás (Mírzá Buzurg), a landowning notable from Núr in Mázandárán. His family traced its origins to the ancient Sassanian kings of Iran; Bahá'ís also believe he was a descendant of Abraham (through Keturah) and Zoroaster (lines 6251–6260). His father was forced out of office in 1835. As a youth Bahá'u'lláh showed strong religious and mystical tendencies. He married his first wife, Ásíyih (Navváb), in 1835, and his second, Fátimih (Mahd-i-'Ulyá), in 1849; his three eldest children ('Abdu'l-Bahá, [[bahiyyih-khanum|Bahíyyih Khánum]], and Mírzá Mihdí) were all born in Tehran (lines 6276–6285). He later married a third wife, Gawhar, whilst in Baghdad (date unknown) (line 6442–6444).

**Babi leadership:** Bahá'u'lláh became one of the most socially eminent Babis, organizing Táhirih's escape from Qazvín in 1847, and in 1848 played a principal role at the [[badasht|Badasht]] conference, where he took the name Bahá. He was arrested attempting to join the Babis at Shaykh Tabarsí (lines 6291–6299). The Báb appointed Bahá'u'lláh's young half-brother, Mírzá Yahyá Subh-i-Azal, to nominally "preserve" the religion (according to Bahá'í accounts, to shield Bahá'u'lláh from government attention) (lines 6305–6308).

**The Síyáh-Chál (1852):** Following the attempt on Násiru'd-Dín Sháh's life by militant Babis (15 August 1852), Bahá'u'lláh was arrested despite not being involved in the plot. He was thrown into the Síyáh-Chál dungeon in August, heavily chained. During his four months' imprisonment he underwent mystical experiences, including a vision of a heavenly Maiden who assured him of his divine mission — an event Bahá'ís regard as marking the birth of the Bahá'í revelation (lines 6321–6352). He was released in December 1852 following representations by his family and the Russian envoy, and was banished from Iran.

**Baghdad period in detail:** Bahá'u'lláh arrived in Baghdad on 8 April 1853, later joined there by Azal who had gone into hiding. Tensions with Azal led Bahá'u'lláh to withdraw to the mountains of Kurdistan (10 April 1854), initially living as a hermit near Sar-Galú, then coming into contact with Sufi leaders in Sulaymániyyih, where he wrote the **Ode of the Dove**. He returned to Baghdad on 19 March 1856, finding the Babi remnant dispirited and divided. From 1856 he worked to revive the community, writing the **Hidden Words** (c.1858), **Kitáb-i-Íqán** (1862), and mystical works addressed to Sufi leaders — the **Four Valleys** and **Seven Valleys** (lines 6380–6428).

**Ridván declaration (April–May 1863):** Immediately prior to departure from Baghdad, Bahá'u'lláh stayed twelve days in a garden he named Ridván ("Paradise"), from 22 April to 3 May 1863. Here he announced to some of his immediate disciples his claim to be the Babi promised one ("He whom God shall make manifest"), and commanded them to avoid sedition. He and his companions — some seventy persons — then traveled to Istanbul (lines 6453–6470).

**Edirne (December 1863–August 1868):** Tensions with Azal came to a head. According to Bahá'í accounts, Azal attempted to poison Bahá'u'lláh (c.1865); Azali sources make the counter-accusation that Bahá'u'lláh tried to poison Azal and consumed the poison himself by accident. Whatever the case, Bahá'u'lláh became very ill and his hair turned white; the effects were felt for the rest of his life (lines 6496–6507). Bahá'u'lláh then openly claimed to be "He Whom God Shall Make Manifest" (c. March 1866) and instituted a two-month separation ("Most Great Separation"), instructing Babis to choose between Azal and himself — almost all chose him (lines 6508–6518). A great outflow of writings followed, including the beginning of his proclamation to rulers. In September 1867 Azal challenged Bahá'u'lláh to *mubáhala* (a mutual appeal to divine judgment) at a local mosque but declined to attend (lines 6527–6532). Azali accusations of sedition to the Ottoman authorities led to investigation and the decision to exile both leaders (lines 6533–6550).

**'Akká and final years (1868–1892):** The Ottoman command of 26 July 1868 condemned Bahá'u'lláh and his entourage to perpetual banishment, imprisonment and isolation. The group of sixty-seven arrived in 'Akká on 31 August 1868; living conditions were at first appalling, with three exiles dying (lines 6563–6576). Bahá'u'lláh's son Mírzá Mihdí died following a fall on 23 June 1870 (line 6576). After leaving the barracks (4 November 1870), relations with local authorities gradually eased, largely through 'Abdu'l-Bahá's diplomacy (lines 6587–6592). After Sultan Abdulaziz's overthrow (30 May 1876) Bahá'u'lláh was able to leave 'Akká. He resided at the mansion of Mazra'ih (from June 1877) and then moved to [[bahji|Bahjí]] (September 1879), remaining there until his death on 29 May 1892 from a slight fever. He was buried in a room in an adjoining house — now the Shrine of Bahá'u'lláh and the Bahá'í *qiblah* (lines 6597–6608).

### Theological Status

Bahá'ís regard Bahá'u'lláh as: (1) the Manifestation of God for the present age; (2) the promised messianic figure of all previous religions; (3) the inaugurator of a new universal religious cycle. For Jews he was the "Everlasting Father" and "Lord of Hosts"; for Christians "Christ returned in the glory of the Father"; for Shi'i Muslims "the return of the Imám Husayn"; for Sunnis "the descent of Jesus, the Spirit of God"; for Zoroastrians Sháh-Bahrám; for Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna; for Buddhists the fifth Buddha; for Babis "He Whom God Shall Make Manifest" (lines 6678–6704). Bahá'u'lláh denied accusations that he had laid claim to divinity; Shoghi Effendi was insistent that while Bahá'u'lláh spoke with the voice of God, he was not to be conceived as God incarnate: "his 'human temple' remained 'entirely distinguished' from that 'eternal Essence of Essences' that was God" (lines 6757–6768).

### Personality

The English orientalist E.G. Browne, who had an audience with Bahá'u'lláh in 1890, described him as "a wondrous and venerable figure" whose face he could neither describe nor forget, with piercing eyes that "seemed to read one's very soul." His face was deeply lined with age; his "ample brow" emanated power. He wore his hair and beard long (the beard almost to his waist) and dyed them jet-black; he wore a tall dervish hat wound with a small white turban (lines 6662–6676).

### Catalog of Major Writings

Bahá'u'lláh composed approximately 15,000 "tablets" (lawhs), ranging from short letters to major treatises (line 6787). A selection from his writings catalog (lines 6813–7441):

**Iran/Síyáh-Chál:**
- *Rashh-i-'Amá* ("The Sprinkling of the Cloud") — short poem of exaltation (line 6824–6826).

**Iraqi period (Baghdad/Kurdistan):**
- *Lawh-i-Kullu't-Ta'ám* ("Tablet of 'All Food'") — Arabic; addressed to Hájí Mírzá Kamálu'd-dín Naráqí; commentary on a Quranic verse (lines 6831–6834).
- *Ode of the Dove* (*Qasída al-Warqá'iyyah*) — Arabic mystical work composed in Kurdistan (lines 6836–6838).
- **Four Valleys** (*Chahár vádí*) — Persian; mystical work addressed to Shaykh 'Abdu'r-Rahím Talabání, a Qádirí Sufi leader (lines 6847–6851).
- **[[hidden-words|Hidden Words]]** (*Kalimát-i-Maknúnih*) — c.1858; Persian and Arabic; counsels on the spiritual and ethical life (lines 6856–6859).
- *Jawáhiru'l-Asrár* ("The Essence of Mysteries") — c.1860–1; Arabic; written for a prominent Shi'i cleric, describing stages of the spiritual quest, commenting on biblical prophecies and Quranic verses related to the Mahdí, prefiguring the Kitáb-i-Íqán (lines 6873–6885).
- **[[kitab-i-iqan|Kitáb-i-Íqán]]** (Book of Certitude) — 1862; Persian; Bahá'u'lláh's foremost theological work (lines 6886–6889).
- *Súriy-i-Sabr* ("Chapter of Patience" / "Tablet of Job") — 1863; Arabic; praises the Babis at Nayrız, details the sufferings of the biblical Job, extols fortitude in suffering (lines 7007–7020).

**Rumelian period (Istanbul/Edirne):**
- *Kitáb-i-Badí'* ("The Wondrous Book") — apologia for Bahá'u'lláh, countering those who would support Azal (lines 7055–7073).
- *Súrat-i-Amr* ("Chapter of Command") — c. March 1866; statement of Bahá'u'lláh's status as divine revealer, read aloud to Azal by Mírzá Aqá Ján; shortly followed by the "Most Great Separation" (lines 7183–7190).
- *Súratu'l-Haykal* ("Chapter of the Temple") — first composed in Edirne, revised in 'Akká (c.1869); Bahá'u'lláh addresses himself as the promised biblical "Temple of God"; later ordered copied together with his five letters to world rulers (lines 7353–7371).
- *Súriy-i-Mulúk* ("Súra of the Kings") — Arabic; c.1867; proclamation to world rulers (line 7239–7240).
- First tablet to [[napoleon-iii|Napoleon III]] (line 7116–7118).

**Syrian period ('Akká/Mazra'ih/Bahjí):**
- **[[kitab-i-aqdas|Kitáb-i-Aqdas]]** ("The Most Holy Book") — Arabic; c.1873; Bahá'u'lláh's book of laws (lines 7250–7253).
- *Lawh-i-Qad Ihtaraqa'l-Mukhlisín* ("The Fire Tablet") — c.1871; Arabic; dialogue between Bahá'u'lláh and God; powerful invocatory quality (lines 7325–7336).
- *Lawh-i-Malikih* — tablet to Queen [[victoria|Victoria]] (line 7297–7298).
- *Lawh-i-Napulyún II* — 1869; Arabic; second tablet to Napoleon III, predicting his downfall (lines 7312–7314).
- *Lawh-i-Práp* — c.1869; Arabic; tablet to Pope Pius IX (line 7315–7316).
- *Lawh-i-Dunyá* ("Tablet of the World") — 1891 (line 7398–7399).
- **[[bisharat|Bishárát]]** ("Glad-Tidings") — late period; Persian (line 7376–7377).
- *Lawh-i-Karmil* ("Tablet of Carmel") — 1891; short Arabic tablet addressed to Mount Carmel (line 7430–7431).
- *Lawh-i-Ibn-i-Dhi'b* (**Epistle to the Son of the Wolf**) — 1891/2 (line 7432–7434).
- *Tarázát* ("Ornaments") — c.1889 (line 7440–7441).
- *Tajallíyát* ("Effulgences") — c.1885/6 (line 7438–7439).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### Book of the Covenant (Kitáb-i-'Ahd)
Bahá'u'lláh's last testament, written in Arabic in his own handwriting, given to 'Abdu'l-Bahá during his final illness (May 1892). The document was read both to a select group of witnesses and to a large gathering of Bahá'ís on the ninth day after Bahá'u'lláh's passing. It explicitly appointed 'Abdu'l-Bahá (the *ghusn-i-a'zam*, Most Great Branch) as his successor, and directed his family and the Afnán relatives of the Báb to turn to him. Bahá'u'lláh's second surviving son, Mírzá Muḥammad-'Alí (*ghusn-i-akbar*, Greatest Branch), was subordinate to 'Abdu'l-Bahá. The document also stated that conflict is forbidden; rulership is entrusted to kings; and the learned among the Bahá'ís are Bahá'u'lláh's trustees (lines 10003–10045). See [[covenant-bahai|Covenant (Bahá'í)]].

### Epistle to the Son of the Wolf
Last major work (c.1891), composed at [[bahji|Bahjí]], addressed to Shaykh Muḥammad-Taqí Iṣfaháni (Áqá Najafí), named 'the Son of the Wolf', a prominent Iranian cleric who persecuted the Bahá'ís. Bahá'u'lláh called on the Shaykh to repent, declared his own mission, summarized his teachings, called for protection for his followers, presented biblical prophecies fulfilled by his coming, and lamented the behaviour of [[subh-i-azal|Subh-i-Azal]] (lines 11775–11804). See [[epistle-to-the-son-of-the-wolf|Epistle to the Son of the Wolf]].

### Divine Judgement
Bahá'u'lláh stated that whilst his mercy 'encompassed all created things', the fierceness of his anger against the wicked could be terrible, such that they might receive grievous chastisement (lines 10791–10796). God did not blink at the tyranny of the oppressor (line 10797–10799). Shoghi Effendi described the early deaths of Muḥammad Sháh, Sultan Abdulaziz, Amír Kabír, and others — as well as the loss of power of Napoleon III and Pope Pius IX — as divine responses to their opposition to the Faith (lines 10800–10843).

### Teachings on Civilization
Bahá'u'lláh stated that whatever passed beyond the bounds of moderation would cease to exert a beneficial influence. Carried to excess, material civilization — as embodied in Western technological advances — has become pernicious, producing terrible weapons of destruction. A true 'ever-advancing' civilization is based on virtues that enhance human dignity (lines 10176–10187).

### Consultation
Bahá'u'lláh taught that consultation enhances understanding, awareness, and certitude; it is 'a light in the heaven of divine wisdom and a prerequisite of human well-being' (lines 10669–10673). See [[consultation|Consultation (Bahá'í)]].

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### Writings: Kitáb-i-Íqán
The [[kitab-i-iqan|Kitáb-i-Íqán]] (Book of Certitude) is regarded by Shoghi Effendi as Bahá'u'lláh's outstanding doctrinal work and "the basis for unity between the religions of the world" (l. 18788–18789). Composed in Persian in Baghdad in 1861–62, it argues that rejection of the Báb echoed earlier rejections of prophets throughout history, and that literalistic interpretation of prophecy prevents recognition of new divine messengers. The book also contains cryptic allusions to Bahá'u'lláh's own forthcoming claims (l. 18872–18879). See [[kitab-i-iqan|Kitáb-i-Íqán]] for full entry.

### Writings: Ishraqát (Splendours)
The Ishraqát is a late Akka-period tablet composed in Arabic and Persian for Jalíl-i-Khá'í, a Bahá'í coppersmith from Ádharbáyján (l. 19400–19404). Key contents include: a call to Bábís to recognize Bahá'u'lláh (l. 19405–19408); proclamation of his "most great infallibility" (l. 19410–19412); nine "splendours" encompassing religion as the stronghold of social order (l. 19430–19432); kingship as a manifestation of divine power (l. 19433–19436); the centrality of divine law (l. 19436–19437); the unity of the human race and the need for a universal language (l. 19444–19447); the importance of education and parenthood (l. 19451–19453); and the responsibilities of the House of Justice (l. 19456–19459). The tablet also permits moderate interest on loans, in contradistinction to Islamic law (l. 19427–19429).

### Writings: Súra of the Kings
The Súra of the Kings (*Súratu'l-Mulúk*) is an Arabic tablet composed in Edirne around 1867 as part of Bahá'u'lláh's proclamation to the rulers (l. 20341–20344). In it the kings of the earth are summoned to heed Bahá'u'lláh's call; they are reproved for not recognizing the Báb; called to rule with justice; warned against excessive armaments; and urged to become reconciled so as to reduce expenditure. Christian monarchs are separately addressed; Sultan 'Abdu'l-'Azíz is also addressed (l. 20345–20383).

### Teaching on Human Nature
Bahá'u'lláh taught that human beings are created because of divine love and in order to love God in return (*Hidden Words* nos. 3–4; l. 17118–17119). Uniquely, the human reality (soul) can mirror all of God's names and attributes, and humans have been made in the "image" of God (l. 17120–17125). Human potential is likened to "a mine rich in gems of inestimable value" (l. 17126–17127), but these treasures can only be revealed through divine grace and spiritual education (l. 17127–17135).

### Teaching on Justice
Bahá'u'lláh declared justice to be "the best beloved of all things in the world" and a divine gift through which human beings see and know for themselves (l. 17922–17927). It is upheld by "the twin pillars of reward and punishment" (l. 17932–17933). If the world's rulers dedicated themselves to the highest interests of humanity as a whole, then "the reign of justice would be established and the world sanctified from tyranny" (l. 17936–17941).

### Teaching on Human Unity
Bahá'u'lláh taught that the human race is "one indivisible whole: the fruits of one tree, and the leaves of one branch" (*Tablets* 164; l. 17185–17188). The earth is a single homeland. Glory lies in upright conduct, not in nationality or rank. Service to the entire human race and promotion of its best interests is called for (l. 17196–17199). Unity would be promoted by tolerance and the adoption of a universal language (l. 17200–17203).

### Teaching on Kingship
Bahá'u'lláh regarded "the majesty of kingship" as one of the "signs of God"; kings manifested divine power and were the "dayspring of his authority" (l. 20267–20274). In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas he declared himself to be the King of Kings (l. 20322–20323). He favoured constitutional monarchy as combining "the majesty of kingship with the advantages of democratic government for the common people" (*Tablets* 28; l. 20288–20296).
