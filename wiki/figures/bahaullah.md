# Bahá'u'lláh

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

Bahá'u'lláh ("The Splendour" or "Glory of God") was the religious title of Mírzá Husayn-'Alí Núrí (1817–92). He was the son of Mírzá 'Abbás, known as Mírzá Buzurg Núrí (d. 1839), a landowning notable, former courtier, and provincial governor. Smith describes him as "one of the most socially prominent of the Babis" (lines 1255–1256). He converted to the Babi religion in 1844 and used his wealth and social position to provide support and protection to his co-religionists — "one of the few non-clerics to occupy a position of importance and authority within the movement" (lines 1257–1259).

**The Black Pit and the visions (1852):** Although Bahá'u'lláh opposed the militant Babi plottings, he was arrested in the aftermath of the attempt on the Shah's life. For four months he was imprisoned in the underground prison in Tehran called the "Black Pit" (Síyáh-Chál). According to his own later account, he experienced initiatory visions there: when he did sleep, he felt "that a torrent flowed down from his head over his body and every limb was set afire. He then 'recited what no man could bear to hear'" (lines 1275–1277). He also saw a sweet-voiced heavenly maiden who informed all in creation that he was the "Beauty of God" and the power of His sovereignty. A voice assured him that God would render him victorious (lines 1278–1281, citing Shoghi Effendi, *God Passes By* 101–2).

**Exile to Baghdad (1853):** Through the efforts of powerful relatives and friends, Bahá'u'lláh's release was secured. Deprived of his property and refusing an offer of asylum in Russia, he was exiled from Iranian territory, arriving in Baghdad in April 1853.

**Kurdistan retreat (1854–56):** Distressed by tensions with his half-brother Subh-i Azal (who resented Bahá'u'lláh's growing prominence but insisted on remaining hidden), Bahá'u'lláh withdrew from Baghdad in April 1854, living "a more or less solitary life as a dervish for two years in the mountains of Kurdistan" (lines 1294–1295), coming into contact with local Sufi leaders. He returned to Baghdad in March 1856 and was quickly recognized as the pre-eminent Babi leader.

**Baghdad period (1856–1863):** During seven years in Baghdad, Bahá'u'lláh worked to reinvigorate the Babi movement, receiving visitors from Iran, maintaining correspondence with Babi groups, and writing several influential works. He "gained increasing sympathy from both residents and visitors in Baghdad, including Ottoman officials, Iranian notables, and even Sunni and Shi'i clerics" (lines 1336–1338).

**Declaration at the Garden of Ridván (1863):** On the eve of his departure from Baghdad, between 22 April and 3 May 1863, in the Garden of "Ridván" (Rezván, "Paradise"), Bahá'u'lláh "announced a claim to spiritual authority to at least some" of his disciples (lines 1377–1378). This event is later commemorated by Bahá'ís as marking the inauguration of his ministry.

**Istanbul and Edirne (1863–68):** The Ottoman authorities invited Bahá'u'lláh to come to Istanbul, where he arrived in August 1863. Refusing to make political contacts and remaining aloof from government ministers, he was exiled to Edirne (Adrianople) in December 1863. It was during the Edirne period that the final break with his half-brother Subh-i Azal occurred; Bahá'u'lláh's claims were made generally known and the great majority of Babis became Bahá'ís.

**'Akká period (1868–1892):** In August 1868 Bahá'u'lláh, his family, and sixty-seven followers were dispatched to 'Akká — "the old crusader city of St Jean d'Acre — in Ottoman Syria" (line 1420). He lived at first in the fortress and then under house confinement. Three of the exiles grew sick and died; one of his sons (Mírzá Mihdí, "the Purest Branch") died in an accident whose sacrificial meaning his father acknowledged. By 1877 the order of confinement was effectively a dead letter and Bahá'u'lláh was allowed to move outside the city, eventually settling in the mansion of **Bahjí**, where he died on 29 May 1892.

## Primary Source Appearances

Smith describes Bahá'u'lláh as having written "at least 15,000 'Tablets' by him, mostly letters, but also including a number of substantial works" (lines 1541–1542). All his writings, including early works composed before his declaration, were accepted by his followers as divine revelation.

**Major early works (Baghdad period):**
- **The Hidden Words** (*Kalimát-i-Maknúnih*, c.1858) — emphasized the importance of the "spiritual path" and outlined its ethical and religious goals (lines 1307–1310).
- **The Book of Certitude** (*Kitáb-i-Íqán*, 1862) — provided the Babis with "a clear account of their religious doctrines, particularly the concept of a succession of Manifestations of God and the fulfilment of prophecy" (lines 1311–1314). Written before Bahá'u'lláh's declaration, in the messenger's role.
- **The Seven Valleys** — addressed themes of Islamic mysticism (Sufism) (line 1320).

**Major later works ('Akká period):**
- **Súriy-i-Mulúk** (Chapter of the Kings, 1868) — notional address to kings and political leaders; part of the proclamation to rulers.
- **Kitáb-i-Aqdas** (The Most Holy Book, c.1873) — the book of holy law, completed while still in 'Akká; supplemented by Bahá'u'lláh's replies to questions about it (lines 1773–1778).
- **Letters to individual rulers:** Sultan Abdulaziz, Nasiri'd-Dín Sháh, Napoleon III, Queen Victoria, Czar Alexander II, and Pope Pius IX; also notional addresses to the Austro-Hungarian Emperor Franz Joseph and the German Kaiser Wilhelm I (lines 1627–1707).
- **The "Fire Tablet"** — a dialogue between the voices of God and Bahá'u'lláh in which Bahá'u'lláh bewails his sufferings and God comforts him (lines 1563–1564).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Bahá'í reception:** Bahá'ís regard Bahá'u'lláh as a Manifestation of God — the promised one foretold by the Báb as "He whom God shall make manifest" and the fulfilment of the eschatological promises of all prior religions. His writings constitute divine revelation. Smith notes that with his claim to be a Manifestation of God, "all his writings assumed the status of divine revelation, including the early work composed prior to his declaration of mission" (lines 1537–1539).

**Contemporary non-Bahá'í reaction:** The English orientalist Edward Granville Browne visited Bahjí in 1890 and described Bahá'u'lláh as "a wondrous and venerable figure" whose face he "could neither describe nor forget" and whose piercing eyes "seemed to read one's very soul." Browne recorded that Bahá'u'lláh was "'the object of a devotion and love' from his followers 'which kings might envy and emperors sigh for in vain'" (lines 1459–1461). Browne's description is the only known account by a Western observer of a meeting with Bahá'u'lláh.

**Context of emergence from Babism:** From the Babi perspective, Bahá'u'lláh was initially one of the most prominent Babi lay leaders. The transition from Babi leader to the founder of a new religion occurred gradually: the declaration in the Garden of Ridván (1863) was made to only some disciples; in Edirne the claims were made generally known; and the great majority of Babis accepted him as the fulfillment of the Báb's prophecy "within a few years" (line 1384).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Smith identifies four major thematic registers in Bahá'u'lláh's writings (lines 1544–1551):

1. **Awareness of the divine presence** — sustained throughout his ministry; his writings give "an impression of both the ineffability and immediacy of the relationship he felt with the Godhead" (lines 1559–1561). Several writings take the form of dialogues between God and Bahá'u'lláh. At times he speaks "with the voice of God"; elsewhere he "compares himself to a leaf stirred by the winds of God's will, and transformed by him — wishing to be silent, but aroused by the Holy Spirit and bidden to speak" (lines 1570–1572, citing Shoghi Effendi, *God Passes By* 102).

2. **Ethics and the spiritual path** — from the Baghdad period onward, Bahá'u'lláh emphasized the requirements of the "true seeker" after God: "complete trustworthiness and honesty in all social dealings; wisdom; righteousness; charity; kindness to all, including animals; forgiveness; steadfastness; patience; humility and avoidance of backbiting" (lines 1583–1586). While employing Sufi phraseology, he was "far more concerned with outlining the ethical and religious requirements of daily life" than with mystical speculation (lines 1578–1579).

3. **Theology and metaphysics** — Bahá'u'lláh's theology "revolves around the Manifestations of God" (line 1607). God is in essence unknowable and can only be approached through his attributes, "most perfectly expressed in the persons of the Manifestations of God" (lines 1608–1610). Each Manifestation "brings a renewal of God's grace and gives his followers the teachings appropriate for that particular age, and on that basis a new divine civilization develops" (lines 1611–1614). Heaven and hell are spiritual states; "there is no bodily resurrection; and creation is eternal" (lines 1623–1624). In addition to the prophets listed in the Quran (Adam, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad), Bahá'u'lláh recognizes Zoroaster as a Manifestation, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá later added Krishna and the Buddha (lines 1614–1618).

4. **Addresses to world rulers and world order** — letters to specific rulers announcing his claims, reproving or praising them, and prescribing a reformed world order. The proclamation: Bahá'u'lláh "declares himself to be the king of kings. The kings are his vassals, but he has not come to seize their kingdoms" (lines 1640–1641). To Western rulers he stated that he was "the fulfilment of Christian prophecy and the return of Christ" (lines 1644–1646). Internationally, he advocated collective security, a world tribunal, and "a single language and script to be taught throughout the world as auxiliaries to the various mother tongues" (lines 1744–1746). He called the peoples of the world to the "Most Great Peace" — "the unification of religions and races and the transformation of the world into a single homeland in which the people would be as the 'leaves of one tree'" (lines 1749–1751).

**Holy Law (Kitáb-i-Aqdas):** The Aqdas lays down "a double obligation on all human beings: recognition of Bahá'u'lláh and obedience to his laws" (lines 1779–1780). Key obligations include individual daily ritual prayer, an annual nineteen-day fast, payment of the "right of God" (*huqúqu'lláh*) religious tax, employment in a trade or profession as a form of worship, education of children, and service to the Bahá'í Cause. In contrast to Islam, "these obligations are made matters of conscience" (lines 1793–1794). Various Islamic and Babi laws are explicitly abrogated, including Islamic restrictions on music, the Shi'i concept of ritual purity, and the Bayanic laws requiring destruction of non-Babi books and prohibiting marriage with non-believers. Bahá'ís are instead bidden "to associate with the followers of all religions in friendship and peace" (lines 1811–1813).

**Political quietism:** A theme already emerging in the Edirne period was Bahá'u'lláh's "strong emphasis on political quietism: the Baha'is were to eschew militancy and be loyal to established governments" (lines 1403–1404). This distinguished the Bahá'ís sharply from the Azalí Babis.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Bahá'u'lláh's writings became the primary authoritative canon of the Bahá'í Faith, treated as divine revelation. The *Kitáb-i-Íqán* established the doctrinal framework of successive Manifestations that revivified the Babi movement in Iran. In his will he appointed [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]] as his successor and "the centre of Bahá'u'lláh's covenant," and indicated that after 'Abdu'l-Bahá the "Universal House of Justice" would guide the Faith. He also stated that "there would be no further Manifestation of God for at least a thousand years" (lines 1473–1474).
