## Biographical Overview

Guru Tegh Bahadur was the ninth Guru of the Sikhs, a younger son of [[guru-hargobind|Guru Hargobind]], passed over as Hargobind's successor because tradition reported he was "too withdrawn" — though McLeod notes that "his later selection as the ninth Guru casts some doubt on this tradition" (McLeod ~L53–55).

He was identified as Guru through the dying pronouncement of [[guru-har-krishan|Guru Har Krishan]] (*Bābā Bakāle*). Legend has it that twenty-two claimants appeared at Bakala; the merchant Makhan Shah identified the true Guru by laying only one or two gold mohurs before each claimant, whereupon Tegh Bahadur alone asked for the balance of the five hundred promised during a sea storm (McLeod ~L253–265). McLeod regards this as "legend, though a very attractive one," suggesting the more probable contest was between followers of Dhir Mai and Tegh Bahadur (McLeod ~L266–278).

## Establishment as Guru

After securing his position against rivals (the Minas, followers of Dhir Mai and Ram Rai), Tegh Bahadur left Bakala and settled at Makhoval in the Shivalik hills (McLeod ~L293–300). In 1665 he departed for a lengthy eastern tour, travelling as far as Assam. His wife was left at Patna, Bihar, where in December 1666 she gave birth to the future [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]] (McLeod ~L301–318).

## The Martyrdom: Two Accounts

The circumstances of Tegh Bahadur's execution in Delhi present a sharp conflict between Sikh and Muslim sources — a case where McLeod explicitly refuses to adjudicate.

**The Muslim account**: the Guru had gathered a body of men and was levying forced exactions from opponents. A warrant was issued; he was apprehended in Agra, taken to Delhi, and "executed there for his misdemeanours. The charge was lawlessness and on this charge he was adjudged guilty" (McLeod ~L337–349).

**The dominant Sikh account**: Aurangzeb was persecuting Brahmans of Kashmir, compelling them to convert to Islam. A deputation of five hundred Brahmans appealed to Guru Tegh Bahadur. The Guru's own son (the future Gobind Singh) urged his father to accept the challenge. Tegh Bahadur told the Brahmans to communicate a challenge to Aurangzeb: if the emperor could persuade him to become a Muslim, they too would convert; if not, they should be freed. Taken to Delhi, "he refused to meet the emperor's demand that he should embrace Islam and was accordingly executed in Chandni Chowk" (McLeod ~L373–374). *Bachitar Nātak*, attributed to Guru Gobind Singh himself, expresses this as: "For the cause of truth he performed this deed, giving his head in obedience to his resolve" (McLeod ~L383–385).

**An alternative Sikh tradition**: The *Chaupa Singh Rahit-nāmā*, dated near the middle of the eighteenth century, makes no mention of the Kashmiri Brahmans. According to this source, Tegh Bahadur was summoned to Delhi on complaints lodged by Dhir Mai, successfully answered them, was allowed to return to Punjab, then recalled, failed to appear, and was arrested (McLeod ~L419–427). McLeod notes this source is "relatively early" and was "written by a Sikh who was also a Brahman" who "normally… lost no opportunity to exalt other members of the same caste" — making its silence on the Kashmiri Brahmans story significant (McLeod ~L425–428).

McLeod concludes: "Whatever the explanation, there can be no doubt concerning the effect of the Guru's death on the Panth. The Mughal administration was identified as the greatest of its enemies" (McLeod ~L429–431).

## Significance of the Martyrdom

Tegh Bahadur's execution had a profound effect on his son and successor, Guru Gobind Singh. It was the direct catalyst for the founding of the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] — the resolution that Sikhs must bear outward symbols making them identifiable in any crowd (McLeod ~L444–450).

## Historicity and Controversies

The conflict between Sikh and Muslim accounts remains unresolved. McLeod treats both accounts as evidence for reconstruction, noting that Aurangzeb "shows increasing signs of serious exaggeration as time passes" as a persecutor in Sikh tradition, while acknowledging he "seems clearly to have been determined to protect the interests of Sunni orthodoxy" (McLeod ~L333–335).
