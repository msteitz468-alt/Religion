# Matelda

The beautiful woman whom [[dante-alighieri|Dante]] finds gathering flowers and
singing in the Garden of Eden atop the mountain of [[purgatorio|Purgatory]] (Cantos
28–33; she is named only at 33:119). She presides over the **Earthly Paradise**,
explains its workings, and serves as Dante's **baptismal sponsor**, drawing him
through the rivers Lethe and Eunoë.

## In the Divine Comedy

Dante first sees her across the stream Lethe, "a donna all alone who walked along, /
singing and choosing flowers to pluck from flowers" (28:40–42), her eyes shining
like Venus's. She explains the meteorology of Eden — the mountain rising above
earthly turbulence, the self-renewing trees, and the twin rivers springing from
God's will: **Lethe**, which removes the memory of sin, and **Eunoë** (Dante's own
coinage, "good memory"), which restores the memory of good done (28:121–30). She
tells Dante that the classical poets who dreamed of a Golden Age "thought, maybe
here's Parnassus" (28:139–41). At the climax she immerses him in Lethe (Canto 31)
and finally leads him, with [[statius|Statius]], to drink from Eunoë (Canto 33).

## Significance and Identity

Kirkpatrick records the long-debated identification with **Matilda, Countess of
Canossa** (1046–1115), the great supporter of the reform papacy — but stresses the
debate is unresolved and the **allegorical** role is clearer: Matelda images the
**natural happiness humanity would have enjoyed without the Fall**, a "new Eve," the
full participation in the created order achievable through the moral and
intellectual virtues. She is what [[virgil|Virgil]] "would have been" had his
virtues not been severed from fulfilment — the bridge between natural goodness and
the revelation embodied in [[beatrice|Beatrice]].
