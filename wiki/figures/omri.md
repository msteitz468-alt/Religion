Omri, commander of the army of Israel who became king after a civil war and founded the dynasty that
bears his name, receives one of the shortest royal notices in Kings and is the most externally
prominent Israelite king before the fall of [[samaria|Samaria]]. He bought the hill of Samaria and
built the capital there; he is the father of [[ahab|Ahab]] and, through the marriage into Judah, the
ancestor behind [[athaliah|Athaliah]].

## Biographical Overview

1 Kings 16 places Omri's rise in the northern kingdom's most unstable stretch. Elah son of Baasha is
assassinated by Zimri, a chariot commander, who reigns seven days. The army, encamped against a
Philistine town, makes Omri king in the field; he goes up and besieges Tirzah, and Zimri burns the
palace over himself. A rival, Tibni son of Ginath, contests the throne; the people are divided until
Tibni's party is overcome and Tibni dies. Only then does Omri reign unopposed.

He reigns in Tirzah, then buys the hill of Samaria from Shemer for a price in silver and builds a
city there, naming it after the hill's owner. The narrator's evaluation is the standard northern
condemnation, intensified: Omri did evil, more than all who were before him, walking in the way of
Jeroboam son of Nebat. He sleeps with his fathers, is buried in Samaria, and is succeeded by Ahab.
That is effectively the whole account.

Later texts refer back to him. Micah 6:16 speaks of the observance of "the statutes of Omri" and the
works of the house of Ahab as a byword for what Israel is judged for — an indication that the
dynasty's name carried weight beyond its narrative space.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 16:15–28** — Zimri's seven days, the army's acclamation of Omri at the siege, the
  Tibni conflict, the purchase and building of Samaria, the evaluation formula, death and burial.
- **1 Kings 16:29–33** — the succession of Ahab, "son of Omri," and the Baal temple in Samaria.
- **2 Kings 8:26** — Athaliah described as "daughter of Omri," while 2 Kgs 8:18 associates her with
  the house of Ahab; the texts do not resolve the relationship (see [[athaliah|Athaliah]]).
- **Micah 6:16** — "the statutes of Omri" as a formula of indictment.
- **2 Chronicles 22:2** — the Chronicler's parallel notice of Athaliah's Omride descent. Omri
  himself is otherwise outside the Chronicler's northern-kingdom omissions.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation in Kings** — Omri is measured entirely by the Jeroboam standard, and
  the political achievements the narrative does record (winning the civil war, founding a capital)
  are given no approving comment. Recorded as the narrator's theological criterion, not as a
  historical assessment.
- **[[iain-provan|Provan]] (NIBC Kings, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] and the source page)**
  — Omri's reign falls in the block Provan treats as "schism through Omride rise," where "the sin of
  Jeroboam" is installed as northern identity and prophecy-fulfillment is the engine of the
  narrative. Attributed to Provan.
- This wiki has ingested no rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic treatment of Omri, and none is asserted
  here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Omri's function in Kings is dynastic rather than personal: he ends the cycle of one-reign usurpations
that began after [[jeroboam|Jeroboam]] and establishes a house stable enough to last four
generations, marry into the Davidic line, and require [[jehu|Jehu]]'s purge to remove. The founding
of Samaria matters structurally — the northern kingdom acquires a capital that is a royal
possession by purchase, and Samaria thereafter stands as the metonym for the north in the prophetic
books and in the account of the Assyrian conquest.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **External attestation [recorded, hedged].** The Mesha Stele, a Moabite royal inscription of King
  Mesha recovered at Dhiban, refers to Israelite domination of Moab in connection with Omri and his
  successor. The standard reading takes the inscription to name Omri as king of Israel and to
  present Moab's liberation as a reversal of oppression begun under him. The stele is a royal
  propaganda text on its own terms; some of its lines are damaged and their restoration is debated,
  and its chronology relative to Kings is disputed. No specific figures, regnal lengths, or restored
  readings are quoted here.
- **The Assyrian dynastic label [recorded, hedged].** Neo-Assyrian royal texts refer to the northern
  kingdom by a designation conventionally rendered "house of Omri," a usage that continues after the
  Omride dynasty itself had been replaced. The conventional reading is that Assyrian scribal practice
  fixed the dynastic name as the name of the polity. The details of individual inscriptions are not
  reproduced here beyond that general point.
- **The tension between textual space and external prominence [recorded tension, not adjudicated].**
  Kings gives Omri roughly six verses and an unqualified condemnation; the external material that
  names him or his house makes the dynasty the most visible Israelite regime of its period. Many
  historians read this as evidence that the biblical narrator's selection criteria are theological
  rather than political — the text is not attempting the assessment the inscriptions permit. Others
  treat the disproportion as a problem for the narrative's historical coverage. Both are recorded as
  positions. The wiki notes that the two bodies of evidence are answering different questions and
  offers no verdict on the reliability of Kings.
- **The Tibni conflict.** The length and nature of the divided rule, and how it fits the regnal
  arithmetic of 1 Kings 16, are disputed; the chronology of the divided monarchy is contested
  generally.
- **Omri's origins.** No patronymic or tribal affiliation is given. Proposals about his background
  are reconstructions, not textual data.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Omri is a fixed point in the historiography of the Iron Age Levant precisely because the biblical
and epigraphic profiles diverge so sharply, and he anchors the debate over the archaeology of
[[samaria|Samaria]] and the reassignment of monumental building programs to the Omride period, a
dispute also recorded on [[ahab|the Ahab page]] and not settled here. Within the biblical text he
supplies the dynastic label — house of Omri, house of Ahab — that organizes the northern narrative
down to [[jehu|Jehu]]. Cross-links: [[ahab|Ahab]], [[jezebel|Jezebel]], [[athaliah|Athaliah]],
