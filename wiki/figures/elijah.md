**Biographical Overview**

Elijah the Tishbite (from Tishbe in Gilead), preeminent prophet in the northern kingdom during the Omride dynasty (Ahab, Ahaziah, 9th cent. BCE). Confronts Ahab and Jezebel over Baal worship introduced via the Sidonian alliance. Key episodes: drought and widow of Zarephath (1 Kgs 17), Carmel confrontation with 450 prophets of Baal (18 — fire from heaven, "The LORD, he is God!"), flight to Horeb and the "still small voice" / "sound of sheer silence" with recommissioning (19), Naboth's vineyard denunciation (21), and ascension in a whirlwind with chariot of fire while Elisha receives the double portion (2 Kgs 2). Also appears in the contest with the prophets of Baal on Carmel and the healing/revival contexts. Malachi promises his return before the great and dreadful day of the LORD (Mal 4:5–6).

**Primary Source Appearances**

- **1 Kings 17–19, 21**: Drought, Zarephath, Carmel (the great theophany contest), Horeb (still small voice), Naboth's vineyard (judicial murder and prophetic denunciation).
- **2 Kings 1–2**: Confrontation with Ahaziah's messengers; ascension witnessed by Elisha.
- **Malachi 4:5–6**: Eschatological return to reconcile families before the Day of YHWH.
- **NT**: John the Baptist identified as the Elijah who "is to come" (Matt 11:14; 17:10–13; Mark 9:11–13; Luke 1:17); appears with Moses at the Transfiguration (Matt 17; Mark 9; Luke 9); Jesus invokes Elijah's Zarephath widow and Elisha's Naaman in Luke 4:25–27.
- **James 5:17–18**: Example of righteous man's effective prayer (the drought).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**

**Judaism**: Elijah is the herald of the Messiah (*Eliyahu ha-Navi*), expected to resolve halakhic disputes, attend every circumcision (*brit milah* — chair for Elijah), and every Passover seder (cup of Elijah, door opened for him). Rich folklore: ascends to heaven alive, appears to sages (e.g., in Talmudic stories), zealot for Torah. Passover liturgy and the "Elijah" songs celebrate his return.

**Christianity**: The paradigmatic prophet of judgment and forerunner. Explicitly identified with John the Baptist (the "Elijah" who prepares the way). At the Transfiguration he represents the Prophets alongside Moses (Law) witnessing to Christ. His miracles (especially provision for the widow and raising the dead) prefigure Jesus' ministry to the marginalized and power over death. James cites him for the power of prayer. In patristic and medieval typology, Elijah models the ascetic prophet and the zeal for God's honor.

**Islam**: Ilyas (Elias), a prophet sent to his people (often linked to the Israelites of the northern kingdom) to call them from worship of Baal (Ba'l) back to Allah alone (Q 6:85; 37:123–132). He is praised for his steadfastness; some traditions connect him with al-Khidr or other immortal figures. The Carmel contest is sometimes referenced in popular piety.

**Kabbalah (the Zohar)**: The [[zohar|Zohar]] inherits and develops the rabbinic motif of Elijah's perpetual, invisible presence at every **circumcision** (*brit milah*). Because he never died but ascended alive (2 Kgs 2), Elijah is appointed to witness Israel keeping the covenant of [[sefirot|Yesod]] — the very sefirah of the covenant and the channel of divine blessing to the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]. The Zohar (Parashat Lekh Lekha, on the covenant of Abraham) explains the custom of preparing a **"chair of Elijah"** (*kisei shel Eliyahu*) at each circumcision: because Elijah had once complained that Israel "forsook the covenant" (1 Kgs 19:10, 14), God decreed that he must be present at every circumcision to *see* that Israel in fact upholds it. The Zohar describes Elijah "sweeping through the world" in an instant to be present at each rite simultaneously — a flight made possible by his angelic, deathless nature. The act of circumcision thus becomes a theurgical sealing of the covenant in the flesh, witnessed by the deathless prophet, that strengthens the bond of Yesod and promotes the union of Tif'eret and the Shekhinah. See [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]], [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]] (Yesod), [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]].

**Theological and Narrative Significance**

In the Deuteronomistic History (Provan), Elijah is the longest and most developed prophetic narrative block in Kings — the "agent of salvation within judgment." He embodies the absolute claim of YHWH alone ("If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him," 18:21) and demonstrates that YHWH, not Baal, controls fertility, rain, life, and death. The Carmel theophany (fire from heaven) is the public, undeniable demonstration that YHWH is God; the still small voice at Horeb shows that YHWH is also present in subtlety after the great theophanies of the past (Sinai). His zeal ("I have been very zealous for the LORD," 19:10) is both model and warning (Jesus rebukes the disciples who want to call down fire "as Elijah did"). In the larger Former Prophets he stands as the great opponent of syncretism and the one who keeps the prophetic word alive in the darkest period of northern apostasy. The ascension (bodily removal) and the double-portion transfer to Elisha underscore the continuity of the prophetic word.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**

- **Historical core**: Widely accepted that a historical prophet Elijah stood behind the cycle; the drought and Carmel events have some ANE parallels (rain-making contests, prophetic guilds).
- **Miraculous elements**: The fire from heaven, the raising of the widow's son, the chariot ascension, and the "still small voice" theophany are debated in critical scholarship as legendary developments or theological dramatizations. Traditional readings affirm the historicity of the events as presented.
- **Relationship to Elisha cycle**: Elijah is the solitary zealot; Elisha is more communal and political. The transfer of the mantle and the "double portion" raise questions about prophetic succession and power.
- **NT identification with John**: The Synoptics and Jesus himself make the identification; Malachi provides the scriptural warrant. Some critical views see this as early Christian midrash rather than historical prediction.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**

Provan (NIBC) gives dedicated excursuses on Elijah (and Elisha) "in Canonical Context," tracing the figure from Kings through Malachi into the NT (John the Baptist, Transfiguration, Luke 4) and emphasizing his role as the paradigmatic prophet who confronts idolatry and calls for exclusive allegiance. The narrative is read as didactic: even in judgment God sends agents of salvation and revelation. Leithart and typological readers see Elijah as the model for the Church's prophetic witness. Jewish liturgy and midrash make him a living presence at every Jewish home and table. Christian preaching uses Carmel for the exclusivity of God, Horeb for discerning God's voice in silence, and the ascension/return for Advent/John the Baptist themes. Islamic tradition honors him as a model of uncompromising monotheistic zeal. Modern scholarship studies the prophetic legends as part of the Deuteronomistic shaping of history and the development of monotheism in Israel. Elijah remains one of the most vivid and theologically charged figures in the OT.
