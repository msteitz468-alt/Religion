**Biographical Overview**:
Zechariah was a Jewish priest belonging to the order of Abijah during the reign of King Herod of Judea. He was married to Elizabeth, a descendant of Aaron. Despite being righteous, they were childless and advanced in years. God miraculously intervened, and Zechariah became the father of John the Baptist. His story is confined to Luke 1; he is the priestly representative of faithful but doubting Israel at the dawn of the new era.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Zechariah's story is confined to Luke 1. While offering incense in the temple sanctuary (chosen by lot—divine choreography per Green), he is visited by Gabriel announcing John's birth. He expresses doubt ("How shall I know this?") and is struck mute until the circumcision, when he obediently names the child "John" (against family tradition). Restored and filled with the Holy Spirit, he utters the Benedictus (1:67-79), a prophetic song of covenant fulfillment, salvation from enemies, and the child's role as forerunner preparing the way of the Lord.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green's commentary stresses that Zechariah and Elizabeth represent the faithful piety of Second Temple Judaism. Zechariah's selection by lot to offer incense brings him as close to God's presence as a priest can come. His initial disbelief (contrasted with Mary's obedient "let it be") leads to silencing; his restoration at the naming of John and the Benedictus interpret God's redemptive work as continuation of the Abrahamic covenant, envisioning salvation from enemies, mercy to the fathers, and light for those in darkness. Green notes the theocentric focus: God's favor to Zechariah parallels God's favor to Israel.
- **Early Church / Patristic**: Celebrated in the birth of John the Baptist feasts; Benedictus incorporated into daily liturgy (Lauds in West). Typological reading as the last OT priest/prophet who sees the fulfillment in his son.
- **Eastern Orthodox / Catholic**: Venerated as saint; role in John the Baptist icons and temple liturgy.
- **Protestant**: Emphasizes his doubt vs. faith arc, obedience in naming, and the Benedictus as covenant theology; model of God's faithfulness despite human weakness.
- **Jewish**: No independent tradition; his portrait draws on priestly and patriarchal (Abraham) models.
- **Islam**: Father of Yahya (John); mentioned in Quran context of John's birth, but details differ.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Zechariah functions as a transitional figure between the Old Covenant and the New—the priest in the temple at the hinge of the ages. His and Elizabeth's childlessness echoes Abraham/Sarah and Elkanah/Hannah, signaling that God's eschatological intervention is about to happen through the "spirit and power of Elijah." The Benedictus (parallel to Mary's Magnificat and Simeon's Nunc Dimittis) celebrates God's visitation, covenant mercy, salvation from enemies (socio-political as well as spiritual), and the child's preparatory role. Green underscores the theocentric focus: the birth of John is not merely personal blessing but a critical moment in the divine plan to rescue and restore God's people. His silencing and restoration dramatize the shift from doubt to prophetic proclamation.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Historical Zechariah**: Highly stylized (angelic vision, mutism, poetic song). Critical views see as theological construction; Green affirms core events while noting literary shaping (chiastic structures, OT parallels).
- **Doubt vs. faith**: Contrast with Mary (and Elizabeth) highlights Lukan interest in responses to God's initiative.
- **Benedictus politics**: "Salvation from our enemies" (1:71, 74) raises questions of the political dimensions of Lukan salvation (Green notes both spiritual and socio-political layers).

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Zechariah's role underscores Luke's theocentric focus and the reversal theme (priest silenced; his wife and the young Mary become prophetic voices). The Benedictus links the new events to Abrahamic covenant and prepares for the holistic salvation (spiritual + socio-political) that unfolds in the Gospel and Acts. His story models God's faithfulness to the pious remnant of Israel.
- Broader: Benedictus as model for covenant theology and praise; inspires reflection on priestly vs. prophetic roles, doubt and restoration. Cross-tradition: highlights Lukan continuity with Israel's story.
