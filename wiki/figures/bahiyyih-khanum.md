# Bahíyyih Khánum

*Populated from [[smith-concise-encyclopedia-bahai-faith|Smith, Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000)]]. All claims are from lines 7442–7533 unless noted.*

## Biographical Overview

Bahíyyih Khánum (1846–1932) was the eldest daughter of [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]] by his first wife [[navvab|Navváb (Ásíyih)]], and [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]]'s full sister. She was devoted to Bahá'u'lláh, 'Abdu'l-Bahá, and [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]], and on several occasions served as acting head of the Faith. She was only six or seven years old when her father was imprisoned in the Síyáh-Chál in 1852, and thereafter shared the poverty, exile, and sufferings of Bahá'u'lláh's family (lines 7442–7458).

Dedicated to serving her father, she renounced the idea of marriage. In 'Akká (1868–1909/10) she managed the affairs of the household, attended to her father's daily needs, and cultivated social relationships with the wives of local notables and officials to shield her father and brother (lines 7459–7465). In the aftermath of Bahá'u'lláh's passing she led the minority of family members who supported 'Abdu'l-Bahá against the "machinations" of Muḥammad-'Alí and his associates (lines 7466–7472).

With the end of governmental restrictions on the family (1908), she received increasing numbers of Bahá'í pilgrims from East and West. During 'Abdu'l-Bahá's Western tours (1911–13) she assumed management of Bahá'í affairs in the Holy Land. During World War I (1914–18) she and her brother superintended the distribution of considerable amounts of food, clothing, and medical aid to local populations suffering from maladministration and potential starvation (lines 7476–7487). After 'Abdu'l-Bahá's death (1921) she supported and advised the youthful Shoghi Effendi during the early years of his guardianship, and during his prolonged absences from Haifa acted as head of the Faith (lines 7488–7494).

She died on 15 July 1932 at age eighty-six from respiratory failure, one hour after midnight, and was buried on Mount Carmel near the joint shrine of the Báb and 'Abdu'l-Bahá. Her funeral was massively attended by the general public and reminiscent of that of 'Abdu'l-Bahá in the prestige and religious diversity of the mourners (lines 7496–7505).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Shoghi Effendi was devastated by her death. He praised her "resignation, serenity, cheerfulness, and love for all," praying that she would intercede for him and for "the toiling masses of thy ardent lovers." He quickly had a domed, pillared, Carrara marble monument constructed to mark her grave — this later becoming the focal center for the administrative buildings of the "ARC" on Mount Carmel (lines 7506–7515).

Shoghi Effendi attributed to her the power of intercession for all humankind, describing her as being in direct communion with God, watching over the Bahá'ís and sending her blessings. He compared her station to that of heroines of previous religions: Sarah wife of Abraham; Asiya the wife of Pharaoh who sought to intercede for Moses; the Virgin Mary; Fátima daughter of Muhammad; and the Babi leader [[tahirih|Táhirih]] (lines 7519–7532).
