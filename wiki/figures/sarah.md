# Sarah

**Sarah** (originally *Sarai*), the wife of [[abraham|Abraham]] and mother of [[isaac|Isaac]], is the **first of the four matriarchs** of Israel. Barren into old age, she is promised a son in her nineties, laughs at the promise, and bears Isaac — "the son of laughter" — the child through whom the covenant line continues. Her story includes the two wife-sister episodes, the giving of Hagar and the later expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael, and her death at 127, occasioning Abraham's purchase of the cave of Machpelah. In the [[zohar|Zohar]] she is intimately associated with the indwelling **[[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]**, which "abides constantly with Sarah, never departing."

---

## Biographical Overview

- **Origins and migration** (Gen 11–12): wife of Abram; barren; accompanies him from Ur and Haran to Canaan.
- **The wife-sister episodes** (Gen 12, 20): in Egypt and in Gerar, Abraham presents Sarah as his sister; she is taken into the royal household and divinely protected.
- **Hagar and Ishmael** (Gen 16, 21): gives her Egyptian servant Hagar to Abraham; later, after Isaac's birth, demands the expulsion of Hagar and [[ishmael|Ishmael]].
- **The promise and Isaac's birth** (Gen 17–18, 21): renamed **Sarah** ("princess"); laughs at the promise of a son at ninety; bears **Isaac** ("he laughs").
- **Death and burial** (Gen 23): dies at 127 in Hebron; Abraham buys the **cave of Machpelah** as her tomb — the first plot of the promised land owned by the family, and the burial place of the patriarchs and matriarchs.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Genesis 11–23** — the matriarchal narrative.
- **Isaiah 51:2** — "look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you," the matriarch as source of the people.
- **Romans 4; 9:9; Hebrews 11:11** — Sarah as an exemplar of faith and the mother of the child of promise.
- **Galatians 4:21–31** — Paul's allegory: **Sarah = the free woman / the heavenly Jerusalem / the covenant of promise**, over against Hagar = Sinai/slavery. See [[circumcision|Circumcision]] and Galatians.
- **1 Peter 3:6** — Sarah as a model of wifely conduct ("she obeyed Abraham, calling him lord").
- **Qur'an** — the wife of Ibrahim, the mother of Ishaq, who receives (and laughs at) the angelic annunciation (Q 11:71–73).

---

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: the first matriarch (*Sarah Imenu*, "Sarah our mother"); rabbinic Midrash exalts her as a prophet greater than Abraham in prophecy (on Gen 21:12, "listen to her voice"), as a convert-maker alongside Abraham, and as the one whose presence kept a blessing on the tent (the cloud, the lamp, the blessed dough). Her death is linked to the Akedah.
- **Christian**: the mother of the child of promise and a model of faith (Heb 11); in **Galatians 4** the figure of the free covenant and the heavenly Jerusalem, allegorically contrasted with Hagar.
- **Islamic**: Sara, the honored wife of Ibrahim and mother of Ishaq; the annunciation narrative parallels Genesis.
- **Kabbalistic (the Zohar)** — see below.

## Kabbalistic Reception (the Zohar)

In the Zohar (Parashat Lekh Lekha, Va-Yera, Ḥayyei Sarah), Sarah is bound to the **[[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]** / the divine feminine:

- **The Shekhinah dwells with Sarah**: explaining the wife-sister episode, the Zohar says Abraham relied not on a miracle but on the fact that "**he saw Shekhinah abiding constantly with Sarah, never departing**." His words "She is my sister" are read through Proverbs 7:4, "Say to **Wisdom**, 'You are my sister'" — aligning Sarah with the Shekhinah/Wisdom (the lower world, *Attah*, "You"). The matriarch is the earthly vessel of the indwelling Presence.
- **Sarah and the annunciation**: when the visitors announce Isaac's birth, Sarah is "sitting behind the Shekhinah"; the joyous word passes through the divine Presence to her.
- **Her death and Machpelah**: Sarah is the first buried in the cave that the Zohar makes the gateway between the grave and the Garden of Eden (see [[zoharic-afterlife|The Soul and the Afterlife]]); the matriarchs and patriarchs there are roused by the dew of resurrection.

Sarah thus inaugurates a pattern the Zohar develops through [[rebekah|Rebekah]], [[rachel|Rachel]], and [[leah|Leah]]: the **four matriarchs as earthly embodiments of aspects of the divine feminine**.

---

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Sarah is the matriarch through whom the covenant is biologically and theologically transmitted: the promise hinges on *her* son, not Hagar's, so that election runs through grace (the miraculous birth) rather than natural means. Her laughter — first of doubt, then of joy — frames the theme of the impossible promise fulfilled. In Pauline theology she becomes the type of the free, promise-based covenant; in the Zohar, the bearer of the Shekhinah.

---

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- Like the patriarchs, Sarah is **not independently attested**; critical scholarship reads the matriarchal narratives as ancestral sagas.
- The **wife-sister stories** (Gen 12, 20, and Rebekah's in 26) are debated as doublets/variants and raise moral questions about the patriarchs' conduct.
- The **expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael** is a focus of Jewish–Christian–Islamic reflection and of feminist and postcolonial readings ([[ishmael|Ishmael]]; Hagar).
