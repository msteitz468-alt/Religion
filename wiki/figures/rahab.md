Rahab is the Canaanite woman of Jericho who hides the Israelite spies, confesses YHWH as God of heaven and earth, secures the survival of her household through the *ḥerem* of Jericho by an act of *ḥesed*, and enters the lineage of David and Jesus.

## Biographical Overview

A resident of Jericho (described as a *zônâ*, prostitute or innkeeper), Rahab receives the two spies sent by [[joshua|Joshua]] (Josh 2:1). She hides them on her roof under stalks of flax, misdirects the king's men, and extracts a solemn oath of protection for her and her household in return for her kindness (*ḥesed*). She confesses: "The LORD your God is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath" (2:11) — the most explicit theological statement by a non-Israelite in the Former Prophets. The spies instruct her to tie a scarlet cord in the window; when Jericho falls, her household is spared amid the devoted destruction (6:17, 22–25). She and her family live among Israel "to this day." Later tradition (Matt 1:5) identifies her as the wife of Salmon and mother of Boaz, placing her in the Davidic and messianic genealogy.

### Woudstra (NICOT) — Josh 2 and 6 (2026-07-18)

Attributed to [[marten-woudstra|Marten Woudstra]] ([[woudstra-joshua-nicot|Joshua NICOT]]). Digests B and C.

For Woudstra, ch. 2 vividly illustrates that God will “give” Canaan; Rahab’s confession and the spies’ report show even mighty cities cannot resist Israel’s God—encouragement to Joshua continuous with ch. 1. Sending spies is a general’s foresight, not unbelief, and does not conflict with the ch. 6 miracle; the red-cord “sign” assumes forcible taking because the wall-miracle is not yet disclosed. Rahab is called *zônâ* (and Gk. *pornē* in Heb 11:31; Jas 2:25); a house frequented by men explains the spies’ choice to escape detection; *šāḵaḇ* “sleep” and “who have come to you” need not mean sexual intent given “who have entered your house.” Narrative does not state special divine care explicitly, yet ready acceptance by the king’s messengers and unsearched house may imply guidance; Hebrew narrative proceeds episodically (pursuit suspended so the reader concentrates on Rahab’s house).

**Confession (2:8–11):** She confirms ch. 1’s gift-language (“the Lord has given you the land”); dread on Canaan fulfils Exod 15:14–16; 23:27 and links holy-war dread toward eschatology; memory of Red Sea and ḥērem of Sihon/Og leads to the monotheistic climax that YHWH is God in heaven above and on earth beneath (// Deut 4:39). Against reading the confession as mere authorial fabrication, Woudstra holds the **substance** to be truly Rahab’s; conversation may have been longer, with Israelite summary possibly tinged by Pentateuchal thought, but her words are not verbatim Pentateuchal quotes. In some ways the confession still shows her emerging from a pagan environment—similar heaven-and-earth utterances appear in pagan literature.

**Ḥesed and household (2:12–14):** She demands that the spies return her *ḥeseḏ* with similar faithfulness to her family. Woudstra: *ḥeseḏ* is hard to render in one English word—principally loyalty to a covenant relationship, with mercy and kindness; spies reply with “faithfulness and loyalty,” the standard expression for covenantal kindness, when the Lord will have *given* the land. She seeks salvation for father, mother, brothers, sisters, and all who belong to them—family/clan thinking (ANE patterns) and an indication of unselfishness. V. 15’s letting down by the window is best read as **proleptic** summary of final escape, not mid-descent conversation; scarlet-cord tying is likewise completed proleptically. Three-day non-detection is another tacit admission of divine guidance of Joshua’s mission. The spies’ report to Joshua reiterates that the Lord has given the whole country and stresses Canaanite panic—thematically the most important element of ch. 2.

**Lie?** Several commentators call her words a lie; others note the account’s moral silence. Woudstra records Holwerda: “truth” in Israel means loyalty toward neighbor and Lord, not mere agreement with fact—thus Rahab’s words need not be called a lie.

**Rescue under ḥerem (6:17, 22–25):** A singular instance of God’s goodness to a Canaanite whose doom was foretold (Gen 15:16); she is saved because of what she did for the agents—faith that “worked” (Heb 11:31; Jas 2:25). Red cord of ch. 2 no longer plays a part at the rescue; connection is to the oath (6:22; 2:12) and the kind act (6:17). Life under *ḥerem* ordinarily could not be redeemed, so sparing is unusual; family solidarity operates positively here (opposite of Achan, ch. 7). Assigned outside the camp for ceremonial uncleanness; “to this day” confirms historical veracity for Woudstra, not etiology. **Purely racial components never defined the people of God** under the old dispensation (Deut 7:6–8)—what Israel was it had by God’s grace.

**Typology caution:** Church Fathers’ scarlet cord as Christ’s blood / Rahab as Church must be handled carefully—real typological continuity may exist, but color coincidence alone will not suffice; possible Exodus doorpost link noted with caution.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Joshua 2**: The spies' mission; Rahab's hiding, confession, negotiation, and the oath with scarlet cord.
- **Joshua 6**: The fall of Jericho; explicit command to spare Rahab and all in her house because she hid the messengers (6:17); the spies fulfill the oath and bring her family out; she lives in Israel thereafter.
- **Matthew 1:5**: Named in the genealogy of Jesus as mother of Boaz (wife of Salmon), four women included to highlight grace, inclusion, and surprising divine providence (with Tamar, Ruth, and "the wife of Uriah").
- **Hebrews 11:31**: Listed in the hall of faith: "By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had given friendly welcome to the spies." Cockerill densification below.
- **James 2:25**: Cited as example that "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone": Rahab "received the messengers and sent them out by another way."

### Cockerill (NICNT) — Hebrews 11:31 (2026-07-18)

Attributed to [[gareth-lee-cockerill|Gareth Lee Cockerill]] ([[cockerill-hebrews-nicnt|Hebrews NICNT]]). Digest R10 (faith hall 11:13–12:3).

For Cockerill, Rahab is a **climactic clear example** concluding 11:1–31 — four factors make her most appropriate: (1) she believed God’s power was real and his promises certain, enacted “by welcoming the spies with peace”; (2) calling her “the prostitute” shows one does not join God’s people by previous merit or birth (believing Canaanite saved vs. the disobedient who perish); (3) she “did not perish” — continues the life-through-faith theme; (4) “by faith” she identified with the people of God, like Moses (v. 25); “Peace” is the last word of the unit (R10 L3921–3923). She models the faith of 11:1, 3, 6 (trust → obedient welcome). Cockerill also pairs her structurally with [[esau|Esau]] (12:16–17) as strategic twin: final faith example vs. climactic apostasy warning (R11). Continuity/discontinuity note on the Pioneer: Christ never had to make a break with the unbelieving world as did Abraham, Moses, and **Rahab** (R10 L4007).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Rahab is celebrated as the paradigmatic proselyte and righteous convert. Rabbinic midrash elaborates her faith, beauty, and the reward of her *ḥesed* (some traditions make her the ancestor of prophets and priests). She exemplifies the inclusion of Gentiles who fear YHWH and the power of repentance. Her confession is read as recognition of the one true God.

**Christianity**: Rahab functions as a type of the Church (Gentiles saved from destruction) and a model of living faith. Patristic interpreters (including Origen in his *Homilies on Joshua*) read her as the Church rescued from the world; the scarlet cord as a sign of Christ's blood or the mark of the cross. In the NT she embodies the union of faith (Heb 11) and works (Jas 2) and demonstrates that the gospel reaches outsiders. In Matthew's genealogy she signals that God's purposes include surprising figures and Gentile blood in the messianic line. Evangelical readings emphasize grace to the undeserving and the cost of discipleship (risking her life for the spies). **Woudstra** affirms a historical core and the confession’s basic veracity while cautioning that patristic cord-as-blood typology must rest on more than color coincidence; he stresses reciprocal *ḥesed*, family solidarity under the oath, and that the people of God were never purely racial. **Cockerill** makes her the climactic faith-example of Heb 11:1–31 and structural foil to Esau’s apostasy.

**Islamic and other**: Limited direct mention; some traditions parallel her with other righteous women who aid prophets. Critical scholarship often treats the Rahab story as etiological (explaining the presence of a Canaanite clan or guild in later Israel) or as a narrative of survival and boundary-crossing. Woudstra rejects pure etiology for “to this day” and treats the confession’s substance as Rahab’s rather than redactional fabrication.

**Cross-tradition note**: The story highlights tensions around *ḥerem* (total devotion to destruction) and mercy: Rahab is the named exception that proves both the severity of the command and the responsiveness of YHWH to *ḥesed* shown to his people.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Rahab embodies the reciprocity of *ḥesed* (covenant loyalty/kindness): her protection of the spies is repaid with protection of her house. Per Woudstra, *ḥeseḏ* is loyalty to a covenant relationship with mercy/kindness; the spies answer with the standard covenantal pair “faithfulness and loyalty.” Her confession places her among the first to acknowledge YHWH's universal sovereignty — "God in heaven above and on the earth beneath" — before Israel has even crossed the Jordan, confirming the book’s land-as-gift theme. Within the *ḥerem* narrative she illustrates that judgment on Canaan is not indiscriminate genocide but responsive to accumulated sin (Gen 15:16) while remaining open to those who align with YHWH's purposes; life under *ḥerem* ordinarily could not be redeemed, so her sparing is unusual and grace-marked. Theologically she anticipates the inclusion of the Gentiles in the people of God and the extension of the covenant beyond ethnic Israel—Woudstra: purely racial components never defined God’s people under the old dispensation (Deut 7:6–8). Narratively, she functions as a foil to the kings of Canaan who arm for war (9:1–2); she chooses peace with Israel and is saved. Her story sits at the hinge of promise and fulfillment: the land is given, yet faith and action are required for participation in that gift. In the NT she becomes a paradigm for justification that is both by faith and evidenced in works, and a sign that the messianic line is carried by grace through unlikely vessels.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Conquest setting**: The Rahab narrative is embedded in the debated conquest of Jericho. Archaeological evidence for destruction layers at Tell eṣ-Ṣultan (traditional Jericho) is problematic for traditional dates; Woudstra notes the limits of identification (whether excavated Jericho is Joshua’s; other nearby tells) and the theological (not primarily apologetic) purpose of the account—the story means to celebrate God’s act; miracle does not exclude natural causes, but the text is silent about them.
- **Herem ethics**: The sparing of Rahab raises questions about the scope and rationale of the ban. Was it total for all Canaanites, or limited to specific cities as judgment? Rahab's case shows the ban could admit exceptions based on response to Israel/YHWH; Woudstra stresses the singularity of the rescue under irrevocable *ḥerem* and the positive family solidarity that mirrors (and inverts) Achan. Modern readers debate whether the narrative justifies violence or critiques partial obedience (remaining Canaanites become snares).
- **Social identity**: "Prostitute" (*zônâ*) or "innkeeper"? Ancient versions and traditions differ; Woudstra retains *zônâ*/harlot language (with NT *pornē*) and explains lodging as concealment strategy, not sexual intent. The text emphasizes her marginal yet pivotal status as an outsider who becomes insider.
- **Rahab’s “lie”**: Commentators divide on moral evaluation; Holwerda (via Woudstra) reframes “truth” as loyalty rather than factual correspondence.
- **Later traditions vs. text**: The Matthean genealogy and rabbinic elaborations supply marriage and descendants not stated in Joshua. These are received as authoritative in their traditions but are expansions beyond the primary narrative.
- **Literary vs. historical**: Some read the story as largely etiological or parabolic; others (including Woudstra) affirm a historical core while recognizing theological shaping, episodic Hebrew narrative technique, and proleptic storytelling. Against fabrication of the confession: substance is Rahab’s; summary may be Israelite-tinged without casting doubt on basic veracity.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

**Woudstra (NICOT)** highlights Rahab as the one through whom "the LORD your God is God" is confessed by a non-Israelite; she is spared explicitly "because she hid the messengers" (6:17) — reward for kindness within a theocentric frame of land-as-gift. He supplies the densest recent evangelical reading in this wiki’s sources: *zônâ* and lodging for concealment; *ḥesed* as covenantal loyalty; confession’s veracity vs redactional fabrication; typology handled with care; rescue under rigorous *ḥerem*; non-racial definition of the people of God; “to this day” as historical confirmation. Patristic and medieval exegesis developed rich typological readings (Rahab = Church; scarlet cord = blood of Christ or sign of faith)—Woudstra allows real typological continuity but rejects mere color coincidence. Reformation interpreters used her for sola fide (Heb 11) while noting Jas 2's emphasis on works as the evidence of living faith. **Cockerill (NICNT Hebrews)** places her as climactic faith example of 11:1–31 and structural twin to Esau. Modern scholarship emphasizes boundary-crossing, survival strategies of marginalized women, and the tension between *ḥerem* ideology and actual mercy. Feminist and postcolonial readings lift up Rahab as a trickster/survivor figure who negotiates power from below; evangelical responses often stress divine grace to the outsider and the missionary implication (a Canaanite becomes part of Israel's story and the Messiah's line). The story remains central to discussions of holy war, just war, land theology, and the universal scope of YHWH's reign.
