Rahab is the Canaanite woman of Jericho who hides the Israelite spies, confesses YHWH as God of heaven and earth, secures the survival of her household through the *ḥerem* of Jericho by an act of *ḥesed*, and enters the lineage of David and Jesus.

## Biographical Overview

A resident of Jericho (described as a *zônâ*, prostitute or innkeeper), Rahab receives the two spies sent by Joshua (Josh 2:1). She hides them on her roof under stalks of flax, misdirects the king's men, and extracts a solemn oath of protection for her and her household in return for her kindness (*ḥesed*). She confesses: "The LORD your God is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath" (2:11) — the most explicit theological statement by a non-Israelite in the Former Prophets. The spies instruct her to tie a scarlet cord in the window; when Jericho falls, her household is spared amid the devoted destruction (6:17, 22–25). She and her family live among Israel "to this day." Later tradition (Matt 1:5) identifies her as the wife of Salmon and mother of Boaz, placing her in the Davidic and messianic genealogy.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Joshua 2**: The spies' mission; Rahab's hiding, confession, negotiation, and the oath with scarlet cord.
- **Joshua 6**: The fall of Jericho; explicit command to spare Rahab and all in her house because she hid the messengers (6:17); the spies fulfill the oath and bring her family out; she lives in Israel thereafter.
- **Matthew 1:5**: Named in the genealogy of Jesus as mother of Boaz (wife of Salmon), four women included to highlight grace, inclusion, and surprising divine providence (with Tamar, Ruth, and "the wife of Uriah").
- **Hebrews 11:31**: Listed in the hall of faith: "By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had given friendly welcome to the spies."
- **James 2:25**: Cited as example that "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone": Rahab "received the messengers and sent them out by another way."

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Rahab is celebrated as the paradigmatic proselyte and righteous convert. Rabbinic midrash elaborates her faith, beauty, and the reward of her *ḥesed* (some traditions make her the ancestor of prophets and priests). She exemplifies the inclusion of Gentiles who fear YHWH and the power of repentance. Her confession is read as recognition of the one true God.

**Christianity**: Rahab functions as a type of the Church (Gentiles saved from destruction) and a model of living faith. Patristic interpreters (including Origen in his *Homilies on Joshua*) read her as the Church rescued from the world; the scarlet cord as a sign of Christ's blood or the mark of the cross. In the NT she embodies the union of faith (Heb 11) and works (Jas 2) and demonstrates that the gospel reaches outsiders. In Matthew's genealogy she signals that God's purposes include surprising figures and Gentile blood in the messianic line. Evangelical readings emphasize grace to the undeserving and the cost of discipleship (risking her life for the spies).

**Islamic and other**: Limited direct mention; some traditions parallel her with other righteous women who aid prophets. Critical scholarship often treats the Rahab story as etiological (explaining the presence of a Canaanite clan or guild in later Israel) or as a narrative of survival and boundary-crossing.

**Cross-tradition note**: The story highlights tensions around *ḥerem* (total devotion to destruction) and mercy: Rahab is the named exception that proves both the severity of the command and the responsiveness of YHWH to *ḥesed* shown to his people.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Rahab embodies the reciprocity of *ḥesed* (covenant loyalty/kindness): her protection of the spies is repaid with protection of her house. Her confession places her among the first to acknowledge YHWH's universal sovereignty — "God in heaven above and on the earth beneath" — before Israel has even crossed the Jordan. Within the *ḥerem* narrative she illustrates that judgment on Canaan is not indiscriminate genocide but responsive to accumulated sin (Gen 15:16) while remaining open to those who align with YHWH's purposes. Theologically she anticipates the inclusion of the Gentiles in the people of God and the extension of the covenant beyond ethnic Israel. Narratively, she functions as a foil to the kings of Canaan who arm for war (9:1–2); she chooses peace with Israel and is saved. Her story sits at the hinge of promise and fulfillment: the land is given, yet faith and action are required for participation in that gift. In the NT she becomes a paradigm for justification that is both by faith and evidenced in works, and a sign that the messianic line is carried by grace through unlikely vessels.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Conquest setting**: The Rahab narrative is embedded in the debated conquest of Jericho. Archaeological evidence for destruction layers at Tell eṣ-Ṣultan (traditional Jericho) is problematic for traditional dates; Woudstra and others note the limits of identification and the theological (not primarily apologetic) purpose of the account.
- **Herem ethics**: The sparing of Rahab raises questions about the scope and rationale of the ban. Was it total for all Canaanites, or limited to specific cities as judgment? Rahab's case shows the ban could admit exceptions based on response to Israel/YHWH. Modern readers debate whether the narrative justifies violence or critiques partial obedience (remaining Canaanites become snares).
- **Social identity**: "Prostitute" (*zônâ*) or "innkeeper"? Ancient versions and traditions differ; the text emphasizes her marginal yet pivotal status as an outsider who becomes insider.
- **Later traditions vs. text**: The Matthean genealogy and rabbinic elaborations supply marriage and descendants not stated in Joshua. These are received as authoritative in their traditions but are expansions beyond the primary narrative.
- **Literary vs. historical**: Some read the story as largely etiological or parabolic; others (including Woudstra) affirm a historical core while recognizing theological shaping and the episodic style of Hebrew narrative.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Woudstra (NICOT) highlights Rahab as the one through whom "the LORD your God is God" is confessed by a non-Israelite; she is spared explicitly "because she hid the messengers" (6:17) — reward for kindness within a theocentric frame. Patristic and medieval exegesis developed rich typological readings (Rahab = Church; scarlet cord = blood of Christ or sign of faith). Reformation interpreters used her for sola fide (Heb 11) while noting Jas 2's emphasis on works as the evidence of living faith. Modern scholarship emphasizes boundary-crossing, survival strategies of marginalized women, and the tension between *ḥerem* ideology and actual mercy. Feminist and postcolonial readings lift up Rahab as a trickster/survivor figure who negotiates power from below; evangelical responses often stress divine grace to the outsider and the missionary implication (a Canaanite becomes part of Israel's story and the Messiah's line). The story remains central to discussions of holy war, just war, land theology, and the universal scope of YHWH's reign.
