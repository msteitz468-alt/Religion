# Amnon

**Amnon** is the firstborn son of [[david|David]] (by Ahinoam of Jezreel) and presumptive
heir, whose rape of his half-sister **Tamar** sets in motion the chain of violence that
consumes David's house (2 Samuel 13). He is murdered two years later by the servants of his
half-brother [[absalom|Absalom]], Tamar's full brother, in revenge.

## The Episode (2 Samuel 13)

Amnon is consumed by desire for Tamar, his beautiful half-sister. On the counsel of his
"very wise" cousin Jonadab, he feigns illness and asks David to send Tamar to prepare food
and feed him in his chamber. When she comes, he seizes her; she pleads with him not to
commit such an outrage "as is not done in Israel," offering that David would give her to him
in marriage if he asked. He overpowers and rapes her. Then **"the hatred with which he hated
her was greater than the love with which he had loved her"** — he has her thrown out and
bolts the door behind her. Tamar tears her ornamented virgin's robe, puts ashes on her head,
and lives "desolate" in Absalom's house. David is "very angry" but does nothing — a paralysis
the narrative leaves unexplained. After two years Absalom invites the king's sons to a
sheep-shearing feast and has Amnon killed when "merry with wine."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Amnon's crime is the first eruption of [[nathan|Nathan's]] curse on David's house following
the [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] affair — sexual sin breeding murder, now within the royal family
itself. David's failure to punish his firstborn both echoes his own guilt and creates the
vacuum Absalom fills with private vengeance, leading toward the revolt. The episode is the
Hebrew Bible's starkest narrative of the rape of a named woman and of the silencing that
follows it.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) emphasizes the
**thematic analogy** the writer constructs: Amnon "lying in a pretended sickbed so that he
can summon his sister Tamar to serve his violent lust" is set deliberately against "David's
rising from his siesta bed to see the bathing Bathsheba and then summon her to the palace for
his illicit pleasure." Both "prohibited sexual acts lead to murder and political disarray" —
the son's crime mirroring and magnifying the father's. The terse notice that Amnon's hatred
now exceeded his former "love" is, for Alter, an instance of the narrative's psychological
precision: desire without regard for the other curdles instantly into revulsion.

## Reception

Amnon is treated in both Jewish and Christian moral tradition as the type of lawless lust and
of the corruption of the privileged heir. Tamar, by contrast, is read sympathetically as the
wronged, articulate victim; her plea and her public mourning give her a moral standing the
narrative denies Amnon. (This Tamar — daughter of David — is distinct from the Genesis
[[tamar|Tamar]], daughter-in-law of Judah.)
