# Namdev (Naam Dayv)

Namdev (c. 1270–1350) was a Maharashtrian [[sant-tradition|Sant]] poet and devotee of Vishnu/Vitthal whose compositions were included in the **[[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]** as part of the **Bhagat bāṇī** — the hymns of non-Guru holy figures approved for inclusion. He is one of the most prominent Bhagats alongside [[kabir|Kabir]], [[ravidas|Ravidas]], and [[sheikh-farid|Sheikh Farid]].

**Social identity**: Calico-printer (*chheepā*). His low-caste background is explicitly noted within the SGGS itself — both by his own compositions and in a meta-commentary by Guru Arjan (Mehl 5): "Naam Dayv's mind was absorbed into God, Gobind, Gobind, Gobind. The calico-printer, worth half a shell, became worth millions." (L8835–8839, Ang 488).

## In the Guru Granth Sahib (Raag Aasaa Bhagat Bāṇī, Angs 484–486)

Namdev's compositions appear in the Bhagat bāṇī section of Raag Aasaa. His theological signature is the **immanence of God as excluding all separate ritual approach**: God is already fully present in every creature and element, which makes external worship (bathing the idol, weaving garlands, offering milk) not merely ineffective but logically impossible — the material used is already saturated with divine life.

### God as the thread through all

> "God is everything, God is everything. Without God, there is nothing at all. As one thread holds hundreds and thousands of beads, He is woven into His creation." (L8314–8324, Ang 485)

> "The waves of the water, the foam and bubbles, are not distinct from the water. This manifested world is the playful game of the Supreme Lord God; reflecting upon it, we find that it is not different from Him." (L8326–8332, Ang 485)

> "In each and every heart, and deep within the very nucleus of all, is the One Lord." (L8355–8358, Ang 485)

### Critique of ritual worship

The most distinctive set of verses in Namdev's SGGS compositions present a series of parallel refusals — three offerings to the idol that cannot be used because the divine already pervades the medium:

> "Bringing the pitcher, I fill it with water, to bathe the Lord. But 4.2 million species of beings are in the water — how can I use it for the Lord, O Siblings of Destiny?" (L8362–8370, Ang 485)

> "I bring flowers to weave a garland, in worshipful adoration of the Lord. But the bumble bee has already sucked out the fragrance — how can I use it for the Lord, O Siblings of Destiny?" (L8378–8386, Ang 485)

> "I carry milk and cook it to make pudding, with which to feed the Lord. But the calf has already tasted the milk — how can I use it for the Lord, O Siblings of Destiny?" (L8388–8394, Ang 485)

The logic of radical immanence makes ritual offerings incoherent: every element already belongs to the Lord because the Lord is already present within it. The worshipper cannot bring God something of which God is not already the substance.

### Caste rejected

> "What do I have to do with social status? What do I have to do with ancestry? I meditate on the Name of the Lord, day and night." (L8418–8423, Ang 486)

> "You have given me birth in the house of a calico-printer, but I have found the Teachings of the Guru. By the Grace of the Saint, Naam Dayv has met the Lord." (L8499–8507, Ang 486)

### Interior versus exterior practice

> "The snake sheds its skin, but does not lose its venom. The heron appears to be meditating, but it is concentrating on the water. Why do you practice meditation and chanting, when your mind is not pure?" (L8449–8462, Ang 486)

## Validation by Mehl 3 (Guru Amar Das)

Within the main body of Raag Aasaa, before the Bhagat bāṇī section, Guru Amar Das (Mehl 3) explicitly validates Namdev and Kabir as having "obtained salvation through the Perfect Guru": "Naam Dayv the printer, and Kabeer the weaver, obtained salvation through the Perfect Guru... Those who know God and recognize His Shabad lose their ego and class consciousness... Their Banis are sung by the angelic beings, and no one can erase them." (Ang 67, Part 1). This internal cross-reference establishes the theological basis for including low-caste non-Sikh poets in the Granth.
