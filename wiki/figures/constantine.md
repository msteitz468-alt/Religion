# Constantine the Great

**Constantine I**, Roman emperor, is significant in [[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|Ehrman's
account]] chiefly for convening and steering the **Council of Nicaea (325 CE)** that addressed
the [[arian-controversy|Arian controversy]].

## Reign and Religious Policy ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]'s framing)

Raised pagan, Constantine had a conversion experience in 312. Ehrman regards the conversion as
genuine but also politically useful: Constantine prized Christian unity as a unifying force for
a fragmented empire — "My first concern was that the attitude towards the Divinity of all the
provinces should be united in one consistent view" (Eusebius, *Life* 2.65). Ehrman corrects a
common error: Constantine made Christianity a **favored**, not the **official**, religion (it was
Theodosius I, late in the fourth century, who made it official); at Constantine's conversion
perhaps 5% of the empire's ~60 million were Christian, rising to ~50% by century's end.

At Nicaea, Constantine called the underlying theological dispute "extremely trivial and quite
unworthy of so much controversy," yet pressed for consensus, compelling 17 of 20 dissenters to
sign the creed. He also reversed three centuries of imperial practice: rather than being
worshiped as a god (cf. the [[roman-religion|emperor cult]]), he had himself depicted on coins
**praying to** the Christian God, and required even non-Christian soldiers to recite a prayer to
that God.

## Ayres on Constantine and Nicaea (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) reconstructs the council
from fragments (no acts survive): Constantine opened the meeting and his support for Alexander's
position was "a powerful force," with Ossius of Cordoba probably chairing. But Ayres cautions
that no one at Nicaea expected the creed to serve as a universal, precise standard of faith
(only two thin precedents existed), and *homoousios* was selected chiefly to exclude
[[arius|Arius]]. Constantine's later policy was inconsistent — he exiled Arius, then within 2–3
years readmitted him, and in 333 angrily ordered his works burned — evidence for Ayres against
any unified post-Nicene "anti-Arian" program. His son [[constantius-ii|Constantius II]] would
later back the [[homoians|Homoian]] settlement.
