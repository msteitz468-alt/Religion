# Lydia

## Biographical Overview

Lydia appears in [[acts|Acts]] 16:13–15, 40 as a dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira,
resident at [[philippi|Philippi]], and the first named convert of Paul's mission in
Macedonia — and thus, in [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s framing, effectively the first convert in
Europe. Acts describes her as a "worshiper of God" (σεβομένη τὸν θεόν), which Bock reads as
a God-fearer: a former polytheist who has adopted monotheism and attends the synagogue
without keeping the whole law (see [[god-fearers]]).

Bock notes that the term for her trade, **πορφυρόπωλις** ("merchant dealing in purple
cloth"), is a New Testament hapax and marks her as a woman of some status. Her home town of
Thyatira was known for its dyeing industry. Bock connects her means and her hospitality to
the later financial support the Philippian church gave Paul (Philippians 4:15–18).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 16:13–14** — Paul and his companions meet a group of women at a riverside "place of
  prayer" outside Philippi; Lydia, a God-fearer and purple-dealer of Thyatira, listens.
- **Acts 16:14** — "The Lord opened her heart" to attend to Paul's message.
- **Acts 16:15** — She and her household are baptized; she urges the missionaries to stay in
  her home.
- **Acts 16:40** — After their release from prison, Paul and Silas return to Lydia's house,
  where the believers are gathered — the emerging Philippian house-church.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's Reading)

Bock inclines (with Schnabel) to read the riverside "place of prayer" (προσευχήν) as likely
a synagogue, while noting that the small Jewish population — only women are mentioned — is
significant. He treats Lydia's conversion as a paradigm of divine initiative: "the Lord
opened her heart" (ὁ κύριος διήνοιξεν τὴν καρδίαν), which Bock glosses as "God creates the
initiative to faith from within." Her household baptism joins the recurring Acts pattern of
household baptisms (Acts 2, 8, 10, 16 — Pentecost, the Ethiopian eunuch, Cornelius, and here
Lydia and the jailer). Bock suggests she was likely single or widowed, since she heads her
own household, and observes that her home "becomes a center for the new faith" (16:40).

Bock also situates Lydia within Luke's larger pairing of conversions at Philippi: the pious
woman Lydia, the liberated slave girl, and the "pure Gentile" jailer together frame the
unit, illustrating (with Stott) that "few people [could be] more diverse ethnically,
socially, psychologically, and culturally," yet the gospel is "intended for all."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Lydia anchors Bock's reading of Philippi as a demonstration that the gospel crosses gender,
social, and ethnic barriers. As the first Macedonian convert and a woman of independent
means who hosts the nascent church, she exemplifies both the Lukan emphasis on responsive
women and the material basis of early Christian mission — the patronage that undergirded
Paul's later work.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock cites Sterck-Degueldre (2004), who reads Lydia as an "exemplary woman of piety," among
studies supporting the historical credibility of the Philippi narrative; Bock concludes that
"Luke is a credible ancient historian." The identification of the riverside "place of
prayer" as a formal synagogue versus an informal gathering spot is left as a probability
rather than a certainty. A text-critical note attaches to 16:13 (ἐνομίζομεν προσευχήν, which
Metzger judged the "least unsatisfactory" reading).
