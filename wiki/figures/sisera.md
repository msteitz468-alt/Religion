Sisera, commander of the army of Jabin king of [[hazor|Hazor]], is the Canaanite antagonist of the
[[deborah|Deborah]]–[[barak|Barak]] cycle in [[judges|Judges]] 4–5. He is routed at the Kishon and
killed in flight by [[jael|Jael]] the Kenite, who drives a tent peg through his temple into the
ground while he sleeps.

## Biographical Overview

Sisera commands nine hundred chariots of iron and oppresses [[israelites|Israel]] cruelly for
twenty years, based at Harosheth-ha-goiim (Judg 4:2–3). Deborah, prophetess and judge, summons
Barak son of Abinoam to muster ten thousand men of Naphtali and Zebulun at Mount Tabor, with the
promise that YHWH will draw Sisera and his chariots to the Wadi Kishon and give him into Barak's
hand. Barak will go only if Deborah goes with him; she agrees, but tells him the road he takes
will not lead to his glory, for YHWH will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman (4:8–9).

At the battle YHWH throws Sisera and all his chariots into a panic before Barak; Sisera dismounts
and flees on foot while his whole army falls by the sword, not one man left (4:15–16). He runs to
the tent of Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite, because there is peace between Jabin and the house of
Heber. Jael comes out to meet him, invites him in, covers him with a rug, and gives him milk when
he asks for water. He tells her to stand at the tent door and deny that anyone is there. Then,
while he lies exhausted and asleep, Jael takes a tent peg and a mallet, goes softly to him, and
drives the peg through his temple into the ground; he dies (4:17–21). When Barak arrives in
pursuit, Jael shows him the corpse.

The Song of Deborah (ch. 5) retells the death in poetry — Sisera falling at Jael's feet, sinking,
lying still — and closes with his mother at the window through the lattice, asking why his chariot
is so long in coming, and her wise ladies answering that they must be dividing the spoil: "a womb
or two for every man," dyed stuffs for Sisera's neck (5:24–30).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 4:1–24** — the prose account: Jabin's oppression, the nine hundred iron chariots,
  Deborah's summons, the rout at the Kishon, the flight to Jael's tent, the tent peg.
- **Judges 5:19–31** — the Song of Deborah: the kings fighting at Taanach by the waters of
  [[megiddo|Megiddo]], the stars fighting from their courses, the torrent Kishon sweeping the
  enemy away, the blessing on Jael above tent-dwelling women, and the scene of Sisera's mother at
  the window.
- **Psalm 83:9** — Sisera and Jabin invoked, with Midian, in a communal prayer against Israel's
  enemies.
- **Ezra 2:53 // Nehemiah 7:55** — "the sons of Sisera" listed among the temple servants
  (Nethinim), unconnected to the commander except by name.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Reception on Sisera in this collection is **thin**. Only the Judges commentary has been ingested;
no rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic reception source treating Sisera has been ingested here.

- **[[barry-webb|Webb]] (NICOT, 2012)**: the scene of **Sisera's mother** at the window is named
  by Webb among the book's satirical set-pieces (with the [[ehud|Ehud]] assassination and
  Abimelech's millstone death) whose humor **sharpens rather than undercuts** the theology. Webb
  also holds that Barak's hesitation transfers the glory of the victory to a woman — for Webb, to
  **[[jael|Jael]] more than to Deborah**; Jael's tent peg fulfils Deborah's prophecy.
- Susan Ackerman's characterization of Judges as a book "not of 'either/or' but of 'both/and'" on
  women (quoted by Webb) frames the Jael–Sisera episode alongside the book's female victims —
  including the women imagined as spoil in the mouth of Sisera's own mother.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Sisera is the book's clearest case of technological and military superiority — nine hundred iron
chariots — undone by weather, terrain, and a woman with a household tool. The narrative locates
the reversal in YHWH's own action (4:15; the Kishon in 5:21), and the double telling in prose and
poetry makes the death itself, not merely the battle, the celebrated event. His mother's closing
speech reverses the perspective one last time: the audience hears the enemy's grief in the same
breath as the enemy's assumed entitlement to captive women, and the Song answers with "so perish
all your enemies, O LORD" (5:31).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Sisera and Jabin have no direct extrabiblical attestation, and the relationship between the prose
account of Judges 4 and the poetry of Judges 5 (differing tribal lists, differing accounts of the
death posture) is a standing critical question. A caution is recorded on the [[judges|Judges]]
page: the older wiki claim that Judg 5 is "among the oldest extant Hebrew poetry" is **not**
attributed to Webb and should not be attributed to him without a locus. This wiki has ingested no
source adjudicating the historicity of Sisera or the prose–poetry relation, and the ethics of
Jael's deed are treated here only under Webb's general violence taxonomy for the book.
