# Jethro

**Biographical Overview**:
Jethro was a Midianite priest and the father-in-law of Moses. He sheltered Moses when he fled from Egypt and gave him his daughter Zipporah in marriage. After the Exodus, Jethro visited Moses in the wilderness, brought Zipporah and her sons, rejoiced at YHWH's deliverance, offered sacrifices, and provided crucial administrative counsel to establish a system of delegated judges for the Israelites.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Exodus**: Shelters Moses (ch. 2, named Reuel). Meets Moses in the wilderness, blesses YHWH, offers a burnt offering, and advises Moses to appoint judges to share the burden of leadership (ch. 18).
- **Numbers/Judges**: Moses asks his father-in-law/brother-in-law (Hobab) to act as a guide. The Kenites (Jethro's descendants) later settle among the Israelites.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Jewish**: Viewed favorably as a righteous Gentile who recognized YHWH's supremacy. The Torah portion containing the Ten Commandments (Yitro) is named after him, honoring his wisdom in establishing the judicial system.
- **Christian**: Often cited as a model for practical church administration, delegation, and the wise integration of outside/secular wisdom into the covenant community.
- **Islamic**: Often identified with the prophet Shu'ayb, who was sent to the Midianites, though some scholars differentiate the two. Shu'ayb is highly revered as a prophet of justice.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Jethro is a paradigm of the believing outsider. His confession ("Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods," Exod 18:11) is a pivotal acknowledgment of God's victory. The administrative structure he proposes forms the foundation of Israel's judicial system before the Sinai covenant is fully ratified.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Names and Identity**: The text refers to Moses' father-in-law as Reuel, Jethro, and perhaps Hobab (though Hobab might be his son). Scholars debate how to reconcile these traditions (often attributing them to different sources, J vs. E).
- **Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis**: Some historical-critical scholars suggest that Moses actually learned the worship of YHWH from Jethro and the Midianites/Kenites, a view known as the Kenite hypothesis.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- Bruckner emphasizes Jethro's role in acknowledging YHWH and providing necessary structural wisdom to Moses, demonstrating that God's grace and practical wisdom can come through outsiders.
