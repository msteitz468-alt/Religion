# Kabir

**Kabir** (c. 1398–1448) is the most famous of the North Indian *Sants* (saints of the bhakti
movement), a weaver-poet of Varanasi who refused to belong fully to either Hinduism or Islam.
Born into a Muslim weaver family (Julaha caste) that had recently converted from Hinduism, Kabir
wove the two traditions together — and condemned both. "One slaughters goats, one slaughters cows;
they squander their birth in isms."

## Birth and Identity

Kabir's caste identity was fluid and contested from the start. The Julaha weavers were a low-caste
Muslim group who may have converted from a Shakta or Nath yogic sect. Early hagiographies offer
multiple origin stories designed to make him more acceptable to different communities:

- A Brahmin widow conceived him immaculately, gave birth through the palm of her hand, set him
  afloat in a basket on a pond (a clear echo of the birth of Karna in the Mahabharata), where a
  Muslim couple found and adopted him.
- Some say he was a Brahmin in a former life who fell from dharma and became a weaver.
- Some say he was of divine origin but adopted by Muslim weavers.

Doniger notes that "all these stories attempt to drag Kabir back over the line from Muslim to Hindu."
He was equally claimed by both after his death; legend says that when his followers quarreled over
whether to bury or cremate his body, they found only flowers.

## The Sant Path: Nirgun Bhakti

Kabir is the paradigmatic exponent of *nirgun* (without qualities) bhakti — devotion to God as
pure, nameless Absolute, without form, story, or sectarian identity. His "Ram" is not Sita's Ram
of the Ramayana but the nirgun God "whose name is complete in itself, a mantra."

He is widely believed to have become a disciple of the Hindu saint **Ramananda** (c. 1370–1440)
through a famous trick: Kabir lay down across the stairs where Ramananda bathed before dawn;
Ramananda tripped over him and cried out "Ram! Ram!" — whereupon Kabir argued that Ramananda
had thus transmitted his own mantra, effectively initiating him as a pupil. Ramananda himself
preached in Hindustani and had many low-caste disciples.

**Language**: "Sanskrit is like water in a well; the language of the people is a flowing stream."
Kabir preached in the vernacular, producing poems (*dohas* and *pads*) in Brajbhasha that are
still sung throughout North India.

## Theology and Social Critique

Kabir's theology fuses the abstract God of Islam with the immanent God of Hindu bhakti, while
attacking the ritual, caste, and institutional structures of both:

> "If God wanted circumcision, why didn't you come out cut? If circumcision makes you a Muslim,
> what do you call your women? Since women are called man's other half, you might as well be Hindus."

> "If putting on the thread makes you a Brahmin, what does the wife put on? That Shudra's touching
> your food, pandit! How can you eat it?"

> "Hindu, Muslim — where did they come from? Who started this road? Look in your heart, send out
> scouts: where is heaven?"

On caste, his challenge is direct:

> "Tell me where untouchability came from, since you believe in it. . . . We eat by touching,
> we wash by touching, from a touch the world was born. So who's untouched? Only she who's free
> from delusion."

Yet Kabir "was not a revolutionary in any political or even social sense. Iconoclastic, yes;
anti-institutional, to be sure; poor and low in status, you bet, but not concerned about putting
an end to poverty. His goal was spiritual rather than economic or political liberation."

## Stories about Kabir

**Kabir, his wife, and the shopkeeper**: When Kabir had no food for visiting dervishes, his wife
promised a shopkeeper she would sleep with him in return for food. When she hesitated, Kabir carried
her to the shopkeeper; the shopkeeper was so ashamed he gave everything in his shop to the poor and
became a sadhu. The story shows both Kabir's casual attitude to his wife's fidelity and the theme
of transformative shame.

**Kabir and the prostitute**: Kabir deliberately went to a prostitute's house and walked through
the bazaar with her, seeking dishonor — a deliberate parallel to the [[pashupata|Pashupata]] ascetic
practice of feigning disgrace to transfer bad karma, though for Kabir the purpose is to expose the
hypocrisy of caste judgment.

**Kabir and the buffalo**: When Brahmins refused to let Kabir (a low-caste man) sit with them
unless he could recite the Veda, Kabir put his hand on a buffalo's head and commanded it to recite.
The buffalo did. The Brahmins were ashamed. This parallels the story of Kannappar and the talking dog
in Shaiva bhakti literature — the animal as vehicle for divine shaming of orthodoxy.

## Pashupata Parallel

Doniger observes that Kabir's willful seeking of dishonor (the prostitute story) "bears a striking
resemblance to the methods used by the Pashupatas, though for an entirely different purpose."
The Pashupata ascetic feigns drunkenness and lechery to transfer bad karma from those who condemn
him; Kabir feigns condemnation to expose the falsity of caste judgment. Same method, opposite
theology.

## Tradition After Kabir

Kabir's followers founded the **Kabir Panth** (Kabir Path), a religious community centered on
his teachings. Sadhus of the Kabir Panth insist he was celibate, despite stories of a wife and
son (Kamal). Both Hindus and Muslims claim his legacy, which Doniger reads as testimony that his
critique cut both ways — neither tradition could afford to let the other have him.

His influence extends into Sikhism: Guru Nanak (1469–1539), who founded Sikhism just after
Kabir's death, shared Kabir's nirgun theology and his refusal of the Hindu-Muslim binary. Several
of Kabir's poems are included in the Sikh *Guru Granth Sahib*.
