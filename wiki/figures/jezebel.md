Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians and wife of [[ahab|Ahab]] of Israel, presented
in [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] as the patron of the prophets of Baal and Asherah, the killer of YHWH's
prophets, the author of the judicial murder of Naboth, and the queen thrown from a window and
trampled by horses in [[jehu|Jehu's]] coup. Her name became a byword in later Christian usage. The
biblical account is a polemical portrait written by her opponents; this page records what it says
without adopting its verdict as the wiki's own.

## Biographical Overview

1 Kings 16 reports that Ahab married Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, and went
and served Baal, building a house and altar for him in [[samaria|Samaria]]. The narrative
attributes to her an active religious policy: she "cut off the prophets of the LORD," so that
Obadiah, the officer over Ahab's household, hid a hundred of them in caves and fed them with bread
and water; and she maintained prophets of Baal and of Asherah who ate at her table. After
[[elijah|Elijah]] wins the contest on Mount Carmel and has the prophets of Baal seized and
slaughtered at the Wadi Kishon, she sends Elijah an oath to make his life like theirs by the next
day, and he flees to Horeb.

In 1 Kings 21 Naboth the Jezreelite refuses to sell his ancestral vineyard to Ahab. Jezebel writes
letters in Ahab's name and seals them with his seal, instructing the elders and nobles to proclaim
a fast, seat Naboth at the head of the people, and set two worthless men opposite him to charge
him with cursing God and the king. Naboth is taken outside and stoned to death, and Jezebel tells
Ahab to go and take possession. Elijah meets Ahab in the vineyard with a sentence on the house of
Ahab and on Jezebel: the dogs will eat her by the wall of Jezreel.

She survives Ahab into the reigns of her sons Ahaziah and Joram. When Jehu, anointed at
Ramoth-gilead, kills Joram and rides into Jezreel, Jezebel paints her eyes, adorns her head, and
looks out the window, greeting him as "Zimri, murderer of your master." At Jehu's word her own
eunuchs throw her down; her blood spatters the wall and the horses, and Jehu drives over her. When
he sends men to bury her — "for she is a king's daughter" — they find only the skull, the feet, and
the palms of her hands, and Jehu recalls Elijah's word that dogs would eat her flesh.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 16:31** — marriage to Ahab; Sidonian descent; the turn to Baal.
- **1 Kings 18:4, 13, 19** — the killing of YHWH's prophets; Obadiah's hidden hundred; the four
  hundred fifty prophets of Baal and four hundred of Asherah who eat at her table.
- **1 Kings 19:1–3** — the oath against Elijah's life.
- **1 Kings 21** — the letters, the rigged fast and trial, Naboth's stoning, Elijah's sentence.
- **2 Kings 9:30–37** — the defenestration at Jezreel, the trampling, the dogs, the fulfillment
  notice.
- **Revelation 2:20** — the risen Christ's message to Thyatira rebukes the church for tolerating
  "the woman Jezebel, who calls herself a prophetess," a use of the name as a type rather than a
  reference to the queen herself.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation in Kings** — Jezebel is the narrative's concentrated image of
  foreign cult imported through a royal marriage, and the counterpart to Elijah. This is the text's
  theological framing, attributed as such.
- **[[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] (NIBC, 1995)**, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|the Kings page]]:
  the survival of the infant Joash from [[athaliah|Athaliah]]'s purge is read as "David" surviving "Jezebel" —
  Jezebel functioning as the type of the dynasty-destroying threat to the Davidic line through her
  daughter or kinswoman Athaliah.
- **Later Christian usage** — after Revelation 2:20 the name operates as a label for a woman
  teaching or leading in ways the writer opposes, and passes into general English usage as an
  epithet for a scheming or sexually transgressive woman. That trajectory belongs to reception, not
  to the Kings narrative.
- **Feminist and reception-critical re-readings.** It is widely noted in modern scholarship that
  the Kings portrait is written by her theological enemies and that its charges are entangled with
  her foreignness and her sex, and a body of feminist and reception-critical work has re-examined
  the portrayal and its afterlife. This wiki has ingested no such source, so the position is
  recorded generally and no specific argument is attributed to any named scholar.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jezebel functions in Kings as the human agent through whom the Baal cult becomes state policy, and
so as the necessary foil to Elijah: the Carmel contest, the Kishon slaughter, and her counter-oath
form a single sequence of religious violence in both directions, which the narrative does not
soften on either side. The Naboth affair makes her the paradigm case of covenant violation as
legal procedure — the crime is committed through a fast, a court, witnesses, and a lawful-looking
stoning. Her death is narrated as the exact fulfillment of Elijah's word, closing the loop the
vineyard opened.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Source character.** Everything known of Jezebel comes from a hostile narrative within a single
  tradition. There is no independent account of her policy, her religion as she would have
  described it, or her death. A seal inscribed with a name that has been read as hers has been
  discussed, but the identification is contested and nothing on this page depends on it.
- **Phoenician religion in Israel.** How far a Sidonian royal marriage implied cult imposition
  rather than the ordinary provision for a foreign consort is disputed; Kings reads it as
  imposition, and other readings treat the account as amplification. Positions, not adjudicated.
- **The verdict versus Omride success [recorded tension].** Historians who read the Omride period
  as one of Israel's most successful — see [[ahab|Ahab]] — necessarily read Jezebel's court role
  differently from the narrator's evaluation. Both are recorded.
- **The Revelation figure** is a distinct person or type at Thyatira, not the queen; conflating
  them is a reception move, not an exegetical result.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Jezebel is a fixed reference in study of Israelite–Phoenician relations, of royal women's power in
the ancient Levant, and of prophetic conflict with the state. In reception history she is one of
the clearest cases of a biblical figure whose name outgrew her narrative, and she is therefore a
standard test case for how polemical portraiture becomes cultural type. Cross-links:
