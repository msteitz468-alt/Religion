# Diotrephes

Diotrephes appears only once in the New Testament, in 3 John 9–10, where "the elder" (the
author of the [[letters-of-john|Johannine letters]]) denounces him. [[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]]
(NICNT) observes that he "has gone down in history as the man who wanted to lead the church."
His name (meaning "nourished by Zeus") suggests a well-born pagan background.

## Biographical Overview

Everything known of Diotrephes derives from 3 John 9–10. He is a figure of authority in a
local church over which "the elder" claims some translocal oversight. Marshall reads his
defining trait, ho philoprōteuōn ("who loves to be first," v. 9), as ambition displayed "in an
unchristian way." According to the elder, Diotrephes:

- **refused to accept the elder's authority** ("will have nothing to do with us," v. 9),
  possibly by suppressing a letter the elder had sent to the church;
- **spread malicious talk** — "gossiping maliciously" / making "unjustified accusations"
  against the elder (v. 10);
- **refused to welcome** the traveling brothers (missionaries); and
- **expelled from the church** those members who wished to receive them (v. 10).

## Primary Source Appearances

3 John 9–10 only. He is set in contrast to [[gaius|Gaius]] (the letter's hospitable
addressee) and to Demetrius (the commended letter-bearer), forming the letter's triad of
positive and negative examples of hospitality toward itinerant missionaries.

## Interpretive Controversies

**Heretic or power-seeker?** [[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]] stresses that "there is no
suggestion that Diotrephes disagreed with the elder on any vital point of doctrine"; his fault
is that "he did not… express his adherence to the truth in love." Two rejected readings:

- **W. Bauer**: Diotrephes was a heretic (with docetic connections). Marshall: the argument is
  "largely based on silence" and "has not found acceptance."
- **E. Käsemann**: the inverse hypothesis — that *the elder* held Gnostic sympathies and
  Diotrephes was "the champion of orthodoxy" who had excommunicated him. Marshall rejects this:
  evidence of the elder's Gnosticism "is lacking," and "the Johannine writings are in fact a
  powerful weapon against Gnosticism." (See [[johannine-secessionists|the secessionist crisis]],
  which is a *doctrinal* dispute; the Diotrephes affair is not.)

**Emerging monarchical episcopacy.** Marshall presents the "ecclesiastical" reading — Diotrephes
as an early exponent of the **monepiscopate**, "in which a 'bishop' came to occupy a position
superior to the 'elders' in a local church" — as "a common reading of the situation" seen from
Diotrephes' point of view, while declining to make it certain, since personal ambition alone
could explain his conduct. On this reading Diotrephes "represented the cause of independency at
a time when the system of oversight by apostles and evangelists was beginning to be replaced by
the development of independent churches." The church of Ignatius's letters (each with its own
bishop, elders, and deacons) marks the later stage; Diotrephes stands before it.

## Significance

Diotrephes is a key datum for reconstructing the transition in early church order from
itinerant apostolic/evangelistic oversight to settled local monarchical leadership. He is
regularly cited in discussions of church polity as the New Testament's cautionary example of a
leader who resists external authority.
