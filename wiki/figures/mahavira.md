## Biographical Overview

Mahāvīra, born with the given name Vardhamāna ("Increasing"), is the twenty-fourth and last [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] of the current cosmic era (*avasarpiṇī*) and the central historical figure of [[jainism|Jainism]]. The name Vardhamāna is explained by the tradition as referring to the increase in his family's prosperity after his birth (Dundas, p. 22).

**Birth**: According to the Śvetāmbara account, Mahāvīra's embryo was initially placed by the deer-headed general of Indra's army (Hari Negameṣī) in the womb of a brahman woman called Devananda, then transferred to the womb of Triśalā, wife of the king Siddhārtha, because fordmakers cannot be born into brahman families (Dundas, pp. 22, 27–28). His birthplace is stated in the [[kalpasutra|Kalpasūtra]] as Kuṇḍagrāma, believed to have been near Vaiśālī, one of the greatest cities of the Ganges basin at the time. The exact location of Kuṇḍagrāma remains uncertain, partly because Jains migrated from this eastern region at an early date (Dundas, pp. 25–26). The [[digambara|Digāmbaras]] reject both the embryo transfer and Mahāvīra's marriage to Yaśodā (Dundas, p. 24).

**Youth and renunciation**: In the Śvetāmbara account, Mahāvīra led a blameless youth, married a princess named Yaśodā, who bore him a daughter, and on his thirtieth birthday the gods performed his initiation ceremony and he renounced the world to become a mendicant ascetic (Dundas, p. 22).

**Ascetic career**: For twelve and a half years Mahāvīra wandered in the Ganges basin. The earliest and oldest account of this period, the ninth chapter of the first book of the Ācārāṅga (the "Pillow Scripture," *Uvahāṇasuya*), describes the violence shown him by householders, attacks by animals and insects, his nakedness, fasting, and lack of concern for sleep or contact with others — all described as "a long struggle to shake off the bondage brought about by body and mind and so effect a state of inner control: to abstain, in other words, from action (*karman*) and win through to spiritual freedom" (Dundas, p. 29). Part of this period was spent with [[makkhali-gosala|Makkhali Gosāla]] (see below).

**Enlightenment**: "Finally, at the end of the thirteenth year after his renunciation, in a field belonging to a farmer called Sāmāga which was situated near a small village on a riverbank, he attained supreme and unique (*kevala*) knowledge, the omniscience which Jains regard as defining enlightenment" (Dundas, p. 34). The Kalpasūtra specifies: after fasting for two and a half days without water, in the full glare of the sun, not far from a tree, in the rigorously ascetic posture of squatting on his haunches (Dundas, p. 34). This omniscience is described as "transcendent knowledge and vision which give direct and simultaneous access to all forms of reality in the universe in every temporal and spatial dimension" (Dundas, p. 35).

**Post-enlightenment ministry**: Mahāvīra preached and converted eleven brahmans who became the *gaṇadharas* ("leaders of the troop"), the heads of the ascetic order. The conversion is portrayed in the later Universal History as Mahāvīra defeating each brahman in doctrinal debate by demonstrating that their Vedic knowledge was incomplete without Jain principles (Dundas, pp. 38–39).

**Death**: Mahāvīra died aged seventy-two at the town of Pāvā, identified by about the thirteenth century with the village of Pāvāpur in the state of Bihar. His body was cremated, with the gods taking his bones to heaven and his ashes being distributed throughout the Ganges region (Dundas, p. 22).

## Primary Source Appearances

The biography of Mahāvīra is stratified across multiple textual layers:

1. **Oldest stratum** (Ācārāṅga 1, Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1; c. 3rd–2nd centuries BCE): The core ascetic biography. The Ācārāṅga does not describe Mahāvīra as all-knowing but only as "all-seeing" (Dundas, p. 26). The Sūtrakṛtāṅga provides a eulogy that credits him with "full omniscience" for the first time (SKS 1.6; Dundas, p. 26).
2. **Second stratum** (Ācārāṅga 2, Kalpasūtra; c. 2nd–1st centuries BCE): The second book of the Ācārāṅga presents Mahāvīra's life as a totality for the first time (AS 2.15). The Kalpasūtra gives his extended biography and for the first time links him to twenty-three predecessor fordmakers (Dundas, pp. 23–24).
3. **Third stratum** (Bhagavatī / Exposition of Explanations, Aupapātika; early centuries CE): The Bhagavatī (Viyākhyāprajñapti) contains extensive material on Mahāvīra's teachings, disciples, and especially his relationship with Makkhali Gosāla, who is not mentioned in the earlier scriptures (Dundas, p. 23–24).
4. **Commentarial expansion** (Avāśyaka commentarial literature, mnemonic verses from c. 2nd–3rd century CE; prose commentary by Jinādāsa, 7th century; expansion by Haribhadra, 8th century): The commentators "organised the raw material for the Universal History, drawing together and expanding it to create an image of the promulgator of the true path" (Dundas, p. 24).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Śvetāmbara reception**: Mahāvīra was married, had a daughter, and his embryo was transferred from a brahman womb to the warrior-caste womb of Triśalā. The Śvetāmbara scriptures are the primary source for understanding the earliest stages of his biography; the Digambara scriptures provide no significant early evidence (Dundas, p. 23).

**Digambara reception**: The Digambaras reject the embryo transfer and Mahāvīra's marriage to Yaśodā. The Digambara poet Jinasena (9th century CE) and his pupil Guṇabhadra produced a version of the Universal History drawing on earlier traditions. The Digambaras also hold a different traditional dating for Mahāvīra's death: 510 BCE rather than the Śvetāmbara 527 BCE (Dundas, p. 24, 25).

**Digambara account of preaching**: The Digambaras explain that at Mahāvīra's first *samavasaraṇa*, only gods assembled. Since gods cannot enter the spiritual path and since only the *gaṇadharas* can interpret the "divine sound" (*divyadhvani*) which Mahāvīra's body emits when preaching, the fordmaker did not preach at that first assembly (Dundas, p. 38).

**In Buddhist sources**: Mahāvīra is known as Nigaṇṭha Nāyaputta ("Bondless Son of the Nayas/Jñātas"). The Buddhist Pali Canon, while not an infallible guide to early Jainism, "consistently identifies the heretical teacher Nigaṇṭha Nāyaputta with the preaching of a Fourfold Restraint alone" (Dundas, p. 32) — a discrepancy with later Jain tradition.

**In Jain self-understanding**: Mahāvīra is not the founder of Jainism in the way Jesus is the founder of Christianity. "For the Jains, however, Mahāvīra is merely one of a chain of teachers who all communicate the same truths in broadly similar ways and his biography, rather than being discrete, has to be treated as part of the larger totality of the Universal History" (Dundas, p. 20). Not until the 9th century CE does a biography appear (Vimalasūri's *Vasudevahiṇḍī*; Aṣṭāga's Sanskrit biography VC) that treats his career without reference to the other fordmakers (Dundas, p. 20).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Mahāvīra exemplifies the supreme Jain virtues: non-violence, austerity, equanimity, and the conquest of karma. The twenty-two "endurances" (*pariṣaha*) — physical and mental afflictions abstracted from his ascetic career — became a stereotyped list encoding "all the difficulties to which Jain ascetics have always been subjected" (Dundas, p. 29). Every Jain monk and nun, through fasting and mendicancy, "partly replicates Mahāvīra's austerities" (Dundas, p. 29).

As [[tirthankara|fordmaker]], Mahāvīra is also the model *mahāpuruṣa* (Great Man). He bears the auspicious wheel mark on his foot, signifying spiritual sovereignty over the universe; Indra declares him "the Lord of the Three Worlds of earth, heaven and the intermediate region, an emperor of religion, benefactor of the world, bestower of safety" (AvNiry / Universal History; cited in Dundas, p. 37).

Mahāvīra's omniscience is central to Jain epistemology: only the omniscient person can "know and see what lies beneath outward reality and as a result teach the correct spiritual path" (Dundas, p. 35). Unlike the Buddha, who "refused to engage in metaphysical speculation not specifically conducive to salvation," Mahāvīra was willing to discuss matters beyond normal human experience (Dundas, p. 35).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Dating controversy**: Mahāvīra's traditional Śvetāmbara dates are 599–527 BCE; the Digambara tradition holds he died in 510 BCE. Since Mahāvīra predeceased the Buddha (and the two were contemporaries), if the scholarly redating of the Buddha's death to between 411 and 400 BCE is accepted, Mahāvīra's death would shift to around 425 BCE or a few years after (Dundas, pp. 25–26). The Jain community has not accepted this redating and continues to employ the "Vīra Era" starting from 527 BCE, the oldest chronological reckoning still in use in India (Dundas, p. 26).

**Birthplace controversy**: The Śvetāmbaras and Digambaras disagree about the location of Kuṇḍagrāma; there is general concord only on the location of his death at Pāvā (Dundas, p. 26).

**The embryo transfer**: The transfer of Mahāvīra's embryo from a brahman womb appears only in later biographical strata (Ācārāṅga 2, Kalpasūtra) and is interpreted by Dundas either as (a) a polemic devaluing brahman authority (fordmakers cannot be born brahman) or (b) an analogy with the anomalous births of other great religious figures (the Buddha, Jesus) that places the fordmaker "both human and at the same time transcending the normal mortal state" (Dundas, pp. 27–28).

**Relationship to Pārśva**: Mahāvīra is in the Universal History presented as renouncing the world alone, without entering any already-existing ascetic corporation (AvNiry 225; Dundas, p. 32). Yet the Ācārāṅga 2 notes that Mahāvīra's parents were "followers of Pārśva and lay devotees of Jain ascetics" (AS 2.15), leading to scholarly conclusions that Mahāvīra "must have renounced within Pārśva's ascetic lineage" (Dundas, p. 28–29). The two fordmakers' codes differ: Pārśva followed a Fourfold Restraint; Mahāvīra stipulated Five Great Vows (adding celibacy). See [[parshvanatha|Pārśvanātha]] and the discussions in Dundas pp. 30–34.

**Relationship to Makkhali Gosāla**: The Jain sources (primarily Bhagavatī 15) claim Gosāla was Mahāvīra's disciple for six years before breaking away. Historians note that "there remains the possibility that Mahāvīra and early Jainism were influenced by Ājīvika teachings" and that "there are inconsistencies in Jain karma theory which could be explained with reference to what little is known of Ājīvika doctrine" (Dundas, p. 30). See [[makkhali-gosala|Makkhali Gosāla]].

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Mahāvīra's figure is the primary reference point throughout Jain scripture and commentary. The twelve chief disciples (*gaṇadharas*) headed by Indrabhūti Gautama were held to have transmitted his teachings. Śvetāmbara ascetic lineages trace their descent from Sudharman, one of the gaṇadharas (Dundas, p. 39). The *samavasaraṇa* (preaching assembly) became the template for Jain temple architecture. Every Jain monk and nun's practice is modeled on Mahāvīra's austerities.

Indrabhūti Gautama (called simply Gautama), the chief *gaṇadhara*, became an object of independent Śvetāmbara devotion by the medieval period, with imagery depicting him as "chubby and jolly" — stemming from an episode in which he preaches austerity while being himself corpulent — and his name is written on the first page of new business account books on the Jain new year (coinciding with Gautama's enlightenment) "to ensure continuing auspiciousness" (Dundas, pp. 39–40).
