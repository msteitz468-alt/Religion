# Tulsidas

**Tulsidas** (c. 1532–1623 CE) is one of the principal architects of **North Indian Vaishnavism** —
specifically the devotion to [[rama|Rāma]] as the supreme deity — and the author of the
*Ramcaritmanas* ("The Holy Lake of the Acts of Rāma"), a Hindi retelling of Valmiki's *Rāmāyaṇa*
that has shaped popular Hinduism in North India more than any other single text.

## Life and Context

Tulsidas was a Brahmin from the Varanasi region and was close to several movers and shakers at the
Mughal court, including Man Singh (Akbar's general and governor of Vrindavan). He composed his poem
at a pilgrimage center that had been attacked by Muslims; in this context he claimed that even Muslims
would be saved by Rāma's name — a remark structurally parallel to earlier assertions that any
pilgrimage site would save even Pariahs.

The Brahmins of Varanasi were reportedly shocked by the composition of a major religious text in a
vernacular language (Hindi/Avadhi), not Sanskrit. They tested its authority by placing it in the
Shiva temple overnight, with the Vedas and Puranas placed on top of it; in the morning, the
*Ramcaritmanas* was on top — confirming its sanctity by the "buoyancy test" (a South Indian motif
— which texts float upstream? — here translated into which text rises to the top of the pile?).

## The *Ramcaritmanas* and the Shadow Sita

Tulsidas's most theologically distinctive contribution was his treatment of Sita's fire ordeal
(*agnipariksha*). In the centuries since Valmiki, as Rāma became one of the two great gods of
Vaishnavism, Sita's abandonment had grown more theologically vexing: why would God treat his wife
this way?

Tulsidas's solution incorporated the **shadow Sita** (*maya-sita*) tradition from earlier Sanskrit
texts: the real Sita entered the fire at the start of Rāvana's abduction and remained safe; the
shadow form took her place. When the shadow Sita enters the fire at the ordeal:

> "Both the shadow form and the stigma of public shame" are consumed in the blazing fire.

This allows Tulsidas to argue that Rāma never tested Sita — he knew the shadow was not the real Sita —
but goaded the shadow Sita into the fire precisely to retrieve the real one. Tulsidas **omits entirely**
the Uttarakanda (Sita bears twin sons in the forest, enters the earth) — his *Ramcaritmanas* ends with
Rāma and Sita together, happily ever after.

## Rāma and the Crow: Caste and the Masking Device

Tulsidas challenges caste most powerfully through the figure of a crow, explicitly identified as an
"uppity Pariah in a former life, cursed by Shiva to be reborn as a crow."

The infant Rāma pursues the crow no matter how high it flies; it falls into the child's mouth and
watches for thousands of years as Rāma is born again and again on earth. When Rāma laughs, the crow
falls out of his mouth, terrified, and Rāma grants it eternal devotion.

This story — giving the manifestation of god's universal form (normally reserved for Brahma, Vishnu,
Arjuna, or Krishna's own mother) to a crow, an unclean scavenger — is "the caste issue explicit here":
a Pariah in the form of a crow experiences the vision of the divine that Brahmins claim as their
birthright. Tulsidas works "through the masking device of an animal" to avoid direct confrontation
with Brahmin authority.

This stands in pointed contrast to Valmiki's *Ramayana*, in which Rāma regards a crow as an enemy
and blinds him. The rewriting reveals Tulsidas's theological commitment: universal grace, even for
Pariahs, even for the form of scavengers.

## Position in North Indian Bhakti

The *Ramcaritmanas* is the defining text of **North Indian Rāma bhakti**, still recited and enacted
each year at the *Rāmlīlā* performances throughout North India, particularly at Ramnagar near
Varanasi. It holds the position in North India that the Tamil *Tiruvāymoḷi* holds in the South —
the central devotional text for mass Hindu practice.

While tulsidas's theology broadly upholds Brahmin social order and caste hierarchy — with "moments of
compassion for Pariahs and tribals" rather than systematic critique — his use of the shadow Sita
and the crow story show that his Vedantic sensibility (illusion allows the substitution; compassion
extends beyond caste) was not merely ornamental.
