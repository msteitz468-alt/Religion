## Biographical Overview

Guru Har Rai was the seventh Guru of the Sikhs, the younger son of Gurditta and grandson of [[guru-hargobind|Guru Hargobind]]. He succeeded his grandfather in 1644. McLeod notes that "at Guru Har Rai's succession the Panth retired into obscurity for at least twenty-five years" (McLeod ~L82–85). Official Mughal disfavour continued: within a year of his succession he was obliged to leave Kiratpur and withdraw further into the Shivalik hills, where the Raja of Bilaspur provided shelter. For much of his period as Guru he lived near the Shivalik town of Sirmur (McLeod ~L87–93).

## Character and Traditions

McLeod reports that "Guru Har Rai was a person of very gentle disposition" (McLeod ~L94–95). Two characteristic anecdotes are preserved: (1) walking through a field, he accidentally broke a flower stem with his clothing and was so distressed that he thereafter always tucked his clothing away; (2) he enjoyed hunting but never killed — instead he kept a large zoo or aviary of live animals (McLeod ~L97–116).

## The Masands and Panth Growth

The period is "exceedingly difficult" to document historically. McLeod allows that growth in Sikh numbers probably continued in the Malwa area (he is credited with converting the family that eventually produced the princely rulers of Patiala, Jind, and Nabha), but there may have been decline of allegiance in Doaba and Majha where people were "cut off from all effective contact with their Guru" (McLeod ~L128–142). The masands — Guru Ram Das's system of deputies — were growing more independent during this period (McLeod ~L144–158).

## Succession Dispute: Ram Rai Disinherited

A significant anecdote explains why the Guruship passed to Har Rai's second son rather than his eldest. Guru Har Rai had a connection with Dara Shikoh, the elder son of Emperor Shahjahan, which attracted Aurangzeb's hostile attention after Dara Shikoh was defeated in the war of succession. Instead of appearing in Delhi himself, Guru Har Rai sent his elder son Ram Rai, who (according to Sikh tradition) proved "the perfect sycophant": challenged on an Adi Granth reference to *mittī musalman* ("the earth formed from the bodies of Muslims"), Ram Rai changed the words to *mittī biman* ("faithless people") to please the emperor. On learning this, "Guru Har Rai declared that Ram Rai should never see his father's face again and should not succeed to the position of Guru" (McLeod ~L184–186). McLeod notes this tradition has no corroboration outside itself, and likely other reasons — probably involving the Mughals, who preferred Ram Rai as successor and granted him land in Dehra Dun — were also at play (McLeod ~L191–213).

## Death and Succession

Guru Har Rai died in 1661. Because Ram Rai was disinherited, the Guruship fell to his second son, a child of six: [[guru-har-krishan|Guru Har Krishan]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The episode of Ram Rai and the altered scripture became a touchstone in Sikh tradition for the inviolability of the Adi Granth's text and for uncompromising integrity before political authority.
