## Biographical Overview

Eusebius of Nicomedia was a bishop who served as the most prominent ecclesiastical supporter of [[arius|Arius]] and as the leader of what Ayres calls the "Eusebian" theological bloc. Ayres notes that the ancient phrase "those around Eusebius" (used first by [[athanasius|Alexander of Alexandria]]) originally designated specifically those who "supported Eusebius of Nicomedia in his theological and ecclesiastical policies" (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*–549). He was a bishop "influential with the Emperors Licinius, Constantine, and Constantius". Only "one complete letter and a few fragments" of his writing survive.

Like Arius and [[asterius-the-sophist|Asterius the Sophist]], Eusebius is linked by the ancient sources to the theologian and martyr **Lucian of Antioch** (martyred c. 312). Arius himself wrote to Eusebius using the epithet "co-Lucianist," marking a shared sense of theological dependence.

## Role in the Fourth-Century Controversy (per Ayres)

Ayres uses "Eusebian" as a broad theological label for all who "would have found common ground with either of Arius' most prominent supporters, Eusebius of Nicomedia or [[eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius of Caesarea]]". The two Eusebii held "distinct and yet close enough" positions "for them to be allied in opposition to Alexander". The label is primarily theological rather than a claim of personal or organizational unity: "Many eastern bishops rallied around the Eusebii even while differing among themselves".

Eusebius of Nicomedia's letter to Paulinus, bishop of Tyre, written to rally support for Arius, is the main surviving evidence of his theology.

## Theological Position / Trajectory

Eusebius belongs to what Ayres calls the trajectory of "theologians of the 'one Unbegotten.'" The central move in his theology is to ground the Son's status in the **will of the Father** rather than in any participation in the divine nature. In his letter to Paulinus he writes:

> [There is] one, the unoriginated, and one produced by him truly and not from his substance, not participating at all in the unoriginated nature nor in his substance, but produced as altogether different in his nature and in his power, being in complete likeness of disposition and power to him who made him... Ayres glosses: "For Eusebius the status of the Word does not stem from sharing in the divine attributes as the only one generated from the divine being, but from a decision of the divine will". The Son is described as possessing "complete likeness of disposition and power" to the Father, but this likeness is a gift of will, not a share of substance.

Ayres notes that this particular fragment "does not provide us with any information about how Eusebius made use of the theme" of the Son being "like the Father" — a gap that limits full reconstruction of his theology.

### Relationship to Lucian of Antioch

Eusebius's connection to Lucian was formative enough that Arius addressed him as "co-Lucianist." Ayres treats Lucian as a "prominent exponent of a theology that did not originate with him," probably emphasizing "three distinct hypostases and... a theology of the Son as revelatory but subordinate image". However, Ayres warns: "we cannot know what his students learnt from him" because "so little survives of Lucian".

## Reception and Later Labelling

Ayres's wider argument is that Eusebius of Nicomedia should not be read as simply "Arius's disciple" or as the head of a disciplined "Arian party." Rather, the "Eusebian" alliance was a loose coalition of theologically diverse eastern bishops who "rallied around the Eusebii" for a range of theological and ecclesiastical reasons. Arius's own teaching — especially his claim that "the Son does not know the Father" — "seems to have been at odds with the theologies of other 'Lucianists'—and with other Eusebians". Eusebius of Nicomedia is thus better understood as the political and episcopal centre of a diverse bloc than as the theological spokesman of a single creed.

## Influence

Eusebius's influence was primarily ecclesiastical and political: his access to successive emperors shaped the institutional fortunes of the controversy in the decades before and immediately after Nicaea. In the decades after Nicaea, Ayres notes, "the forces that came together under 'Eusebian' guidance" included many bishops united above all in "opposition to the theology of [[marcellus-of-ancyra|Marcellus of Ancyra]]". Theological diversity within this bloc eventually produced both those who moved toward an increasingly sharp subordinationism and those who contributed to [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene theology]].
