Jehu son of Nimshi, an Israelite army commander anointed by a prophet at Ramoth-gilead, who
destroyed the house of [[ahab|Ahab]], killed [[jezebel|Jezebel]] and the kings of both Israel and
Judah, exterminated the Baal worshippers of [[samaria|Samaria]], and founded the longest-lasting
northern dynasty — and whom [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] praises and faults in the same breath.

## Biographical Overview

At Horeb, [[elijah|Elijah]] is commissioned to anoint Hazael over Aram, Jehu over Israel, and
[[elisha|Elisha]] as prophet. The commission is executed in 2 Kings 9, when Elisha sends a member
of the prophetic guild to Ramoth-gilead to pour oil on Jehu's head with the charge to strike down
the house of Ahab and avenge the blood of the prophets and of Jezebel's victims. His fellow
officers spread their garments under him and blow the trumpet.

Jehu drives to Jezreel, where the wounded king Joram of Israel is convalescing and Ahaziah of Judah
is visiting. To Joram's "Is it peace?" he answers, "What peace, so long as the harlotries and
sorceries of your mother Jezebel are so many?" and shoots him between the shoulders, ordering the
body thrown on the plot of Naboth the Jezreelite in explicit fulfillment of Elijah's oracle.
Ahaziah is pursued and shot as well. Jezebel, at the window, is thrown down by her own eunuchs,
trampled by the horses, and left to the dogs.

He then writes to the guardians of Ahab's seventy sons in Samaria, daring them to fight; they
submit, and the sons are beheaded and their heads sent in baskets and piled in two heaps at the
city gate, where Jehu stands in the morning and tells the people, "You are innocent." He
slaughters the remaining house of Ahab in Jezreel, meets and kills forty-two kinsmen of Ahaziah at
the pit of Beth-eked, takes Jehonadab son of Rechab up into his chariot to "see my zeal for the
LORD," and proclaims a great assembly for Baal, saying Ahab served Baal a little but Jehu will
serve him much. When the temple of Baal is packed and vestments distributed, eighty men posted
outside cut down the worshippers; the pillar and the house are pulled down and made a latrine.

The narrator commends him: because he did well in carrying out what was right, his sons to the
fourth generation will sit on Israel's throne. In the same passage the narrator faults him for not
turning from the sins of [[jeroboam|Jeroboam]] — the calves at [[bethel|Bethel]] and [[dan|Dan]]
remained. His reign is also when Hazael of Aram begins to cut off territory from Israel.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 19:16–17** — Elijah's Horeb commission naming Jehu.
- **2 Kings 9:1–13** — the anointing at Ramoth-gilead and the acclamation.
- **2 Kings 9:14–37** — the killing of Joram on Naboth's plot, the death of Ahaziah of Judah, the
  defenestration and trampling of Jezebel.
- **2 Kings 10:1–17** — the letters to Samaria; the seventy heads in baskets at the gate; the pit
  of Beth-eked; Jehonadab in the chariot.
- **2 Kings 10:18–28** — the Baal assembly, the massacre, the demolition of the temple.
- **2 Kings 10:29–36** — the double verdict: the dynastic promise to the fourth generation, and the
  charge that he kept the calves; Hazael's encroachment; his death and burial in Samaria.
- **2 Chronicles 22:7–9** — the Chronicler's brief and differing notice of Ahaziah's death at
  Jehu's hands.
- **Hosea 1:4** — the naming of Jezreel, "for yet a little while and I will punish the house of
  Jehu for the blood of Jezreel," a prophetic evaluation in tension with the Kings commendation.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation in Kings** — Jehu is the one northern king given a positive
  dynastic reward, yet still measured against the standing charge of the sin of Jeroboam. Both
  halves of that verdict are the narrator's theology and are attributed as such.
- **[[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] (NIBC, 1995)**, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|the Kings page]]:
  Jehu's purge is read as **incomplete** — a reform that removes the Baal cult without removing the
  calf cult — within the larger pattern of salvation within judgment across the Elisha material.
- **Prophetic counter-verdict.** Hosea 1:4 sets a later prophetic condemnation of "the blood of
  Jezreel" against Kings' commendation. Recorded as a tension internal to the canon.
- No rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic treatment of Jehu has been ingested into this wiki; none is
  attributed here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jehu is the point where Kings' engine of prophetic word and fulfillment runs at full force: Elijah's
sentence in Naboth's vineyard is cited as the coup unfolds, and the narrative marks each killing
against a prior oracle. He is also where the book's moral grammar is most strained. The same
narrative that authorizes his commission by prophetic anointing describes deception, mass
execution, and a cult massacre carried out by a ruse, and then withholds full approval on grounds
unrelated to any of it. The result is a portrait of zeal that is simultaneously commissioned,
rewarded, limited, and — in Hosea — later held against his house.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **External attestation.** The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III, an Assyrian monument, carries a
  register showing a submitting tributary whose accompanying caption is standardly read as naming
  Jehu, identified in the Assyrian text by a dynastic label conventionally rendered "of the house
  of Omri" — a designation in evident tension with Jehu's overthrow of that house, and much
  discussed for that reason. The identification with the biblical Jehu is widely accepted; the
  reading of the label, and whether the depicted figure is the king himself or an envoy, are
  debated. No tribute list details are asserted here.
- **The Tel Dan inscription**, an Aramaic victory stele fragment found at [[dan|Dan]], is generally
  read as a king of Aram boasting of killing an Israelite and a Judahite king, and it contains the
  phrase widely read as "house of David." Some historians take it as a rival account of the deaths
  of Joram and Ahaziah, ascribing them to an Aramean king rather than to Jehu. The readings and the
  reconstruction of the broken text are contested; this is recorded as a live position and not
  adjudicated against 2 Kings 9.
- **The coup and Assyria.** Whether Jehu's revolt is best explained in religious, factional, or
  geopolitical terms — including any relation to Assyrian pressure and the collapse of the Omride
  alliance system — is disputed among historians; the biblical account gives a prophetic
  explanation, and the two kinds of account are not treated here as competing on the same terms.
- **Kings vs Chronicles [recorded tension].** The circumstances of Ahaziah's death differ between
  2 Kings 9 and 2 Chronicles 22; both are preserved.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Jehu is a standard case in discussion of prophetically sanctioned violence, of the limits of the
Deuteronomistic evaluation formula, and of intra-canonical disagreement, since Hosea's verdict on
Jezreel is difficult to reconcile with the Kings commendation. In historical scholarship he is one
of the few Israelite kings for whom an external depiction is claimed, and so a fixture in debate
over correlating biblical and Assyrian records. Cross-links: [[ahab|Ahab]], [[jezebel|Jezebel]],
