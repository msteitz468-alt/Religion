Duryodhana is the chief Kaurava prince and primary antagonist of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], eldest of the hundred sons of blind king [[dhritarashtra]] and Queen [[gandhari]]. In Rajagopalachari's retelling he is driven by inordinate ambition, jealousy, and fierce pride — but dies with unbroken spirit, earning the shower of flowers from the gods and recognition from Krishna himself that he was invincible in fair battle.

## Biographical Overview

Duryodhana is the eldest son of Dhritarashtra and Gandhari. His birth is framed by the ill omens he represents: from his youth he is the source of enmity toward the Pandavas. Rajagopalachari consistently presents him as a man of genuine valour corrupted by greed and bad counsel — particularly that of [[shakuni]] and Karna. Dhritarashtra himself laments: "The Destroyer entered his mind and he sought his own ruin. Drona deprecated war, so also did Bhishma, Bhurisravas, Kripa and others. But my obstinate son would not listen. Impelled by inordinate ambition, he got entangled in anger and hatred, and invited this ruinous war." (Ch. LXXXIII, lines 9093–9096)

## Primary Source Appearances

### Duryodhana in the Kurukshetra Battle (Chs. LXXIX–XCI)

Throughout the battle chapters in this range, Duryodhana repeatedly confronts and reproaches Drona for failing to capture Yudhishthira or neutralize Arjuna. In Ch. LXXIX (lines 8630–8648), he accuses Drona of deliberate inaction: "I cannot understand what makes it hard for you to carry out your promise to me." Drona bears the insult with nobility: "You entertain thoughts unworthy of a king." Rajagopalachari notes this recurrent pattern: "Having undertaken a war of adharma, he was often led to speak in this manner and hurt the feelings of the acharyas who stood loyally by him, even when they saw the wickedness of his ways." (lines 8759–8762)

In Ch. LXXXIII (lines 9117–9151), Drona gives Duryodhana charmed armour to intercept Arjuna. When Arjuna faces him, he notes Duryodhana wears the armour "as a bullock might do" and systematically pierces the unarmoured gaps until "he could bear it no longer and turned and fled." (lines 9179–9187)

In Ch. LXXXVI (lines 9401–9428), again reproaching Drona: "Arjuna, Bhima and Satyaki have treated us with contempt...Everyone asks how it is that the great Drona with all his mastery of the science of war, has been so badly outmanoeuvred." Drona replies unperturbed: "Duryodhana, your accusations are as unworthy as they are contrary to truth."

### The Final Stand (Ch. XCII, lines 10062–10329)

After Karna's death, Duryodhana's grief is immediate and deep: "his grief knew no bounds." (line 10063) Kripacharya urges peace; Duryodhana refuses with dignity:

> "Perhaps, there was a time for that, but it is long past. What talk can there be of peace between the Pandavas and us with all this inexpiable blood between us...If I surrender in order to escape death, how can I escape the contempt of the world? What happiness can I hope to have in a life so ignobly saved?" (lines 10072–10078)

Salya is named supreme commander; he falls to Yudhishthira. The remaining sons of Dhritarashtra are slain by Bhima; Sakuni falls to Sahadeva. The army disintegrates. Duryodhana, "left almost alone, took up his mace and walked towards a pool of water. His whole frame was burning like fire, and water attracted him." (lines 10110–10113) "The wise Vidura knew what would happen and he told us," he reflects.

When the Pandavas arrive, Yudhishthira taunts him. Duryodhana's reply is strikingly self-possessed:

> "I have not come here, Dharmaputra, a fugitive for my life. It was not fear that brought me here. I stepped into the water to cool the fire that is raging within me. I neither fear death nor wish to live, but why should I fight? The earth has now nothing left that I came to fight for! All those who stood by me have been slain. My desire for kingdom is gone. I leave the world to you without a rival." (lines 10124–10130)

He offers the Pandavas the entire kingdom. Yudhishthira refuses, citing the outrage on Draupadi: "The wrongs you did us, and the outrage you perpetrated on Draupadi, cannot be expiated except with your life." (lines 10135–10137)

Stepping from the water, Duryodhana offers single combat against any one of them: "Come, one by one, all of you, for I am single." Yudhishthira's reply invokes Abhimanyu. Duryodhana selects Bhima as his opponent.

The mace duel is long and equal — "Duryodhana and Bhima were equal in strength and skill." (line 10154) At Krishna's signal, Bhima strikes Duryodhana's thighs, breaking them. Duryodhana falls "mortally wounded." Bhima stamps on his head; Yudhishthira rebukes him. Duryodhana addresses Krishna with a prolonged denunciation, listing each stratagem by which Krishna engineered the deaths of Bhishma, Drona, Karna, Ghatotkacha, Bhurisravas, and Jayadratha. He identifies Krishna as "sole cause of our destruction." (lines 10172–10296) Then:

> "Living, I was a great prince, generous friend, and a terrible foe. All human joys, such joys as kings wish for in vain, and even Gods do not despise, have been mine, in their fullness, and warrior's death is the fitting crown of such a life. Dying, I go triumphantly to swarg to join my friends and my brothers who have gone there already and are waiting to welcome me. You remain here below, your objects defeated and yourselves the object of contempt of all kshatriyas...Who is more blest, I, or you who, doomed to linger here, mourning for slaughtered friends in desolate homes, find the long sought triumph but ashes in your mouth?" (lines 10180–10324)

When Duryodhana says this, "flowers were showered down from the heavens by the gods. Inordinate desire took Duryodhana into the wrong path...but no one could question the unconquerable spirit of Dhritarashtra's son." (lines 10190–10192) Krishna acknowledges: "There is truth in what Duryodhana said. You could not have defeated him by fair means. This wicked man was invincible in battle." (lines 10328–10330)

### The Night Attack and Death (Ch. XCIV–XCV)

When Ashvatthama reports that he has killed all the Pandava sons and the Panchalas in the night attack, Duryodhana "slowly opened his eyes and, with struggling breath, gasped out these words: 'Aswatthama, you have indeed done for me what neither the great Bhishma nor the valiant Karna could achieve! You have gladdened my heart and I die happy.'" (lines 10450–10452) He then expires.

### Duryodhana in Heaven (Ch. CVI)

In Yudhishthira's final trial, when he arrives in swarga he finds Duryodhana "seated on a beautiful throne and he shone with the splendour of the sun and around him stood in attendance the goddess of heroism and other angels." (lines 11376–11377) Yudhishthira is indignant and refuses to remain; he demands to find his brothers. Narada rebukes him: "The brave Duryodhana has attained his present state by force of kshatriya dharma. It is not right to let the things of the flesh stay in the mind and breed ill-will." (lines 11393–11396) In the end it emerges that the apparent hell where Yudhishthira's brothers suffer was an illusion designed to test Yudhishthira; all — including Duryodhana — are in fact serene in their divine states.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Rajagopalachari's Duryodhana is a complex figure: undeniably the source of calamity, yet possessed of genuine warrior virtues — courage, generosity to friends, loyalty to those who served him, and a willingness to face death without flinching. Rajagopalachari does not sentimentalize this or exculpate Duryodhana, but he allows the epic's own ambivalence to stand. The flowers from heaven and Krishna's admission of Duryodhana's invincibility signal the tradition's recognition that kshatriya dharma, even in an unjust cause, can earn its own reward.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Duryodhana functions in the narrative as the engine of adharma whose removal is necessary for the world's moral order to be restored. Yet the epic also uses him to interrogate what that restoration costs: the Pandavas win a "long sought triumph" that proves "ashes in their mouths" (Duryodhana's words at line 10324); Rajagopalachari finds this observation to contain "truth." The final image — Duryodhana in heaven while the victors grieve — forces the reader to question whether victory through stratagem is morally superior to defeat with honour.

The episode of Balarama's reproach (Ch. XCIII) develops this further: Balarama condemns Bhima's blow below the navel as a violation of single-combat laws. Though Krishna argues Duryodhana earned this through thirteen years of wrongs, Balarama insists: "Bhima's fame has been tarnished for all time." This debate is unresolved in the text.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Duryodhana is a legendary figure. The ethics of his death — struck below the navel in violation of mace-combat law — are explicitly flagged as controversial within the narrative itself. The positions are:
- **Balarama**: Bhima's blow was a breach of kshatriya law, a "great blot on his good name." (Ch. XCIII, lines 10239–10242)
- **Krishna**: Duryodhana's lifelong wrongs against the Pandavas justified the oath Bhima had sworn; the era of Kali has arrived and the laws of a previous age cannot apply. (lines 10220–10235)
- **Duryodhana himself**: "By base tricks you contrived the death of warriors who fought bravely according to the laws of war." (lines 10172–10175)

Rajagopalachari does not adjudicate but presents all three positions.
