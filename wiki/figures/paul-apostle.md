# Paul the Apostle

## Biographical Overview
Born in Tarsus (a Roman citizen), educated as a Pharisee under Gamaliel, initially a zealous persecutor of the early Christian movement (Acts 7-8; Gal. 1:13-14; Phil. 3:4-6). Converted via a direct Christophany on the road to Damascus (Acts 9; Gal. 1:15-16; 1 Cor. 15:8). Undertook multiple missionary journeys across Asia Minor, Macedonia, and Achaia, founding numerous Gentile churches. Imprisoned in Caesarea and Rome; traditionally martyred under Nero c. AD 64-67.

From Bock's Acts (this batch): Paul's story is a major thread in the second half of Acts, but God remains the central character. His conversion is by Jesus' direct intervention; his protection and direction (shipwreck, etc.) illustrate divine orchestration. Bock highlights the "we passages" as eyewitness support and the speeches as summaries of the gospel message.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Acts of the Apostles** (Bock): The primary narrative source for Paul's missionary journeys, trials, and journey to Rome. Bock stresses the selectivity (not every community detailed) and the God-centered perspective: Paul is the instrument through whom the risen Lord continues his work.
- **Pauline Epistles**: Provide primary, first-hand theological and autobiographical information about his ministry, theology, and relationships with specific churches. In **Galatians 1-2**, he provides a crucial autobiographical sketch detailing his conversion, his early travels to Arabia and Damascus, and key meetings with the Jerusalem apostles, emphasizing his apostolic independence.
- **Ephesians**: Paul presents himself uniquely as the "prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles" (Eph 3:1), reframing his physical imprisonment as a structurally necessary, divinely ordained suffering that serves to unite Jews and Gentiles and reveal God's mystery to the cosmic powers.
- **2 Peter**: Refers to Paul's letters as scripture (2 Pet 3:15-16).
- Cross-references in Gospels/Acts for pre-conversion (persecutor) and post-conversion (e.g., with Barnabas, Silas, Timothy, Titus as companions).

## Theological and Narrative Significance
The preeminent Apostle to the Gentiles. His letters form the theological foundation of much of Christian doctrine, particularly concerning justification by faith, the inclusion of Gentiles without the Mosaic Law, the nature of the Church as the Body of Christ, new creation, and eschatology. In Acts (Bock), he models the faithful witness who, under God's direction, takes the message "to the ends of the earth" despite opposition. His arc (persecutor → apostle to Gentiles) embodies the reversal and grace themes of Luke-Acts.

From this batch's sources: In Acts, Paul's conversion and missions illustrate the four dominant issues (Gentile inclusion, Jewish response, Jesus' ongoing lordship, faithful witnesses). His companions (Barnabas, Silas, Timothy, Titus) are co-workers in the mission, not subordinates.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
The major interpretive controversy surrounds the relationship between the Paul of Acts (portrayed as more compliant with Jewish customs) and the Paul of the Epistles (who vehemently opposes the imposition of the Law on Gentiles). There is also significant debate regarding the authorship of the Pastoral Epistles and some other letters (Deutero-Paulines). The "New Perspective on Paul" debates his understanding of first-century Judaism and justification.

Bock (Acts) defends the speeches as authentic summaries and the "we passages" as historical; he notes the selectivity serves the theological narrative of God's plan.

### Paul's Pre-Conversion Persecution and the Phinehas Tradition (Hurtado)

[[larry-hurtado|Larry W. Hurtado]] (*Destroyer of the Gods*, ch. 2) provides a detailed account of Paul's pre-conversion persecution that situates it within a specific Jewish tradition. The key texts are Paul's own: *"I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it"* (Gal 1:13, using the Greek *porthein*, which denotes violent destruction, not mere harassment); *"as to zeal, a persecutor of the church"* (Phil 3:6).

The word "zeal" (*zēlos*) in Paul's self-description is not a generic term for intensity. Hurtado argues it invokes the **Phinehas tradition** — a specific strand of Jewish precedent derived from Numbers 25:1-15. Phinehas, the grandson of Aaron, killed an Israelite man and his Midianite partner while they were engaging in pagan worship, thereby turning aside a plague from Israel. God commended Phinehas' action as displaying "zeal for his God" and granted him an eternal covenant of priesthood. This tradition was alive in Paul's time:
- **1 Maccabees 2:24-26**: Mattathias' killing of an apostate Jew echoes the Phinehas action, explicitly invoking the comparison.
- **Philo of Alexandria** (*On the Special Laws* 1.55; 3.124-125): Describes "holy zeal" that is prepared to punish covenant violators personally, even without legal process.

The implication of Hurtado's reading: Paul did not regard the early Jesus-movement merely as a legal deviation requiring correction but as a **public shaming of God's covenant identity** — specifically, the attribution of divine-status devotion to the crucified Jesus, whom Torah declared accursed (Deut 21:23; Gal 3:13). From a rigorously monotheistic Pharisaic standpoint, the [[dyadic-devotion|dyadic devotion]] the Jesus-movement directed to the crucified Jesus would have looked like the most egregious kind of covenant violation. Paul's persecution was thus — on this reading — not ethnic tribalism or institutional self-protection but principled theological violence, the precise kind the Phinehas tradition authorized.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Virtually all Christian theology is an extended commentary on Pauline thought. Commentators like Augustine, Luther, and Calvin drew heavily on Paul for doctrines of grace, predestination, and justification. Modern critical scholarship often focuses on his social context and apocalyptic worldview.

- **Bock (Acts)**: Paul as the instrument of the risen Lord; his story shows how God works through human agents to fulfill the promise to Abraham and bring light to the Gentiles. Miracles and protection undergird his authority.
- **Yarbrough** (Pastoral Epistles, cross-referenced in broader NT): Paul's self-understanding as servant and apostle — suffering and humiliation as part of the commission. Strong defense of the historicity of the miraculous as foundational to authority and the transmission of revelation to delegates like Timothy and Titus.
- **Hurtado** (*Destroyer of the Gods*): Paul's letters are the earliest documentary evidence for the full [[dyadic-devotion|dyadic devotion]] pattern. In 1 Corinthians 8:4-6, Paul reformulates the [[shema|Shema]] to include Jesus as the "one Lord" alongside God as the "one Father" — the most concentrated single text for the new devotional pattern. In Philippians 2:9-11, he applies the language of Isaiah 45:23 (every knee bowing to YHWH) directly to Jesus. In Romans 10:9-13, calling on the name of the Lord (= Jesus) is salvation. These texts, written c. AD 50-55 but reflecting practices and confessions from the very beginnings of the movement (Paul had been in the Jesus-movement since c. 32-34 AD), are Hurtado's primary literary evidence that high Christology is *original*, not a late development.
- **[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]]** (*[[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|How Jesus Became God]]*): reads Paul as the earliest witness whose Christology is already an **[[incarnation|incarnation]]** Christology — "higher" than the Synoptics despite being earlier — because Paul understood Christ as a pre-existent **angel** who became human ([[angel-of-the-lord|Angel of the Lord]]; Gal 4:14 *equating* Christ with an angel; 1 Cor 10:4 "the rock was Christ"; 1 Cor 15:47 "the second man is from heaven"; possibly Rom 9:5 calling Jesus "God"). The **Philippians 2:6–11 "Christ Poem"** (pre-Pauline) is a *transitional* Christology: a pre-existent divine being not yet *equal* with God, who after obedience unto death is **[[exaltation-christology|exalted]]** to that equality and given the divine name [[kyrios|Lord]] (alluding to Isa 45:23). Paul also quotes the older **resurrection-exaltation creed** of Rom 1:3–4. For visions as the ground of Paul's own faith, see [[resurrection-appearances-visions]]. (Ehrman's developmental account contrasts with Hurtado's "high Christology was original"; both recorded without adjudication — cf. [[kyrios-christology-origins]].)

### Thessalonian correspondence (Weima)

[[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]] (*[[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|1–2 Thessalonians, BECNT]]*) supplies the wiki's fullest reading of Paul's earliest surviving letters to a single congregation. In Weima's reconstruction: Paul, with cosenders [[silas|Silas/Silvanus]] and [[timothy|Timothy]], plants the church on the second journey (Philippi → Via Egnatia → Thessalonica); after a riot and bail that likely barred Paul and Silas from returning, Timothy's Athens→Thessalonica mission and "good news" report occasion **1 Thessalonians** from Corinth (late AD 50 / early 51); **2 Thessalonians** follows soon after, addressing a false claim that "the day of the Lord has come," intensified persecution, and rebellious idlers. Weima affirms both letters as Pauline, treats 2 Thess 2 pastorally (comfort that the day is not present—not an end-times timetable), and reads the 3:17 autograph as intensifying personal authority as well as authenticity. Full letter pages: [[1-thessalonians|1 Thessalonians]], [[2-thessalonians|2 Thessalonians]]; authorship debate: [[2-thessalonians-authorship|2 Thessalonians Authorship]].

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names. Bock treats both the Paul-of-Acts and the Paul-of-letters as
valuable, holding (with Witherington) that Luke was "a traveling companion of Paul's."*

Bock traces Paul from persecutor to apostle across the second half of Acts while insisting God, not
Paul, remains the hero. Saul is introduced consenting to Stephen's stoning and "trying to destroy"
the church (8:3); his **conversion** is told three times (Acts 9 in the third person, retold by Paul
in 22 and 26). Bock defends the term "conversion" (with Kern, against Stendahl and Gaventa): the God
worshiped does not change, but "the way this God is understood changes significantly." He treats the
event as a public Christophany rather than an inner crisis, harmonizing the apparent tension between
9:7 and 22:9 (the companions hear sound but nothing intelligible, see light but not Jesus), and reads
the differences among the three accounts as Lukan telescoping. A non-apostle, Ananias of Damascus,
mediates Saul's sight and the Spirit — Bock notes the significance of a non-apostolic mediator. Saul
escapes Damascus in a basket (harmonized with 2 Cor. 11:32–33 and the Arabia sojourn of Gal. 1:17),
and Barnabas vouches for him to the wary Jerusalem church (9:26–30).

On the **first journey** (13–14) Saul becomes Paul at Cyprus (13:9 — his Roman name, marking his
emergence as leader) and delivers the synagogue sermon at Pisidian Antioch (13:16–41), a Davidic-
promise argument built on Ps. 2:7, Isa. 55:3, and Ps. 16:10 linked by *gezerah shewa*. Its climax is
the **justification** statement (13:38–39): "Jesus can provide what Moses and the law could not,
namely, justification"; Bock reads the law as "utterly inadequate" and — against Vielhauer and
Barrett, who deny the phrase a full Pauline sense — argues this is "nothing more than a Lukan
telescoping of Paul's view," where the legal declaration of forgiveness leads to release from sin and
the gift of the Spirit (cf. Rom. 3–8). At Lystra Paul's natural-theology speech (14:15–17), stopping
a pagan sacrifice after the Zeus/Hermes acclamation, models preaching to purely pagan Gentiles and
anticipates the Areopagus; he is then stoned and left for dead (14:19). At the **Jerusalem
Consultation** (15) Paul and Barnabas report signs and wonders and are vindicated in the law-free
Gentile mission.

On the **second and third journeys** Bock highlights Paul's discernment between principle and
strategy. He **circumcises Timothy** (16:3) — because Timothy has a Jewish mother and a Greek father,
a mixed-ancestry case, not the unmixed-Gentile question of Acts 15 — while **Titus**, an unmixed
Gentile, is pointedly *not* circumcised (Gal. 2:3–5); Bock makes this contrast load-bearing for the
Acts–Paul question, arguing Paul "is not standing up for the law… but is affirming Jewish heritage
alongside Gentile heritage." The Spirit of Jesus redirects the itinerary to Macedonia (16:6–10); at
Philippi Paul reveals his **Roman citizenship** only after the beating, a deliberate strategic
delay. The **Areopagus speech** (17:22–31) is, for Bock, the fullest model of addressing a Gentile
audience — "natural theology" rooted in Jewish thought (Gärtner), quoting Aratus, halting at
resurrection because the Greeks lacked the category; against Vielhauer's authenticity doubt, Bock
sees Paul contextualizing, not contradicting Rom. 1. Ephesus (ch. 19) and the Miletus farewell to
the elders (20:17–38, including 20:28, "the church of God… obtained with his own blood") frame Paul
the pastor.

The arrest cycle turns on Paul's innocence and Roman law protecting him from Jewish plots. In
Jerusalem Paul undertakes a **Nazirite/purification vow** with four men at James's request (21:23–26)
— which Bock reads (with the 1 Cor. 9:20 "a Jew to the Jews" harmonization, and pointing back to
Timothy's circumcision) as sensitivity, not law-observance for salvation. Successive hearings before
**Felix** (24), **Festus** (25), and **Agrippa II and Bernice** (25:13–26:32) repeatedly declare
Paul innocent; his Pharisaic "hope and resurrection of the dead" (23:6; 26:6–8) is, per Fitzmyer, the
real "refrain" of the charge. Paul's **appeal to Caesar** (*provocatio*, 25:11) as a Roman citizen
carries him, through storm and shipwreck, to **Rome** (27–28), where Bock reads the "unhindered"
open ending (28:31) as deliberate authorial design — the word reaching the empire's center — not a
sign Luke died or ran out of material; church tradition (1 Clem. 5; Eusebius) has Paul released and
later martyred under Nero. Throughout, Bock engages the **Paul-of-Acts vs. Paul-of-letters** question
(Vielhauer's four alleged inconsistencies; the law; justification; the Nazirite vow) as a controversy
to be recorded and largely harmonized rather than a proof of Lukan fiction — see
[[acts-paul-tensions|Acts–Paul Tensions]].
