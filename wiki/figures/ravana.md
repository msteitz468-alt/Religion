# Rāvaṇa

**Rāvaṇa** (also Ravana; Buck's text addresses him as "Ten-Necks") is the ten-headed, twenty-armed king of the Rākṣasas (demons), lord of [[lanka|Laṅkā]], and the principal antagonist of the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]. He abducts [[sita|Sītā]] to force [[rama|Rāma]] into direct combat, and is ultimately killed by Rāma. He is a figure of paradox: immensely learned, a devotee of Śiva, a great warrior and ruler, yet morally catastrophic in his treatment of women and his defiance of cosmic dharma.

## Biographical Overview

### Origin and Family

Rāvaṇa is presented in Buck's text as the Rakshasa king whose empire extends in all ten directions. His twenty hands hold ten ornamental golden arrows tied with golden string — "ten arrows for the ten directions; South and the other three, and the four in between those four, and up and down, all was Ravana's" (lines 6595–6598). His son **Indrajit** (also called Meghanāda) had already defeated Indra in heaven before the events of this narrative (7137). His chief queen is **Mandodari** (7140).

### Physical Form

When Rāvaṇa drops his disguise before Sīta, Buck describes him thus:

> "He was tall as a tree. He had ten dark faces and twenty dark arms, and twenty red eyes red-rimmed like fire. He had yellow up-pointing fangs. He licked his lips with sharp tongues. He wore golden armor, long heavy gold earrings swaying, gold bracelets, gold armbands, ten golden crowns set with golden pearls, gold belt-chains crashing and gold rings all over his fingers. Fragrant white flower-garlands went over his shoulders and around his ten necks." (6902–6911)

He carries a long ivory bow backed with pearls, a blue steel sword in a blue case, and a quiver formed of human skins stretched over a frame of men's bones, its exterior painted with demon faces drawn in blood that moved and changed expression of their own accord (6912–6920).

In an earlier, more casual register, "The Demon King took a sip of wine with each head, one after another, and wiped his moustaches" (6730–6731).

### The Empire

Rāvaṇa's reach before the narrative is immense. His son Indrajit had defeated the Lord of Heaven; Rāvaṇa had taken "all creation into his supposed Empire" (7138–7139); he traveled in the celestial Pushpaka chariot capturing women from gods, kings, mermaids, bird-maidens, hermits' daughters, and Naga girls (7141–7146). When Sīta laughs at his overtures, he claims: "I stand in space and I pick up Earth in my hand. I close the Moon within my fingers and put the Sun in my pocket, and arrest the aimless planets" (6931–6933).

Fourteen thousand Rakshasa veterans garrison **Dandaka Forest**, commanded by the general **Khara** (5893–5894), until [[rama|Rāma]] kills them all — Khara and all fourteen thousand — alone, with arrows Viśvāmitra taught him (6496–6500).

### The Curse on Rāvaṇa

Rāvaṇa operates under a specific divine constraint. [[indra|Indra]] tells Sītā in Laṅkā: "Don't fear Ravana. He cannot force you, for he is under a strong curse, and he'll die if he does" (7110–7111). The curse was laid by **Nalakubara**, son of the Treasure King Vaishravana (Kubera):

> When Rāvaṇa raped the Apsarasa Rambha on her way to meet Nalakubara — against her protests that she was "married, in my heart" and that he was her king and therefore her protector (7186–7193) — Nalakubara stood on a bare rock and cursed him: *"Ravana, when you next attack a woman who won't have you, your ten heads will burst!"* (7206–7207).

Since then, Rāvaṇa has not been able to force any unwilling woman. Indra notes that this has paradoxically made him more appealing to many of his captives, who feel sorry for him (7210–7213). Rambha and Sītā are described as "the only women who have refused themselves to Ravana since that time" (7214–7215).

## The Abduction of Sītā

### The Maricha Stratagem

After learning from his sister **Surpanakha** that [[rama|Rāma]] has destroyed Khara's garrison, and hearing her description of the beautiful Sītā, Rāvaṇa goes alone to **Gokarna** on the northern continent to visit his uncle **Maricha** — the demon Rāma had shot but not killed when Rāma was a boy (6591–6593). He asks Maricha to transform into a golden deer and lure Rāma away from Sītā.

Maricha repeatedly warns Rāvaṇa that this will be his doom:

> "This is the severing of the summit of the Rakshasa race. It is for your death that the forest hermits have so long poured butter on their fires." (6661–6663)

Maricha predicts Rāvaṇa's death, describes Rāma's limitless power, and calls the plan ruinous. But when Rāvaṇa threatens to kill him, Maricha reasons: "it is better to be killed by the better person. I won't escape him but I will lead him away and let him kill me" (7748–7750). He performs his own funeral before departing (7753–7754).

Maricha transforms into a spectacular golden deer — "His face was of living blue sapphire like a moving mask made of a blue mirror. His antlers were ivory tipped with moonstone points and his hooves were glassy black flint. His lighted eyes were violet amethyst" (6764–6767) — and runs past Rāma's house. Sītā, captivated, asks Rāma to catch him. Lakshmana recognizes it as an illusion, but Rāma goes after the deer alone.

When Rāma shoots Maricha, the demon dies resuming his true form and shouts in Rāma's voice: *"Help me!"* (6811), loud enough to be heard at Panchavati. Sita panics and forces Lakshmana to leave her.

### Ravana's Disguise and Abduction

Rāvaṇa approaches Sītā disguised as a wandering holy man: "He wore soft red silk. There was one lock of white hair left long on his shaven head. He held a parasol and wore sandals, and he carried resting over his left shoulder a long triple bamboo staff with a waterjar slung on it" (6857–6861). He stands silently as holy men do when begging. Sītā steps over the protective circle Lakshmana had drawn and invites him inside.

He reveals his identity and drops his disguise. The reaction of the natural world to his revealed form is notable: "seeing that evil one revealed, the leaves did not flutter. The trees of Dandaka did not move. No breath of wind dared stir about in the woods. The fast-streaming Godavari river slackened her speed from fright. The glorious Sun, who every day looks down upon our world, this time dimmed his light from the sadness of what he saw" (6923–6928).

Sītā refuses him with contempt ("Garuda mating with a goose? A firefly courting the Sun?" — 6891). Rāvaṇa seizes her: "In one left hand he held her long dark hair. He caught her legs in two of his right arms and lifted her, and his demon chariot came to meet him through the air" (6939–6942).

### Jatayu's Resistance

The vulture king **Jatayu** — sixty thousand years old, king of Dandaka's birds, and Daśaratha's lifelong friend — overtakes Rāvaṇa's chariot and confronts him (6967–6976). Jatayu tears off the chariot rail, lifts Sītā free, and sets her safely beneath a tree. Rāvaṇa attacks; Jatayu snaps his ivory bow with one bite, claws the armor from Ravana's back, strikes him down with his wings, destroys the chariot, and tears the asses to shreds (6987–6996). Rāvaṇa's arms are ripped off by Jatayu's talons — but "those ten arms grew right back" (7008). Exhausted and blinded, Jatayu cannot see Rāvaṇa's regenerated arms; Rāvaṇa draws his sword and cuts off Jatayu's wings (7010–7011). Jatayu falls dying. Rāvaṇa seizes Sītā again and flees south.

## Ravana's Treatment of Sita in Lanka

Rāvaṇa brings Sītā "to the back of his palace in the city of Lanka" (7058). He presents himself as offering her love, not coercion:

> "You will not see Rama again. Here you are safe, you have no more husband, and so there can be nothing wrong in our love." (7060–7062)

Sītā's response: "To Hell with you. You took me like a thief. And barely made it past an old vulture" (7063–7064). She refuses to enter his quarters ("I won't enter your kennel" — 7077). Rāvaṇa then has her imprisoned in the **Asoka grove** behind his room, guarded by Rakshasi women day and night, with orders to use "hard words and soft, threats and gifts and temptations" to break her will (7082–7087).

He calls after her: "When winter begins and the Sun turns north, if you haven't come into my bed I'll eat you minced for breakfast" (7089–7090). Yet the curse prevents him from forcing her, and the text's depiction of his court — his physicians restoring Surpanakha's ears, his coming down from his throne to sit beside her, his composing himself after shouting at Sītā ("at once he composed himself. 'My dear . . . capture is a good way to get a wife . . . I want your love. I will wait for you to love me'" — 7066–7068) — portrays a ruler capable of self-control and even a kind of courtly care, alongside violence.

## Character Portrait: The Learned Demon

Rāvaṇa's own sister Surpanakha rebukes him for his moral failures even while asking for his help:

> "Brother, you're a disgrace. You're all muscle and brawn, you rule by brute force and ignorance! Here you are lost in pleasure, idle and greedy and useless." (6552–6555)

Yet Maricha's account and the text's framing also acknowledge Rāvaṇa's greatness. Maricha concedes that Rāvaṇa has "done all right so far" (6706) and that his victories have been real. The paradox: learned, devoted to Śiva, beloved by many wives, capable of self-restraint (the curse forces it) — yet the same person who raped Rambha, murdered those who resisted him, and whose greed has "drawn him swiftly to his doom" (6681).

Maricha's final verdict: "However strong and great you once were, you are a straw figure now, falling into poverty" (6711–6712).

## Theological Significance

Rāvaṇa's armies dominate the gods — Indra's son Indrajit defeated Indra in heaven (7137) — yet Rāma, a mortal, destroys his entire Dandaka garrison alone. Maricha reads this correctly as Rāvaṇa's doom, not an accident. The theological frame (not stated as such in this range but implicit in Maricha's speech) is that Rāvaṇa's boon from Brahma, which protected him from gods and demons, did not protect him from a mortal human — which is precisely why Vishnu incarnated as the human Rāma.

The brahminicide dimension: Rāvaṇa has Brahmin lineage (through Pulastya), making his death by Rāma's hands also a killing of a Brahmin — a moral weight the tradition acknowledges in Rāma's later penance.

## Rāvaṇa Seen Through Hanumān's Eyes (Lines 8501–11500)

### Physical Portrait: Asleep

When Hanumān first sees Rāvaṇa sleeping in his bedroom, Buck gives a second major physical portrait: "heroic and dark, wearing white, with twenty arms like gate pillars marked with cool blood-red sandal paste, with ten devilishly handsome heads, with his faces aglow from long heavy gold earrings, sleeping like a deep-breathing hill" (lines 9316–9320). He "slept unsuspecting, all worn out from love and wine. He was the dear desire of every Rakshasa daughter, and the happiness of every Lanka warrior" (9321–9323).

The scars on his body record his history of cosmic combat: "On the Demon King's broad breast were the old curving white scars from the tusks of Indra's elephant Airavata; on his strong dark arms were the burns of thunderbolts and lightnings and scars from the bowstring; around nine of his necks ran thin lines to show where he had once cut off nine heads and burned them as an offering to the gods" (9337–9342). The moral verdict that follows is unambiguous: "Rāvaṇa lay like a collection of wrongs, a mass of harm and injury and brutality and darkness of heart" (9335–9336). Yet the text also states: "Rāvaṇa had earned all his wealth and power by his own strength. He was fortunate and splendid and rich. Rāvaṇa had the patience and strength to protect all Creation from harm, but he did just the opposite and took the worlds for his possessions" (10067–10070).

### The Nandin Prophecy Fulfilled

When Hanumān is brought before the throne in chains, Rāvaṇa looks at him and "remembered what the bull Nandin, wearing the face of a monkey, had told him on Shiva's Hill of Reeds long before — Animals with faces like mine will destroy you" (10090–10094). The prophecy is now visible in the room.

### Daily Courtship of Sītā

Every morning Rāvaṇa goes to the Asoka Grove and kneels at Sītā's feet before pressing his case (9551–9552). His arguments are wealth-based: "Don't be shy . . . how does it become you to lie on the bare ground when you can be Mistress of my kingdom?" (9553–9558). When she refuses, he roars in fury — "his twenty red eyes went spinning and rolling in different-turning circles and opposite ways" (9590–9591) — but Queen Mandodari always leads him away before he can act on his anger. The text makes Mandodari structurally necessary to Rāvaṇa's self-control.

### In Court: Receiving Hanumān

Rāvaṇa sits on his throne — "in a better hall than any room in heaven" (10065–10066) — wearing "ten crowns of flaming red flowers and gleaming gold" (10080), with a gold breast-chain of devil-faces (10083–10084). He speaks to Hanumān with initial contempt but genuine curiosity: "You have now seen us both. Tell me truly, how Rama compares to me" (10154–10155). When Hanumān answers with the New Moon riddle, Rāvaṇa takes it as flattery and orders the tail-fire ritual rather than execution — Vibhīṣaṇa prevents him from killing the messenger (10129–10133). The backfire of this decision burns his city.

### Rāvaṇa and the Burning of Laṅkā

When Hanumān's tail-fire engulfs the city, Rāvaṇa does not fight it directly. His son Indrajit performs an independent water ritual to quench the fires. Afterward, Rāvaṇa calls a war council and asks for advice: "We have repaired the damage he did, but yet Hanumān all by himself invaded my unreachable city and will return with Rāma. What must I do?" (10771–10773). His court elders respond with empty flattery. When Vibhīṣaṇa warns that Rāma will kill him regardless, Rāvaṇa replies: "What do I care? I do what I please!" (10799) and expels his brother.

### The Illusory Head of Rāma

With Rāma's army assembled outside the walls, Rāvaṇa summons two magicians to create by illusion a severed head of Rāma and his broken bow (11031–11043). He presents these to Sītā and tells her Prahasta killed Rāma at midnight. Sītā believes it and begins to die. When Prahasta arrives with actual war news, Rāvaṇa smiles at Sītā: "You must excuse me, Princess, there seems to be something. . . ." (11107–11109) — and leaves. The illusory head melts once he has gone. The episode reveals the depth of his self-defeating obsession: facing imminent battle, he still spends energy on romantic manipulation.

### Before Battle: The View from the Ramparts

Rāvaṇa watches Rāma's army from the walls in white robes "like fame and glory, adorned with rubies red as blood" (11326–11328), leaning on his twenty arms spread along the ramparts. He identifies each enemy warrior carefully (11329–11337). When Prince Angada brings Rāma's final ultimatum — "bid farewell to your friends and come out to me, I am waiting to kill you" — Rāvaṇa answers confidently: "I will not blow out like a light from windy words. When I win this battle I will at last finally impress Sita and she will love me back" (11259–11261). His delusion about Sītā and the war are structurally identical.

He gives the simultaneous signal to begin battle by raising a mace over his head as Lakṣmaṇa points his bow at Laṅkā (11358–11359). He commits his youngest third of the demon army by day; retains the veterans for the night campaign.

## The Siege: Kumbhakarna's Counsel and Death

With Laṅkā besieged and his general Prahasta dead, Rāvaṇa commands his minister Suka to awaken Kumbhakarna — a giant who sleeps six months at a stretch, roused with cymbals and eighteen trained elephants (ll. 11527–11560). Kumbhakarna immediately counsels peace: "Give her back" (l. 11606). He names the theft "a tremendous mistake" and warns that Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa are "the flame and the wind meeting" (l. 11652–11653). He names the monkey army "the animals of Lord Nārāyaṇa, and this is the death of the Rākṣasas" (ll. 11614–11615).

Rāvaṇa's response reveals his self-awareness of ruin: "If I'm wrong I need you to save me all the more" (l. 11637). He even concedes he cannot use magic to take Rāma's form to deceive Sītā — "the transformation would have to be complete; I'd have to take on all Rāma's virtues as well to fool her, and then I could do no wrong, I couldn't lie to her" (ll. 11666–11669). Kumbhakarna, departing to arm himself, reflects privately: "Dharma is the root of all good fortune, and you have uprooted Dharma; the happiness of others is light for the spirit but you have darkened the worlds" (ll. 11687–11690).

Kumbhakarna goes to battle alone — his war cry breaks the animal army (ll. 11760–11763); [[hanuman|Hanumān]] bites his ear and is struck down; Rāma dismembers him with divine arrows: right arm, left arm (which "fell into the sea and bruised the whales"), both legs — each blow met with defiant cries — then beheads him at dawn (ll. 11800–11831). Rāvaṇa watches from the wall as his brother's limbs block the fields, weeps for a long time, and says: "Where does Kumbhakarna go all alone?" (l. 11869). He pledges: "I must avenge you, Kumbhakarna. If I cannot kill Rāma, then Death is my good fortune" (ll. 11875–11878).

## Indrajit's Two Campaigns and Death

Rāvaṇa sends his son **Indrajit** ("Conqueror of Indra") to battle twice. Indrajit's first assault is fought from an invisible tiger-chariot called from a fire — boons won from Brahmā — giving him 108 kinds of illusion, an inexhaustible quiver, and power of invisible flight. Vibhīṣaṇa warns: "even Brahmā cannot see him" (l. 11957). Indrajit's arrows kill Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa and virtually the entire army; he retires to meditate by breathing (ll. 11997–12029). The army is revived by [[hanuman|Hanumān]]'s healing herbs.

For his second campaign Indrajit creates a magic illusion of Sītā and "kills" her in front of Rāma's army (ll. 12295–12313). Before departing Rāvaṇa, Indrajit delivers a speech of extraordinary lucidity about dharma: "All is Dharma, Majesty; there is no second thing in existence; there is Dharma alone in the world" (ll. 12271–12273). He calls the monkey army "the fear and anger of the helpless" that "has taken on the form of an army of animals" (ll. 12263–12264).

[[lakshmana|Lakṣmaṇa]] and Vibhīṣaṇa interrupt Indrajit's fire sacrifice before the tiger-chariot can fully materialize, stripping him of divine protection (ll. 12382–12395). Indrajit fights in single combat in silver armor; Lakṣmaṇa beheads him with Agastya's grass arrow (ll. 12445–12449). Rāvaṇa weeps burning tears that sear through the floor: *"My son, my son"* (l. 12475). He laments: "The old should die before the young — why am I left here all alone to make your funeral?" (ll. 12488–12489). He attempts in grief to kill Sītā; his minister Suka blocks the door bodily and warns him away from shaming himself (ll. 12499–12510).

## The Final Council and Farewell to Mandodari

On the eve of his last battle, Rāvaṇa holds council. The herald names him "Protector of Heaven and the Guardian of Truth, the Defender of Love and Enemy of Evil" (ll. 12764–12770). Rāvaṇa acknowledges defeat plainly: "Rāma defeats me by his mere presence, what to speak of his arrows?" (l. 12774). His minister Suka, asked why he has stayed when he could escape, answers: "Once taking you for my King I think it wrong to leave you, Majesty" (l. 12817). Suka describes the doomed city: "the like of this citadel and city of Beautiful Lanka as she was during your long reign was never seen before, nor will she ever be seen anywhere again" (ll. 12833–12836). Rāvaṇa entrusts Suka with a sealed stone letter addressed to Rāma, to be delivered after the battle.

He walks to his queen **Mandodari** through the palace halls, holds her gently in his twenty arms, and says: "It's nearly done, everything will be alright." She replies: "You are my Lord, and I will ever hold this thought, near to my heart, like a treasure." Rāvaṇa embraces her: "Farewell my very love" (ll. 12844–12856).

## Rāvaṇa and Time (Kāla)

Alone on a watchtower at midnight, Rāvaṇa performs a great dance, calling down the North Wind, which blows out "every fire in the Universe and every light" (ll. 12858–12918). Coming down the stairway afterward, he encounters **Time** (Kāla) in personified form — "a body so shriveled and old that he looked dead even when he moved" — who tells Rāvaṇa his life is over, that Hell awaits him for eternity, and that his memory will be lost in endless silence (ll. 12926–12943).

Rāvaṇa refuses to be cowed. He mocks Kāla as "a marketplace of sorrow, a welt of lies" and a fraud who "take[s] whole lives in payment" for a few moments of pleasure (ll. 12966–12972). He declares: *"Good Love never dies"* and *"Love is eternal and we are beyond your reach"* (ll. 13038–13015). His verse: *"When my dancing footfalls stopped, / I broke Time's small back / With my last step"* (ll. 12993–12995). He commands Kāla to stand aside, and Kāla fades and vanishes. The poet Valmiki, watching, calls out: *"Well done, Demon King!"* (l. 13069).

## The Final Battle and Death

Rāvaṇa exits Laṅkā through the Gate of Illusion (the fifth gate) in a chariot of four white horses, armed with one hundred bows and a thousand quivers, wearing black night-armor and ten dark helmets (ll. 13070–13099). [[rama|Rāma]] receives Indra's celestial chariot and charioteer Mātali (ll. 13136–13166). The battle deploys elemental cosmic weapons: fire, rain, wind, mountains raised from earth and answered with Brahmā's own *Brahmāstra* scattering as 180,000 thunderbolts (ll. 13196–13255). Rāma breaks ninety-nine of Rāvaṇa's bows; Rāvaṇa's iron mace injures Mātali; Rāma catches the mace and breaks it over his thigh (ll. 13330–13332). When Rāma breaks the ten golden arrows of Rāvaṇa's empire, "the running demon car lost its rattle and clatter and its wheels turned on in mournful silence. The flag of Lanka lay in the dust" (ll. 13321–13325).

In the final moment, Rāvaṇa takes on the brilliant form of Indra, king of heaven — "glorious and gracious, all illumined; he could scarcely be looked at" — to prevent Rāma from shooting. The wounded Mātali breaks the spell from the ground: *"Strike, Strike, That's not my Master"* (ll. 13383–13384). Rāma fires Agastya's brass-bladed grass arrow: it breaks Rāvaṇa's sword and bow, then "struck through his heart, stealing his life, and never stopped, but came out from his back and entered the Earth" (ll. 13388–13392). Rāvaṇa dies at dawn. "He was a fire not burning, an ocean still with no tides" (ll. 13401–13402).

## Ravana's Posthumous Letter: The Divine Revelation

Suka delivers Rāvaṇa's sealed stone letter to Rāma the morning after the battle. The letter reveals that Rāvaṇa knew Rāma's divine identity as [[vishnu|Nārāyaṇa]] all along, while Rāma himself did not: *"You do not know who you are again. I knew it all along, but even still you do not know"* (ll. 13457–13458). Rāvaṇa claims he stole Sītā ("I kept Lakṣmī to lure you here") to force his death at Rāma's hands — the only path to divine union: "Except dying at your hand, how else could I make you take me into your own Self?" (ll. 13452–13454). He was, he says, "nowhere careless" — the abduction was a deliberate theological act.

The letter declares a non-dual vision: *"You are Nārāyaṇa who moves on the waters. You flow through us all. You are Rāma and Sītā born out of Earth and Rāvaṇa the Demon King, you are Hanumān like the wind, you are Lakṣmaṇa like a mirror, you are Indrajit and Indra, you are the Poet and the Players and the Play. And born as a man you forget this, you lose the memory, and take on man's ignorance again, as you will, every time"* (ll. 13476–13483). Rāma's response: he sighs, smiles, breaks the letter, and says: "Go, go, I do not care what all this means" (ll. 13489–13491) — neither affirming nor denying.

## Theological Complexity

1. **Brahmin-scholar**: Kumbhakarna reproaches him for wasting his learning — "For nothing did you read the holy Veda and throw your heads into the fire when we lived in the high Himalya Hills" (ll. 11633–11635). His Brahmin lineage makes his crimes carry the weight of *brahminicide*.
2. **Lover, not merely villain**: He refuses to violate Sītā despite holding her captive, describes his love as genuine suffering: "I have lost myself in longing" (l. 11631). Kumbhakarna frames this as *saṃsāric* attachment, not mere lust.
3. **Cosmic dramatist**: The posthumous letter claims the entire war was a *līlā* (divine play) that Rāvaṇa understood and chose to enact.
4. **Defier of death**: His confrontation with Kāla is played as philosophical triumph; the poet Valmiki explicitly applauds it.
