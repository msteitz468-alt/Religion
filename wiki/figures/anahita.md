# Anahita (Aredvi Sura Anahita)

**Anahita** — Avestan **Aredvi Sura Anahita**, "the Moist, the Strong, the Undefiled" —
is the great Iranian **[[mitra|yazata]]** of the **waters**, of **fertility and
fecundity**, and of **martial sovereignty**, and the principal female divinity of
[[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]. She is praised in the long **Aban Yasht** (Yasht 5)
as the mighty heavenly river that pours down to make waters, plants, herds, and human
fertility increase.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Aban Yasht (Yt 5)** — depicts Anahita as a beautiful, strong maiden, richly robed and
  crowned, driving a chariot drawn by four horses (wind, rain, cloud, sleet), to whom
  heroes and kings sacrifice for victory and offspring. She is the cosmic source of the
  world's waters, descending from the star **Tishtrya**'s realm to the **Vourukasha
  Sea**.
- **Achaemenid inscriptions** — an inscription of **[[persians|Artaxerxes II]]** (405–359
  BCE) at Susa invokes, after [[ahura-mazda|Ahura Mazda]], both **Anahita and
  [[mitra|Mithra]]** — the **earliest reference to "Anahita" outside the Avesta**, and
  the marker (per [[rose-zoroastrianism-an-introduction|Rose]], following Berossus) of a
  new **image-cult** that this king promoted across the empire, erecting statues of
  "Aphrodite Anaitis."

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Zoroastrian**: a high yazata under Ahura Mazda — guardian of the waters (one of the
  seven creations) and patroness of priestly purification and royal legitimacy. Temples
  and dynastic fires were dedicated to her (e.g. the **Adur-Anahid** temple at Istakhr in
  Fars, where the high priest [[zoroastrianism|Kerdir]] later served).
- **Greco-Roman**: classical writers, seeking parallels, identified Anaitis with
  **Aphrodite** (Berossus), **Artemis** (Plutarch's "Persian Diana"), and **Athena** (a
  "warlike goddess" at Pasargadae). Her water/fertility and martial aspects map onto
  several Greek goddesses at once.
- **Later Iran**: survives as **Nahid** (the planet Venus) and in place- and
  personal-names; associated with springs and water-shrines.

## Significance and Controversies

Anahita raises the recurring question of Zoroastrian **"monotheism vs. polytheism"**: a
named, anthropomorphic, statue-receiving goddess sits awkwardly beside the austere,
imageless worship of Ahura Mazda that Herodotus reports for the early Persians. Rose
treats the **rise of the *yazatas*** (Anahita, Mithra, Tishtrya) in the Young Avesta and
the **Artaxerxes II image-cult** as a development *within* a religion that still upheld
Ahura Mazda's supremacy — not a relapse into paganism — though some (especially Gatha-only
[[parsis|reformers]] after Haug) read exactly such veneration as a later corruption.
