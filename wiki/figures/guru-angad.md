# Guru Angad

## Biographical Overview

Guru Angad was born Lahina, son of Pheru, a Khatri of the Trehan sub-caste. His ancestral home was located near the village of Matte di Sarai in Firozpur District. Traditional accounts give his date of birth as corresponding to 31 March 1504. He was married to Khivi, daughter of a Khatri from Khadur village; they had two sons (Dasu and Data) and at least one daughter (Amro) (McLeod ~L1200–1215).

Amro's piety is emphasized in tradition: "According to tradition it was through overhearing her singing a shabad that the third Guru, Amar Das, was led to Guru Angad and so to conversion" (McLeod ~L1213–1215).

**Conversion to Nanak's Panth**: Two conflicting traditions exist. (1) The Puratan tradition reports that Lahina was the pujari (temple official) of Khadur, affiliated with the worship of the goddess Devi (Durga). He overheard a Sikh singing a hymn of Guru Nanak, was convinced of its truth, and abandoned Durga-worship to become a Sikh (McLeod ~L1249–1266). (2) A separate janam-sakhi tradition says Lahina led an annual pilgrimage to a "shrine of Durga" (identified by the *Mahima Prakash Kavita* as Jwalamukhi). The party passed through Kartarpur; Lahina received darshan of Baba Nanak and was instantly converted, declaring the pilgrimage fulfilled at Kartarpur (McLeod ~L1267–1279). Santokh Singh's *Nanak Prakash* (1823) conflates the two accounts and remains the standard modern version, though McLeod notes neither may be fully trusted (McLeod ~L1280–1285).

**Renaming**: Guru Nanak bestowed the name Angad on Lahina to signify that "the disciple had become as much a part of him as his own limbs (ang)" (McLeod ~L1219–1220). This implied special status confirmed when Lahina was formally designated successor shortly before Nanak's death.

**Period as Guru (1539–1552)**: Angad abandoned Kartarpur after Nanak's death — evidently because the succession was disputed by Nanak's two sons — and took up permanent residence in **Khadur**, where he received his Sikhs and imparted instruction. "A feature of this period was evidently the free distribution of food to people of all castes, and tradition relates that in this respect he was ably assisted by his wife, Khivi" (McLeod ~L1228–1232). He died 29 March 1552 and appointed [[guru-amar-das|Amar Das]] as his successor.

## Sources for His Life

McLeod emphasizes the extreme thinness of reliable sources: "The lives of Gurus Angad, Ram Das, Arjan, Hargobind, Har Rai and Har Krishan rely largely on tradition" (McLeod ~L214–215). The principal sources are: brief Adi Granth references; slight supplementation by Bhai Gurdas; and the janam-sakhi material, especially the **Mahima Prakash** tradition (both prose and poetic versions, composed mid-18th century — "trusted only as the voice of tradition"). Santokh Singh's *Suraj Prakash* (19th c.) is the traditional standard life, drawing on these sources plus oral tradition (McLeod ~L1145–1168).

## Teaching and Character

Angad's compositions in the Adi Granth are brief and few, but "one feature which is evident from his compositions is a particular fondness for the early-morning bathe as the prelude to devotion" (McLeod ~L1171–1177). His life as Guru is characterized by the same emphasis on the divine Name that Nanak had preached.

## Gurmukhi Script

A widespread belief holds that Guru Angad invented the Gurmukhi script (the script in which Punjabi is written). McLeod cautions: "This conviction is a comparatively late development, for the earlier sources do not make such a sweeping claim. The script is very like that used by the traders of the Punjab and it would be perfectly natural for Guru Angad (a Khatri by caste) also to write in it. Because it thus communicated the Guru's utterances, it came to be known as Gurmukhi ('from the Guru's mouth')" (McLeod ~L1360–1387).

## Role in the Developing Panth

Angad received a Panth "which consisted of members who were bound directly to their Guru by ties of personal allegiance" and transmitted it essentially unchanged to [[guru-amar-das|Amar Das]] twelve years later. "It was a religious Panth with a very loose structure holding it together. The headquarters of the Panth travelled with the Guru" (McLeod ~L1398–1407). Under Angad the Sikhs had no distinctive festivals or practices beyond their allegiance to the Guru's teaching of liberation through the divine Name.

## The Succession Anecdote

The most famous janam-sakhi anecdote about the succession presents Nanak testing his followers by placing coins, then jewels, then a shrouded "corpse" before them. All but Lahina retreated. Lahina lifted the shroud obediently — and found Nanak himself beneath it. The story testifies to Angad's "devoted obedience to Guru Nanak, fulfilling his every wish as a welcome command" (McLeod ~L1286–1326).

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Ch. 3)

Khushwant Singh takes the stronger traditional view that **McLeod cautions against**: that Angad actively
**codified the Gurmukhi script**, taking "the thirty-five letters of the acrostic composed by Nanak,
selected the appropriate letters from other scripts current in northern India, and called the new script
Gurmukhī." For Singh this was decisive for Sikh identity — Angad's compilation of Nanak's hymns "became the
nucleus of the sacred writings of the Sikhs… and thus fostered a sense of their being a separate people."
(Contrast McLeod's "Gurmukhi Script" section above, which treats the invention claim as a late development.)
Singh also stresses Angad's emphasis on physical fitness — drill and a wrestling arena at every community
centre — and his move to Khadur to avoid schism with Siri Chand's [[udasi|Udasis]].
