# Valentinus

## Biographical Overview

Valentinus was the most influential gnostic theologian of the second century. Born c.100 CE in Egypt (probably Alexandria), he received an excellent Greek education and moved to Rome c.130-140 CE. According to ancient sources (Tertullian, Epiphanius), he was a member of the orthodox Roman church and reportedly came close to being elected bishop of Rome — but was passed over, which reportedly led to his break with the mainstream church. He was active in Rome during the pontificates of Hyginus, Pius, and Anicetus (c.136-165 CE). Later traditions place him in Cyprus, but this is uncertain.

All primary sources agree that Valentinus was a brilliant teacher who attracted numerous disciples, and that the Valentinian school became the most theologically sophisticated and numerically significant gnostic movement of the 2nd century. [[irenaeus|Irenaeus]] devotes more than half of *Against Heresies* Book 1 to describing the Valentinian system.

## Primary Works

Most of Valentinus' own writings are lost, surviving only in patristic quotation. The *Nag Hammadi Library* (discovered 1945) includes texts associated with the Valentinian school:

### *Gospel of Truth* (possibly by Valentinus himself)
A meditative, homiletical text — notably not a "gospel" in the narrative sense but a theological meditation on how the Father's children, lost in ignorance, find salvation through the Son's revelation. Sophisticated use of Johannine themes. Many scholars (Quispel, Grant) believe Valentinus himself authored this text.

### *Gospel of Philip* (later Valentinian)
A collection of aphorisms and theological reflections with sacramental themes (five sacraments: baptism, chrism, Eucharist, redemption, bridal chamber). Includes the famous ambiguous passage about Mary Magdalene.

### Fragments
Several homiletic and epistolary fragments in Clement of Alexandria's *Stromateis* and *Excerpta ex Theodoto*. These show Valentinus using mainstream Christian language — "father," "son," "spirit," "church," "gospel" — with a distinctive metaphysical overlay.

## The Valentinian System

Valentinus constructed the most elaborate second-century theological cosmology. Key features (as reconstructed from Irenaeus and Valentinian texts):

### The Pleroma (Fullness)
The divine realm consists of 30 *Aeons* (divine emanations) arranged in pairs (*syzygies*). The highest is the *Bythos* ("Depth") and *Sigē* ("Silence"). From them emanate successively: *Nous/Aletheia*, *Logos/Zoe*, *Anthropos/Ecclesia*, and further aeons. The 30th and lowest aeon, *Sophia* ("Wisdom"), falls from the Pleroma through her presumptuous desire to know the Father without her consort — a catastrophic act that initiates the creation of the material world. (See [[pleroma|Pleroma]] and [[sophia-gnostic|Sophia (Gnostic)]] for fuller treatment.)

### Creation and the Demiurge
Sophia's fall produces an inferior being — the Demiurge — who creates the material world in ignorance of the higher divine realm. The Demiurge is the OT God — the God worshipped by the *psychics* (mainstream Christians). He believes himself to be the only God. (See [[demiurge|Demiurge]].)

### Three Classes of Humanity
- **Pneumatics** (*pneumatikoi*): those who possess a divine spark (*pneuma*); they will inevitably be saved through gnosis
- **Psychics** (*psychikoi*): those with soul but no spirit; they can be saved through faith and ethics — these are ordinary Christians
- **Hylics** (*hylikoi*): purely material; incapable of salvation

This tripartite anthropology explains why Valentinian teachers like Theodotus and Heracleon described themselves as "the few" and ordinary Christians as "the many" (*psychics*) — a crucial piece of evidence against Bauer's thesis that gnostics were numerically dominant. See [[bauer-thesis|Bauer Thesis]].

### Salvation
Salvation for the pneumatics consists in *gnosis* — recognition of one's divine origin, the revelation brought by the Savior (*Soter*). The Savior descended through the regions, collected the seeds of Sophia's pneumatic substance, and will restore them to the Pleroma. The material body is irrelevant; bodily resurrection is either allegorized or denied. (See [[gnosis|Gnosis]].)

Rudolph (*Gnosis*) treats Valentinianism as the most elaborate and influential of the "great systems" of the 2nd century, with a particularly sophisticated development of the Sophia myth, the Pleroma of 30 aeons, and the tripartite anthropology. He notes the social and cultic dimensions (sacraments including the "bridal chamber") and the irenic potential in some Valentinian texts (e.g., Ptolemy's *Letter to Flora* distinguishes portions of the OT while engaging mainstream Christian questions). Heracleon's commentary on John (the first known NT commentary) shows Valentinians claiming to be the true interpreters of Christian scripture. See [[rudolph-gnosis-the-nature-and-history-of-gnosticism|Rudolph, *Gnosis*]] and [[gnostics|Gnostics]].

## The Valentinian School

Valentinus' school was diverse. Major Valentinian teachers:
- **Ptolemy**: Active in Rome; his *Letter to Flora* (preserved by Epiphanius) is the most irenic and intellectually accessible Valentinian text — it argues against Marcion's total rejection of the OT while distinguishing portions of the OT that are "of God," "of Moses," and "of the elders"
- **Heracleon**: Wrote the earliest known commentary on any NT text — a commentary on the Gospel of John; preserved in fragments by Origen. Heracleon explicitly calls himself one of "the few" vs. the orthodox "many"
- **Marcus**: Numerological gnostic; active in Gaul, refuted extensively by Irenaeus
- **Theodotus**: Active in the East; his excerpts are preserved in Clement of Alexandria's *Excerpta ex Theodoto*

Irenaeus distinguished an Eastern and Western school of Valentinianism with slightly different cosmological accounts.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Proto-Orthodox / Catholic / Protestant

Valentinus is the definitive example of sophisticated theological heresy in the patristic tradition. The anti-Valentinian polemic of Irenaeus shaped Christian theologizing: the insistence on one God, the goodness of creation, the real humanity of Christ, and the eschatological resurrection of the body are all articulated in direct counter to Valentinianism.

### Modern / Academic

Since the Nag Hammadi discovery, scholars (Pagels, Turner, Williams) have re-evaluated Valentinianism as a theologically serious movement, not mere "myth" or "fantasy." The *Gospel of Truth* in particular is considered a significant theological text. Elaine Pagels' *The Gnostic Gospels* (1979) brought Valentinian texts to popular attention.

The relationship between Valentinianism and Johannine Christianity is extensively studied — the Valentinians claimed to be the true interpreters of John's Gospel. Heracleon's commentary on John was the first, and the Fourth Gospel's language (Logos, light/darkness, truth) lent itself to Valentinian interpretation.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Episcopal candidacy**: The tradition that Valentinus nearly became bishop of Rome (Tertullian, *Against Valentinians* 4) may be a polemical exaggeration, though most scholars accept a version of it.

**The *Gospel of Truth***: Attribution to Valentinus himself vs. a disciple is debated. The literary quality and the theological sophistication are consistent with authorship by the teacher himself.

**Valentinianism and Christianity**: Was the Valentinian school "Christian"? It claimed to be — using the Bible, Jesus, and Christian vocabulary. But it operated with a fundamentally different cosmology and soteriology. Scholars like Michael Williams (*Rethinking "Gnosticism,"* 1996) question whether "gnosticism" is even a coherent category.
