# Arius

**Arius** was a presbyter of Alexandria whose teaching on the status of Christ touched off
the [[arian-controversy|Arian controversy]] and occasioned the **Council of Nicaea (325 CE)**.
He is the eponym of [[arianism|Arianism]]. Unusually for a condemned figure, some of his own
words survive (fragments of letters and his poetic *Thalia*, quoted — apparently accurately —
by [[athanasius|Athanasius]]), because the orthodox cited them to refute him.

## Biographical Overview

Born c. 256 in Libya; ordained presbyter in Alexandria c. 312 and assigned his own church.
From 318 he publicly disputed his bishop, **Alexander of Alexandria**, and was excommunicated
(318/319) with some twenty supporters, finding refuge in Palestine and the backing of Eusebius
of Caesarea. At Nicaea (325) he and two Libyan bishops alone refused to sign the creed and were
exiled.

## Theology (per [[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

Arius held that the pre-existent Son/Logos was **begotten** by God and therefore had a
beginning — "a perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures," "begotten God" yet
"removed from the greatness of the Father by an 'infinity of glories.'" God alone is without
beginning, so the Son is not "true" God in that absolute sense. Christ should be **worshiped**,
but never as the Father's equal: "It is the Father who is above all things, even the Son, by an
infinite degree." Ehrman stresses this was a **high incarnational Christology** that had been
acceptable a century earlier; the anathematized slogan attached to it was "**There was when he
was not.**"

## Significance

Arius's subordinationism forced the church to define the Son as *[[trinity|homoousios]]* — "of
one substance" with the Father. The controversy outlived him: Arianism persisted through the
fourth century and was finally condemned at the Council of Constantinople (381).

## Ayres's Reassessment (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) **decentres** Arius:
"Arius' own theology is of little importance in understanding the major debates of the rest of
the century" — the *Thalia* "appears… to have circulated only in Alexandria," and his death
(335/336) "marks no significant turning point." His earliest supporters (the two Eusebiuses,
admirers of Lucian of Antioch) rallied chiefly *against* Alexander's theology, "wary of some
aspects of Arius' theology — especially his insistence on the unknowability of the Father." On
Ayres's reading Arius is **one Eusebian among several**, not the founder of a school: his
distinctive claim that "the Son does not know the Father… seems to have been at odds with the
theologies of other 'Lucianists'."

From the *Thalia* (per Ayres): "He possesses nothing proper to God… for he is not equal to God,
nor yet is he of the same substance"; "God is inexpressible to the Son." Arius may have said the
Son is "from the things that did not exist" but "later seems to have retracted such language"
while still holding "the Logos is potentially changeable."

Ayres weighs and partly resists two influential readings: Gregg–Groh's *Early Arianism*
(exemplarist/adoptionist Christology — "evidence is lacking") and Rowan Williams's location of
Arius in Alexandrian liturgy and late-third-century Neoplatonic participation theory (with
[[christopher-stead|Stead]] disputing the Neoplatonic link). The construction of **"Arianism"**
as Arius's enduring movement is, for Ayres, a later polemical genealogy by
[[athanasius|Athanasius]] — see [[arian-controversy]] and [[pro-nicene-theology]].
