Naboth the Jezreelite is the owner of a vineyard beside the palace of [[ahab|Ahab]] in
[[jezreel|Jezreel]] who refuses to sell or trade his ancestral inheritance and is killed for it by a
staged legal proceeding: a proclaimed fast, two worthless men who charge him with cursing God and
the king, and a stoning outside the city. He speaks two clauses in the narrative and is dead by the
middle of the chapter, yet his death generates the sentence on the house of Ahab that [[jehu|Jehu]]
executes two books' worth of narrative later.

## Biographical Overview

1 Kings 21 gives Naboth no genealogy beyond his town. He holds a vineyard in Jezreel adjoining the
royal property. Ahab proposes an exchange — a better vineyard, or its value in silver — and Naboth
refuses in the language of inheritance: the LORD forbid that he should give the inheritance of his
fathers. The refusal is not commercial but covenantal; the land is patrimony, not a marketable
asset. Ahab goes home sullen and refuses food.

[[jezebel|Jezebel]] takes the matter over. She writes letters in Ahab's name, seals them with his
seal, and sends them to the elders and nobles who live in Naboth's city. The letters instruct them
to proclaim a fast and to set Naboth at the head of the people, and to seat two worthless men
opposite him to testify that he has cursed God and the king. The town's own leadership carries the
instructions out exactly as written. Naboth is taken outside the city and stoned to death. Word is
sent back to Jezebel — Naboth has been stoned and is dead — and she tells Ahab to go down and take
possession of the vineyard.

There [[elijah|Elijah]] meets him with the question "Have you killed and also taken possession?" and
the sentence that dogs will lick Ahab's blood in the place where they licked Naboth's, that Jezebel
will be eaten by dogs by the wall of Jezreel, and that the house of Ahab will be swept away. Ahab
tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and fasts; the sentence is deferred into his son's days.

The reckoning is stated retrospectively in 2 Kings 9. When Jehu has Joram shot between the shoulders
he orders the body thrown onto the plot of ground belonging to Naboth the Jezreelite, and recalls an
oracle spoken against Ahab: "I saw yesterday the blood of Naboth and the blood of his sons" — the
plural indicating that Naboth's sons died with him, which 1 Kings 21 does not narrate. Jehu treats
the disposal of the corpse on that specific field as the payment of that specific debt.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 21:1–16** — the offer, the refusal on grounds of ancestral inheritance, Ahab's sulk,
  Jezebel's letters, the proclaimed fast, the two worthless witnesses, the stoning, the seizure.
- **1 Kings 21:17–29** — Elijah's confrontation in the vineyard; the dynastic sentence; Ahab's
  self-abasement and the deferral of judgment to the next generation.
- **2 Kings 9:21–26** — Jehu kills Joram at the plot of Naboth and cites the oracle over "the blood
  of Naboth and the blood of his sons," ordering the body cast on that field.
- **2 Kings 9:30–37** — the death of Jezebel at Jezreel, narrated as the fulfillment of the Naboth
  sentence.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic framing in Kings** — Naboth's death is the specific crime the narrator keeps
  returning to; the Omride collapse is presented as its consequence. This is the text's own
  causal-theological account, attributed as such.
- **[[iain-provan|Provan]] (NIBC Kings, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]])** — the Naboth
  episode belongs under the heading of **justice** within Provan's larger reading of the
  Elijah–Elisha block as salvation within judgment.
- This wiki has not ingested a rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic treatment of Naboth. None is
  attributed here. Naboth's standing as a stock example of judicial murder and royal land-seizure in
  later Jewish and Christian moral discourse is noted on [[ahab|the Ahab page]] in general terms
  only, without a named source behind it.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The episode ties cultic apostasy and social injustice together in a single indictment: the king who
patronizes Baal is the king who kills a smallholder for his land, and the prophet who confronted him
at [[carmel|Carmel]] confronts him again in a stolen vineyard. Naboth's refusal grounds itself in
inheritance law rather than price, so the story functions as a test case for whether Israelite
kingship stands under covenant land tenure or above it.

The mechanism of the killing is legal, not violent in the first instance. A fast is proclaimed —
the form of communal repentance — and used as the setting for perjury; the required plurality of
witnesses is satisfied by two men the narrator calls worthless; the elders and nobles of Naboth's
own city cooperate. The narrative's indictment falls on a working judicial procedure turned to
murder, not on a mob.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The sons.** 2 Kings 9:26 refers to the blood of Naboth's sons; 1 Kings 21 narrates only Naboth's
  death. Whether this reflects a fuller tradition, a legal assumption that the heirs were removed so
  the property could pass to the crown, or a separate source is disputed. The wiki records both
  notices without harmonizing them.
- **The legal basis of the seizure.** How Ahab acquires the vineyard after the execution is not
  stated. Proposals that convicted men's property escheated to the crown, or that the land passed
  for want of heirs, are reconstructions from outside the text; the narrative simply has Ahab go
  down and take possession.
- **Deferral of the sentence.** Elijah pronounces judgment on Ahab and it falls on his house in the
  next generation. Readers dispute whether this is corporate liability, a genuine response to Ahab's
  repentance, or a redactional accommodation of the chronology of Jehu's coup. Positions only.
- **Historicity.** The episode is not externally attested. It is recorded here as narrative, with no
  verdict on whether an event underlies it.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Within the wiki's ingested material Naboth functions chiefly as Provan's justice node in the
Elijah cycle and as the hinge that makes [[jehu|Jehu]]'s purge legible as retribution rather than
mere coup. More broadly he is a standard reference point for prophetic critique of royal power and
for the biblical treatment of inalienable ancestral land. Cross-links: [[ahab|Ahab]],
