# Barnabas

**Biographical Overview**:
Born Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, he was nicknamed Barnabas ("Son of Encouragement") by the apostles (Acts 4:36). He was an early, generous member of the Jerusalem church who introduced the newly converted Paul to the apostles (Acts 9:27). He led the church in Antioch and recruited Paul to help him (Acts 11:22-26). Together, they undertook the first missionary journey (Acts 13-14). In Galatians, Paul mentions Barnabas as his companion during the critical second Jerusalem visit to consult with the "pillars" (Gal. 2:1, 9). However, they later experienced conflict at Antioch regarding table fellowship with Gentiles, where even Barnabas was led astray by Peter's hypocrisy (Gal. 2:13). They later parted ways over John Mark (Acts 15:36-41).

From Bock (Acts, this batch): Barnabas is a model of generosity, bridge-building (introducing Paul), and Spirit-led mission. The separation from Paul is an honest disagreement within the divine plan; both resulting teams advance the gospel.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Acts**: A prominent leader in the early chapters, central to the church in Antioch and the first missionary journey.
- **Galatians 2**: Appears alongside Paul at the Jerusalem meeting where they receive the "right hand of fellowship" from the pillars. He is later rebuked by implication when Paul confronts Peter at Antioch, since Barnabas too had withdrawn from eating with Gentiles.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
In Christian tradition, Barnabas is honored as a pioneer of the Gentile mission. The apocryphal *Epistle of Barnabas* was attributed to him in the early church, though modern scholarship considers it pseudepigraphal. Bock highlights his role as the one who vouches for Paul and models encouragement.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Barnabas serves as a bridge figure between the Jerusalem church and the Gentile mission in Antioch. In Galatians, his temporary failure at Antioch underscores the power of the "Judaizing" pressure, illustrating that even Paul's closest ally in the Gentile mission could waver on the practical implications of "the truth of the gospel." In Acts (Bock), he exemplifies the "son of encouragement" who risks on new converts and whose partnership with Paul models collaborative mission; the split shows the mission is larger than any one team.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
Commentators like [[douglas-moo|Moo]] note the significance of Barnabas's presence at the Jerusalem consultation (Gal. 2:1) as a representative of the Antioch church, and highlight the tragedy of his temporary defection during the Antioch incident (Gal. 2:13). The exact timeline of his travels with Paul and their eventual split remains a subject of chronological harmonization between Acts and Galatians. Bock treats the Acts portrait as reliable and complementary.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
Bock (Acts) presents Barnabas as the generous advocate who introduces Paul, models the encouraging character of the Spirit-led mission, and demonstrates that God uses human agents (with their disagreements) to advance the gospel. His story reinforces the theocentric historiography of Acts.
