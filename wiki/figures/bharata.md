# Bharata

**Bharata** is the second son of King [[dasaratha|Daśaratha]] of Ayodhyā, born of Queen Kaikeyī. He is absent in his maternal grandfather's kingdom when his mother engineers [[rama|Rāma]]'s exile and the transfer of the throne to Bharata himself. His response — total repudiation of the throne, a perilous journey to find Rāma and beg his return, and a fourteen-year ascetic regency in which Rāma's sandals govern in his place — makes him the Rāmāyaṇa's exemplar of dharmic loyalty, fraternal love, and the faithful prince who refuses illegitimate power.

## Biographical Overview

In William Buck's retelling, Bharata is described as having **red skin and red hair** (line 4881–4882). He is residing at the Kekaya court of his maternal grandfather King Aswapati when the events of the exile unfold. While there he suffers **evil prophetic dreams**: he saw the ocean dry, the Moon fall to Earth, the tusks of Dasaratha's elephants shatter, the fires of Ayodhya go out, and smoke rising from the hills of Kosala (lines 4873–4877). "Bharata awoke in the night shaking and frightened" (line 4878); his brother Satrughna, standing beside him, says simply: "Someone has died but I don't know who" (line 4888).

When Ayodhya's riders arrive with the summons home, they refuse to name what has happened — they only say "Do not delay. Restless fate and fortune call you home" (line 4898). Bharata asks three questions: *How is my father? How is my mother? How is Rama?* — three questions that together establish the axis of his anxieties and the moral order he will spend the rest of the episode defending.

Arriving at a silent, shuttered Ayodhya before dawn, the brothers see a city drained of life. Bharata visits Kaikeyi first. She rises to tell him the news with transparent pride: Dasaratha is dead; the kingdom is Bharata's; she used her two wishes. When Bharata presses her — "Whose wife did Rama take?" "What did Rama steal?" "Whom did Rama wrong?" — and each time she says "nothing, no one, no crime," Bharata's response is immediate: "How hateful!" He runs from the room (lines 4964–4972).

## Reaction: Repudiation of the Throne

Bharata's repudiation of Kaikeyi is unequivocal: "Not any more," he says of her as mother (line 4988). He goes immediately to Queen Kausalya and kneels: "I did not know! I'll never take it. I don't want to rule others, I don't want possessions. I know nothing . . ." (lines 4976–4978). Kausalya lays her hand on his head and tells him he is the injured party — no one blames him (lines 4980–4982).

When the priest Vasishtha presses him to accept the kingship as a practical necessity ("In a kingless land there is no rain . . . Disputes will arise and not be solved," lines 5023–5033), Bharata refuses: "No. For my part I will go to the forest and bring Rama back" (line 5037). When Vasishtha presses again, Bharata retorts with characteristic bluntness: "Watch it! Do not sell treasures with one hand and hold back happiness with your other. Or I will move out against you" (lines 5043–5045).

He eventually agrees to perform Dasaratha's funeral — but only "in Rama's name" (line 5069). After throwing his father's bones into the Sarayu (line 5140), when the Kosalas awaken him with royal trumpets, he turns them away: "Never do that again, I am not the King" (line 5143). His declaration: "Only the son first-born may be King in our Solar race. I will take Rama's place in the forest" (lines 5145–5147).

## The Journey to Chitrakuta and the Meeting with Rama

Bharata sets out with a vast Kosala retinue — nine thousand war-mounted elephants, six thousand chariots, horsemen, infantry, the three Queens — to find Rama. He meets Guha the Hunter King at the Ganga crossing; Guha's bird and animal oracle says simply "Don't hinder him" (lines 5175–5179). Guha shows Bharata the ground where Rama slept: "the hard rough grass still shows where it was pressed by Rama's limbs . . . here are silk threads pulled from Sita's robe. Here slept Sumantra, here Lakshmana sat" (lines 5209–5212). Bharata says: "It seems incredible to me. Oh, had I not gone to Kekaya . . ." (lines 5213–5214).

At Bharadwaja's hermitage Bharata comes alone, unarmed, in a single robe. When Bharadwaja offers to host the whole army, Bharata's initial reply is a model of simple contentment: "I have been entertained by water and the sight of you. By your words have you well received us all" (lines 5271–5273). Bharadwaja hosts an extraordinary divine feast conjured by the architect Viswakarman (lines 5280–5326).

Bharadwaja then gives Bharata Kaikeyi's absolution, saying Rama's exile "will truly be for the great good of all the worlds" (lines 5366–5370). Bharata puts his arm around his mother and draws her close (line 5371).

At Chitrakuta, Bharata finds Rama standing still in black deerskin with matted hair (lines 5485–5486). He kneels, then rises and speaks plainly: "I decline the ownership and possession of the Kosala kingdom. We have no King. Return to Ayodhya with these people, I will replace you here" (lines 5508–5511). He argues urgently: "A wife ought to love her husband more than even a thousand fine sons. Gentle Sita deserves to live in a palace . . . The eldest son must be King" (lines 5519–5522).

When Rama refuses on grounds of upholding Dasaratha's word, Bharata tries a deeper argument — that rigid adherence to the dead king's wishes is itself a kind of error: "Men lose their sense as their Death approaches . . . I can now never get another brother" (lines 5537–5547). Rama's counter — "The first betrayal may be easy or it may be hard, but after the first betrayal then the others soon follow . . . let us keep the truth" (lines 5548–5558) — carries the day, and the Kosalas respond: "Well said! Well spoken!" (lines 5591–5592).

## The Sandals Episode

The exchange culminates in the Rāmāyaṇa's most resonant political symbol. Bharata says, "Give me your sandals." Rama removes his **wooden-soled sandals with colored flowers painted on them** (lines 5594–5595). Bharata accepts them and vows: "Rama, you are the true King. I will go back, I will rule Kosala in trust for you for fourteen years, and your sandals will be on the throne. If I do not see you on the first day after those fourteen years I'll walk into a fire and die" (lines 5596–5601).

The sandal-regency is installed at the village of **Nandigrama** (not in Ayodhya itself): Satrughna brings the royal throne there; Vasishtha brings all signs of royalty; Bharata leads the elephant bearing the sandals. The sandals are placed on the throne beneath the gold-ribbed white silk umbrella and the white yak-tail fan. Bharata and Satrughna sit at the foot of the throne in barkcloth. "Every day the ministers and noblemen of Kosala came out from Ayodhya all in bark like hermits. They would bow to Rama's shoes as to a King" (lines 5612–5614). Most strikingly: "Rama's sandals lay quiet if justice was done before the throne, but if any case was wrongly judged the sandals swiftly beat together their loud wooden soles" (lines 5616–5618).

The sandals govern actively: justice is enforced through them. Bharata's dress, the ministers' dress, and the demotion of the court from Ayodhya to the village of Nandigrama together constitute a collective act of ascetic mourning and political suspension — the kingdom holds its breath until Rama returns.

Ayodhya under this arrangement is drained: "For fourteen years there was silence in Fair Ayodhya and no sound of mirth or learning. Many days the Sun was clouded because of heaven's dark-hearted sorrow. Musicians unstrung their lutes . . . The Kosalas took the wheels off their chariots and walked where they had to go, because Rama had no chariot and had to walk in the woods" (lines 5619–5626).

## Forgiveness of Kaikeyi

When Vasishtha details the terrible future lives Kaikeyi will live — a mad beggar eating dirt from a skull, a miser robbed by thieves, a teacher who forgets all learning and turns to dice, one who poisons wells and betrays friends (lines 5087–5103) — Bharata listens, and is asked whether he forgives her. He says: "I forgive." And then adds: "A son comes to life from the blood of his mother's heart, therefore he is dear to her" (lines 5109–5113). Vasishtha responds: "Compassion is great enough where it can have no effect. Used to accomplish something it is supreme!" (lines 5110–5111).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bharata occupies a structural position opposite to Kaikeyi and complementary to Rāma. Where Kaikeyi acts entirely from desire (*kāma*) and disrupts the natural order of succession, Bharata acts entirely from dharma and fraternal love (*bhakti*) and restores order symbolically in Rāma's absence. He does not simply refuse power — he creates a ritual political form (the sandal-regency) that preserves Rāma's kingship even through fourteen years of absence.

Guha — the outsider, the Hunter King, whose birds and animals see clearly — characterizes Bharata from an external perspective: "The bird people are singing of your fame, the deer praise you. They say you are an unclouded sky, beautiful in the daytime, beautiful at night. They call you a true man, a real friend, they wonder if there is anyone else like you on Earth, who would give back a kingdom . . ." (lines 5199–5203).

The sandals themselves acquire a semi-divine character in Buck's telling: they are not merely symbols but active agents of justice, signaling when a judgment is wrong. They are the displaced presence of the righteous king.

Bharata's argument at Chitrakuta — that promises made by dying men need not bind the living, that men matter more than obligations (lines 5537–5547) — represents a genuine dharmic counter-position to Rāma's rigidity. The text does not simply dismiss it; the Kosalas cheer Rāma's reply, but Bharata's love-argument is accorded real dignity. The narrative sets up a contrast between two kinds of righteous men: the one who keeps his word absolutely and the one who keeps his love absolutely. Bharata cannot outargue Rāma on the law; but Rāma cannot outargue Bharata on love.
