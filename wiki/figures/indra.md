# Indra

**Indra** is the foremost god of the [[rig-veda|Rig Veda]] — the **warrior-king of the gods**, wielder of the
thunderbolt (*vajra*), god of **storm, war, and rain**, to whom about a **quarter of all Rigvedic hymns** are
addressed. Vigorous, heroic, and fond of the exhilarating [[soma|soma]], he is the champion who makes the
world habitable.

## Myths in Detail — the Slaying of Vritra

Indra's defining deed — the **central myth of the Rig Veda** — is the slaying of **[[vritra|Vritra]]**, the
great serpent-dragon of drought who **"encompassed" and held back the cosmic waters**, coiled around the
mountains so that the rivers could not flow and the world lay parched and lifeless. Strengthened by drinking
the [[soma|soma]] and armed with the **thunderbolt (*vajra*)** forged by Tvashtri, **Indra smashed Vritra's
head**, split open the mountain, and **released the pent-up waters**, which rushed down to the sea — freeing
the rivers, bringing the rains, and **establishing the ordered cosmos**. "He slew the dragon, then disclosed
the waters" (RV 1.32). This **storm-god-vs-chaos-dragon** combat is a deep **Indo-European** pattern (compare
[[marduk|Marduk]] vs. [[tiamat|Tiamat]], [[zeus|Zeus]] vs. Typhon, Thor vs. the World-Serpent), and Indra's
victory is repeatedly celebrated as the act that **upholds [[rita|rita]]** against chaos.

## From Vedic King to Hindu Demigod

In Vedic religion Indra is the **supreme active god**; but in later [[hinduism|Hinduism]] he is dramatically
**demoted** — still king of the lesser gods (*devas*) and lord of the heavens, but subordinate to the great
gods Vishnu and Shiva, and often humbled in the Puranic and Buddhist stories. This decline is the clearest
single marker of the shift from **Vedic religion to Hinduism**.
