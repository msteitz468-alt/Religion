# Trilochan

Trilochan (c. 14th century) was a Maharashtrian [[sant-tradition|Sant]] poet of the Vaishnava tradition whose compositions appear in the **[[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]** in two raag sections: Siree Raag (Part 1, Ang 92) and Raag Goojaree (Part 2, Ang 526). He is associated with the Maharashtra Sant tradition that includes Namdev, Jnandev, and Eknath.

## In the Guru Granth Sahib

### Siree Raag (Part 1, Ang 92)

Trilochan's composition in Siree Raag treats forgetfulness of death and attachment to family while Death approaches. The composition emphasizes the cost of worldly attachment at the moment of death:

> "If someone is going to teach me something, let it be that the Lord is pervading the forests and fields." (L20575, Ang 92)

The composition belongs to a group of Bhagat bāṇī in Siree Raag that meditates on mortality and the rarity of those who use the human birth for spiritual practice.

### Raag Goojaree (Part 2, Ang 526) — Consciousness at Death

Trilochan's composition in Goojaree contains the **consciousness at death** teaching — among the most doctrinally specific statements in the Bhagat bāṇī:

> "At the last moment, one who thinks of women, dies such a death — he is reincarnated over and over again as a prostitute. At the last moment, one who thinks of property, dies such a death — he is reincarnated over and over again as a snake. At the last moment, one who thinks of his children, dies such a death — he is reincarnated over and over again as a pig. At the last moment, one who thinks of the Lord, Trilochan says, is liberated; the Lord dwells in his heart." (paraphrase, L8097–8128, Ang 526)

This formulation directly parallels the *Bhagavad Gita* 8.6 ("Whoever, at the time of death, gives up his body while remembering Me alone, reaches My state") and Buddhist consciousness-at-death doctrine in Theravada thought. Within the SGGS, the teaching is presented as an argument for constant Naam-remembrance: if what one thinks at the moment of death determines rebirth, the only safe mental state is continuous meditation on the Lord — because death can come at any moment.

## Theological Significance

Trilochan's compositions contribute to the SGGS's cross-regional and cross-lineage picture of the Sant movement. His Maharashtrian Vaishnava background (from the tradition of Namdev, his contemporary) connects the SGGS's inclusivity to the Marathi *warkari* tradition of pilgrimage to Pandharpur and devotion to Vithoba/Vitthal. His inclusion alongside Namdev, who is also Maharashtrian, suggests that the Guru Granth Sahib's compilers recognized a distinct stream of Maharashtra bhakti as theologically consonant with the Sikh Guru tradition.
