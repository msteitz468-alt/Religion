## Biographical Overview

Asterius, known as "the sophist," was a lay theologian in the [[arian-controversy|Arian controversy]] and one of the major intellectual figures of the "Eusebian" trajectory. He "had sacrificed to the gods during the last great persecution and was thus ineligible for ordination" (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*–591). Despite his lay status, Asterius was "an important defender of Arius before Nicaea". "Unfortunately only fragments of his works survive", principally through quotations in Athanasius's *Orations against the Arians* and through Eusebius of Caesarea's *Contra Marcellum*. Ayres follows the critical edition and commentary by Markus Vinzent (*Asterius von Kappadokien*, Leiden: Brill, 1993) for fragment numbering.

## Role in the Fourth-Century Controversy (per Ayres)

Asterius played a decisive role in shaping the controversies both before and after Nicaea. Before Nicaea he was "an important defender of Arius". After Nicaea, "his theology became one of the main sparks for the continuing controversy as he attempted to defend [[eusebius-of-nicomedia|Eusebius of Nicomedia]] against the attacks of [[marcellus-of-ancyra|Marcellus of Ancyra]]". His *Syntagmation* provoked Marcellus's *Contra Asterium*, which in turn provoked [[eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius of Caesarea]]'s extensive reply — making Asterius the pivot of the central post-Nicene polemical exchange.

A key fragment is cited with approval by Eusebius of Caesarea (*C. Marcellum* 1. 4): "the only begotten Logos (John 1: 18) and first born of all creation, the alone [begetting] the alone, perfect the perfect, King the King, Lord the Lord, God [begetting] God, exact image of the being and will and power and glory". Ayres notes this text "reappears at an important council in 341", indicating its continued canonical status in Eusebian circles.

## Theological Position / Trajectory

Asterius belongs to the "Eusebians," the trajectory of "theologians of the 'one Unbegotten.'" His most distinctive contribution is a doctrine of **multiple powers and wisdoms** that distinguishes Christ from God's own intrinsic power and wisdom:

> God's own power and wisdom is the source of Christ and of all things (it is God's own power and wisdom that Paul describes as being seen in the creation at Rom. 1: 20), but Christ himself is a recent power and wisdom, the first and 'only begotten' of the many powers created by the Father.

In developing this, Asterius gives an exegetical argument from 1 Corinthians 1:

> The blessed Paul said not that he preached Christ the power of God or the wisdom of God, but, without the article, 'God's power and God's wisdom'; thus preaching that the proper power of God Himself, which is natural to him and coexistent with him unoriginatedly, is something besides. (quoted in Athanasius, *C. Ar.* 1. 32, Vinzent fr. 64–621)

Ayres glosses: "Asterius understands the terminology of power and wisdom in a manner directly opposed to that found in Alexander, and in Athanasius, where Christ is the one power and wisdom of the Father". The anarthrous Pauline phrase becomes a proof that God possesses an "unoriginate" power distinct from Christ.

### The Son as Mediator and First-Created Power

Asterius argues that "the Son was generated because the Father foresaw the inability of created nature to bear his direct touch" (citing Athanasius, *C. Ar.* 2. 24, Vinzent fr. 26). The Son thus functions as a necessary intermediate — created first precisely to serve as the medium through which "all things might thereupon be brought to be" (*C. Ar.* 2. 24).

Ayres notes Michel Barnes's observation that while Asterius's doctrine appears similar to Origen's "Son as one of the many powers," Asterius "uses this doctrine not to support an account of the Son's separate existence from the Father, but to emphasize Christ's status as the first of many" (Barnes, *Power of God*, 126–7–634). Asterius "does clearly emphasize the uniqueness of the Son's status as the first and as the one who can effect a transformation of all created things". The Son is "Logos for the sake of rational things, and Wisdom for the sake of things endowed with wisdom" (Athanasius, *C. Ar.* 2. 38–638).

### Three Hypostases

Asterius "insists also that Father, Son, and Spirit are three hypostases and he uses the logical formula that 'the Father is truly Father, the Son truly Son and the Spirit likewise' to emphasize that the distinctions between the three are implied by the names we accord them".

### Image Language and Subordination

When Asterius speaks of the Son as "exact image of the being and will and power and glory" of the Father, Ayres notes he "is happy to speak of the Logos as the image of the Father's ousia, presumably because for him the term image carries a clear sense of difference and subordination".

## Reception and Later Labelling

Ayres's account corrects any reading of Asterius as a simple repetition of Arius. Asterius's emphasis on the Son's unique primacy and his insistence on the Son's "transforming" capacity for all created things represent a more developed theological programme than a bare assertion of the Son's creaturely status. At the same time, his distinction between God's own intrinsic power/wisdom and "a recent power and wisdom" — Christ — is the clearest articulation of what the Alexander-Athanasius trajectory found most objectionable. His work was condemned by [[marcellus-of-ancyra|Marcellus of Ancyra]], whose *Contra Asterium* in turn provoked [[eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius of Caesarea]]'s major theological response.

## Influence

Asterius's *Syntagmation* catalysed the post-Nicene polemical exchange and helped fix the theological vocabulary of the Eusebian side. His fragment on the "exact image of the being and will and power and glory" was important enough to be deployed at the second Antiochene council of 341, and Eusebius of Caesarea's citation of it shows it functioned as something close to a creedal touchstone within Eusebian circles.
