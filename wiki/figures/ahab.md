Ahab son of Omri, king of Israel and the central royal antagonist of the [[elijah|Elijah]] cycle:
husband of [[jezebel|Jezebel]] of Sidon, patron of a Baal temple in [[samaria|Samaria]], the king
of the Naboth affair and of the Aramean wars, killed by a chance arrow at Ramoth-gilead. The
biblical portrait is a sustained theological indictment; it is recorded here as the narrative's
verdict, not as this wiki's judgment of the man.

## Biographical Overview

1 Kings 16 introduces Ahab as the son of Omri, the general who won the civil war after Zimri's
seven-day usurpation and who bought the hill of Samaria and built the new capital there. Ahab
married Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, erected an altar and house for Baal in
Samaria, and made an Asherah. The narrator's summary is that he "did more to provoke the LORD… than
all the kings of Israel who were before him."

The Elijah narratives dominate his reign: the drought announced by Elijah; the contest on Mount
Carmel, after which Elijah has the prophets of Baal seized and slaughtered at the Wadi Kishon;
Jezebel's death threat and Elijah's flight to Horeb. Ahab twice defeats Ben-hadad of the
[[arameans|Arameans]] and is condemned by a prophet for sparing him under treaty. In 1 Kings 21
Naboth the Jezreelite refuses to sell his ancestral vineyard; Jezebel writes letters in Ahab's
name, proclaims a fast, and has two worthless men charge Naboth with cursing God and the king, so
that he is taken out and stoned. Ahab goes down to take possession and meets Elijah with the
sentence that dogs will lick his blood and eat Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel. Ahab tears his
clothes and fasts, and the sentence is deferred to his son's days.

He dies at Ramoth-gilead in alliance with Jehoshaphat of Judah, having gone into battle disguised
after Micaiah son of Imlah alone predicted disaster against four hundred prophets. An archer draws
his bow at random and strikes him between the scale armor and the joints; he is propped up in his
chariot until evening and dies, and dogs lick his blood at the pool of Samaria.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 16:29–34** — accession, Jezebel, the Baal temple, the narrator's evaluation.
- **1 Kings 17–19** — drought, Carmel, the slaughter at the Kishon, Horeb.
- **1 Kings 20** — the two Aramean campaigns and the prophetic condemnation for releasing
  Ben-hadad.
- **1 Kings 21** — Naboth's vineyard: the judicial murder, the stoning, Elijah's sentence, Ahab's
  self-abasement and the deferral of judgment.
- **1 Kings 22** — Micaiah, the lying spirit in the mouth of the prophets, the disguise, the random
  arrow, death and burial; the ivory house and cities he built noted in the closing formula.
- **2 Chronicles 18** — the Ramoth-gilead campaign retold from Jehoshaphat's side, with a rebuke
  of the Judahite king for the alliance.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation in Kings** — Ahab is the worst of the northern kings by the
  narrator's cultic standard, and the Omride house becomes the type of the condemned dynasty. This
  is the text's theological verdict, attributed as such.
- **[[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] (NIBC, 1995)**, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|the Kings page]]:
  the Elijah–Elisha block, in which Ahab is the principal royal foil, is read as **salvation
  within judgment**; the Naboth episode is read under the heading of justice, and Micaiah's
  oracle as the true word against the mass of court prophecy.
- **Christian reception** — the Naboth affair is a standing biblical example of judicial murder and
  royal seizure of a poor man's inheritance. This wiki has not ingested a patristic or rabbinic
  treatment of Ahab, and none is attributed here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Ahab's reign is where Kings poses the question of exclusive loyalty most sharply: Carmel stages it
as a public contest ("if the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him"), and the
Naboth episode insists that worship and social justice stand or fall together — the covenant
violation is a land seizure and a rigged trial, not only a cultic offense. The Micaiah scene is one
of the Hebrew Bible's most direct treatments of true and false prophecy. The deferral of judgment
after Ahab's self-humbling is a recorded instance of a sentence held over into a later generation.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **External attestation.** An Assyrian royal monument of Shalmaneser III, the Kurkh Monolith,
  records a western coalition met in battle at Qarqar; the standard reading of the text includes an
  Israelite contingent under a ruler identified with the biblical Ahab. That identification is
  widely accepted but rests on a single damaged inscription, and the very large chariot figure the
  monolith gives for that contingent is frequently regarded as inflated or textually uncertain — no
  figure is asserted here.
- **The Mesha Stele** (Moabite inscription of King Mesha, from Dhiban) refers to Israelite
  domination of Moab under Omri and his successor; it is regularly read alongside the Kings notice
  of Moabite tribute and revolt. The stele is a royal propaganda text on its own terms, and its
  chronology relative to Kings is debated.
- **Modern historical assessment [recorded tension].** Many historians read the Omrides as the most
  politically and militarily successful dynasty of the northern kingdom — builders of Samaria,
  participants in international coalitions, attested abroad in a way earlier kings are not — in
  direct tension with the biblical narrator's judgment that Ahab was the worst of Israel's kings.
  Both are recorded as positions; the wiki does not adjudicate between a theological verdict and a
  political one, and notes that they are not answering the same question.
- **The Aramean war narratives of 1 Kings 20 and 22** are argued by some to fit a later reign
  better than Ahab's; recorded as a position without endorsement.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Ahab is the fixed point for the Baal-versus-YHWH conflict in Israelite religion and for the study
of prophetic opposition to monarchy. He also anchors the archaeological debate over Iron Age
building programs at [[megiddo|Megiddo]], [[hazor|Hazor]], and Samaria, where a body of scholarship
has argued for reassigning monumental construction from the Solomonic to the Omride period — a
