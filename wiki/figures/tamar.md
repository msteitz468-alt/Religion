# Tamar

**Tamar** is the Canaanite (or, in the Zohar, priestly-descended) daughter-in-law of [[judah|Judah]] whose story in **Genesis 38** makes her an ancestress of the royal and messianic line. Widowed by Judah's sons Er and Onan and denied the third son Shelah, she disguises herself, conceives by Judah himself, and — when threatened with death for "harlotry" — produces his pledge with the words that vindicate her; Judah declares, "**She is more righteous than I**." Her twins, Perez and Zerah, carry the line to David. Both Jewish tradition and the [[zohar|Zohar]] read her bold act not as transgression but as **righteous, wisdom-driven initiative** to draw the messianic seed into the world — pairing her with [[ruth|Ruth]].

---

## Biographical Overview (Genesis 38)

- Married to **Er**, Judah's firstborn, who dies; then by levirate custom to **Onan**, who "spills his seed" and also dies.
- Judah, fearing for his third son **Shelah**, sends Tamar back to her father's house as a widow, withholding Shelah.
- Seeing that Shelah is grown and she has not been given to him, Tamar **disguises herself as a prostitute** at the roadside; Judah, not recognizing her, takes her, leaving his **seal, cord, and staff** as a pledge.
- Pregnant, she is accused of harlotry; as she is brought to be burned, she sends the pledge with "**by the man to whom these belong I am pregnant**" — and "**Recognize, please**, whose these are."
- Judah confesses: "**She is more righteous than I**, since I did not give her to my son Shelah."
- She bears twins, **Perez and Zerah**; Perez is the ancestor of Boaz, Jesse, and David.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Genesis 38** — the narrative in full.
- **Ruth 4:12** — the elders bless Boaz: "may your house be like the house of **Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah**."
- **Matthew 1:3** — Tamar named in the genealogy of Jesus (one of five women, with Rahab, Ruth, "the wife of Uriah," and Mary).

---

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: Tamar is praised as righteous and modest; her willingness to be burned rather than publicly shame Judah ("Recognize, please" rather than "this is the man") is a model of discretion (the Talmud derives from it the principle that one should rather be cast into a fiery furnace than shame another publicly, *Berakhot* 43b; *Sotah* 10b). She is honored as a mother of royalty.
- **Christian**: her inclusion in Matthew's genealogy marks the messianic line as marked by irregularity and grace; patristic readers treat her variously as a type of the church drawn from the nations or as an example of faith reaching toward the promise.
- **Kabbalistic (the Zohar)** — see below.

---

## Kabbalistic Reception (the Zohar)

The Zohar (Parashat Va-Yeshev) defends and exalts Tamar at the **Sod** level:

- **Righteous, not wanton**: "Would you ever imagine that she set out to whore with her father-in-law, given that she was inherently modest? Rather, she was **righteous and did this out of wisdom**… because she possessed knowledge and contemplated wisdom." Her act is a deliberate, contemplative drawing-down of the messianic seed, "to act kindly and faithfully."
- **The two mothers of the royal seed**: "There were **two women through whom the seed of Judah was established**, from whom issued King David, King Solomon, and **King Messiah**. These two correspond to one another: **Tamar and [[ruth|Ruth]]**" — both widows whose husbands died, both of whom "exerted themselves in this action." The Zohar thereby reads the irregular unions of Genesis 38 and the book of Ruth as parallel, providential channels for the messianic soul to enter the world through [[judah|Judah]]'s line.
- The episode stands in pointed contrast to **Er and Onan**, who wasted seed and fed the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]]: Tamar's initiative *rescues* the line that her husbands' sin had endangered, turning the misuse of [[tsaddiq|Yesod]] back toward the establishment of the kingdom ([[shekhinah|Malkhut]]).

---

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Tamar belongs to a biblical pattern of **bold women who secure the covenant line against male failure or inertia** (with Ruth, and arguably Rebekah and the midwives). The narrative insists that the messianic genealogy runs through human risk, irregularity, and a woman's righteousness recognized only retrospectively. The Zohar raises this to cosmology: the entrance of the royal-messianic soul into history is itself a theurgical act, accomplished by women who "contemplated wisdom."

---

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- Genesis 38 is widely read as in part **etiological** for the clans of Judah (Perez, Zerah, Shelah) and for the absorption of Canaanite lineage into the tribe; critical scholarship debates its date and its function as an interruption of the Joseph novella.
- The **levirate custom** (later codified in Deut 25:5–10) underlies the plot; the legal and ethical reading of Onan's act ("spilling seed") has a long and contested afterlife.
- Distinguish this Tamar from **Tamar daughter of David** (2 Sam 13), an unrelated figure.
