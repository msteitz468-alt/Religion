# Táhirih

*Populated from [[peter-smith|Smith, A Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

Táhirih ("the Pure") was the title given by the Báb to Fátimih Baraghání (1814–52), born into a prominent clerical family in the northern Iranian city of Qazvin (line 30245). Her father, Mullá Muhammad Sálih, was a respected and powerful *mujtahid*, as was his brother, Mullá [[mulla-muhammad-taqi-baraghani|Muhammad-Taqí Baraghání]] (line 30247). Unusually for a 19th-century Iranian woman, she was allowed to undertake higher Islamic studies, gaining a considerable reputation for scholarship (lines 30251–30255). At fourteen she was married to her uncle's son, Muhammad, by whom she had three sons and one daughter (lines 30256–30261).

While her elder uncle Muhammad-Taqí was one of the most vehement opponents of [[shaykhism|Shaykhism]], several of her relatives became Shaykhis; Fátimih followed suit and entered into secret correspondence with Sayyid [[kazim-rashti|Kázim Rashtí]], who called her Qurratu'l-'Ayn (lines 30266–30272).

## Conversion and Early Activity

Following Sayyid Kázim's death she proceeded to Karbala, where she resided in Rashtí's house with the support of his widow, and energetically promoted Babism, attracting many Shaykhis in Karbala (lines 30292–30295). At some point she sent a message of recognition to the Báb in Shiraz, being enrolled by him as a [[letters-of-the-living|Letter of the Living]] — one of his eighteen original disciples (lines 30288–30291).

Her leadership on the part of a woman, combined with her increasingly radical understanding of Babism as a messianic new age — reflected in her disregard for Islamic convention — generated opposition from both conservative Babis and Shaykhis (lines 30296–30302). The Báb supported her, however. When allegations of immorality arose (she apparently appeared unveiled before her disciples), the Báb named her "the Pure" (Táhirih) (lines 30303–30307).

Mounting opposition caused her to leave Karbala for Baghdad, where she resided in the house of the chief mufti Shaykh Mahmúd Álúsí, who was favourably impressed by her intellect and devotion (lines 30309–30314). In March 1847, on Ottoman orders, she was deported. Travelling through western Iran, she openly proclaimed Babism to Shi'is, Ahl-i-Haqq, and Jews, before returning to Qazvin in July (lines 30315–30321). She refused to live with her husband, regarding him as an infidel, and tensions with her family escalated, already made worse by the growth of a Babi group in Qazvin (lines 30322–30329).

## Conference of Badasht and Execution

Táhirih was one of the principals at the [[conference-of-badasht|conference of Badasht]] (June–July 1848), where she advocated a radical break with Islam (lines 30330–30334). After going into hiding, she was discovered in January 1850 and transferred to Tehran, remaining in confinement until her execution — strangled or choked to death — in September 1852, in the aftermath of the assassination attempt on [[nasiru-d-din-shah|Násiru'd-Dín Sháh]] (lines 30335–30342).

## Significance

Táhirih's role in the development of Babism was crucial, particularly in its "radicalization" (line 30345). She was a charismatic figure who sought to transcend the restrictions placed on women in a traditionalistic society (lines 30346–30349). Her immediate followers regarded her as the "Return" of the Prophet Muhammad's daughter Fátima (lines 30352–30354). She wrote extensively — apologetic works, a large correspondence on Babi matters — but little has survived apart from her poems, which remain widely regarded in the Persian cultural world (lines 30354–30361). Accounts of her life came to inspire later generations of feminists (lines 30361–30363).
