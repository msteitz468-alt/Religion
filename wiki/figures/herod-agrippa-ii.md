# Herod Agrippa II

## Biographical Overview

Marcus Julius Agrippa — Herod Agrippa II (AD 28–100) — was the last of the Herodian client
kings and the ruler before whom Paul made his fullest defense, as narrated in [[acts|Acts]]
25:13–26:32. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] identifies him as the only surviving son of
[[herod-agrippa-i|Herod Agrippa I]] (whose death is recorded at Acts 12:23) and the
great-grandson of Herod the Great. Rome granted Agrippa II rule over a northeastern portion
of the region (from Lebanon eastward, with parts of Galilee) and the right to appoint the
Jewish high priest. Bock describes him as "very pious" and an "expert in Jewish issues," a
faithful vassal who sided with Rome in the war of AD 70.

> **Disambiguation.** Herod Agrippa II is distinct from his father
> [[herod-agrippa-i|Herod Agrippa I]] (Acts 12), who persecuted the Jerusalem church, had
> [[james-son-of-zebedee|James son of Zebedee]] executed, and died at Caesarea. Acts 25–26
> concerns the son.

He is accompanied throughout the hearing by his sister **Bernice** (Julia Bernice), formerly
wife of Herod of Chalcis and of Polemon of Cilicia. Bock notes the ancient rumor (Juvenal)
of an incestuous relationship between Agrippa and Bernice, and that Bernice later became the
mistress of the future emperor Titus, who "did not [marry her] because of Roman antipathy to
Jews."

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 25:13–22** — Agrippa II and Bernice arrive at [[caesarea|Caesarea]] to greet
  [[festus|Festus]]; Festus lays Paul's case before Agrippa, describing the dispute as being
  over "a certain Jesus, who was dead but whom Paul asserted to be alive."
- **Acts 25:23–27** — The hearing convenes "with great pomp" (φαντασία, a New Testament
  hapax).
- **Acts 26:1–23** — Paul delivers his longest and most stylized defense speech, recounting
  his life as a Pharisee, his conversion (the third telling in Acts), and his commission to
  Jews and Gentiles.
- **Acts 26:28** — Agrippa: "In a little while you [try to] persuade me to make me a
  Christian" (πείθεις, conative) — the second and final New Testament use of "Christian."
- **Acts 26:30–32** — Agrippa concurs Paul has done "nothing to deserve death or
  imprisonment," and observes that Paul "could have been set free if he had not appealed to
  Caesar."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's Reading)

Bock reads the hearing before Agrippa as "the fullest discussion in Acts of Paul's
situation" and quotes Johnson's characterization of it as a "show trial." He finds pointed
irony in the tribunal's composition: "There is irony in having such a couple sit in judgment
on Paul... a world turned upside down." Agrippa's expertise in Jewish custom is precisely why
Festus enlists him — Festus needs someone who can frame a charge he himself cannot articulate.

On Agrippa's famous reply (26:28), Bock reads πείθεις as conative ("you are trying to
persuade") and judges the king's response as falling "somewhere between earnestness and
irony." Paul's rejoinder — an optative wish (εὐξαίμην, "I would pray") that all his hearers
might become as he is, "except for these chains" (26:29) — turns the exchange back on the
court. Bock counts the verdict at 26:31 as the **third innocence declaration** in this
sequence and the fifth in Acts (with 23:9, 29), noting that its scope has now grown to
include imprisonment as well as death. Agrippa's observation that Paul might have been freed
is immediately qualified by Bock: "Nothing... will short-circuit the appeal" once lodged,
given "Caesar's auctoritas."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock situates the Agrippa hearing within the Lukan theme that Paul, testifying before "kings,"
fulfills the commission of Acts 9:15 and the promise of Luke 21:12–15. The Herodian
king — himself the descendant of a dynasty that had opposed both Jesus and the church — hears
the gospel in its fullest apologetic form and pronounces Paul innocent, underscoring both the
legitimacy of the movement and the "injustice—but providence—of his situation."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Agrippa II and Bernice are well attested in Josephus and Roman sources, and Bock uses their
documented biographies (the appointment power over the high priesthood, Bernice's marriages
and liaisons, Agrippa's loyalty to Rome in AD 70) to anchor the scene historically. The tone
of Agrippa's reply at 26:28 — sincere interest versus dismissive irony — is left open by
Bock rather than adjudicated. Text-critical notes attach to 26:15 (which adds "Nazorean,"
paralleling 22:8) and 26:28 (πείθεις taken as original).
