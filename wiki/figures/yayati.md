## Biographical Overview

Yayāti was an emperor of the Bharata race and an ancestor of the Pāṇḍavas. He never knew defeat in battle, followed the dictates of the śāstras, worshipped the gods, and was famous as a ruler devoted to his subjects' welfare (lines 1146–1149).

**The curse and the exchange of youth:** Yayāti had married [[devayani|Devayānī]], daughter of Śukrācārya, under unusual circumstances (pratiloma, i.e., a brahmin woman marrying a kṣatriya) — see the Devayānī page. In Yayāti's household, Śarmiṣṭhā (the asura princess who had attended Devayānī as her handmaiden) secretly asked Yayāti to also marry her and bear her children. Yayāti yielded and secretly married her. When Devayānī discovered this and complained to her father, Śukrācārya cursed Yayāti with premature old age in the very prime of his manhood (lines 1136–1138).

Humbled, Yayāti begged so sincerely for forgiveness that Śukrācārya softened the curse: the old age could not be recalled, but if any person were willing to take Yayāti's old age in exchange for his youth, the exchange would stand. Yayāti could then enjoy life in that borrowed youth (lines 1138–1144).

**The sons' refusals (Chapter VI):** Yayāti called each of his five sons in turn and asked for his youth. The four elder sons each refused in turn: the first feared the mockery of women and servants; the second feared losing wisdom along with youth; the third would be unable to ride horses or elephants; the fourth could not bear the indignities of old age such as depending on others for bodily cleanliness. Yayāti was struck with sorrow at each refusal (lines 1157–1184).

His youngest son Puru, "moved by filial love," willingly accepted: "Father, I gladly give you my youth and relieve you of the sorrows of old age and cares of State. Be happy." At Yayāti's touch, Yayāti became a youth and Puru took the old age. Puru ruled the kingdom with great renown during his father's absence (lines 1192–1198).

**Restitution and the moral of desire:** Yayāti spent long years enjoying life in Puru's borrowed youth, including time in the garden of Kubera with an apsarā. After all these years, the truth dawned on him that sensual desire is never quenched by indulgence — "No object of desire - corn, gold, cattle or women - nothing can ever satisfy the desire of man" — just as fire is never quenched by pouring ghee into it. He returned to Puru, restored his youth, and retired to the forest to spend his remaining years in austerity, eventually attaining heaven (lines 1199–1211).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Yayāti is presented as a parable within the epic's genealogy: he is an ancestor of the Pāṇḍavas (specifically through Puru's line), but the episode attached to him is not primarily dynastic; it is a moral exemplum on the nature of desire. Rajagopalachari frames the story with Yayāti's own concluding statement, which Rajagopalachari treats as a fundamental teaching: "We can reach peace only by a mental poise beyond likes and dislikes. Such is the state of Brahman" (lines 1206–1208). The story thus functions as a dharmic interlude illustrating the danger of unrestrained sensual craving, embedded within the ancestral history of the epic's main families.

The Yayāti episode also establishes Puru as the most righteous and filially devoted of the five sons, which explains why Yayāti made Puru king after recovering his old age rather than giving the throne to an elder son (line 1210). Puru's line carries the dynastic succession that leads eventually to the Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari treats Yayāti as a legendary ancestral figure. No historical-critical commentary is offered. The episode is consistent with broader Purāṇic accounts of Yayāti (not in Rajagopalachari's account), though Rajagopalachari's emphasis falls on the moral-philosophical teaching rather than the genealogical detail.
