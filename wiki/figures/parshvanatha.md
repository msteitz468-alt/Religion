## Biographical Overview

Pārśvanātha (commonly referred to as Pārśva) is the twenty-third [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] of the current cosmic era, predecessor and near-contemporary of [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]] in Jain tradition. "The historicity of Mahāvīra and his predecessor Pārśva alone of all the fordmakers is not in question" (Dundas, p. 20) — circumstantial evidence, including the description of his teachings in the oldest Jain texts, makes him a plausible historical figure.

The first description of Pārśva's life appears in the [[kalpasutra|Kalpasūtra]], which is "extremely short in extent and probably modelled on that of Mahāvīra" (Dundas, p. 28). It states that he was born in Benares (Vārāṇasī), renounced the world, founded a community of ascetics and lay people, lived one hundred years, and attained liberation on Mount Sammeta in the Ganges basin two hundred and fifty years prior to Mahāvīra (Dundas, p. 28). If the scholarly redating of Mahāvīra's death to around 425 BCE is accepted, this would place Pārśva in the eighth or seventh centuries BCE (Dundas, p. 28).

The Kalpasūtra does not formally link Pārśva to Mahāvīra in the text's ordering, placing his biography after that of the last fordmaker. However, a passing remark in Ācārāṅga 2 — that Mahāvīra's parents "were followers of Pārśva and lay devotees of Jain ascetics" (AS 2.15) — has led to the widespread scholarly conclusion that Mahāvīra "must have renounced within Pārśva's ascetic lineage" (Dundas, p. 28–29).

## Previous Life and the Cobra Canopy Legend

According to a famous story that does not appear in literary form until the eighth century CE, Pārśva in his previous birth saved a snake that was being burnt in a brahman's sacrificial fire. Reborn as the twenty-third fordmaker, Pārśva was attacked in meditation by the brahman — now reborn in demonic form — with fire and rocks. The snake, also reborn, this time as a mighty cobra prince named Dharaṇendra, "shielded Pārśva by spreading his hoods over his head" (Dundas, p. 30). This story encapsulates central Jain ethical themes: "compassion, non-violence, fellowship with all living creatures, rejection of the Vedic sacrifice and awareness of the fact that actions have consequences which bind individuals together" (Dundas, p. 30).

Images of Pārśva with a canopy of cobra hoods are attested from just before and after the common era, and historians point to these as "evidence for Jainism's early assimilation of popular snake cults" (Dundas, p. 30). Pārśva is the only fordmaker identifiable by image alone — without a name inscription — from this early period (Dundas, p. 41).

## Pārśva's Fourfold Restraint

Jain tradition holds that Pārśva and his ascetic community followed a **Fourfold Restraint** (Prakrit *caujjama*): abstention from violence, lying, taking what has not been given, and possession — as recorded in the Sthānāṅga (c. 2nd–3rd century CE; Sth 266). Mahāvīra, by contrast, is held to have preached **Five Great Vows** (*mahāvrata*), which add the avoidance of sexual relations to Pārśva's four (Dundas, pp. 28–29).

The Uttarādhyayana-sūtra (UttS 23) preserves a dialogue between Keśin (a follower of Pārśva) and Gautama (a disciple of Mahāvīra) debating which represents the true doctrine. Gautama's explanation: the discrepancy in outward appearance is explained by differences in the "moral and intellectual capabilities of the followers of the fordmakers." In the time of the first fordmaker, the doctrine was being preached for the first time and followers had difficulty understanding it; in Mahāvīra's time, moral and spiritual decline meant people had difficulty putting it into practice; thus both the first and last fordmakers formulated their teachings with the explicit prohibition on sexual relations, whereas the followers of the intermediate twenty-two fordmakers understood this prohibition as incorporated in the ban on possession (Dundas, pp. 30–31).

A further difference: "the male ascetic followers of Ṛṣabha and Mahāvīra were naked, while those of Pārśva and the other fordmakers wore clothes" (Dundas, p. 31). This has a direct historical parallel: Buddhist sources appear to "include Mahāvīra's naked followers in their ranks (but not the clothed followers of Pārśva)" (Dundas, p. 30).

Modern scholars have proposed that Pārśva's Fourfold Restraint actually related to the four modalities of the body (mind, body, speech, senses), and Mahāvīra's Great Vows were "simply slightly different articulations of the same basic ethical and sensory equanimity" (P. S. Jainī / P. K. Modi; discussed in Dundas, pp. 31–32). This explanation has merit but is criticised as deriving from an insistence on a formal link between the two fordmakers that the texts do not clearly sustain (Dundas, p. 32).

## Mahāvīra's Relationship to Pārśva

The 'Exposition of Explanations' (Bhagavatī) is the best source for the relationship between the two fordmakers. In one passage, Mahāvīra converts Pārśvite monks by enunciating cosmological views he attributes to Pārśva with respect, yet the conversion requires those monks to "abandon the Fourfold Restraint and take the five Great Vows" — the two codes are not presented as parallel expressions of one vow (Bh 5.9; Dundas, p. 32). Elsewhere, a description of the mechanism of rebirth is affirmed to be both based on Pārśva's teachings and independently established by Mahāvīra's omniscience (Bh 9.3; Dundas, p. 32).

Dundas's tentative synthesis: "early Jainism coalesced out of an interaction between the cosmological ideas of Pārśva and a more rigorous form of orthopraxy advocated by Mahāvīra, with the relationship between the two teachers eventually being formalised within the gradually evolving fordmaker lineage" (Dundas, p. 32). Later Jain writers did not view Pārśva's monks as precursors of their own tradition: from the Sūtrakṛtāṅga onward, the Pārśvites came to be viewed "as pseudo-ascetics who gained their livelihood from the dubious practices of magic and astrology" (Dundas, p. 32–33).

## Tradition-Specific Reception and Devotion

Whatever the historical and doctrinal debates, for the majority of Jains Pārśva's significance is devotional: "Pārśva, as the fordmaker who removes obstacles and has the capacity to save, is the greatest focus of devotional activity within the religion and is indeed the most popular of all the fordmakers, as a census of images in Jain temples throughout India would clearly demonstrate" (Dundas, pp. 30–31).

The cobra-canopy iconography is ubiquitous in Jain temple art and is the primary visual identifier of Pārśvanātha. He is invoked particularly for the removal of obstacles.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Dating**: The 250-year gap before Mahāvīra, combined with the scholarly redating controversy around Mahāvīra's death, creates uncertainty about Pārśva's dates. If Mahāvīra died around 425 BCE, Pārśva's death would fall around 675 BCE (8th century BCE).
- **Whether the two fordmakers were historically connected**: The most cautious position is that "it is impossible to be certain about the relationship between Mahāvīra and Pārśva and in actuality the chronological distance between the two teachers may have been much less than two and a half centuries" (Dundas, p. 32).
- **Status of Pārśvite monks in Jain history**: Once celebrated as predecessors, the Pārśvites were by medieval times demoted to quasi-ascetics of dubious practice — illustrating how the construction of fordmaker lineage shaped and sometimes distorted historical memory.
