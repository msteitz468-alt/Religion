# Gamaliel

## Biographical Overview

Gamaliel is the only rabbi named in [[acts|Acts]]. On [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s account he is "a [[pharisees|Pharisee]]... a teacher of the Law (νομοδιδάσκαλος) honored among all the people," a student of Hillel, and the teacher under whom [[paul-apostle|Paul]] studied (Acts 22:3). Bock identifies him as "Gamaliel the Elder" / Gamaliel I and cites the rabbinic tradition (m. Soṭah 9.15) that at his death "the glory of the law ceased." As a Pharisee, Bock notes, Gamaliel represents a party that "believed in oral tradition, resurrection, demons, God's sovereignty" — in contrast to the [[sadducees|Sadducees]] — and whom Luke "treats more positively" than the temple leadership.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 5:34–39** — rises in the [[sanhedrin|Sanhedrin]] to counsel restraint after Peter and the apostles are arrested; cites the failed movements of Theudas and Judas the Galilean; advises that if the movement "is of men it will self-destruct," but if "it is of God you cannot destroy it," lest the council "be found opponents of God."
- **Acts 22:3** — named (in Paul's own speech) as the teacher at whose feet Paul was "brought up... according to the strict manner of the law of our fathers."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's reading)

**The counsel of restraint (5:34–39).** [[darrell-bock|Bock]] calls Gamaliel's intervention "the first speech by a non-Christian in Acts" — a warning "not to act too fast" delivered by "a seeming opponent," which Bock parallels to Pilate declaring Jesus innocent. Bock analyzes the grammar closely: the "of men" option is a **third-class condition** (Gamaliel takes no firm position on it), while the "of God" option is framed with a **first-class condition** (εἰ), which "rhetorically" makes it "the more likely option per Luke." Bock adduces the rabbinic parallel of m. ʾAbot 4.11 ("any assembling for heaven's sake will endure") and Wis. 12:13–14.

**The precedents cited (5:36–37).** Bock reports the two rebel precedents Gamaliel names: **Theudas** (~400 men, killed, followers scattered) and **Judas the Galilean**, who arose "at the census" (~AD 6) leading a "zealous anti-taxation movement" (Josephus). Bock notes only Luke reports Judas's death.

**Bock's own verdict.** Bock argues the release miracle of 5:19–21 "already answered Gamaliel's options" — God is behind the movement — so that Gamaliel, "like other Pharisees, sees the truth but won't join." On Bock's reading, Gamaliel's principle "IS valid, read eschatologically."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

For Bock, Gamaliel functions in Luke's narrative as an unexpected witness from within the Jewish establishment: a respected Pharisaic teacher whose measured counsel implicitly concedes that the apostolic movement may be God's work, foreshadowing the very point Luke wants his readers to draw. Gamaliel also anchors the biography of [[paul-apostle|Paul]], establishing Paul's credentials as a rigorously trained Pharisee before his call.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Sound principle or Lukan irony?** Bock cites Lyons (1997) on the debate over whether Gamaliel's speech states a genuinely valid principle or is deployed by Luke with irony (Lyons applies it to the "Toronto blessing"). Bock argues against a purely ironic reading: Gamaliel's principle "IS valid, read eschatologically."
- **The Theudas chronology.** Bock notes the dating problem: Josephus (*Ant.* 20.5.1) places a Theudas under Fadus (AD 44–46), too late for Gamaliel's speech. Barrett sees Luke erring; Witherington and Bruce posit an earlier Theudas amid the turmoil after 4 BC. Bock records these as positions.
- **Pharisaic influence.** Bock notes the Sanders vs. Deines/Hengel debate on how influential the Pharisees actually were, with Deines and Hengel holding they were "most influential at the popular level."
