# Polycarp of Smyrna

## Biographical Overview

Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna (modern Izmir, Turkey) from approximately 100 CE until his martyrdom, traditionally dated to 155 CE (though 167-168 CE is also proposed). He is one of the most important figures of the Apostolic Fathers period, serving as a crucial link between the apostolic generation and the second-century church.

[[irenaeus|Irenaeus of Lyons]] — who as a young man heard Polycarp preach — records that Polycarp "had been instructed by the apostles and had conversed with many who had seen Christ, and had also been appointed by the apostles in Asia as bishop in the church of Smyrna" (*Haer.* 3.3.4). He mentions that Polycarp had been appointed by John (traditionally the apostle). This chain — Jesus → Apostle John → Polycarp → Irenaeus — gave the later church a claim to a traceable connection to the eyewitness generation.

In his old age Polycarp journeyed to Rome to confer with Bishop Anicetus, primarily about the Quartodeciman controversy (whether Easter should be celebrated on the 14th of Nisan or the following Sunday). Despite disagreement, the two parted in communion. Polycarp also opposed Marcion of Sinope — in a famous encounter, Polycarp reportedly called Marcion "the firstborn of Satan" when Marcion asked if he recognized him.

## Primary Texts

**Primary Greek text**: The Greek text of Polycarp's letter and the *Martyrdom* are available in [[holmes-apostolic-fathers|Holmes, *The Apostolic Fathers* (3rd ed., 2007)]], which provides the critical Greek text with English translation and apparatus. The letter survives in Greek primarily via Eusebius' quotations (chs. 9, 12, 13) and in a complete Latin translation; the full Greek is preserved in a 13th-century manuscript group (the "Pseudo-Ignatian" corpus). The *Martyrdom* survives in five Greek MSS plus a Latin translation.

### Epistle to the Philippians (c.107–110 CE)

Polycarp's letter to the Philippians is his only surviving writing. It was written at the request of the Philippians (who had asked for copies of Ignatius' letters) shortly after Ignatius' martyrdom. Key features:
- Exhorts the Philippians to submit to "the presbyters and deacons" — notably no bishop is mentioned, suggesting Philippi still had plural elder governance at this time
- Quotes heavily from NT texts — Paul's letters (Romans, 1-2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, 2 Thessalonians, 1-2 Timothy), 1 Peter, 1 John, Acts, and the Gospels
- Explicitly cites **Ephesians as "Scripture"**: "as it is written in these Scriptures, Be angry and sin not" (*Phil.* 12.1) — this is the earliest surviving explicit scriptural citation formula (*graphai*) applied to a Pauline letter
- Combats early forms of Docetism: "Everyone who does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is an antichrist" (*Phil.* 7.1 — echoing 1 John)

**Holmes' two-letter theory**: Holmes argues that what we call the "Epistle to the Philippians" is actually two letters combined — an earlier short covering letter (chs. 13–14) sent with Ignatius' letters shortly after the martyrdom, and a longer letter (chs. 1–12) sent later after Polycarp received news that Valens, a presbyter at Philippi, had fallen into financial misconduct. The two letters were concatenated when copied, producing our current document. This is a minority view (Hartog and others defend a single letter), but it explains the awkward structure of the document as we have it.

### Martyrdom of Polycarp (ca. 155–160 CE)

The *Martyrdom of Polycarp* is the earliest extant Christian martyrdom narrative outside the NT, written shortly after his death by the church of Smyrna. Key features:
- Polycarp is 86 years old when martyred: "For eighty-six years I have been his servant, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" — this statement implies he became a Christian in infancy or very early childhood, which would be consistent with an apostolic-era connection
- He is arrested, brought before the proconsul, and given the chance to recant; he refuses
- He is burned at the stake; according to the account, the flames form an arch around him; a soldier stabs him with a sword
- The account is notable for: formal martyrdom vocabulary (*martyria*); emphasis on Polycarp as a "type of Christ" (*typos Christou*) — like Christ, he is betrayed, enters the city on a donkey, arrested on a Friday; it explicitly states this typological connection
- The account is sometimes called the first hagiography in Christian history

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Catholic / Orthodox Tradition

Polycarp is venerated as a martyr and Father of the Church (feast: February 23). His connection to John the Apostle is valued as an authentic link to the apostolic generation. The *Martyrdom* is the paradigmatic text of the martyrdom genre.

### Protestant Tradition

Polycarp is valued primarily as a canon witness — his explicit citation of Pauline letters as Scripture (*graphai*) is important evidence for the early emergence of a NT canon. His letter to the Philippians is extensively mined in canon studies.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### Canon Evidence

Polycarp's explicit use of the scriptural formula on a Pauline letter (*Epistle to the Philippians*, citing Ephesians as "these Scriptures") is the earliest clear instance of a Pauline letter being cited with the same formula used for OT Scripture. This is crucial evidence in the [[nt-canon-formation|NT canon formation]] debate: it places canonical thinking about Paul in the early second century, predating Marcion.

His letter also demonstrates familiarity with: at least 6-8 Pauline letters; 1 Peter; 1 John; and Gospel traditions — all treated as authoritative. The density of NT citation in such a short letter reflects a community for whom these texts were already central.

### Ecclesiology

Polycarp's letter to Philippi addresses "presbyters" — plural elder governance. This is in contrast to [[ignatius-of-antioch|Ignatius']] letters, written at the same time, which consistently advocate for a singular bishop. The coexistence of both patterns c.110 CE in different churches is important evidence that the [[monepiscopate|monepiscopate]] was still a developing, contested structure rather than a universal norm.

Polycarp himself, as bishop of Smyrna, represents a transitional figure — a single prominent bishop, but his own letter does not use the language of Ignatian episcopacy.

### The Apostolic Chain

Irenaeus' emphasis on the Polycarp-John connection establishes the concept of "apostolic tradition" as both personal and traceable. The tradition is not merely an abstract theological deposit; it was transmitted through named, known individuals. This personal-chain argument became central to Irenaeus' anti-gnostic strategy: the gnostics claimed secret apostolic tradition, but no one had ever *heard* their alleged chains of transmission.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Date of martyrdom**: Traditional date is 155/156 CE (under proconsul Statius Quadratus); some scholars (Hartog, Dehandschutter) argue for 167-168 CE under Marcus Aurelius. Both dates have supporting arguments; 155 CE remains the majority view.

**The John connection**: Irenaeus claims Polycarp was appointed by "the apostles" and knew John. This is plausible if John lived into the 90s CE (as tradition holds); it cannot be independently confirmed. The tradition is consistent across multiple sources (Irenaeus, Eusebius, Jerome).

**The *Martyrdom* text**: Contains some legendary accretion (the fire forming an arch; Polycarp's blood quenching the flames; the typological detail about the entry into the city). Scholars generally accept the core narrative as historical while noting the hagiographic overlay.

## Influence on Commentary Tradition

Polycarp's letter to the Philippians and the *Martyrdom* became model texts — the letter as a practical pastoral epistle drawing heavily on Paul, the *Martyrdom* as the template for martyr accounts. Polycarp's connection to both Ignatius (whom he collected) and Irenaeus (his spiritual descendant) made him a nodal figure in the preservation of second-century Christianity's documentary record.
