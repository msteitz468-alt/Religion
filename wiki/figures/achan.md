Achan son of Carmi, of the tribe of Judah, takes devoted things from [[jericho|Jericho]] in
violation of the *[[herem|ḥerem]]*, causing [[israelites|Israel]]'s defeat at [[ai|Ai]] and dying
with his household in the Valley of Achor ([[book-of-joshua|Joshua]] 7). His episode is the
Conquest narrative's central case of covenant trespass from within.

## Biographical Overview

Joshua 7 opens with the verdict before the story: the Israelites broke faith (*maʿal*) in regard
to the devoted things, for Achan son of Carmi son of Zabdi son of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah,
took some of them — and the anger of the LORD burned against Israel. The attack on Ai then fails;
about thirty-six Israelites are killed, and the people's hearts melt like water. Joshua tears his
clothes and lies before the ark until evening, and the LORD tells him Israel has sinned, has
transgressed the covenant, has taken of the devoted things, and can no longer stand before its
enemies until the devoted thing is destroyed from among them (7:1–13).

The people are sanctified and brought near by lot — tribe, clan, household, man — narrowing to
Achan. Joshua tells him to give glory to the LORD and confess. Achan confesses: he saw among the
spoil a beautiful mantle from Shinar, two hundred shekels of silver, and a bar of gold weighing
fifty shekels; he coveted them and took them, and they are hidden in the earth inside his tent
with the silver underneath. Messengers find them and lay them out before the LORD (7:16–23).

Joshua and all Israel take Achan, the silver, the mantle, the bar of gold, **his sons and his
daughters, his oxen, his donkeys, his sheep, his tent, and everything he had**, and bring them up
to the Valley of Achor. All Israel **stones them and burns them**, and raises over him a great heap
of stones that remains to this day; the place is named the Valley of Achor ("trouble"), and the
LORD turns from his burning anger (7:24–26). Ai is then taken by ambush, and unlike Jericho its
livestock and spoil are taken as plunder (8:1–29).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Joshua 7:1–26** — the trespass, the defeat at Ai, Joshua's complaint, the casting of lots, the
  confession, and the execution and burning in the Valley of Achor.
- **Joshua 22:20** — [[phinehas|Phinehas]] and the ten chiefs invoke "Achan son of Zerah" as
  precedent when confronting the eastern tribes over the altar at Geliloth: he broke faith in the
  matter of the devoted thing, and wrath fell on all Israel.
- **1 Chronicles 2:7** — "Achar, the troubler of Israel, who broke faith in the matter of the
  devoted thing," in the Judahite genealogy.
- **Hosea 2:15** — the Valley of Achor made "a door of hope," a reversal of the place-name.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **[[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] (NICOT, 1981)** supplies this wiki's fullest reading. On Josh
  7:1: the offense term ***māʿal*** ("trespass") literally means "to act under cover," hence
  treacherously — a **breach of trust** against the LORD by purloining what was sanctified (cf. Lev
  5:15). **Corporate solidarity and individual responsibility go hand in hand**: one man offends,
  yet the whole people are viewed as trespassing; Achan robbed the nation of its purity and
  holiness before God, and "folly in Israel" (7:15) is corporate language. By partaking of the
  accursed, Israel itself became accursed (7:12). The sin is named as **breach of covenant**
  (7:11, 15), and its root process is **coveting** (Exod 20:17) of the Shinar robe, the silver, and
  the gold. The execution in the Valley of Achor is **not vindictiveness but covenant retribution**;
  the family's fate may reflect their common knowledge, the goods being in the tent; and
  conclusions about eternal destiny "had better not be made rashly."
- **Woudstra, book-level structure**: the same *maʿal* vocabulary marks **actual** corporate
  trespass after the first city (ch. 7) and **potential** trespass after the conquest cycle in the
  eastern-altar controversy (22:16, 20) — a frame teaching that the promised land can be truly
  possessed only if the people adhere to God's commandments. On Jericho, he stresses that the
  metals reserved for the treasury are what explain the Achan story: Israel must keep clean of
  accursed things or bring the curse on the camp. Woudstra also rejects unravelling Ai and Achan
  into independent Judahite/Benjaminite tales.
- **Christian typological use (via Woudstra)**: several commentators draw a parallel between Achan
  and Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:1–11) — concealment of goods belonging to God, sudden death, at
  the inauguration of a new community. Woudstra notes the parallel, and also the parallel with the
  expulsion in 1 Cor 5:1–11, while **cautioning against collapsing the distinction between the old
  and new dispensations**.
- **Jewish reception** (as recorded on the [[book-of-joshua|Joshua]] page): some rabbinic opinions
  grant Achan a share in the world to come after his confession. No rabbinic source has been
  ingested directly in this collection; this is recorded as a reception note, thinly attested here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Achan is the counterpart to [[rahab|Rahab]]: the Canaanite woman who confesses the LORD and is
spared, and the Judahite who breaks faith and is destroyed. As recorded on the [[herem|ḥerem]]
page, the episode establishes the ban as a **two-edged category** — Israel can violate it from
within just as the Canaanites violated it from without — and makes holiness, not military
capacity, the condition of Israel's standing before its enemies.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The archaeological silence at Ai (commonly identified with et-Tell) is treated by
[[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] with the same caution he applies to Jericho: site identification is
fragile and silence does not overturn the book's prophetical-historiographic claim. Two live
tensions attach to the episode and are recorded without adjudication here: the **corporate
punishment** of Achan's sons, daughters, livestock, and household alongside himself, which
Woudstra treats as covenant retribution possibly reflecting family complicity; and the broader
ethics of the *ḥerem*, treated on [[herem-canaanite-conquest|the ḥerem controversy page]] where
Woudstra's judicial-judgment framing (with Gen 15:16) stands as one position among others.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Achan anchors the *maʿal* / holy-people theme that [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] makes structural
for [[book-of-joshua|Joshua]], and supplies the standing Old Testament analogue in Christian
discussions of communal purity and judgment (Ananias and Sapphira; 1 Cor 5). Cross-links:
