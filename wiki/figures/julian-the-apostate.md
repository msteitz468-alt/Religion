# Julian the Apostate

See also: [[julian-the-apostate|Julian the Apostate (commentator page)]]

## Biographical Overview

Julian was born in 331 CE, the son of Julius Constantius (half brother of Constantine) and Basilina, a wealthy woman from Bithynia (rel. lines ~85–86). His world was shattered when Constantine died in 337 CE; Constantius, the strongest of Constantine's three sons, "proceeded to murder Julian's father and eight of his relatives. Only Julian and his half brother Gallus were spared" (rel. lines ~91–93).

At age six Julian was sent to Nicomedia, placed in the charge of his maternal grandmother and a Christian bishop named Eusebius, where he received his first instruction in Homer and Hesiod alongside Christian learning (rel. lines ~94–98). In his early teens he was transferred, with his brother Gallus, to an imperial estate in Cappadocia and "lived there in complete isolation from society for the next six years" (rel. lines ~103–105), studying rhetoric and philosophy.

Around age eighteen he began rhetorical studies under Nicocles (pagan) and Hecebolius (Christian), then moved to Nicomedia to study under Libanius of Antioch (rel. lines ~107–111). His philosophical studies led him to Pergamum and the Neoplatonist Aedesius, a disciple of Iamblichus, who "emphasized the religious and ritualistic elements of the Neoplatonic school" (rel. line ~115–116). Through Aedesius's student Eusebius he heard of Maximus of Ephesus, a philosopher known for theurgic feats, and immediately sought him out (rel. lines ~146–150). Maximus initiated him first into the cult of Cybele, then into the cult of Mithras (rel. lines ~153–157).

Julian dated his conversion away from Christianity to age twenty under Maximus's influence (rel. line ~193). For the following ten years, as his cousin Emperor Constantius was a Christian, "he continued to hide his new faith and to go through the motions of Christian ritual. On occasion he even served as lector in the church" (rel. lines ~210–212).

Constantius summoned Julian to Gaul as Caesar to address threats from Germanic tribes; Julian proved "a bookish" man who nonetheless "won fame as a military leader" (rel. lines ~218–219), subduing the Franks and restoring security to the Rhine frontier (rel. lines ~220–221). When Constantius ordered Julian to send troops east, Julian's army refused and proclaimed him Augustus in Paris in February 360 CE (rel. lines ~225–226). As Julian marched east to confront Constantius, the emperor died on 3 November 361 CE (rel. lines ~230–231).

Julian was now sole emperor. He immediately declared his paganism openly, writing to Maximus: "We worship the gods openly … We sacrifice oxen in public … The gods command me to purify everything as far as possible" (*Ep.* 8; rel. lines ~234–238).

## Primary Source Appearances

Julian is not a figure *within* the religious texts studied in this wiki; he is a historical figure who engaged those texts polemically. His primary work is *Against the Galilaeans* (*Contra Galilaeos*), written winter 362–63 CE in Antioch. The work no longer survives independently; it must be reconstructed from Cyril of Alexandria's *Contra Julianum* (c. 440 CE). Julian also wrote numerous letters and orations that provide additional evidence of his religious views.

For his interpretation of biblical texts (Genesis, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Daniel, Matthew, Luke, John, Paul's letters), see the [[julian-the-apostate|commentator page]].

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Christian Reception: "the Apostate"

The epithet "the Apostate" is Christian in origin. As Wilken notes, "in the historical tradition of the West, shaped as it has been by Christianity, Julian earned the name Apostate … for he committed the unpardonable sin: raised as a Christian, he later forsook his hereditary piety … to embrace paganism with enthusiasm" (rel. lines ~14–17).

Contemporary Christian responses were fierce. Gregory Nazianzus — once a personal friend and fellow student under Libanius — composed "two bitter invectives against him," calling Julian "the dragon, the apostate, the great mind, the Assyrian, the public and private enemy of all in common" (*Or.* 4.1; rel. lines ~32–36). Gregory charged Julian with acting as though "the Greek language belonged to religion exclusively and not to the tongue" (*Or.* 4.5; rel. lines ~378–382) and warned that if the Temple were rebuilt it would "restore the authority of the ancient tradition" (*Or.* 3.5; rel. lines ~1109–1110).

John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch in 386 CE (more than twenty years after Julian's death), still had to contend with Jews emboldened by the Temple episode, saying they were "boasting that they will get back their city again" (*Jud.* 7.1; rel. lines ~1114–1115).

The medieval chronicler Michael the Syrian wrote of Julian: "May his very memory be a curse! Amen!" (rel. lines ~747–748).

Cyril of Alexandria, writing around 440 CE, identified Julian as especially dangerous precisely because of his insider knowledge: Julian "before he became emperor … was worthy of holy Baptism, and he was trained in the Holy Scriptures" (PG 76.508c; rel. lines ~470–472). Yet Cyril also admitted Julian's books "had disturbed many and done much harm" and that pagans were circulating them with the taunt that "none of our teachers is capable of rebutting or refuting his ideas" (PG 76.508c; rel. lines ~476–486).

### Pagan Reception

The great pagan rhetor Libanius — teacher of both Gregory Nazianzus and Julian — mourned his passing in extravagant terms: "Gone is the glory of the good; the company of the wicked and the licentious is uplifted" (*Or.* 17.1–2; rel. lines ~46–48). For Libanius, Julian was a noble defender of classical culture. The Princeton classicist Glenn Bowersock and the British historian Robert Browning both produced modern biographies of Julian (rel. lines ~12–13), reflecting his continuing fascination.

The modern Greek poet C. P. Cavafy made Julian the basis for a series of poems (rel. lines ~9–11), and Gore Vidal wrote a best-selling novel on him (rel. lines ~7–9).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Julian's significance for the history of religion lies in his combination of political power, philosophical sophistication, and Christian insider knowledge deployed against Christianity. Wilken's assessment:

> "Julian saw clearly what others had grasped but dimly — that Christianity had an irrevocable tie to Judaism and that its deviation from the mother religion had left it with a permanently bad conscience." (rel. lines ~1163–1165)

He was "the first 'convert' to paganism" in the sense of a conscious, passionate, personal embrace of the traditional gods — neither the cultural conservatism of Libanius nor the civic religion of Symmachus, but "all the signs of an exuberant convert" (rel. lines ~255–257, ~283–285).

His attempt to rebuild the Jerusalem Temple was "the final, and most brilliant, stroke in the ancient conflict between paganism and Christianity" (rel. lines ~1128–1129), because it sought to transform a literary argument (Christianity is an apostasy from Judaism) into a historical fact (Judaism continues, the Temple stands, Jesus' prophecy fails).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Julian's death on the Persian campaign in June 363 CE ended the pagan reaction. The date of birth is given by Wilken as "331 C.E." (rel. line ~85). The circumstances of the construction halt at the Temple site are disputed in ancient sources: Ammianus Marcellinus (a pagan) attributed it to balls of fire from beneath the foundations (*Amm. Marc.* 23.1.2–3); Christian historians claimed miraculous fire from heaven (rel. lines ~1093–1098).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Julian's *Contra Galilaeos* shaped the development of Christian apologetics. Cyril of Alexandria's massive refutation (twelve books) devoted enormous energy to rebutting him. Wilken's larger thesis — the Epilogue argument — is that critics like Celsus, Porphyry, and Julian "helped Christians to find their authentic voice, and without them Christianity would have been the poorer" (rel. lines ~1440–1441). The doctrine of creation *ex nihilo*, the Christian adoption of the term "piety," the defense of historical revelation against philosophical universalism — all took shape partly in response to such critics.
