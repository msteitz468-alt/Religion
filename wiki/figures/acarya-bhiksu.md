# Acarya Bhiksu

**Acarya Bhikṣu** (Rajasthani: *Bhikhanji*; 1726–1803) is the founder of the [[terapanth|Terapanth]] sect of Śvetāmbara Jainism and is, Dundas notes, "one of the best-documented figures in the history of Jainism" — an assessment owed to the Terapanthīs' meticulous preservation of records of his life, teachings, itinerary, and extensive anecdotes (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Biographical Overview

Bhikṣu was born in 1726 in the **desert region of Marwar, Rajasthan**. He belonged to the **Bisa Osvāl merchant caste**, which remains the dominant caste among Terapanth adherents. Dundas notes that "the rugged and often harsh nature of the landscape of this area imparted something of itself to the uncompromising and vigorous Jainism which he preached" (ch. 9), and the Terapanth has retained a particularly Rajasthani, indeed Marwari, character.

His family had a history of renunciation, and in 1751 he took ascetic initiation from a leading Sthānakvāsī *ācārya* called **Raghunāthji**. Over eight years with Raghunāthji, Bhikṣu accumulated multiple objections:
- Sthānakvāsī ascetics lived permanently in lodging-houses built specially for them, took food from the same families every day, and compelled laity to receive initiation from them exclusively.
- Monks showed no serious interest in their vows or in the quality of potential recruits.
- The community had become so distanced from its origins that "it even saw no wrong in possessing money" (Dundas, ch. 9).

The catalyst for final separation was a lay community's complaint about the authenticity of current ascetic practice. After studying the scriptures intensely during *cāturmāsa*, Bhikṣu concluded "Jain monks had forgotten the path taught by Mahāvīra." On Raghunāthji's refusal to verify this from scripture, Bhikṣu formally broke away in **1759** by terminating his commensal relationship (*sambhoga*), reinitiated himself, and left with six monks.

He died in **1803** after 44 years of mendicant life in Marwar and Mewar, having initiated 56 nuns and 49 monks.

## Primary Source Appearances

Bhikṣu produced no scholarly treatises in the traditional sense but was extensively quoted and documented by his followers. His teachings are preserved in preaching records and in the *maryādā* documents he issued from 1772 to 1794. The Terapanthi hagiographic tradition also preserves anecdotes about him in abundance.

Key scriptural proof-text: *Daśavaikālika Sūtra* 6.9 — "the first thing which Mahāvīra had taught, non-violence, involved restraint towards all creatures" — which Bhikṣu used as the basis for his rigorous doctrine of self-discipline (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Doctrinal Positions

**Non-violence as the essence.** Bhikṣu held that Jainism's core was not compassion, giving, or merit-making but "continual self-discipline, based on a correct understanding of the nature of life-forms, which alone could stop karma being bound to the soul and eventually effect spiritual release" (Dundas, ch. 9). He viewed any act of violence as bad irrespective of necessity or intent: "a one-sensed creature would be no more happy about being robbed of its life for the good of the many than anybody would be to be robbed of his coat so that it could be given to someone else" (ch. 9).

**Laukika vs. lokottara distinction.** Bhikṣu made a sharp distinction between the worldly (*laukika*) realm — which included virtually all merit-directed religious practice commonly followed by Jains — and the transcendent (*lokottara*) religious sphere. The two had no connection. Charity and buying animals from butchers to free them were purely social acts, not *lokottara*. Genuine non-violence "must be conjoined with correct understanding and ascetic restraint" (Dundas, ch. 9). This made his brand of Jainism particularly severe compared to other sects.

**Total commitment.** When his teacher Raghunāthji argued that in the corrupt world age, anyone who maintained fully correct ascetic behaviour for even an hour would become omniscient, Bhikṣu "mockingly retorted that if that was the way to achieve the goal, he would sit and hold his breath for that period" (Dundas, ch. 9). For Bhikṣu, nothing less than total commitment was possible.

## The Maryada: Single-Acarya Structure

Bhikṣu's most distinctive institutional innovation was the *maryādā* — a fixed code of practice for Terapanthi ascetics — framed to prevent the fission and emergence of rival lineages he blamed for the corruption of Jainism. Its core principle: **total subordination of all ascetics to the single** ***ācārya***, who alone administers discipline, appoints his successor, grants initiation, and assigns each ascetic his itinerary and rain-retreat location (Dundas, ch. 9). Bhikṣu issued a series of *maryādā*s from 1772 to 1794; the fourth *ācārya* Jaya condensed them into a daily-read rule.

## Historical Significance and Succession

The Terapanth community was formally constituted in 1764. Bhikṣu's successor and closest disciple was Bharimaljī, compared by Terapanthi tradition to Gautama. Of the nine *ācāryas* who succeeded Bhikṣu, Dundas identifies **Acarya Tulsi** (1914–97; ninth *ācārya*, 1936–94) as the most significant in modern times, presiding over the sect's post-independence expansion, the founding of the first Jain university at Lādnūm, the **Anuvrat movement** (1949), and the eventual creation of lower-order ascetics called *samaṇas* and *samaṇīs* who can travel abroad by mechanical transport.
