Agur son of Jakeh is the named author of **Collection VI** of Proverbs — the discrete
autobiographical-and-numerical collection at [[proverbs|Proverbs]] 30:1–33, headed "The sayings
of Agur son of Jakeh." He appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible and is otherwise unknown. He
is a figure page (not merely a concept) because his significance is authorial and biographical:
a sage who frames his teaching as a personal confession, claims prophetic inspiration for it,
and addresses it to a named hearer.

## Biographical Overview

The primary datum is the superscription (30:1) and the confession that follows. Everything
below distinguishes what the text states from the reconstructions of the wiki's ingested
commentator, [[bruce-waltke|Bruce K. Waltke]] (NICOT).

- **"Otherwise unknown."** Waltke reads the name as a straightforward proper name — "the
  formula, proper noun + 'son of' + proper noun, occurs over 1500x in the Old Testament" — and
  declines to allegorize it. He notes that "'Agur' and 'Jakeh' show up as proper personal names
  in the cognate Semitic languages," which "may suggest he is a proselyte to Israel's faith."
- **Proselyte hypothesis (Waltke, attributed).** Waltke repeatedly frames Agur as a **Gentile
  proselyte**: a sage who "only learned [wisdom] when he accepted Israel's spiritual and
  intellectual inheritance from men like Moses, David and presumably from Solomon," having
  failed to attain it under "his pagan teacher." On internal evidence "Agur cannot be dated"
  (even if 30:4 parodies Job 38–41).
- **Ithiel, the addressee.** Agur "addresses his inspired saying to Ithiel" (a real personal
  name, attested Neh 11:7), but "in its canonical context the editor of Proverbs addresses the
  universal people of God" — Ithiel "represents all Israel."

## Primary Source Appearances

Agur appears only in Proverbs 30. Waltke reads the chapter as a unity in three parts:

1. **Autobiographical confession (30:1–9).** A superscription (v.1) craftily "sewn into the
   confession in a janus verset" that switches from third to first person; then a confession
   that his sayings are inspired (vv.2–6) and two petitions (vv.7–9).
2. **Seven numerical sayings (30:10–31).** The graded "*x, x+1*" sayings — the leech's two
   daughters ("Give! Give!") and the four insatiables (Sheol, barren womb, earth, fire; vv.15–16);
   the four things "too wonderful" (eagle in the sky, serpent on rock, ship at sea, a man with a
   maiden; vv.18–19, with the adulteress as counter-image, v.20); four things the earth cannot
   bear (vv.21–23); four "small but exceedingly wise" (ants, rock-badgers, locusts, wall-lizard;
   vv.24–28); four "stately in stride" (lion, strutting cock/he-goat, and a king; vv.29–31).
3. **Concluding warning (30:32–33).** Churning milk yields butter, wringing the nose blood, and
   pressing anger strife — a caution against stirring up conflict.

Verse 10 functions as a **janus** (its "I–thou" continues the autobiography; the catchword
"curse" binds it to v.11) and, with 30:32–33, forms an **inclusio** framing the numerical
sayings.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

- **Claim to inspiration — the biographical core.** Agur "represents his sayings … as inspired
  (i.e., divine-human speech, originating with God and invested with his authority) by placing
  the prophetic genre term *oracle* (or 'burden,' *maśśāʾ*) in apposition to the wisdom genre
  term 'sayings.'" Waltke: his claim "matches Solomon's claim for the inspiration of his words"
  (2:6). The phrase *neʾûm haggeber* ("the inspired utterance of the man") is decisive: of the
  ten non-*neʾûm-YHWH* occurrences of *neʾûm*, six are Balaam's oracles and two David's inspired
  hymns; "both Balaam and David use exactly the same formula as Agur." Waltke follows R. Moore in
  seeing "a striking confluence of sapient and prophetic traditions," against McKane (who calls
  the claim "very odd") and Whybray.
- **Epistemology: agnosticism resolved by revelation (30:2–6).** Waltke reads the confession as
  moving "from human ignorance of wisdom to the possession of it" (paralleling [[book-of-job|Job]]
  28:12–28 and Baruch 3:29–4:1). On his own the sage "cannot attain to wisdom (vv.1b–4) but by
  God's revelation of it he can (vv.5–6)." The four "who has ascended?" questions (v.4) answer
  "No human being, but only God." Waltke uses the confession as a Reformed epistemology polemic —
  "his confession rebuts the rationalism of the so-called 'Enlightenment'" — citing Van Til and
  Blocher that "nothing can be truly interpreted independently of God." This is the same
  anti–natural-theology point the [[proverbs|Proverbs]] page and [[fear-of-the-lord|fear of the
  LORD]] ground in special revelation.
- **"What is his son's name?" (30:4b).** In Proverbs "son" is the son the father teaches; the
  OT answer, Waltke argues, is **Israel**, God's firstborn (Exod 4:22). His **typological**
  reading: "In the New Testament Jesus Christ fulfills typical Israel … the incarnate Son of God
  … the eternal Son of God (John 17)."
- **Sufficiency and purity of God's word (30:5–6).** "Every word of God is purified; he is a
  shield to those who take refuge in him." Verse 5 adapts David (Ps 18:30); v.6 quotes the
  **canonical formula** "Do not add to his word" (Deut 4:2; 12:32). Waltke: Agur "asserts the
  canonical status of his sayings," referring to them "as part of a developing canon."
- **The golden mean (30:7–9) — the only prayer in Proverbs.** "Give me neither poverty nor
  riches; feed me my apportioned bread." Wealth risks pride and apostasy ("Who is the LORD?"),
  poverty risks theft and profaning God's name. Waltke notes R. Byargeon's argument that the
  Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9–13) echoes Agur's prayer in structure and theology.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The 30:1b crux ("Ithiel" vs. "I am weary, O God").** MT reads "to Ithiel, to Ithiel and
  Ucal." Waltke redivides the consonants to *lāʾîtî ʾēl* = "**I am weary, O God**" + "but I can
  prevail," arguing *ʾūkāl* "is a verb and never attested as a proper name," and that "the
  transparent pun … readily explains the confusion in MT."
- **The blasphemy-emendation (Torrey, Scott, Crenshaw), rejected.** These critics read a scribal
  "cover-up" of a skeptic's denial ("there is no God … I am not able") and split the chapter into
  a skeptic (vv.1–4) answered by an orthodox voice (vv.5–6). Waltke rejects this: "nothing in the
  text … suggests a change of speaker," and it "dismiss[es] too easily Agur's claim of
  inspiration." (Position recorded, not adjudicated.)
- **The Massaite reading.** Delitzsch and others emend *maśśāʾ* ("oracle") to *mimmassāʾ* ("from
  Massa," a NW Arabian district) or "the Massaite," thinking a prophetic term unfitting in
  wisdom. Waltke keeps MT, arguing *neʾûm haggeber* is itself prophetic and re-enforces it.
- **The Solomonic-authorship "fiction."** The **LXX** relocates Prov 30–31 (splitting 30:1–14 and
  30:15–31:9) to keep the whole book under Solomon's name; the **Midrash on Proverbs** allegorizes
  the name ("Agur — he who girded his loins for wisdom"). Waltke treats both as secondary attempts
  to preserve Solomonic authorship against the plain sense that Agur is a distinct author. See
  [[solomon|Solomon]].

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Agur's confession is a locus classicus for the wisdom tradition's self-limitation — the sage who
begins by confessing he has *not* attained wisdom. Waltke reads the numerical sayings as
teaching "subordination to authority to retain cosmic and social order." Whether Prov 30 forms a
single collection of thirty-plus sayings or a looser anthology remains debated; Waltke argues for
its unity ("scholarly differences over the chapter's division attest the chapter's unity, not its
disunity").
