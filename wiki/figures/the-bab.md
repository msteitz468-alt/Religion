# The Báb

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

Sayyid 'Alí-Muhammad, the Báb, was born in the southern Iranian city of Shiraz on 20 October 1819 (Smith gives this date; note the frontmatter above follows the traditional dating of 1817 — Smith's text at line 419 gives 1819) into a family of traders and merchants. His father, Sayyid Muhammad-Ridá, died while the Báb was still a child (c.1826) and he was brought up by his mother, Fátima Bagum, and under the guardianship of her brother, Hájí Mírzá Sayyid 'Alí. His family claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad, hence the title *sayyid*. He was an only child.

As a boy, 'Alí-Muhammad attended a local Quranic school for elementary education and received commercial training from his family. At fifteen he began work in his uncle's business in the port city of Bushihr, later setting up as an independent merchant.

Several accounts emphasize the boy's extreme piety. By the time he was in Bushihr, religious concerns were of primary importance to him. Around 1841 he felt impelled to close his office and embark on an extended pilgrimage to the Shi'i shrine cities in Iraq, where his fervent devotion attracted attention from individuals who later became his disciples. He returned to Shiraz and in August 1842 married a cousin, Khadíjih. The couple's only child died in infancy in 1843.

His extreme piety and asceticism attracted a growing reputation for holiness. By his own account he now experienced visionary dreams: in April 1844 he saw the severed head of the Imam Husayn, "drops of whose blood he drank and whose grace he thus imbibed. He felt as if the spirit of God had 'permeated' and 'taken possession' of his soul" (lines 448–451, citing Nabil 253). He also began to write on religious themes — "a highly unusual activity for someone who had not received a specialized clerical education" (lines 452–453).

**Declaration of mission:** This growing consciousness culminated in the Báb's declaration of mission in May 1844. The key event was the conversion of the prominent young Shaykhi, Mullá Husayn Bushrú'í (c.1813/14–49), on the evening of 22 May 1844. Not only did 'Alí-Muhammad now claim to bear a divine mission, but he had gained his first disciple. His mission commenced in the year AH 1260 (1844), the thousandth anniversary of the disappearance of the Twelfth Imam in AH 260 — a detail that accentuated millenarian sentiment (lines 520–523).

**Imprisonment:** The Báb was placed under house arrest in Shiraz upon his return from Mecca (July 1845). He was induced to disclaim publicly that he was the báb of the Imam, but in Shi'i terms this denial under duress could be accepted as pious dissimulation (*taqiyya*) and did not affect his higher claims. He left Shiraz in September 1846 amid a cholera outbreak, traveled to Isfahan as the guest of the governor Manúchihr Khán, but lost his protector when Manúchihr Khán died (February 1847). He was then transferred under armed escort toward Tehran but redirected to the remote prison fortress of **Maku**, near Iran's north-western border, where he remained from summer 1847 until spring 1848. He was then transferred to the even more remote fortress of **Chihriq** (April 1848), under stricter confinement, though he continued to communicate with followers through particular disciples.

**Tabriz trial (July 1848):** The Báb was brought before a religious tribunal in Tabriz, the capital of Iranian Azerbaijan, in the presence of the crown prince. He openly announced his claim to be the return of the Imam Mahdi. The assembled clerics (all Shaykhis) sought to ridicule him and faulted his grammar. A religious fatwa declared him an apostate but granted a reprieve from execution if there was doubt as to his sanity. He was given the bastinado (beaten on the soles of the feet) in part to humiliate him. He was returned to Chihriq.

**Execution:** Faced with continuing Babi armed resistance at Zanjan, Chief Minister Mirzá Taqí Khán ordered the Báb's execution. On 8 or 9 July 1850 the Báb was shot before a large crowd in Tabriz. He and one disciple were tied together and suspended from a wall. A first regiment of Armenian Christians apparently aimed to miss, reluctant to shed the blood of a holy man, and the two were unscathed. A second Muslim regiment completed the task. "The strangeness of these events ensured that even in death, the Báb's aura of holiness was preserved" (lines 1200–1201). His body and that of his disciple were cast into a ditch but later carried away by a Babi. The remains were eventually smuggled to Iran and buried in the shrine on Mount Carmel in Haifa, completed under 'Abdu'l-Bahá's direction.

## Primary Source Appearances

Smith's account draws on the following texts where the Báb features:

- **Qayyúmu'l-Asmá'** — his earliest major writing, begun the night of his declaration; a commentary on the Quranic Sura of Joseph, composed in the manner of the Quran and presenting "new verses from God" (line 481); used by Babis as a proof-text of his claims.
- **The Bayán** (Persian and Arabic versions) — his book of holy law and doctrine, composed at Maku; "a new holy book superseding the Qur'an" (line 855). Contains his legislation, doctrine, and the prophecy of "He whom God shall make manifest."
- **Various prayers, letters, treatises** — including proclamatory letters to the Shah and to clerics, treatises on gnostic truth, nine commentaries on the whole Quran, and a discussion of the science of talismans.
- **Nabil's chronicle** — cited by Smith for the Báb's visionary experience (line 450, citing Nabil 253).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Babi reception:** For his followers, the Báb was first understood as the intermediary or "gate" (báb) between the Hidden Imam and the faithful; subsequently as the Imam Mahdi himself; and finally — as expressed in his own later writings — as an independent Manifestation of God in his own right, the "Point of the Bayán" from whom a succession of future Manifestations would proceed.

**Bahá'í reception:** The Bahá'í tradition regards the Báb as a co-founder of their faith and as a fully independent Manifestation of God who "inaugurated a new cycle" — not merely a forerunner, but the one who "opened the door" for Bahá'u'lláh (identified by Baha'is as "He whom God shall make manifest" foretold in the Bayán). Smith notes that "within a few years, the great majority of Babis had become Baha'is, accepting Baha'u'llah as the messianic figure of Man-yuzhiruhu'lláh ('He whom God shall make manifest') foretold by the Bab" (lines 1384–1386). The Báb's remains were buried in the shrine on Mount Carmel constructed under 'Abdu'l-Bahá's direction.

**Islamic rejection:** The Shi'i 'ulamá generally condemned the Báb as an apostate and heretic. Clerical opponents issued fatwas against him and incited persecution of his followers. His proof — the revelation of verses — was dismissed (the clerics at Tabriz "only faulted his grammar," line 795). The Shaykhi leader Hájí Muhammad Karím Khán led the campaign against him in successive writings.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within Babi doctrine the Báb held several simultaneous stations:

1. **The Gate (Báb):** Initially the intermediary to the Hidden Imam, and hence his title; this was understood as his public claim, though more may have been intended from the start.
2. **The Qá'im / Mahdi:** Announced at Tabriz in 1848, his claim to be the return of the Twelfth Imam. This "marked the era of resurrection (qiyáma) and represented a challenge to the entire social order" (lines 780–782).
3. **Independent Manifestation of God:** In his later theology, the Báb is the Manifestation for the present age — the "Point of the Bayán," around whom his chief disciples (the "Letters of the Living") circle. He is the rising of the same divine sun that has shone through all previous Manifestations.
4. **Forerunner of "He whom God shall make manifest":** The Bayán introduces a new eschatological promise: the coming one whose "thousand perusals of the Bayán would not be equal to reading one of his verses" (lines 927–928). The Báb instructs all to leave a vacant place for him in every meeting and to rise on hearing his name (lines 930–931). In Bahá'í interpretation this refers to Bahá'u'lláh.

The Báb's claim was also significant socially: he challenged the authority of the 'ulamá directly. Even if he was only the Imam's intermediary, "he was now to be seen as the supreme authority in the Shi'i world, and the 'ulama could only continue to exercise their own authority as his agents" (lines 497–499). His popular appeal as a saintly ascetic amplified his religious authority.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**The radicalization question:** Smith discusses the contrast between the early Báb — who insisted on orthodox Islamic practice, instituted extra fasts and prayers, and directed his disciples to promulgate his message peacefully — and the later Báb, whose Bayán authorized future Babi kings to lead holy war and whose followers engaged in armed conflict. Smith treats this as a genuine "radicalization" of the movement (chapter 4), driven by the Báb's advancing claims, Babi militancy, clerical violence, and the Báb's alienation from the Qajar regime, rather than as a simple development inherent in his original teaching.

**The nature of his early claims:** "The exact nature of 'Alí-Muhammad's early claims and of the understanding of them within the fledgling Babi movement remains ambiguous" (lines 466–468). Was his claim to be merely the Gate a deliberate concealment of higher claims? Smith notes that in 1847 the Báb "referred to the progression of his claims, his initial position being intended to prevent people from becoming agitated by the coming of a 'new book' (or revelation)" (lines 490–492, citing Amanat 199).

**The relationship with Shaykhism:** The Báb attended some of Sayyid Kázim's classes but "never made any formal study of Shaykhism" (lines 617–618). While he shared the Shaykhi tradition of gnostic exposition and claimed leadership of the Shaykhis after Sayyid Kázim's death, he simultaneously claimed a status that transcended that of the first two Shaykhi leaders.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

The Báb's writings, particularly the Bayán, established the doctrinal framework — especially the concept of the [[manifestation-of-god|Manifestation of God]] and the prophecy of "He whom God shall make manifest" — that Bahá'u'lláh developed and fulfilled (from the Bahá'í perspective). Smith notes that Bahá'u'lláh's early writings (notably the *Kitáb-i-Íqán*, 1862) provided the Babis with "a clear account of their religious doctrines, particularly the concept of a succession of Manifestations of God and the fulfilment of prophecy" (lines 1311–1314).

### Family of the Báb

**Maternal family:** The Báb's mother, Fátimih Bagum, had three brothers: (1) Hájí Mírzá Sayyid Muhammad (d. 1876), Khál-i-Akbar ("the Greatest Uncle"), the recipient of the *Kitáb-i-Íqán*, who became a believer after receiving it; (2) Hájí Mírzá Sayyid 'Alí, known as Khál-i-A'zam ("Most Great Uncle"), the Báb's guardian after his father's death and the first male family member to accept his nephew's claims — he subsequently died as one of the Seven Martyrs of Tehran (1850); (3) Hájí Mírzá Hasan-'Alí, Khál-i-Asghar ("Younger Uncle"). The son of Sayyid Muhammad, Hájí Mírzá Muḥammad-Taqí (the Vakílu'd-Dawlih), was the chief builder of the Bahá'í House of Worship in Ashkhabad (lines 4935–4956).

The Báb's first wife, [[khadijih-bagum|Khadíjih Bagum]] (a niece of his mother's father), had two brothers: Hájí Mírzá Abu'l-Qásim (1811–87) and Hájí Mírzá Sayyid Hasan (d. 1892), "Afnán-i-Kabír" — sons of whom married daughters of 'Abdu'l-Bahá. One of these sons, Mírzá Hádí, who married Díyá'iyyih, was the father of [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] (lines 4960–4971). Bahá'u'lláh appointed the descendants of the Báb's family (the *Afnán*) as hereditary custodians of the House of the Báb in Shiraz (line 5012–5018).

Whilst in Isfahan (1846–7), the Báb married a second wife, Fátimih (d. 1916), the sister of Mullá Rajab-'Alí (line 5021–5023). After the Báb's death, Subh-i-Azal briefly married the second wife (for one month), then divorced her and married her to Sayyid Muhammad Isfahání — an action Bahá'ís cite as contrary to the Báb's instructions that his wives remain unmarried after his death (lines 5010–5019).

**Paternal family:** Details of the Báb's father, Mírzá Muḥammad-Ridá, are less available; most of that side did not become Bahá'ís. Two of his father's cousins were eminent Shi'i clerics — Mírzá-yi-Shírází (1815–95), the leading mujtahid of his time, and Hájí Sayyid Javád (d. 1870/1), the Friday prayer leader in Kirmán — both reportedly secret Babi/Bahá'ís who protected Babis and Bahá'ís from persecution (lines 4980–4991). Aqá Mírzá Aqá (1842–1903), a son of another cousin, became the second male member of the Báb's paternal family to accept the Faith in his teens, and was later an active Bahá'í teacher; Bahá'u'lláh honored him with the title Núru'd-Dín ("Light of Faith") and designated him recipient of the Tablet of the World (lines 4997–5011).

### Writings of the Báb

The Báb wrote extensively, either in his own hand — mostly during the early stage of his mission — or dictated to his companion and secretary, Sayyid Husayn Yazdí ("'Azíz"), one of the Letters of the Living. He referred to having composed 500,000 verses by 1848 (the equivalent of eighty books the size of the Quran), one-fifth of which had been disseminated. Suppression of Babism led to significant losses, but widespread copying preserved many manuscripts. Little has been published in accessible form, even in the original Arabic and Persian (lines 5032–5051).

**Pre-mission works (before May 1844):** Five works are known, including a Quran commentary, a treatise on "right behaviour," and a pilgrimage prayer honoring Muhammad, Fátima, and the Imáms (lines 5052–5058).

**First work of the mission:** The [[qayyumu-l-asma|Qayyúmu'l-Asmá']] (May–June 1844) (lines 5060–5062).

**Works composed up to departure for Isfahan (September 1846):** Eleven commentaries on Quranic verses or Islamic traditions (the best known being on the Chapter of Kawthar, composed for Vahíd); thirteen treatises on theological and legal issues including elements of specifically Babi ritual; homilies; and collections of prayers (lines 5063–5072).

**Works composed in Isfahan (September 1846–March 1847):** A commentary on the Quranic *súra* of Wa'l-'Asr for the imam-jum'a (Friday prayer leader) Sayyid Muhammad; a treatise on the "specific prophethood" of Muhammad for the city governor Manúchihr Khán (lines 5073–5081).

**Works from Azerbaijan (most important):** The Persian **[[the-bayan|Bayán]]** and its companion Arabic volume; the **Seven Proofs** (*Dalá'il-i-Sab'ih*); the Book of Names; nine further commentaries on the Quran; and the *Kitáb-i-Panj Sha'n* ("Book of the Five Grades") — a book in which the Báb wrote in five modes: Quranic-style Arabic verses, prayers, homilies, commentaries, and Persian-language writing (lines 5083–5095). The Báb also fashioned many talismans (line 5102).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### Honorific Titles Given to Followers
The Báb gave various of his followers honorific religious titles (l. 16999–17007). The best known are the [[letters-of-the-living|Letters of the Living]]. Others were named "Mirrors." Individuals were also given specific religious names: [[quddus|Quddús]] ("Holy"), [[tahirih|Táhirih]] ("Pure"), Vaḥíd ("Unique"), etc.

### Letters of the Living
The [[letters-of-the-living|Letters of the Living]] (*Ḥurúfu'l-Ḥayy*) were the Báb's first eighteen disciples (l. 20855). The Báb gave them a cosmic role, identifying them with the *sábiqún* (forerunners) — the first members of the human race to respond to God's pre-eternal covenant (l. 20922–20927). They were also to form the first *waḥíd* ("unity") in a proposed hierarchy of nineteen-member groups (l. 20929–20930). Most had been followers of Shaykhi leader Sayyid [[kazim-rashti|Kázim Rashtí]] (l. 20917–20919); nine were killed during or following the Shaykh Ṭabarsí conflict (l. 20920–20921).

### Wife: Khadíjih Bagum
The Báb's wife was [[khadijih-bagum|Khadíjih Bagum]] (d. 1882), his second cousin and childhood playmate, whom he married in August 1842 (l. 20075–20080). She later became a Bahá'í. According to her own account she recognized her husband's religious station before his declaration in 1844 (l. 20083–20089).
