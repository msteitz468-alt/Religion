Jeroboam son of Nebat, an Ephraimite officer over [[solomon|Solomon's]] forced labor who became the
first king of the seceded northern tribes, and whose sanctuaries at [[bethel|Bethel]] and
[[dan|Dan]] give [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]] its most repeated formula of condemnation — "the sin of
Jeroboam." That formula is the evaluative verdict of the biblical narrator, and this page records
it as such rather than adopting it in wiki voice.

## Biographical Overview

According to 1 Kings 11, Jeroboam was a capable young man whom Solomon set over the labor gangs of
the house of Joseph. The prophet Ahijah of Shiloh met him outside [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]], tore a
new cloak into twelve pieces, and gave him ten, announcing that YHWH would tear the kingdom from
Solomon's son. Solomon sought his life and Jeroboam fled to Egypt to Shishak, returning after
Solomon's death. At [[shechem|Shechem]] he headed the northern delegation that petitioned
[[rehoboam|Rehoboam]] for relief from the yoke; when Rehoboam refused, the ten tribes made
Jeroboam king. He fortified Shechem and Penuel, and — the text says, reasoning that pilgrimage to
Jerusalem would return the people's loyalty to the house of David — made two golden calves,
placing one at Bethel and one at Dan, with the words "Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you
up out of the land of Egypt." He installed priests who were not [[levites|Levites]], built high
places, and moved the autumn feast to the eighth month.

The narrative reports an unnamed man of God from Judah who cried against the Bethel altar, the
withering and restoring of Jeroboam's hand, and the prophet's later death by a lion after he ate
in the north against his own oracle. When Jeroboam's son Abijah fell ill, his disguised wife
consulted the now-blind Ahijah, who pronounced the sweeping away of the house of Jeroboam "as one
burns up dung." His son Nadab was assassinated by Baasha, who destroyed the dynasty.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Kings 11:26–40** — Solomonic service; Ahijah's torn-cloak oracle; flight to Egypt.
- **1 Kings 12** — Shechem, the secession, the calves at Bethel and Dan, the non-Levitical
  priesthood and the altered festival calendar.
- **1 Kings 13** — the man of God from Judah against the Bethel altar; the withered hand.
- **1 Kings 14:1–20** — Ahijah's oracle on the dynasty; Abijah's death; regnal summary.
- **1 Kings 15:25–30** — the extermination of the house of Jeroboam by Baasha.
- **2 Kings 17:21–23** — the concluding indictment: Israel "walked in all the sins of Jeroboam."
- **2 Chronicles 10–13** — the Chronicler's parallel, which narrates the schism from Judah's side
  and adds Abijah of Judah's battle-speech against Jeroboam (2 Chr 13).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Deuteronomistic evaluation within Kings** — the narrator makes "the sin of Jeroboam" the
  standing charge against every northern king down to the fall of [[samaria|Samaria]]. This is a
  theological verdict of the text, not an independent historical assessment.
- **[[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]] (NIBC, 1995)**, as recorded on [[1-2-kings|the Kings page]]:
  the calf-cult functions as **political religion** and as northern identity-marker, with a
  deliberate echo of Exodus 32; the refrain runs from the schism to the exile. Provan is recorded
  there as resisting "sincere Yahwist calves" readings — i.e. readings that treat the calves as
  legitimate YHWH-pedestals unfairly maligned by a Judahite narrator. That disagreement is a
  live position, recorded and not adjudicated here.
- **Later Jewish and Christian usage** — the name became shorthand for the founder of illegitimate
  worship. This wiki has not yet ingested a rabbinic or patristic source on Jeroboam, so no
  specific midrashic or patristic reading is recorded.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jeroboam stands at the hinge where prophetic word and political event meet: Ahijah announces the
tearing, Rehoboam's folly executes it, and Kings frames the whole northern monarchy as living out
a sin defined at its foundation. Two theological currents run in tension across the account — the
schism is divinely authorized (Ahijah's oracle, and the word to Rehoboam not to fight), yet
Jeroboam's cult is condemned absolutely. The text does not resolve that tension, and neither does
this page. The calf language of 1 Kings 12:28 binds the northern shrine to the golden calf of
Exodus 32, making the north's founding act a repetition of Israel's archetypal apostasy — a
literary linkage the narrative itself invites.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The calves.** One reading takes them as images of YHWH's mount or pedestal, comparable to the
  cherubim of the Jerusalem temple, and the condemnation as Judahite polemic; another takes the
  plural "your gods" and the non-Levitical priesthood as evidence of genuine cultic rupture.
  Positions, not adjudicated. Provan is recorded as against the first.
- **Kings vs Chronicles [CONTRADICTION / recorded tension].** Chronicles omits much of the
  northern material and, in 2 Chronicles 13, places a long condemnation of Jeroboam in the mouth
  of Abijah of Judah, reporting a crushing Judahite victory that Kings does not narrate. The two
  accounts are preserved side by side here.
- **Chronology.** Regnal dates for Jeroboam depend on synchronisms with Judah and on the dating of
  Shishak's campaign; the figures given above are approximate and contested.
- **External attestation.** There is no inscription naming Jeroboam I that this page can rely on.
  Later northern kingship is attested in Assyrian and Moabite material (see [[ahab|Ahab]] and
  [[jehu|Jehu]]), but nothing here supports or refutes the founding narrative.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Jeroboam anchors debate over the Deuteronomistic History's evaluative scheme: the recurring
formula is one of the clearest markers used to argue for a unified editorial hand across Kings.
He also anchors discussion of cult centralization and its relation to [[deuteronomy|Deuteronomy]],
and of how far a hostile narrator's account of a rival sanctuary can be used as history.
