# Virgil

Roman poet (70–19 BCE), author of the [[virgil-aeneid|*Aeneid*]], the *Eclogues*,
and the *Georgics*. In the [[divine-comedy|*Commedia*]] he appears as a
**character**: Dante's guide through [[inferno|Hell]] and [[purgatorio|Purgatory]],
and an allegorical figure of unaided human **reason**.

## In the Divine Comedy

Virgil emerges enigmatically in Inferno 1, refusing at first to name himself, and
claims to have taught Dante "the fine-tuned style." Dante enrolls him among the
great ancient poets (Inf. 4). Their relationship develops from heroic
companionship toward "intimate nuance and, at times, comedy" (Kirkpatrick
compares Don Quixote and Sancho Panza); in Inferno 24 Virgil even hoists his
pupil and slides down a cliff "like a mother saving her child from a burning
house."

As a virtuous pagan, Virgil is an inhabitant of [[limbo|Limbo]] — suffering no
pain but "neither happy nor sad" (Inf. 4:84) — and so cannot enter Heaven. He
**disappears** at the summit of Purgatory (Purgatorio 30) at the arrival of
[[beatrice|Beatrice]], who supplants reason with revelation. His last words to
Dante (Purg. 27:131): "take what pleases you to be your guide."

Dante perpetrates "many liberties" on Virgil. The Fourth *Eclogue* — read in
Dante's time as a prophecy of Christ's nativity — makes Virgil an unwitting
herald of Christianity who, in the poem, converts [[statius|Statius]] without
himself being saved. The recurring question of *why* the virtuous pagan must be
condemned runs through the whole poem (sharpened in Paradiso 19–20).

## Significance

Virgil embodies the limits and dignity of natural reason within Dante's scheme:
indispensable as guide and teacher, yet incapable of the joy that revelation
brings. For his role in classical epic and his own reception, see
[[virgil-aeneid|the Aeneid]].
