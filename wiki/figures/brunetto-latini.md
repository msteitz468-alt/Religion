# Brunetto Latini

Florentine administrator, rhetorician, and author of the encyclopedic *Livres dou
Trésor* and the *Tesoretto* (c. 1220–c. 1294); met by [[dante-alighieri|Dante]]
among the **sodomites** of [[inferno|Inferno]] 15, running beneath the rain of fire.
Kirkpatrick corrects the common error that Brunetto was Dante's schoolmaster; rather
he was a leading intellectual from whose circle Dante gained an interest in
classical rhetoric and French literature.

## In the Divine Comedy

The encounter is tender and reverent despite the circle: Dante addresses him as
"Sir Brunetto" and credits him — "you taught me how man makes himself eternal"
(15:85) — while Brunetto prophesies Dante's exile and bitter reward from the
"ungrateful, malign" Florentines, the "sacred seed of Rome" overrun by the
Fiesolan rabble. No specific charge of sodomy is laid; Brunetto is even known to
have written against it, and critics debate the precise "sin against nature" at
issue. He commends his own *Tesoretto* to Dante's care and runs off "like one who
runs… for the green cloth at Verona," seeming "the one that wins, not loses."

## Significance

The canto enacts Dante's complex debt to a mentor he must nonetheless place in
Hell: Brunetto's own definition of a city — "a coming-together of persons… to live
according to reason" — undergirds Dante's political thought, even as the Roman
[[virgil|Virgil]] (silent throughout the canto) has displaced him as Dante's model
of civic and rhetorical practice.
