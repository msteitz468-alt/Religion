# Sheikh Farid

Sheikh Farid (1173–1266), formally Fariduddin Masud Ganj-i-Shakar ("treasury of sugar"), was a Punjabi Sufi saint of the **Chishti order** whose Punjabi verses (*shaloks*) were incorporated into the **[[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]** as **Bhagat bāṇī**. He is notable as the sole Muslim figure among the primary Bhagats in the Granth (the Bhagat bāṇī section), and his inclusion illustrates the Sikh Gurus' recognition of divine truth beyond religious boundaries. Farid predated Guru Nanak by roughly three centuries; it was his compositions, not the man himself, that entered the Sikh scriptural corpus.

## In the Guru Granth Sahib (Raag Aasaa Bhagat Bāṇī, Angs 488–489)

Farid's compositions appear at the close of the Bhagat bāṇī section of Raag Aasaa, following Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, and Dhanna. He uses Islamic vocabulary throughout — Allah, Khuda, dervish (*darvesh*), *bandagī* (worship), *murīd* (disciple), *Parvardagaar* (Sustainer-Cherisher) — while employing the same Guru-disciple framework as the Sikh Gurus and addressing themes of impermanence, sincerity, and the body's return to dust.

**On sincerity versus profession:**

> "They alone are true, whose love for God is deep and heart-felt. Those who have one thing in their heart, and something else in their mouth, are judged to be false." (L8951–8959, Ang 488)

> "Those who are imbued with love for the Lord, are delighted by His Vision. Those who forget the Naam, the Name of the Lord, are a burden on the earth." (L8961–8968, Ang 488)

**On the dervish:**

> "Those whom the Lord attaches to the hem of His robe, are the true dervishes at His Door. Blessed are the mothers who gave birth to them, and fruitful is their coming into the world." (L8975–8983, Ang 488)

**On impermanence and death:**

> "Says Shaykh Fareed, O my dear friend, attach yourself to the Lord. This body shall turn to dust, and its home shall be a neglected graveyard." (L9001–9006, Ang 488)

> "If I had known that I was to die, and not return again, I would not have ruined myself by clinging to the world of falsehood." (L9018–9023, Ang 488)

**Seasonal imagery for transience:**

> "As the swallows appear in the month of Katik, forest fires in the month of Chayt, and lightning in Saawan, and as the bride's arms adorn her husband's neck in winter; just so, the transitory human bodies pass away. Reflect upon this in your mind." (L9060–9071, Ang 489)

> "It takes six months to form the body, but it breaks in an instant." (L9074–9075, Ang 489)

**On truth in speech:**

> "So speak the Truth, in righteousness, and do not speak falsehood. The disciple ought to travel the route, pointed out by the Guru." (L9026–9030, Ang 489)

**On restraining the wandering mind:**

> "You can meet the Lord today, O Shaykh Fareed, if you restrain your bird-like desires which keep your mind in turmoil." (L9012–9014, Ang 488)

## Theological Significance

The inclusion of Farid's compositions in the SGGS makes a structural theological statement: the Sikh Gurus recognized the same reality in the Sufi saint's pursuit of God (through Islamic devotional vocabulary, the master-disciple framework, and the renunciation of worldly attachment) as in the Hindu Bhagats' path. Farid uses the same Guru-disciple teaching relationship that the Sikh tradition centers: "The disciple ought to travel the route, pointed out by the Guru."

The Farid corpus in the SGGS also includes an extensive separate collection of *shaloks* (couplets) in a later raag section (Raag Sorat'h / Siri Raag), making him the only Bhagat with two distinct collections in different locations.
