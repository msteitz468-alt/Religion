# Gandhi

**Mahatma Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** (1869–1948 CE) was the preeminent leader of the Indian
independence movement against British rule and one of the defining religious-political figures of
the twentieth century. His doctrine of *satyagraha* ("holding firmly to truth"), his reinvention of
[[ahimsa|ahiṃsā]] (nonviolence) as political strategy, and his deeply complex relationship to Hindu
tradition make him both the product and the interpreter of the Hinduism described throughout this
wiki.

## Biographical Overview

Gandhi was born into a *Baniya* (merchant caste) Vaishnava family in Gujarat — a region with a
strong Jaina presence and tradition of vegetarianism. He trained as a barrister in London, where he
first read the **Bhagavad Gita** (in 1888–1889, in Sir Edwin Arnold's translation *The Song
Celestial*), though he had grown up in a household where the text was recited. He developed his
political method first in South Africa (on behalf of the Indian community there) and then in India.

Gandhi was assassinated on January 30, 1948, at Birla House in New Delhi, by **Nathuram Godse**,
a Pune Brahmin with ties to the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, National Volunteers' Organization).

## Satyagraha: Theology and Method

Gandhi coined the term **satyagraha** ("holding firmly to truth" — *satya* from the Sanskrit verb
"to be," cognate with *sati*, "good woman," root of the practice of widow immolation). Satyagraha was:

- A mode of **active nonviolent resistance** — not passive noncooperation, but what Doniger calls
  "passive-aggressive nonresistance"
- Rooted in the Jaina and Vaishnava traditions of his native Gujarat but transposed into a *political
  strategy* against British rule
- Based on the idea that "you cannot fast against a tyrant" — fasting works only against "those who
  love me," because it operates by awakening the conscience of the oppressor who cares about the
  victim

Gandhi frequently deployed **fasting** as his primary weapon: against the British (to win his
release from prison); against Congress (to prevent separate Harijan electorates); against communal
violence. He said he fasted "to reform those who loved me" — drawing on the ancient Hindu practice
of fasting as a restoration for sins or a means of moral coercion.

## The Bhagavad Gita and Violence

Gandhi's relationship with the Gita is one of the most revealing paradoxes in his thought:

- He wrote a translation (into Gujarati) and commentary in which he **interpreted the Mahabharata
  war as symbolic** — the war is internal, Arjuna's enemies are his own passions, the battlefield
  is the soul
- Yet he acknowledged: "The Gita was not written to establish ahimsa" — Kṛṣṇa's exhortation to
  Arjuna is literally to kill his cousins
- And he recognized the irony that the Vedic reverence for violence, which he hoped nonviolence
  would displace, had "a deeper grip on real emotions in the twentieth century": the Vedantic ethic
  of nonviolence flowered in Gandhi; the Vedic ethic of violence flowered in the Partition massacres

The Gita's famous dictum — "karma without kāma," action without desire for results — Gandhi read
as authorizing self-sacrificial political action. His fasting *was* a form of karma yoga.

## Nonviolence, Caste, and the Harijan Question

Gandhi supported the abolition of Untouchability but **not** the abolition of caste itself. This
put him in direct conflict with [[ambedkar|B. R. Ambedkar]], who argued that caste and
Untouchability could not be separated.

Gandhi used the term **Harijan** ("People of God") for the Untouchable castes — a term that
Ambedkar rejected as patronizing. When Ambedkar sought a separate electorate for Harijans, Gandhi
fasted to prevent it — successfully, from Gandhi's political standpoint; Ambedkar signed the Poona
Pact. Ambedkar never forgave Gandhi, arguing that Gandhi's fast was moral coercion that subordinated
Dalit political autonomy to Hindu communal unity.

## Cow Protection and Religion

Gandhi used the image of **calf love** (*vatsalya*) — the love of and for a mother cow — as a key
symbol for his imagined Indian nation, and cow protection was central to his version of nonviolence
(ahimsa). However, this commitment was a significant factor in the failure of his movement to attract
large-scale Muslim support.

## The Failure of Nonviolence: Partition

Gandhi's nonviolence "succeeded against the British but could not avert the tragedy of Partition."
His method failed because, as Doniger argues, it did not adequately contend with the older and more
tenacious Hindu ideal of *violence* — precisely the violence that Krishna invoked in the Gita and
that erupted in the communal slaughters of 1947.

After his assassination, a deified form of Gandhi had briefly replaced the goddess in the Devi
movement among Adivasis of Gujarat — spiders writing his name in cobwebs; Gandhi seen in wells,
in the rising sun, in kerosene bottles. Gandhi himself put a stop to the mythologizing of his image.

## Opium, Alcohol, and the Adivasis

Gandhi's campaign for prohibition was closely tied to the Devi movement in Gujarat (1920s), in
which Adivasi women used the goddess's command to abandon toddy and daru (local alcohol) as a form
of liberation from colonial exploitation by Parsi liquor dealers. The Devi movement anticipated
Alcoholics Anonymous; Gandhi's movement picked up where the Devi left off.
