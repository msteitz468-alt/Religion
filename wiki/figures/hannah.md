Hannah, the barren wife of Elkanah of the Ephraimite hill country, prays at the [[shiloh|Shiloh]]
sanctuary for a son, vows him to YHWH, bears [[samuel|Samuel]], and gives him up — and her song in
1 Sam 2:1-10 opens the poetic frame of the whole of [[1-samuel|1]]–[[2-samuel|2 Samuel]].

## Biographical Overview

Elkanah has two wives: Peninnah, who has children, and Hannah, who is barren. At the annual
sacrifice at Shiloh Peninnah provokes her rival, and Hannah weeps and will not eat. Praying
silently at the sanctuary with her lips moving and no voice audible, she is accused of drunkenness
by [[eli|Eli]] the priest; she answers that she has been pouring out her soul, and Eli blesses her
petition. She vows that if given a son she will give him to YHWH all his days and that no razor
will touch his head. She bears Samuel, weans him, and brings him to Eli at Shiloh with a sacrifice
— "for this child I prayed" — leaving him there. The narrative reports that she visits yearly with
a little robe and that she subsequently bears further children.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Sam 1:1-18**: the two wives, the provocation, the silent prayer, Eli's accusation and
  blessing, the vow.
- **1 Sam 1:19-28**: Samuel's birth and naming, the weaning, the presentation at Shiloh.
- **1 Sam 2:1-10 — the Song of Hannah**: praise of the God who breaks the bows of the mighty and
  girds the stumbling with strength, who raises the poor from the dust, who "kills and makes
  alive," who "brings down to Sheol and raises up," and who "will give strength to his king and
  exalt the horn of his anointed" (*māšîaḥ*).
- **1 Sam 2:18-21**: the yearly robe; Eli's blessing; further children.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Historical-philological / evangelical ([[david-tsumura|Tsumura]], NICOT)**: as recorded on the
  [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]] and [[2-samuel|2 Samuel]] pages, Tsumura treats the Song of Hannah as the
  **thematic overture to the whole of 1–2 Samuel**, paired at the far end with David's Song of
  Thanks (2 Sam 22) and Last Words (2 Sam 23) as a poetic inclusio around a single unified work.
  Among the themes it announces, the wiki records the **reversal of human fortunes** — "the bows
  of the mighty are broken, but those who stumbled are girded with strength" (2:4) — as the
  pattern that recurs in Hannah → Samuel → Saul → David, "every elevation coming through divine
  reversal of human expectation." Tsumura's reading of the book's purpose is also recorded as
  including a messianic trajectory via Hannah's *māšîaḥ* toward the Davidic line.
- **Christian reception (as recorded on [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]])**: Hannah's Song is read as the
  model for the Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55), Mary's prayer being structured on it with the same
  reversal-of-fortune theology. The wiki records this as an established connection in the Christian
  reading of the text; the collection has not ingested a source treating the patristic or medieval
  Hannah tradition at length.
- **Jewish reception**: no source ingested in this wiki treats the rabbinic Hannah material (her
  prayer as a halakhic paradigm, etc.) at length. Nothing is asserted here beyond that.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Hannah stands at the hinge between the era of the judges and the monarchy. Her petition produces
the prophet who will anoint both [[saul|Saul]] and [[david|David]]; her song supplies the
theological vocabulary — reversal, divine sovereignty over life and death, the exalted horn of the
anointed — in which the rest of the narrative evaluates its kings. The vow is notable in that the
child asked for is immediately surrendered: the answered prayer is not retained but given back,
and the sanctuary that receives him is the one whose priestly house is about to fall
([[eli|Eli]]).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The messianic reading of 2:10**: whether Hannah's *māšîaḥ* in a pre-monarchic setting is
  anticipatory prophecy, a later compositional frame, or an idiom for a future king is a live
  question in critical scholarship; the wiki records only Tsumura's position (early composition of
  the major blocks, MT-first text criticism, an early Divided Monarchy audience) and does not
  adjudicate against the redactional alternatives.
- **Text of 1 Samuel 1–2**: 4QSam^a diverges from MT in this stretch. The wiki records the general
  methodological split — Tsumura's MT-first approach vs. McCarter/Tov-style eclectic
  reconstruction — as positions (see [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]]).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Hannah's Song is the standard anchor for arguments about the literary unity of 1–2 Samuel, for the
reversal-theology reading of the Samuel narratives, and, in Christian exegesis, for the Magnificat
comparison. Cross-links: [[samuel|Samuel]], [[eli|Eli]], [[shiloh|Shiloh]], [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]],
[[2-samuel|2 Samuel]], [[david|David]].
