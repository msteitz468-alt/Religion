# Brân the Blessed

**Brân the Blessed** — Welsh **Bendigeidfran**, "Brân the Blessed," son of **Llŷr** — is the **giant-king of
Britain** in the **Second Branch** of the **[[mabinogion|Mabinogion]]**, so vast that **no house could ever
hold him** and he wades the Irish Sea as other men ford a stream. He is the brother of **[[branwen|Branwen]]**
and **Manawydan** ([[manannan|Manannán]]'s Welsh reflex), and a god reduced to a king in the telling.

## Myths in Detail

### Branwen's marriage and the war

Brân gives his sister **[[branwen|Branwen]]** in marriage to **Matholwch, king of Ireland**. But their
half-brother **Efnisien**, enraged at not being consulted, **mutilates Matholwch's horses**, nearly breaking
the peace. To make amends Brân gives Matholwch a great treasure: the **Cauldron of Rebirth**, in which **slain
warriors boiled overnight come out alive the next day — though without the power of speech**. In Ireland,
though, Branwen is **humiliated and beaten**, set to menial kitchen-work; she tames a **starling**, teaches it
speech, and sends it across the sea with a message bound to its wing. Brân raises all Britain and **wades the
sea to war**, lying across the river Liffey as a **living bridge** for his army (*"He who would be a chief, let
him be a bridge,"* he says — a proverb born here).

### The cauldron, the head, and the burial

The battle is catastrophic: the Irish keep **resurrecting their dead in Brân's own cauldron** until Efnisien,
in remorse, hides among the corpses, is thrown in, and **bursts the cauldron, shattering his own heart**. Only
**seven men survive** on the British side — and **Branwen, who dies of grief**. Brân is wounded in the foot by
a **poisoned spear** and, dying, gives an extraordinary command: **cut off his head**. The head **remains
alive and speaking**, a genial companion, and the seven survivors **feast with it for eighty-seven years** in
an enchanted timelessness (at Harlech, then on the island of Gwales) — the **"Assembly of the Wondrous Head"**
— until a forbidden door is opened and sorrow returns. They then bury the **still-uncorrupted head at the White
Hill (the Tower of London), facing France**, where it wards Britain from invasion until, in later legend,
Arthur arrogantly digs it up.

## Significance

Brân fuses the **talismanic severed head** (a central Celtic cult motif — the head as seat of life and
prophecy) with the **cauldron of rebirth** (the Otherworld vessel of resurrection) and the **protector-king**
whose buried head guards the land. The Otherworld feast and the **timeless joy interrupted by a forbidden
door** are classic Celtic [[the-otherworld|Otherworld]] motifs.
