# Festus

## Biographical Overview

Porcius Festus was the Roman governor of Judea who succeeded [[felix|Antonius Felix]]
(around AD 59–60) and before whom Paul, refusing transfer to Jerusalem, appealed to Caesar,
as narrated in [[acts|Acts]] 25:1–26:32. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats Festus as a more
competent and correct administrator than Felix, one who nonetheless found himself unable to
resolve a case he did not understand and who convened the hearing before
[[herod-agrippa-ii|Herod Agrippa II]] precisely because he had "nothing definite to write"
to the emperor.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 24:27** — Festus succeeds Felix, who has left Paul imprisoned as a favor to the
  Jews.
- **Acts 25:1–5** — Jewish leaders ask Festus to move Paul to Jerusalem, "planning an ambush
  to kill him"; Festus insists the case be heard at [[caesarea|Caesarea]].
- **Acts 25:6–8** — Paul denies wrongdoing against the Jewish law, the temple, or Caesar.
- **Acts 25:9–12** — Rather than be tried at Jerusalem, Paul appeals to Caesar (provocatio);
  Festus, after conferring with his council, declares: "To Caesar you shall go."
- **Acts 25:13–27** — Festus reviews the case for the visiting Agrippa II and Bernice,
  admitting the dispute concerns "religious beliefs" and "a certain Jesus, who was dead but
  whom Paul asserted to be alive."
- **Acts 26:24–32** — During Paul's defense, Festus interrupts: "Paul, you are mad"; after
  the hearing he concurs that Paul has done "nothing to deserve death or imprisonment."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's Reading)

Bock reads Festus's insistence on hearing the case at Caesarea rather than Jerusalem as an
instance of divine protection: Festus "unknowingly protects Paul... another example of
divine protection for Paul, unwittingly aided by the Romans." He identifies **Paul's appeal
to Caesar** as a *provocatio* — an appeal of a citizen for the emperor's judgment before a
verdict, one of the oldest Roman rights (dating to 509 BC), the case being *extra ordinem*.
Bock notes the "nice parallel" between this appeal (invocatio) and "calling upon the name of
the Lord" (Vulgate at Acts 2:21), and identifies the Caesar in view as Nero in his early
reign, advised by Seneca and Burrus.

Bock highlights that Festus's admission of having "nothing definite to write" constitutes the
**second of three innocence declarations** in this sequence (23:29; 25:25; 26:31). He also
observes Festus's respect for Roman procedure requiring the accused to meet accusers
face-to-face (*aequitas romana*, "Roman fairness"; Justinian, *Digest* 48.17.1), which Bock
reads as "subtle polemic here, since a pagan state's procedure is protecting Paul from the
murderous plots coming from God's nation."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Festus is the hinge on which Paul's journey to Rome turns: his procedural correctness, his
inability to formulate a charge, and his convening of the Agrippa hearing all serve Bock's
theme that "God's sovereignty has protected Paul through the means of the state's law." The
appeal to Caesar lodged before Festus becomes irreversible ("Caesar's auctoritas"), so that
even Agrippa's later opinion that Paul "could have been set free" cannot short-circuit it.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock accepts Festus as a historical procurator (attested by Josephus) and reads the appeal
scene as conforming to genuine Roman legal practice. He notes an ambiguity in the appeal's
purpose — whether Paul's aim is apologetic (securing Roman recognition of Christianity) or
missionary (reaching Rome to preach) — citing Jervell that "nowhere is there an indication
that Paul expects justice in Rome," and concluding that Paul's "greater concern is to take
the gospel to Rome."
