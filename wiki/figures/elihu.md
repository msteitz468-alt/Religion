# Elihu

The fourth speaker in the [[book-of-job|book of Job]], unannounced in the prologue and unmentioned in
the epilogue. He intervenes after [[job|Job]]'s avowal of innocence and before the Yahweh speeches,
delivering four discourses (32:6–37:24) — one more than each of the three comforters.

## Biographical Overview

Elihu is introduced in prose at 32:1–5 with a full patronym: Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite, of the
family of Ram. [[john-hartley|Hartley]]'s onomastic reading is worth quoting whole:

> *"Elihu is introduced in the traditional style with a full patronym. His father, Barachel, is named
> along with his tribe, Buz, and his clan, Ram. This full genealogy reflects Elihu's youth and lack
> of personal accomplishment. The name Elihu means 'he is my God.' It is similar to the name Elijah
> (ʾēlîyāhû), 'Yahweh is my God.' His name bears witness that El is the highest God. His father's
> name, Barachel, means 'Bless, Oh God' or 'God has blessed.' Although the setting of this book is to
> the east of Canaan, the combined weight of the names in Elihu's lineage identifies him with the kin
> of Abraham, a faithful worshiper of the one true God. His name and his function in the book
> indicate that he is Yahweh's forerunner. True to his name, Elihu will defend God's honor, teaching
> that God mercifully and justly disciplines his servants. His teaching prepares for Yahweh's
> appearance to Job."* ([p. 429], on 32:2)

**A necessary distinction.** The *"family of Ram"* in Job 32:2 is Elihu's clan. It must not be
conflated with the Judahite Ram of the Perez genealogy (Ruth 4:19; 1 Chr. 2:9). Hartley makes no such
identification; he says only that the names taken together point to *"the kin of Abraham."* The
similarity of name is not, in this source, an identification of persons.

Hartley gives prominence to Elihu's anger: *"In this introduction Elihu's youth and his anger are
given prominence. In four verses (vv. 2–5) there are four references to his anger. His anger is
righteous indignation, for he sees the whole dialogue between Job and the three friends as having
been argued poorly on both sides"* ([p. 429]). He reads 32:3 against ancient legal custom — Elihu
takes the friends' falling silent as a concession that Job has won.

Hartley also declines the standard caricature of Elihu's verbosity. Elihu's *"wordiness… may be a
sign of uncertainty, for in challenging the tradition, he appears to be quite self-conscious. While
his manner may suggest self-importance, his actual attitude depends on the tone in which he offered
these words. If, for instance, Elihu paused after a statement to allow the friends an opportunity to
respond, his demeanor would certainly be less pompous than appears at first reading"* ([p. 435]).

## Primary Source Appearances

Elihu appears only in Job 32–37, in a prose introduction (32:1–5) and four speeches (32:6–37:24). He
is not named in the prologue, receives no reply from Job, and is neither condemned nor commended in
the epilogue.

Hartley's own précis of the four speeches:

> *"In his first speech Elihu develops the theme that God instructs a person through dreams and
> afflictions… To guarantee the release of that person from death's grip, he sends a special
> mediating angel to offer that person's ransom should he turn and pray for God's help (32:6–33:33).
> In his second speech Elihu defends the tenet that God governs the world in justice without
> exception (34:1–37). In the third speech Elihu reasons that human beings cannot affect God either
> by their sins or by their righteous acts… (35:1–16). In his last speech Elihu returns to his
> opening thesis that God disciplines anyone in jeopardy, even the mightiest, with suffering. Then he
> prepares Job for God's appearing by contemplating the divine glory that is manifest in a
> thunderstorm. He concludes with the conviction that the wise are those who fear God (36:1–37:24)."*
> ([p. 430])

Elihu claims inspiration for his teaching (32:8, 32:18–22; 33:4). Hartley reports this as Elihu's
claim and treats it as functionally significant: *"Thus Elihu seems to be asserting that having been
inspired by the Spirit of God… he has insight that may be trusted despite his youth."*

## Tradition-Specific Reception

The ingested source records one reception datum and no more: *"Because Elihu is so overly apologetic
in a boastful manner, his stature has suffered greatly in biblical interpretation. For example, some
of the early church fathers consider him to be overconfident and arrogant"* ([p. 449]). Hartley names
no fathers in the running text and argues *against* that reading. Beyond this, this wiki's ingested
sources carry no material on Jewish, Islamic, or later Christian reception of Elihu.

One reception option is catalogued but not adopted: among six proposed identities for the mediating
angel of 33:23, Hartley lists *"(6) the concealed Christ"* — the Christian-reception reading. He
records it and moves past it; his own identification is different (below).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The distinctive theology: discipline rather than punishment

This is the heart of Hartley's case for Elihu, stated at the outset of the section:

> *"Although Elihu's approach is close to that of the three friends, he differs from them in that he
> does not assume that all suffering is punishment for past sins. He teaches that misfortune may
> befall a person in order to awaken him to some wrongful attitude or unconscious error and thus keep
> him from taking a wrong course. Another major difference in his teaching is the emphasis that
> suffering may be an expression of God's mercy more than his wrath."* ([p. 427])

He restates it repeatedly: Elihu argues *"that Job is wrong for his bold complaints against God,
rather than for some undisclosed past sins"* (on 33:12); *"Elihu thus locates Job's plight in his
inflamed rhetoric rather than in any continuance of past sins"* ([p. 462]); and, against
[[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz]] specifically, *"he does not compose a theoretical list of sins Job
has committed"* ([p. 467], contrast 22:5–9).

Hartley's summative verdict, in the section headed "Elihu's Contribution":

> *"**Elihu's major contribution to ancient Israel's Wisdom tradition is a significant reshaping of
> the doctrine of retribution.** Though he is more verbose and less skilled in the use of poetic
> metaphor than the three comforters, **his understanding of the dynamic of the interaction between
> God and human beings is far more complex and realistic. He accepts the possibility that the
> righteous may suffer.**"* ([p. 485])

> *"With this teaching Elihu focuses more on Job's present plight than on his past life. He thereby
> avoids a futile search like that of the comforters for some sin that Job may have committed as the
> cause of his pain."* ([p. 486])

See [[doctrine-of-retribution|the doctrine of retribution]].

### A tension in Hartley's own account — recorded, not resolved

Hartley's "Elihu departs from the friends" thesis sits uneasily with his own comment on Elihu's
second speech. On 34:12 he writes: *"The second line is a reiteration of Bildad's thesis: 'Does God
pervert justice… or Shaddai pervert the right…?' (8:3). **This teaching means that whatever
misfortune a person experiences is justly deserved. There are no exceptions, for God makes no
mistakes**"* ([p. 454]). On 34:10–11 he identifies Elihu's position as *"exact retribution."* And on
36:17: *"God has brought on him the kind of judgment due to the wicked, a just judgment… Elihu has no
doubt that God has acted justly in Job's case"* ([p. 474]).

So the same commentary that credits Elihu with breaking the friends' retribution logic also records
Elihu restating [[bildad-the-shuhite|Bildad]]'s thesis in its strongest exceptionless form. Hartley
does not reconcile the two. This wiki flags it as a genuine tension in Hartley's reading rather than
smoothing it into either direction.

Hartley is elsewhere critical of Elihu as well. On the second speech: *"Elihu's compassion and
openness seem to have hardened into a rigid concern to protect God's just rule from the challenge of
a rebel like Job… He too fails to allow for the particulars of an individual case… unfortunately at
the high price of blatantly condemning him"* ([p. 462]). And: *"Unfortunately Elihu perceives only
the negative implications of Job's sincere questioning."* Hartley also catches Elihu misquoting Job
at 33:8–11 — putting a claim of moral *purity* in Job's mouth where Job claimed only to be *tām*,
blameless — while granting that at 34:5–6 *"Elihu has quite accurately capsuled Job's position."*

### The mediating angel and the ransom (33:23–28)

Elihu's most distinctive teaching, and the one Hartley treats at greatest length. In Elihu's mouth:
*"there is a heavenly intercessor who takes up the sufferer's case. This helper is an angel who
functions as a mediator (mēlîṣ), one who will help whomever God is afflicting for disciplinary
reasons"* ([p. 446]). The angel *"acts graciously on behalf of the one suffering by commanding the
death angel that has come to take this person's life, Release him! He has authority to order that
person's release, for he has found a ransom (kōp̄er)."* The ransom's content is left open: *"the
exact nature of the ransom this angel makes is not specified. Whatever it is it compensates the
divine justice for that person's failures."* Hartley adds that *"Only God himself can provide the
ransom that the angel offers."*

Hartley reports six proposed identities for the mediator: *"(1) another human being, e.g., a covenant
friend, a prophet, or a teacher; (2) the sufferer's own conscience; (3) one of the angelic host;
(4) the heavenly witness mentioned in 16:19; (5) the special angel or messenger of Yahweh (malʾaḵ
Yhwh…); (6) the concealed Christ."* He adopts the fifth:

> *"**Since the speeches of Elihu are intricately tied to the entire book of Job, Elihu is no doubt
> showing Job who his witness (16:19) or his redeemer (19:25) really is** and what his task will be
> (cf. 9:33)… **Therefore, this mediating angel is a very special heavenly creature. He may be
> identified with 'the angel of Yahweh.'** … The role of this angel allows God himself to affect
> events on earth without compromising his exalted transcendence."* ([p. 446–447])

He does *not* adopt the christological option, and this wiki does not attribute it to him. See
[[job-19-25-redeemer|Job 19:25 and the Redeemer]]. Hartley notes the polemical edge: *"Elihu is also
countering Eliphaz's position that there is no holy one to whom Job may turn for help"* — Eliphaz
having rejected precisely this idea at 5:1.

### Dramatic function

Hartley assigns Elihu two structural jobs. First, he prepares Job to hear God: *"Elihu's description
of a theophany prepares Job to hear Yahweh's words out of the tempest… His teaching thus prepares Job
to abandon his avowal of innocence in an act of full submission to God as his Lord"* ([p. 485]). His
closing line on the fear of the Lord (37:24) is matched to 28:28, forming an inclusio around the
block. Second, his delay preserves God's freedom: *"**If God had spoken immediately after Job's oath,
it would appear that Job's oath had compelled him to answer. That God remains silent indicates that
his coming appearance rests in his sovereign decision. God always keeps the initiative with
himself**"* ([p. 427–428]). Hartley also assigns him a tonal function: *"Elihu's verbose, overly
apologetic style offers comic relief to break the tight, fearful atmosphere created by Job's oath."*

Hartley concedes one difficulty in his own case: *"Elihu's optimism is supported by the fact that he
makes a contribution found nowhere else. **If, however, he should give the insight that he indicates
he can give, there would be no need for the Yahweh speeches**"* ([p. 435]).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

### The epilogue's silence

Elihu is neither condemned with the three friends at 42:7–9 nor commended. Hartley calls this
*"puzzling"* and offers two options without deciding: *"It may be that Yahweh did not find any
serious error in his speeches or that his speeches are viewed as standing outside the main
dialogue"* ([p. 538]). This wiki records it as a crux Hartley leaves open.

### Authenticity of the speeches

Whether Job 32–37 is original to the book or a later insertion is a long-standing dispute; see
[[elihu-speeches-authenticity|the Elihu speeches authenticity controversy]]. Within the commentary
ranges ingested here, Hartley states no explicit verdict and surveys no proponents of interpolation —
that material, if present, lies in the volume's introduction or footnotes not captured in this pass.
What he does supply is a sustained *functional* argument for integration: Elihu is *"intricately tied
to the entire book of Job,"* he is structurally necessary to preserve God's initiative, his four
speeches outnumber each comforter's three, and his prose introduction is treated as deliberate
characterization rather than a redactional seam (*"Since Elihu had not been mentioned in the
prologue, the author introduces him in a prose section… a foreshadowing of Elihu's style"*). Job's
silence afterward is likewise plot, not seam: *"it must not then be thought that Elihu has reduced
Job to silence."* Hartley's practical position is retention; his stated position on the authenticity
debate is not recoverable from what has been read.

Hartley offers no argument about Elihu's historicity. He is a character in a work set, by authorial
design, east of Canaan in the patriarchal period.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Within the ingested source Elihu's importance is that he reorganizes the book's central question:
Hartley credits him with *"a significant reshaping of the doctrine of retribution"* and with making
*"a significant contribution to the core issue of the book, namely, how the righteous should respond
to suffering."* His mediating-angel teaching is the point at which Job's scattered hopes for a
witness (16:19), an umpire (9:33), and a redeemer (19:25) are, on Hartley's reading, given a single
referent. His concluding equation of the fear of the Lord with wisdom ties the Elihu block to the
hymn of ch. 28 and forward to the Yahweh speeches, where Hartley hears the same conclusion echoed
again. On the reception side, Hartley reports and resists an early Christian tradition of reading
Elihu as arrogant, and catalogues but declines the christological identification of his mediator.
