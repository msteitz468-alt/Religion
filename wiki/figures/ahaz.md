Ahaz, king of Judah during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis (735–732 BCE), father of
[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]], and the negative pole of the book of [[isaiah|Isaiah]]'s theology of
trust.

## Biographical Overview

Ahaz's accession in 735 marked "a new pro-Assyrian foreign policy" (Oswalt), which provoked
the attack by Pekah of Israel and Rezin of Damascus (with concurrent Edomite and Philistine
incursions). "Terrified by the Syro-Israelite threat (Isa 7:2)," he appealed to
Tiglath-pileser III — for Oswalt "like one mouse asking a cat for help against another
mouse." After Damascus fell in 732 he was summoned there and bound into a treaty that
"required a recognition of the Assyrian gods," yielding the Assyrianizing altar of
2 Kings 16:10–16. Oswalt notes the irony of Isa 7:14–16: the respite Ahaz bought "would
have been his in any event."

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Isaiah 7**: the confrontation at the conduit of the upper pool; the offered sign "as
  deep as Sheol or as high as the heights"; Ahaz's refusal ("I will not ask, and I will not
  put the LORD to the test"); the Immanuel oracle.
- **2 Kings 16 / 2 Chronicles 28**: the Assyrian appeal, the Damascus altar, cultic
  innovations.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Oswalt (evangelical)**: Ahaz's refusal of the sign is not piety but "an unbelief profound
enough that it will not even permit evidence that it is wrong" — his citation of Deut 6:16
takes "the scriptural statement out of its context." The shift from "your God" (7:11) to
Isaiah's "my God" (7:13) is "ominous": Ahaz "has alienated himself and his house from God."
Oswalt sympathetically reports the Delitzsch/G. A. Smith view that 7:12 is "the turning
point in the fortunes of the house of David," after which "the only hope is in God's
eventual miraculous provision." In the book's pedagogical structure Ahaz fails the
"pre-test" of trust that Hezekiah later passes (7:3 // 36:2, same location, same dynasty,
worse odds). "Ahaz believed that God was not trustworthy"; and "to pretend to be king in any
absolute sense is to be truly powerless, as Ahaz learned."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Ahaz embodies Oswalt's axiom "Whatever a man trusts in place of God will one day turn to
devour him": the Assyria he trusted became the razor "hired from beyond the River" turned on
Judah (7:20). His unbelief is the narrative occasion for the Immanuel sign — the pivot from
the reigning Davidic house to the coming child of 7:14; 9:6; 11:1.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Chronology is contested (death 727 vs 716/15, tied to the Hezekiah accession problem);
Thiele's co-regency proposals are reported by Oswalt. The identity and timing of the
Immanuel child given to Ahaz's generation is a live crux — see [[isaiah|Isaiah]] (Key
Interpretive Controversies).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

The Ahaz–Hezekiah contrast structures much later commentary on Isa 7–39 (so Oswalt,
following the book's own design). Cross-links: [[isaiah-prophet|Isaiah (prophet)]],
[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]], [[assyrians|Assyrians]].
