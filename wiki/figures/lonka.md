# Lonka

Lonka (also known as **Lonka Sah**) is "one of the few more mysterious individuals in Jainism" (Dundas, ch. 9) — a fifteenth-century Śvetāmbara layman from Gujarat whose rejection of image worship on scriptural grounds inspired the two principal Śvetāmbara aniconic sub-sects, the [[sthanakvasi|Sthānakvāsīs]] and the [[terapanth|Terapanthīs]], though neither traces its pupillary descent directly to him.

## Biographical Overview

Almost nothing about Lonka's life can be stated with confidence. Dundas describes the traditional Sthānakvāsī picture — "a rich, mighty and learned layman, with powerful connections among the Muslim authorities in Ahmedabad, whose skills in calligraphy led to an invitation to copy the scriptures, as a result of the serious study of which he became convinced that the practice of Jainism he saw around him was without any textual basis and totally corrupt" — as "the product of little more than fanciful speculation" (ch. 9).

Key uncertainties include:
- **Date of birth**: ranges in sources from 1418 to 1425 CE.
- **Place of birth**: disputed; Jñānsundar (see below) argues Limbdi rather than Ahmedabad; other sources suggest Sirohi in south Rajasthan.
- **Date of death**: "most likely about 1475" (Dundas, ch. 9).

The earliest accounts of Lonka are given by opponents such as Dharmasāgara, a Tapa Gaccha polemicist who lived well after him; no contemporary followers produced a biography. The Sthānakvāsīs themselves did not begin writing seriously about Lonka until the early nineteenth century.

## Doctrinal Position

Lonka's central conviction, derived from his study of the Śvetāmbara scriptures (particularly texts like the *Ācārāṅga* and *Daśavaikālika*), was that image worship had no genuine textual basis and was incompatible with authentic Jainism. He also objected to much ritual of all kinds. Malvaniya (see below) argues his rejection developed incrementally, "with his initial rejection of image worship being succeeded, as his familiarity with the scriptures increased, by a refusal to accept the relevance of other aspects of Jain religious practice" (Dundas, ch. 9). Lonka himself never took formal ascetic initiation but gave up the householder's life and became a mendicant. His followers styled themselves "the followers of the path of Mahāvīra."

**Canonical truncation.** "Tradition presents Lonka as rejecting the authority of thirteen (or fourteen) texts which had become accepted into the image-worshipping version of the Śvetāmbara scriptural canon," with the result that both the Sthānakvāsīs and Terapanthīs claim "only thirty-two texts have genuine authority" (Dundas, ch. 9). Where image worship does appear in these thirty-two texts, the standard interpretive strategy was to reread the Ardhamāgadhī term *ceiya* (conventionally "temple") as "monk" or another sense satisfying sectarian criteria.

## The Lonka Gaccha

Lonka's immediate followers founded the Lonka Gaccha, apparently in the 1470s. The group did not long preserve its founder's aniconic principles: in 1572, a prominent Lonka Gaccha *ācārya* converted publicly to image worship along with several hundred monks. "Eventually the Lonka Gaccha, while keeping the name of its founding teacher, abandoned his teachings about a century after his death" (Dundas, ch. 9). Vestiges of the original stance persisted in ordering practice (visiting the *upāśraya* before the temple), but by 1644 even communal table-fellowship with image-worshippers was becoming normalised. Today the Lonka Gaccha "has no fully initiated ascetics and exists only at the fringes of Śvetāmbara society, most notably in Baroda" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Historiographical Controversy: Jñānsundar vs. Malvaniya

Two twentieth-century scholars offer competing assessments:

**Muni Jñānsundar** (1936): a Tapa Gaccha image-worshipping monk and pugnacious controversialist. He argued that Lonka was driven by poverty to Ahmedabad, worked as an "undistinguished scribe" with insufficient learning to read Ardhamāgadhī, and formulated his anti-image doctrine through contact with Muslims who were then destroying Jain shrines. Neither Lonka nor his immediate followers wrote anything, and the biography supposedly dating from the sixteenth century and written by a Tapa Gaccha monk is "on linguistic and circumstantial grounds most likely a forgery" (Dundas, ch. 9). Jñānsundar further concludes Lonka did not die the religious death of *sallekhanā*, contra Sthānakvāsī claims.

**Pandit Dalsukh Malvaniya** (1910–2000): a Sthānakvāsī layman and distinguished scholar of the Jain intellectual tradition. More positive, he accepts the unanimity of tradition that Lonka was a scribe who studied the *Ācārāṅga* and *Daśavaikālika* and adduces two manuscript works held at the Lalbhai Dalpatbhai Institute of Indology in Ahmedabad as possibly written by Lonka or someone close to him, showing "close familiarity with the Śvetāmbara scriptures, as can be seen from copious quotations from them" (Dundas, ch. 9). Malvaniya also allows (as does Jñānsundar) that Muslim influence may have shaped Lonka's aniconic tendency.

Dundas's own conclusion: "there is however no real need to invoke, as do both Jhānsundar and Malvaniya, Muslim influence to explain his aniconic tendencies. From a strictly doctrinal point of view, Lonka was in a sense correct, both because image worship is hardly an important theme in the scriptures … and also on the grounds that there are scriptural statements pointing to the destruction of life-forms entailed in the construction of any building" (ch. 9).

## Influence

Lonka is the ultimate — if indirect — inspiration for both the [[sthanakvasi|Sthānakvāsī]] and [[terapanth|Terapanth]] traditions. Neither, however, considers him a founder or traces lineage to him. He is nonetheless "held to be a great precursor of the Sthānakvāsī interpretation of Jainism" (Dundas, ch. 9).
