Eglon, king of [[moabites|Moab]], is the oppressor of Israel in the second cycle of
[[judges|Judges]] and the victim of the book's most grotesque killing: a very fat king stabbed in
the belly in his own cool roof chamber by [[ehud|Ehud]] the left-handed Benjaminite, with the fat
closing over the blade and the dung coming out.

## Biographical Overview

Eglon appears only as an antagonist and only within Judg 3:12-30. The narrative gives him no
genealogy, no capital, and no reign length of his own; what it gives instead is a function and a
body. Israel "again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD," and YHWH **strengthened** Eglon
against Israel — the oppressor is explicitly raised up by Israel's own God as the instrument of
discipline (3:12). Eglon gathers the [[ammonites|Ammonites]] and the [[amalekites|Amalekites]] to
him, takes the City of Palms, and Israel serves him **eighteen years** (3:13-14).

He is described in 3:17 as *ʾîš bārîʾ məʾōd* — a very fat man. The detail is not incidental
color; it is the mechanism of his death and the engine of the episode's derision. His name,
formed on the same consonants as the Hebrew word for calf (*ʿēgel*), stands in the text beside
that description; whether the pun is deliberate is a matter of interpretation, not of anything the
narrative states.

Ehud brings the tribute, dismisses the bearers, turns back from the sculptured stones at Gilgal,
and requests a private word. Eglon dismisses his attendants and rises from his seat when Ehud says
"I have a message from God for you." Ehud draws the cubit-long two-edged sword from his right
thigh with his left hand and drives it into Eglon's belly: the hilt goes in after the blade, the
fat closes over it so that Ehud cannot withdraw it, and the dung comes out (3:21-22). Ehud locks
the doors of the roof chamber and goes out through the porch. The servants, finding the doors
locked, wait "to the point of embarrassment," supposing the king is relieving himself in the cool
chamber; only when they finally take the key and open the doors do they find their lord fallen
dead on the floor (3:24-25). By then Ehud has escaped, blown the trumpet in the hill country of
Ephraim, and seized the fords of the Jordan; about ten thousand Moabites, "all strong, able-bodied
men," are killed, and none escapes. The land has rest eighty years — the longest rest in the book.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judg 3:12-14** — YHWH strengthens Eglon against Israel; the coalition with Ammon and Amalek;
  the seizure of the City of Palms; eighteen years of Israelite servitude.
- **Judg 3:15-19** — the tribute embassy, Ehud's concealed sword, the dismissal of the tribute
  bearers, the turn back at the sculptured stones near Gilgal.
- **Judg 3:20-25** — the private audience in the roof chamber, the killing, the fat closing over
  the blade, the dung, the locked doors, the servants' delay, the discovery of the body.
- **Judg 3:26-30** — the Moabite rout at the Jordan fords and the eighty years of rest.

Eglon is named nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible or the New Testament. The town of Eglon among the
Amorite cities of [[book-of-joshua|Joshua]] 10 is an unrelated place-name sharing the same
consonants; the wiki keeps them distinct.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Reception material specific to Eglon in this collection is **thin**. No rabbinic, patristic, or
Islamic reception source on this figure has been ingested, and nothing is recorded here beyond
what the collection supports.

- **[[barry-webb|Webb]] (NICOT Judges, 2012)**, as recorded on [[ehud|the Ehud page]] and
  [[judges|the Judges page]]: humor and satire in Judges — the Ehud/Eglon episode named among the
  book's satirical set pieces alongside Sisera's mother and the millstone death — **sharpen rather
  than undercut** the book's theology. Webb reads Judges as a downward spiral rather than a flat
  cycle, and places this episode early, before the moral compromise that overtakes the later
  deliverers. Recorded as Webb's position.
- Recorded on [[judges|the Judges page]]: the narrative's coarse humor makes the point that
  tyrants are ridiculous and that YHWH can work through subversive means.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Eglon is the clearest case in Judges of the book's own theology of oppression: the oppressor is
strengthened *by YHWH* (3:12) as judgment on Israel's apostasy, and then removed when Israel cries
out. He is a divine instrument who is nevertheless not spared. The narrative's treatment of his
body — obese, penetrated, voiding — refuses the dignity ordinarily accorded to a king's death, and
the servants' embarrassed misreading of the locked door extends the indignity past the moment of
death itself. The text does not moralize about this; it simply lets the tyrant be ridiculous.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **No extrabiblical attestation.** No Moabite, Egyptian, or Mesopotamian source known to this
  collection names Eglon, and the chronology of the judges period is disputed in scholarship
  generally (see [[judges|Judges]] on the Deuteronomistic History debate). The wiki records no
  ingested source adjudicating his historicity.
- **The name-pun.** Whether *ʿeglôn* is meant to evoke "fatted calf" and so to mark the king as a
  sacrificial animal is a reading, not a statement of the text. Recorded as an interpretive option;
  the wiki does not attribute it to any commentator it has not ingested.
- **The ethics of the assassination.** Treated in the ingested material only under the broader
  heading of Judges' violence, not as a dedicated dispute — see [[ehud|Ehud]].

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Eglon's chief afterlife in commentary is as the test case for whether narrative crudity in
Scripture is theologically load-bearing. Within the wiki's holdings that debate is framed by the
