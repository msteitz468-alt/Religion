Ehud son of Gera, a left-handed Benjaminite, is the second judge in [[judges|Judges]] and the
deliverer of Israel from eighteen years of Moabite domination. His story (Judg 3:12–30) is the
book's most physically graphic assassination narrative: a concealed sword, a private audience, a
king's belly, and a locked upper room.

## Biographical Overview

Ehud belongs to [[israelites|Israel]]'s tribe of Benjamin and is described as "restricted /
bound as to his right hand" — conventionally rendered left-handed, the very trait that lets him
carry a weapon on the side a searcher would not check. He appears without genealogy beyond his
father Gera and without a call narrative; the text says only that YHWH "raised up a deliverer"
after Israel cried out (3:15). Israel had again done evil, and YHWH had strengthened [[eglon|Eglon]] king of
[[moabites|Moab]], who with Ammonite and Amalekite allies seized the City of Palms and ruled
Israel eighteen years.

Ehud makes a two-edged sword a cubit long, straps it under his clothes on his right thigh, and
delivers Israel's tribute. He dismisses the bearers, returns from the sculptured stones at Gilgal,
and tells Eglon he has a secret message for him. Alone with the king in the cool roof chamber, he
says "I have a message from God for you," and as Eglon rises Ehud draws the sword with his left
hand and drives it into the king's belly. The fat closes over the blade, the hilt goes in after
it, and the dung comes out (3:21–22). Ehud locks the doors and leaves by the porch while the
servants wait outside, supposing the king is relieving himself in the cool chamber. He escapes
past the stones, blows the trumpet in the hill country of Ephraim, and seizes the fords of the
Jordan against Moab; about ten thousand Moabites — "all strong, able-bodied men" — are killed, and
none escapes. The land has rest eighty years (3:26–30), the longest rest period recorded in the
book. Shamgar's brief notice of six hundred [[philistines|Philistines]] killed with an oxgoad
(3:31) follows immediately.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 3:12–30** — the whole of Ehud's career: Moabite oppression under Eglon, the tribute
  embassy, the concealed sword, the assassination in the roof chamber, the escape, the seizure of
  the Jordan fords, the slaughter of ten thousand Moabites, and eighty years of rest.
- **Judges 4:1** — Ehud's death is the hinge into the [[deborah|Deborah]]–[[barak|Barak]] cycle:
  "the Israelites again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, after Ehud died."

Ehud is not named in the New Testament faith list of Hebrews 11:32, which cites [[gideon|Gideon]],
Barak, [[samson|Samson]], and Jephthah.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Reception material on Ehud in this collection is **thin**; only one ingested commentary treats him
directly, and no rabbinic, patristic, or Islamic reception source on this figure has been ingested.

- **[[barry-webb|Webb]] (NICOT, 2012)**: humor and satire in Judges — Webb names the Ehud episode
  alongside Sisera's mother and the millstone death — **sharpen rather than undercut** the book's
  theology. Webb reads Judges as a downward spiral rather than a flat cycle, placing Ehud early,
  before the moral compromise that overtakes the later deliverers.
- Recorded on the [[judges|Judges]] page (Key Episodes): the narrative's coarse humor makes the
  point that tyrants are ridiculous and that YHWH can work through subversive means — the fat
  absorbs the sword, and the servants wait in embarrassed confusion outside a locked door.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Ehud stands in the still-favorable early stretch of the judges sequence: like [[othniel|Othniel]]
before him he delivers and secures rest, and the narrative registers no censure of him. The
deliverance runs through a disability turned to advantage and through deception rather than
pitched battle — YHWH's word ("I have a message from God for you") arrives as a blade. The eighty
years of rest is the book's high-water mark, and its erosion after Ehud's death (4:1) is part of
the descending pattern.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

No extrabiblical source names Ehud or Eglon, and the chronology of the judges period is disputed
in scholarship generally (see [[judges|Judges]] on the Deuteronomistic History debate and Webb's
modified acceptance of it). This wiki records no source specifically adjudicating Ehud's
historicity. The ethical question raised by an assassin-deliverer is treated in the ingested
material only under the broader heading of Judges' violence taxonomy (Webb distinguishes
liberation war, vendetta, and civil war) rather than as a dedicated dispute.
