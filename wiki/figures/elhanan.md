Elhanan is a Bethlehemite warrior in [[david|David]]'s service who is credited, in the Masoretic
text of **2 Sam 21:19**, with killing **[[goliath|Goliath]] the Gittite** — the same champion whom
[[1-samuel|1 Samuel 17]] at length credits to David — and, in **1 Chr 20:5**, with killing
**Lahmi the brother of Goliath**. He is therefore less a biography than a textual crux. The
positions are recorded, unresolved, at
[[who-killed-goliath|Who Killed Goliath — and How Tall Was He?]]; this page does not adjudicate
between them.

## Biographical Overview

The text tells almost nothing about Elhanan beyond patronymic, town, and a kill. 2 Sam 21:19 names
him **Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite**; the parallel at 1 Chr 20:5 names him
**Elhanan son of Jair**. The differing patronymics are themselves part of the textual problem: the
Hebrew behind "Jaare-oregim" contains the word *ʾōrəgîm*, "weavers," which also appears in the
following clause describing the spear shaft "like a weaver's beam," and the doubling is one of the
standard observations in discussion of the verse.

He appears in a short catalogue of four Philistine giants killed by David's warriors (2 Sam
21:15-22 // 1 Chr 20:4-8): Ishbi-benob, whom Abishai kills after David tires; Saph (Sippai);
Elhanan's opponent; and an unnamed man of great stature with six fingers on each hand and six toes
on each foot, killed by Jonathan son of Shimei, David's brother. The unit closes: "These four were
descended from the giants in Gath, and they fell by the hand of David and by the hand of his
servants."

A second Elhanan appears in the roster of David's Thirty at 2 Sam 23:24 // 1 Chr 11:26 —
**Elhanan son of Dodo of Bethlehem**. Whether this is the same man under a different patronymic or
a second Bethlehemite of the same name is not stated by the text and is not settled here.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Sam 21:19 (MT)** — "Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite killed Goliath the Gittite,
  the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam."
- **1 Chr 20:5** — "Elhanan son of Jair killed Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite, the shaft
  of whose spear was like a weaver's beam."
- **2 Sam 21:15-22 // 1 Chr 20:4-8** — the giant-slayer catalogue in which the notice sits.
- **2 Sam 23:24 // 1 Chr 11:26** — Elhanan son of Dodo of Bethlehem, among the Thirty; identity
  with the above unresolved.
- **[[1-samuel|1 Sam 17]]** — the long narrative crediting the killing of Goliath to David. The
  relation of this chapter to 2 Sam 21:19 is the substance of the dispute.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Reception material naming Elhanan specifically is **thin** in this collection; no rabbinic,
patristic, or Islamic source on him has been ingested. What the wiki holds is method, not
Elhanan-specific exegesis.

- **Traditional harmonization (Jewish and later Christian exegesis)**: Elhanan is taken as a
  personal or birth name of David himself, paired with the throne-name. Recorded on
  [[who-killed-goliath|the controversy page]] as a position held, not as a finding of this wiki.
- **Confessional and much text-critical treatment**: 1 Chr 20:5 preserves the original wording and
  2 Sam 21:19 is a scribal corruption in which "Lahmi the brother of" dropped out. Recorded as a
  position.
- **Historical-critical reconstruction**: 2 Sam 21:19 preserves the older tradition and the Goliath
  duel was later attached to David, with Chronicles as the harmonization. Recorded as a position.
- **Minority harmonization**: two distinct Philistines named Goliath.
- **[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] (NICOT 1 and 2 Samuel)**: as recorded on
  [[who-killed-goliath|the controversy page]], the ingested Samuel commentaries in this collection
  take a **text-critically conservative, MT-first** approach, resisting easy emendation toward LXX
  or 4QSam readings. That is a stated method, and the wiki records it as such rather than as a
  ruling on this verse.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within [[2-samuel|2 Samuel]]'s own shape the Elhanan notice belongs to an appendix that
deliberately distributes David's glory: the giants fall "by the hand of David **and by the hand of
his servants**," and in the same unit David himself grows tired and must be rescued and is then
barred by his men from going out to battle, "lest you quench the lamp of Israel." Read on its own
terms, the passage is about the king's champions inheriting the king's work as the king ages —
a narrative function that does not depend on resolving the textual question, and one that the
1 Chronicles parallel preserves in its own way.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The whole of Elhanan's significance is controversial, and the wiki's controversy page is the
governing record: **[[who-killed-goliath|Who Killed Goliath — and How Tall Was He?]]**, classified
there as `dispute_type: source-reliability`, `resolution_status: open`.

Four positions are recorded there and none is adopted here:

1. Chronicles preserves the original; 2 Sam 21:19 MT is damaged.
2. 2 Sam 21:19 MT preserves the older tradition; the duel was later attached to David.
3. Elhanan is an alternative name for David.
4. Two distinct Philistines named Goliath.

As the controversy page notes, positions 1 and 2 are **mirror images** — each treats the other's
preferred witness as secondary — and the manuscript evidence alone does not settle which is the
corruption. The controversy page also flags that this collection currently holds only the
MT-first Samuel commentaries and **no source arguing the historical-critical reconstruction at
length**, and that this is a **coverage gap, not a verdict**. The same caution governs this page.

The secondary questions — whether "Jaare-oregim" is a corrupt patronymic, and whether the Elhanan
of 2 Sam 23:24 is the same man — are noted above and likewise left open.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Elhanan is a standing exhibit in three long-running discussions: the relationship between Samuel
and Chronicles as parallel accounts; the status of the Masoretic text of Samuel against the
versions and the Qumran evidence; and the growth of the David tradition. He appears in commentary
far more often as a problem than as a person. Cross-links:
