# Sītā

**Sītā** (the word means "furrow") is the wife of **[[rama|Rāma]]** and the central female figure of
the **[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]**. She is simultaneously the ideal Hindu wife (*pativratā*), an earthborn
goddess (daughter of the Earth herself), and — in Doniger's reading — a figure of "supreme, defiant
protest" whose repeated ordeals are acts of resistance, not submission.

## Biographical Overview

### Birth

Sītā was born when her father, King Janaka of Videha, was plowing a sacrificial field. His semen fell
on the ground at the sight of the celestial nymph Menakā, and Sītā arose from the furrow: "Since she
arose from the surface of the earth, and was born from no womb, she is called Sītā, the Furrow." She
is thus **born of no womb** (*ayonijā*) — an elemental being, the earth's daughter. Her birth and
her final vanishing are both framed by sacrifices: she is born in the plowing of a sacrificial arena
and disappears during Rāma's horse sacrifice.

### Marriage and Exile

Sītā marries Rāma after he alone lifts Janaka's great bow. She insists on accompanying him into
fourteen years of forest exile, despite his attempts to leave her behind — telling him he is "like
a procurer, handing me over to other people, though I came to you a virgin."

### Abduction and Ordeal

In the forest, Sītā is abducted by Rāvaṇa, king of the ogres, who has been lured there through a
golden deer (an ogre in disguise) that she asks Rāma to capture. She lives captive in Laṅkā, refusing
Rāvaṇa's advances — insisting, "A mortal woman cannot become the wife of an ogre."

After Rāma defeats Rāvaṇa and recovers Sītā, he doubts her chastity and makes her **enter a fire**
to prove her purity before the assembled court. She does so — entering the fire as a protest ("that
is the medicine for this calamity") rather than a suicide. Fire (Agni) returns her to Rāma's arms
as a witnessed attestation of her purity.

### Banishments

Rumors return after their return to Ayodhyā. Rāma banishes the pregnant Sītā to Valmiki's hermitage,
where she gives birth to twin sons Kuśa and Lava. At Rāma's horse sacrifice years later, the twins
recite the Rāmāyaṇa (as Valmiki has taught it). Rāma summons Sītā to publicly swear her innocence
once more. Valmiki does so; Sītā swears her fidelity — "As my heart never wavered from Rāma, let
the goddess Earth receive me" — and **descends into the earth**, returning to her mother. Rāma cannot
bring her back.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Rāmāyaṇa (Valmiki)** — the primary source; Sītā appears in all seven books.
- **Mahābhārata** — when Rāma throws Sītā out the first time, she is compared to "an oblation that
  a dog has licked" (3.275.14). Draupadi's polyandry is contrasted with Sītā's model of monogamous
  fidelity.

## Goddess Status

Sītā is an **ex-goddess** — a being with divine origins that the story does not erase but largely
ignores while it foregrounds her human vulnerability. She is explicitly associated with the **equine
goddess pattern** (Saraṇyū, Urvaśī): a goddess who comes from another world to a mortal king, bears
him children (twins), is mistreated, and leaves him forever, with only the twins as consolation.
**Śakuntalā** (from the Mahābhārata and Kālidāsa's play) is her structural "cousin" in this pattern —
born of a nymph (*apsarā*), mother of the great king Bharata, abandoned by Duṣyanta. Doniger argues
that Kālidāsa's Gupta-era retelling systematically softened Śakuntalā by substituting a curse for
Duṣyanta's deliberate repudiation — part of a broader Gupta literary program of stripping equine-goddess
figures of their protest and independent agency (Ch. 14 note).

She descends into the earth when her husband "violates the contract" by mistreating her — a
contractual release, not a defeat. Rāma's return to heaven as Viṣṇu is described in rich detail;
Sītā is not there. They "revert to their divine status, but in opposite places."

Brahma tells Rāma that Sītā is an incarnation of Lakṣmī; however, the text never shows their
heavenly reunion — an absence Doniger marks as significant.

Later tradition worships Sītā as the ideal wife; in South India she has independent cult followings.

## Doniger's Reading (Modern Critical)

Doniger reads Sītā as a figure systematically misread by the Brahmin imaginary, which edited out her
assertiveness to make her the "ideal subservient wife." The **Valmiki Rāmāyaṇa** in fact shows a
Sītā who:
- Bullies Rāma when she thinks he has made a mistake ("You are like a procurer…").
- Questions his violence: warns him that carrying weapons in an ascetic's attire will put "wicked
  thoughts in his mind."
- Names her own comparison class — "some women who do that sort of thing" — making explicit that she
  knows she is being confused with unfaithful women.
- Performs the fire ordeal as an "anti-suttee": entering fire while her husband is very much alive,
  as **a threat or ultimatum**, not as self-sacrifice.
- Abandons Rāma in the end — and blames him implicitly — by entering the earth.

"How different the lives of actual women in India would have been had Sita as she is actually portrayed
in Valmiki's Ramayana been their official role model."

The shadow-women of the text (Kaikeyī, Śūrpaṇakhā, Ṭāṭakā) represent the "inner ogresses" the
Brahmin tradition had to suppress in order to construct Sītā as the perfect wife — "to keep those
ogresses shackled."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Sītā is the narrative engine of the Rāmāyaṇa: "Sītā, not Rāma, is primarily responsible for the
death of Rāvaṇa." The gods describe her as "a woman, Sītā the slayer of ogres" who will destroy
them. Rāvaṇa's brother Vibhīṣaṇa sees her as a "great serpent in the form of a woman."

Her suffering as a mortal expresses the divine plan's paradox: she must be mortal (to be abducted
and held captive, generating the conditions for Rāvaṇa's defeat) and immortal (to return, purified,
from the fire). The tension is never resolved; it is embodied.

## Controversies

- **Did the fire ordeal prove or merely theatricalize chastity?** Rāma says he knew all along that
  she was pure; she had to enter fire "for everyone's sake." The text itself undercuts this by showing
  Rāma's genuinely tortured ambivalence.
- **The second banishment.** The banishment of a pregnant Sītā to appease popular gossip is morally
  indefensible by any reading of dharma; the text never justifies it, only narrates it.
- **The identity of the "shadow Sītas."** Regional traditions and later retellings multiply Sītā
  figures (a shadow Sītā is substituted before the abduction in some versions); the "real" Sītā's
  location during the Lanka captivity is disputed across traditions.
