# Sītā

**Sītā** (the word means "furrow") is the wife of **[[rama|Rāma]]** and the central female figure of
the **[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]**. She is simultaneously the ideal Hindu wife (*pativratā*), an earthborn
goddess (daughter of the Earth herself), and — in Doniger's reading — a figure of "supreme, defiant
protest" whose repeated ordeals are acts of resistance, not submission.

## Biographical Overview

### Birth

Sītā was born when her father, King Janaka of Videha, was plowing a sacrificial field. His semen fell
on the ground at the sight of the celestial nymph Menakā, and Sītā arose from the furrow: "Since she
arose from the surface of the earth, and was born from no womb, she is called Sītā, the Furrow." She
is thus **born of no womb** (*ayonijā*) — an elemental being, the earth's daughter. Her birth and
her final vanishing are both framed by sacrifices: she is born in the plowing of a sacrificial arena
and disappears during Rāma's horse sacrifice.

### Marriage and Exile

Sītā marries Rāma after he alone lifts Janaka's great bow. She insists on accompanying him into
fourteen years of forest exile, despite his attempts to leave her behind — telling him he is "like
a procurer, handing me over to other people, though I came to you a virgin."

### Abduction and Ordeal

In the forest, Sītā is abducted by Rāvaṇa, king of the ogres, who has been lured there through a
golden deer (an ogre in disguise) that she asks Rāma to capture. She lives captive in Laṅkā, refusing
Rāvaṇa's advances — insisting, "A mortal woman cannot become the wife of an ogre."

After Rāma defeats Rāvaṇa and recovers Sītā, he doubts her chastity and makes her **enter a fire**
to prove her purity before the assembled court. She does so — entering the fire as a protest ("that
is the medicine for this calamity") rather than a suicide. Fire (Agni) returns her to Rāma's arms
as a witnessed attestation of her purity.

### Banishments

Rumors return after their return to Ayodhyā. Rāma banishes the pregnant Sītā to Valmiki's hermitage,
where she gives birth to twin sons Kuśa and Lava. At Rāma's horse sacrifice years later, the twins
recite the Rāmāyaṇa (as Valmiki has taught it). Rāma summons Sītā to publicly swear her innocence
once more. Valmiki does so; Sītā swears her fidelity — "As my heart never wavered from Rāma, let
the goddess Earth receive me" — and **descends into the earth**, returning to her mother. Rāma cannot
bring her back.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Rāmāyaṇa (Valmiki)** — the primary source; Sītā appears in all seven books.
- **Mahābhārata** — when Rāma throws Sītā out the first time, she is compared to "an oblation that
  a dog has licked" (3.275.14). Draupadi's polyandry is contrasted with Sītā's model of monogamous
  fidelity.

## Goddess Status

Sītā is an **ex-goddess** — a being with divine origins that the story does not erase but largely
ignores while it foregrounds her human vulnerability. She is explicitly associated with the **equine
goddess pattern** (Saraṇyū, Urvaśī): a goddess who comes from another world to a mortal king, bears
him children (twins), is mistreated, and leaves him forever, with only the twins as consolation.
**Śakuntalā** (from the Mahābhārata and Kālidāsa's play) is her structural "cousin" in this pattern —
born of a nymph (*apsarā*), mother of the great king Bharata, abandoned by Duṣyanta. Doniger argues
that Kālidāsa's Gupta-era retelling systematically softened Śakuntalā by substituting a curse for
Duṣyanta's deliberate repudiation — part of a broader Gupta literary program of stripping equine-goddess
figures of their protest and independent agency (Ch. 14 note).

She descends into the earth when her husband "violates the contract" by mistreating her — a
contractual release, not a defeat. Rāma's return to heaven as Viṣṇu is described in rich detail;
Sītā is not there. They "revert to their divine status, but in opposite places."

Brahma tells Rāma that Sītā is an incarnation of Lakṣmī; however, the text never shows their
heavenly reunion — an absence Doniger marks as significant.

Later tradition worships Sītā as the ideal wife; in South India she has independent cult followings.

## Doniger's Reading (Modern Critical)

Doniger reads Sītā as a figure systematically misread by the Brahmin imaginary, which edited out her
assertiveness to make her the "ideal subservient wife." The **Valmiki Rāmāyaṇa** in fact shows a
Sītā who:
- Bullies Rāma when she thinks he has made a mistake ("You are like a procurer…").
- Questions his violence: warns him that carrying weapons in an ascetic's attire will put "wicked
  thoughts in his mind."
- Names her own comparison class — "some women who do that sort of thing" — making explicit that she
  knows she is being confused with unfaithful women.
- Performs the fire ordeal as an "anti-suttee": entering fire while her husband is very much alive,
  as **a threat or ultimatum**, not as self-sacrifice.
- Abandons Rāma in the end — and blames him implicitly — by entering the earth.

"How different the lives of actual women in India would have been had Sita as she is actually portrayed
in Valmiki's Ramayana been their official role model."

The shadow-women of the text (Kaikeyī, Śūrpaṇakhā, Ṭāṭakā) represent the "inner ogresses" the
Brahmin tradition had to suppress in order to construct Sītā as the perfect wife — "to keep those
ogresses shackled."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Sītā is the narrative engine of the Rāmāyaṇa: "Sītā, not Rāma, is primarily responsible for the
death of Rāvaṇa." The gods describe her as "a woman, Sītā the slayer of ogres" who will destroy
them. Rāvaṇa's brother Vibhīṣaṇa sees her as a "great serpent in the form of a woman."

Her suffering as a mortal expresses the divine plan's paradox: she must be mortal (to be abducted
and held captive, generating the conditions for Rāvaṇa's defeat) and immortal (to return, purified,
from the fire). The tension is never resolved; it is embodied.

## Controversies

- **Did the fire ordeal prove or merely theatricalize chastity?** Rāma says he knew all along that
  she was pure; she had to enter fire "for everyone's sake." The text itself undercuts this by showing
  Rāma's genuinely tortured ambivalence.
- **The second banishment.** The banishment of a pregnant Sītā to appease popular gossip is morally
  indefensible by any reading of dharma; the text never justifies it, only narrates it.
- **The identity of the "shadow Sītas."** Regional traditions and later retellings multiply Sītā
  figures (a shadow Sītā is substituted before the abduction in some versions); the "real" Sītā's
  location during the Lanka captivity is disputed across traditions.

## William Buck's Retelling: The Abduction and Captivity Opens (Lines 5731–8500)

### The Golden Deer

Sītā sees the golden deer — Maricha the Rakshasa transformed by Rāvaṇa — and is captivated: "Rāma, come quickly. Can you catch him?" (6780–6781). When Lakshmana warns it is an illusion (6783–6784), Sītā pleads for the deer regardless. When Maricha cries out in Rāma's voice, Sītā panics and turns on Lakshmana: "How much has Bharata paid you to betray us?" (6822). When he refuses to leave her unguarded, she threatens to go after Rāma herself: "Then is it me you want? Take me but save Rāma!" (6837). Lakshmana draws a protective circle around her with his bow tip, then leaves. Sītā ignores the instruction and steps over the line to invite the disguised Rāvaṇa inside (6866–6868).

### The Abduction

Sītā's refusal of Rāvaṇa is contemptuous and immediate — comparing him to a gnat, a firefly, a goose beside Garuda (6891–6895). Rāvaṇa seizes her: "In one left hand he held her long dark hair. He caught her legs in two of his right arms and lifted her" (6939–6941). She cries "Rāma! Rāma!" many times and struggles uselessly (6951–6952). As they fly south, she mentally calls on the Godavari river, the forest trees, and the deer — "Tell Rāma. Tell him" (6955–6957) — and calls aloud to the vulture king Jatayu asleep in a treetop (6958–6959). When Jatayu falls dying defending her, Sītā runs to hold his head and weep over him (7012–7013) before Rāvaṇa tears her away.

Flying south, Sītā deliberately drops her ornaments as a trail: she breaks off her anklets, removes her earrings, drops the ornaments given to her by the sage Anasuya, and ties Guha's necklace in her yellow scarf and drops it too (7035–7039). She observes two monkeys (Sugrīva and Hanumān) on a hill below and directs the ornaments toward them — a resourceful act of resistance even in the moment of capture.

### First Days in Lanka

In Rāvaṇa's palace, Sītā maintains complete defiance. When Rāvaṇa tells her "you have no more husband, and so there can be nothing wrong in our love" (7061–7062), she answers: "To Hell with you. You took me like a thief. And barely made it past an old vulture" (7063–7064). She refuses even to enter his quarters: "I won't enter your kennel" (7077). She is imprisoned in the Asoka grove — called "scarlet" by the text (7101) — behind Rāvaṇa's room, guarded by Rakshasi women day and night.

When Indra visits secretly at night, Sītā first drops a little wheat to the ground for Rāma before eating herself: "Whether Rāma lives or has died let this nourish him" (7108–7109). Indra tells her she bears "no sign of widowhood" and that Rāma will rescue her (7231–7233). A divine constraint protects her: Rāvaṇa cannot force any unwilling woman without his ten heads bursting — the curse of Nalakubara (7110–7111, 7206–7207).

## William Buck's Retelling: Key Episodes (Lines 13566–16313)

### The Reunion with Rama — Buck's Fire Scene

Buck's version of the reunion (lines 13865–13937) differs significantly from Valmiki's Sanskrit. In
Buck, there is **no harsh rejection of Sita by Rama**: Agni the Fire God appears beside Sita, takes
her hand, and leads her to Rama. Agni declares her innocence directly: "I am the true witness never
deceived. Again and again you have forgotten your secret. Sita has not the smallest fault, and she is
innocent. Lakshmi of good fortune is your wife, and she loves you" (l. 13887–13891). Indra arrives and
also calls Rama "Narayana" and "Searcher of Hearts" — but Rama responds, "I am Rama, a mortal man"
(l. 13883), declining to acknowledge his divinity. Agni places Sita's hand in Rama's — then draws
back "quick as a serpent's tongue. Rama had burned him!" (l. 13930–13931). Sita herself says: "With
excellent joy, with delight cast looks on me. I am a fair free woman, I surrender to you of my own
will, command me anything" (l. 13932–13934). Rama ties her wedding pearl-leaf back in her hair and
declares, "Now you're mine again, with Fire for the witness" (l. 13935–13937).

The removal of Rama's harsh rejection is Buck's clearest editorial departure from Valmiki's Sanskrit.
(The academic introduction to the Buck edition notes this change explicitly, though those lines fall
outside this passage.) In Buck, the fire scene is one of cosmic reunion — not ordeal, proof, or
protest — with Sita as a willing, self-possessed actor.

### The Second Banishment

After nearly ten thousand years of reign, Sita is pregnant with twins and asks to visit the Ganges
retreats (l. 14800–14806). Rama consults his ministers and learns what "the lowest men" are saying:
"The King desires Sita although Ravana touched her. How could he forget she lived with another? And the
Queen must also remember the Demon King" (l. 14843–14845). Rama summons Lakshmana and orders him to
take Sita to the forest and abandon her there, citing the dharmic principle that a king must be above
reproach: "A king must be blameless. Ill fame is evil to kings; they above all men must be beyond
reproach" (l. 14864–14866). Lakshmana protests — "She will die. Your child will die!" (l. 14879) —
but obeys, saying he cannot willingly disobey Rama since "right and wrong are very subtle and hard to
tell apart, and the Dharma Law is difficult to know" (l. 14896–14898).

Sita is deceived: she believes she is going on a day trip to the forest hermitages as she had wished.
Only at the Ganges does Lakshmana break down and tell her the truth. Sumantra the charioteer provides
the cosmic explanation: Vasishtha long ago cursed Narayana (Vishnu) to be born on earth and be parted
from his wife, because Vishnu had broken sanctuary when fighting drought-demons in the time of
Dasaratha's father — "Vasishtha threw down his staff and cursed Narayana to be born on Earth, in a
royal family rich and wise… and once born to be parted from his wife" (l. 15107–15110). Sumantra tells
Sita: "Suspend your reason and get a glimpse of Eternity… all is spirit, there is nothing else in
existence. War and peace, love and separation are hidden gateways to other worlds" (l. 14955–14965).

Sita weeps but does not resist. Ganga the river goddess tempts her into the water ("Come home, come
home, dive into me"), but Valmiki appears from his anthill and offers shelter (l. 15158–15179).

### Life at Valmiki's Hermitage and the Birth of Kusha and Lava

"For twelve years Sita and her sons lived with Valmiki while he composed this Ramayana" (l. 15193–15194).
The twins were born at night in summer; midwives woke Valmiki, who rushed with fresh-cut kusa grass to
name them: "He rubbed the firstborn with the grass tips and named him Kusa. He rubbed his brother with
the cut grass ends and named him Lava" (l. 15190–15192). Valmiki taught them every verse of the
Ramayana, "and they learned to sing this story to the music of a lute and drum" (l. 15195–15197).

### The Festival of Naimisha and Sita's Departure

After ruling ten thousand years, Rama declares a yearlong thanksgiving festival in Naimisha Forest
(not a horse sacrifice in Buck's retelling — described as "a great public festival, a happy gathering
somewhere in the countryside," l. 15201–15204). He has a golden statue of Sita made to stand beside
him, unable to bring Sita herself back. Kusa and Lava come with Valmiki and sing the Ramayana daily
for a year; on the last day Rama recognizes them ("Those twin brothers are my two sons," l. 15585),
summons Valmiki, and asks for Sita.

### Sita's Return to the Earth

At the festival, Sita stands before Rama surrounded by all the assembled kings and peoples. She says:
"Rama, let me prove my innocence, here before everyone" (l. 15722–15723). She calls on her mother:
"Mother Earth, if I have been faithful to Rama take me home, hide me!" (l. 15727–15728). The earth
opens; four Nagas rise at the corners; Mother Earth herself ascends on a jeweled throne — "not old, she
was fair to look on, she was not sad but smiling" — and takes Sita on her lap: "She folded her beautiful
arms around her daughter and laid Sita's head softly against her shoulder as a mother would. Earth
stroked her hair with her fair hands, and Sita closed her eyes like a little girl" (l. 15753–15757).
They descend underground "forever." The gods declare, "Well done, Oh Sita. Praise to you" (l. 15761).
Rama says: "I am King and Lord of Earth, but she has taken my wife away before my very eyes. I will
never meet Sita again while I live as a man" — and "still he was smiling" (l. 15770–15772).

### Theological Significance: Sita's Suffering and Vindication in Buck

In Buck's retelling, the theological arc of Sita's story is one of progressive withdrawal from an
impossible cosmic situation: Vishnu's avatar is under Vasishtha's curse; the parting of Rama and Sita
was predetermined before Rama's birth. Sita's two modes of self-presentation — as a willing, self-
possessed woman at the reunion ("I am a fair free woman") and as an earth-daughter returning to her
mother — are both triumphant, not defeated. Her descent is explicitly framed as victory: the entire
universe "was everywhere equal… Happiness and great delight spread over the three worlds" (l. 15764–15765).
Mother Earth — "this was the end of her long patience with Rama" (l. 15750) — reclaims her daughter.
Meanwhile Rama's golden statue of Sita is his compromise with an irreversible cosmic fact.

Janaka (Sita's father) frames this theologically at the festival: "Dissolution is the end of all things
compounded out of the elements… Sita has died to this world. May all beings everywhere be happy and
safe!" (l. 15856–15860).

## Sītā in Laṅkā: William Buck's Prose Retelling (Lines 8501–11500)

### Condition in the Asoka Grove

When [[hanuman|Hanumān]] finds Sītā at dawn in the Asoka Grove behind Rāvaṇa's palace, she has been sleeping on bare earth: "a fair woman of the human race arise from sleeping in the dirt under another nearby tree . . . her worn dusty yellow robe covering all her body, her glossy black hair tied in a long braid" (lines 9520–9525). She is "thin from grief and fasting, lean and trembling" (9538) — but still recognizably beautiful: "Even in captivity her every limb was lovely; she was still beautiful to all eyes, as is crisp Autumn's sky after the rain's end" (9543–9544).

### Confronting Rāvaṇa

Every morning Rāvaṇa courts Sītā in the Asoka Grove; she refuses him daily. Key speeches from lines 9551–9611:

- "For your own death you touched me" (9562)
- "You see everything reversed, because you are so near to your own death. If you are really strong, follow Dharma and take me back, no one will harm you" (9572–9574)
- "I am his alone. After the Lord of Men has held me I can never seek another. For you, I am a red-hot iron image awaiting your embrace!" (9583–9585)
- "Then know me for the daughter of a real King and a true man's wife!" (9587–9588)

Behind Rāvaṇa's back, "his other wives, daughters of gods and of great kings, consoled Sītā with their eyes" (9609–9610). The Rakshasi guards threaten her with cannibalistic menace; the only protection is Trijata, the old demoness who reports a prophetic dream and commands the others to seek Sītā's forgiveness. Sītā responds: "When this becomes true, I will save you all" (9702–9703).

### Encounter with Hanumān

Sītā's first reaction to Hanumān's whispered "Rāma-Rāma-" in animal language (9712) is to look up in wonder, then doubt: "Who are you? Now I have let my mind slip into illusion" (9724). She "silently began to cry and tears poured from her eyes" upon hearing Rāma's name (9729). She verifies Hanumān by reading his "inmost thoughts and feelings" from her own heart (9741–9742). Receiving Rāma's ring — inscribed "Rāma, Rāma, Rāma" — she says: "Rāma seemed to be there touching her" (9767–9768), and: "If one can keep on living somehow, real true happiness comes to each of us once in a hundred years! I remember all the good times I'd forgotten!" (9769–9771).

She asks whether Rāma's love has faded (9794–9795); Hanumān answers that "to him the cold nights are like flame and the gentle moonbeams burn him" (9796–9797).

### Refusal to Escape

Hanumān offers to carry her home; Sītā refuses: "No, let Rāma recover me if he will" (9809–9810). The rescue must be Rāma's act structurally — its cosmic meaning depends on who performs it. She asks Hanumān to stay one more night: "When you leave I will miss you, and who knows if you will ever return? It is very hard to enter Laṅkā, and I fear no one but you can cross the sea" (9857–9859). She sends two tokens: a message invoking the crow episode, and the wedding pearl her father tied in her hair on their wedding day. Her final word to Hanumān: "I will live on here for a month and not longer . . . you are loved by me, you are loved by me" (9289–9293).

### Sītā Protects Hanumān by Prayer

When Rāvaṇa orders Hanumān's tail set afire, Sītā goes to a small flame burning in a clay cup and prays: "If I've kept faith, be cool to Hanumān" (10175). The flame bends to the right in assent. "Fire let the Wind be cool right next to his son's tail" (10180) — Sītā's fidelity generates miraculous protective force. The text comments: "How could Fire have burned Sītā? It is she who can burn Fire if she will" (10255–10256) — a statement of Sītā's latent elemental power beneath her captive state.

When Hanumān returns frightened he has burned her, she consoles him and gives the tactical instruction: "if you so heedlessly destroy these demons you will cloud Rāma's fame. Take this to Rāma, get him to act" (10283–10284).

### Rāvaṇa's Illusory Deception

After Rāma's army crosses and assembles outside Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa uses magic to create an illusory severed head of Rāma with his broken bow (lines 11031–11043) and presents it to Sītā claiming Prahasta killed Rāma at midnight. Sītā believes it and begins to let go of her life (11081–11082). She is saved by the sound of actual war drums and the old demoness Trijata, who shakes her back with three repetitions: "Disbelieve it! Disbelieve it! Disbelieve it!" (11128). Three repetitions break through "like welcome rain falling on the hard dry fields of Earth" (11131–11132). The illusory head and bow melt once Rāvaṇa has left the grove (11111).
