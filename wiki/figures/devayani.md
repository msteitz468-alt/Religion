## Biographical Overview

Devayānī was the daughter of Śukrācārya, the preceptor of the asuras, and his only child; her father was devoted to her more than to his life. She appears in the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] in the Kacha-Devayānī and Devayānī-Yayāti episodes that form part of the ancestral genealogy of the Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas.

**The Kacha episode (Chapter IV):** When the devas sent Kacha (son of Bṛhaspati) to Śukrācārya to acquire the secret of the Sañjīvanī (the art of reviving the dead), he took up residence as a pupil in Śukrācārya's house. Devayānī was charmed by him; Kacha cultivated her affection with "song and dance and pastime" while scrupulously maintaining his vows of brahmacarya. The asuras, alarmed, killed Kacha three times. Each time Devayānī went in tears to her father and begged him to revive Kacha using the Sañjīvanī. The third time, the asuras had burnt Kacha's body, mixed the ashes in wine, and served it to Śukrācārya, who drank it unknowingly. When Sañjīvanī was used again, Kacha revived inside Śukrācārya's stomach and could communicate from within; the only exit was to tear through the preceptor's body, which would kill him. Śukrācārya resolved the dilemma by teaching Kacha the Sañjīvanī himself so that Kacha could revive him after emerging. Thus the devas' object was achieved: Kacha obtained the art. When Kacha prepared to depart, Devayānī declared her love and asked him to marry her; Kacha replied that having been reborn from Śukrācārya's body he was in effect her brother, and it was therefore improper for him to wed her. Devayānī's arguments failed; she pronounced a curse that Kacha's brahmavidyā would never avail him — and Kacha in turn said that her curse had no force against one who had not wronged her, and that she would find no brahmin husband (lines 848–989).

**The Sarmishṭhā quarrel and Yayāti (Chapter V):** Devayānī was accustomed to sporting in the forest with Śarmiṣṭhā, the daughter of Vṛṣaparva (king of the asuras). After bathing in a pool, a wind mixed up their garments; Śarmiṣṭhā inadvertently put on Devayānī's clothes. When Devayānī objected, Śarmiṣṭhā turned on her with arrogant contempt — calling her the daughter of a beggar and a sycophant — slapped her, and pushed her into a dry well, believing she had killed her (lines 991–1010).

King Yayāti of the Bharata race came in search of water and found Devayānī in the well; he helped her out, taking her hand. Devayānī declared that because he had taken her right hand, he was obliged to marry her. Yayāti demurred on the grounds that it was improper for a brahmin woman (pratiloma) to marry a kṣatriya, and departed. Devayānī refused to return to the asura capital; Śukrācārya found her and urged patience with wise words about bearing the wrongs of others, but she was implacable: she would not live in the territory of Vṛṣaparva (lines 1011–1063).

Śukrācārya threatened to leave Vṛṣaparva's kingdom; the king came in supplication. Devayānī demanded that Śarmiṣṭhā become her personal handmaid for life. Śarmiṣṭhā accepted, and Devayānī returned home (lines 1063–1122).

When Devayānī met Yayāti again in the forest, she repeated her claim and together they went to Śukrācārya, who gave his consent to the pratiloma match, conceding that a marriage once contracted cannot be made void by the law. She married Yayāti, with Śarmiṣṭhā attending her as promised (lines 1123–1135).

In Yayāti's household, Śarmiṣṭhā met the king in secret and, earnestly desiring children, asked him to marry her. He yielded and married her without Devayānī's knowledge. When Devayānī discovered it she complained to her father. Śukrācārya cursed Yayāti with premature old age in his prime — an event directly introduced by her complaint. Later, when Yayāti pleaded so humbly for forgiveness, Śukrācārya relented to the extent of allowing Yayāti to exchange his old age with any willing son (lines 1135–1144).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Devayānī is a significant ancestral figure: her marriage to [[yayati|Yayāti]] begins the dynastic chain traced in the preface to the epic — Yayāti's youngest son Puru accepts the old age, Puru's line eventually produces the Bharata race including both Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas.

Her character in Rajagopalachari's telling is consistent: she is proud, emotionally intense, keenly aware of her status as the daughter of the world's most indispensable preceptor, and capable of both deep love (for Kacha) and fierce anger (at Śarmiṣṭhā, at Yayāti's betrayal). She is also portrayed as someone who uses her leverage — over her father, over Vṛṣaparva — effectively rather than merely reactively. Rajagopalachari notes approvingly that in the ancient world, "wise and learned brahmana ladies" would speak their mind with "honourable frankness" (line 973), and Devayānī's declaration of love to Kacha is offered within this frame.

The Śukrācārya declaration against wine-drinking, triggered by the asuras' trick (lines 935–940), is presented by the narrator as a piece of moral legislation offered to humanity and embedded in the Devayānī story — a dharmic digression not about Devayānī herself but arising from the danger to which her grief exposes her father.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari treats the narrative as legend. No historical-critical commentary is offered.
