**Biographical Overview**

Son of David and Bathsheba (Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD"), third king of the united monarchy (traditional dates c. 970–931 BCE). Succeeds after a succession struggle (Adonijah vs. Solomon, 1 Kgs 1–2); David charges him to keep the law of Moses and act with wisdom. Famous for the prayer for a "discerning heart" (1 Kgs 3:9), international renown, vast building projects (temple chs. 6–8, palace, fortifications), and wealth (gold, horses, trade). Later years marked by 700 wives + 300 concubines (many foreign), leading to high places for foreign gods and the divine announcement that the kingdom will be torn from his house — though not in his lifetime, for David's sake (11:11–13). Dies and is buried in the city of David; Rehoboam succeeds amid the secession of the north.

**Primary Source Appearances**

- **1 Kings 1–11**: Rise (succession, wisdom request, judgment of prostitutes), administration (ch. 4), temple and palace construction (5–7), dedication prayer (8 — theological heart), wealth and Sheba (9–10), apostasy and judgment oracle (11). Frame: wisdom at both ends — initially God-given and positive, finally self-serving and leading to idolatry.
- **2 Chronicles 1–9**: More uniformly positive portrait. Focus on temple preparations (David's prior work), construction, dedication (fire from heaven, "He is good; his steadfast love endures forever"), Queen of Sheba, wealth as sign of blessing. Omits the foreign wives/idolatry and the division announcement.
- **Psalms**: Attributed as the author or subject of Psalms 72 (the coronation hymn closing Book Two) and 127 (a Song of Ascents).
- **Wisdom books**: Traditional Solomonic attribution (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs); patron of Israelite wisdom.
- **NT**: Matt 12:42 // Luke 11:31 ("something greater than Solomon is here"); genealogy in Matt 1.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**

**Judaism**: Builder of the First Temple (Beit ha-Miqdash), archetypal wise king (*ḥakham*). Rich aggadah: judgment with the two prostitutes, throne with mechanical lions, control over demons (Ashmedai), encounter with Queen of Sheba (sometimes identified with the "Shulammite"), magical/esoteric traditions (Sefer ha-Razim, etc.). Ideal of peaceful prosperity under Torah.

**Christianity**: Type of the peaceful, wise Messianic king; "something greater than Solomon" points to Christ (Matt 12:42). Temple as type of the Church/body of Christ or the heavenly sanctuary (Hebrews). Dedication prayer (universal access, repentance from exile) informs soteriology and ecclesiology. Solomon's fall warns that wisdom without covenant fidelity leads to ruin. In medieval typology, Solomon sometimes prefigures Christ the bridegroom (Song of Songs).

**Islam**: Sulayman (Solomon), major prophet and king given *mulk* (dominion) over jinn, animals, wind, and the ability to understand the speech of birds (Q 27:15–44; 34:12–14; 38:30–40). The Queen of Sheba (Bilqis) submits to the true God. Entirely exonerated from idolatry charge; the "foreign wives" episode is reinterpreted or denied. Famous for the *miḥrāb* (prayer niche) and the wind-borne throne.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**

In the Deuteronomistic History (Provan), Solomon embodies the apex and the turning point: the realization of rest and the Davidic promise (temple as the place where YHWH's name dwells, prayer for all peoples) and simultaneously the demonstration that even the wisest king falls when wisdom is detached from exclusive fidelity to YHWH. The temple prayer (1 Kgs 8) is the single most important speech in Kings — YHWH cannot be contained, yet he hears from heaven when his people repent. The arc from gift of wisdom (ch. 3) to apostasy (ch. 11) structures the whole book: true wisdom is covenantal. In Chronicles (Leithart), Solomon completes the "sapiential conquest" — Joshua typology through wisdom rather than sword; temple as new creation (seven-day vocabulary, fire from heaven at dedication like Pentecost, Queen Sheba's seven observations); the land at rest. His reign is the high point of liturgical "all Israel" assembled in qahal before the division. The Davidic covenant tension (conditional obedience vs. unconditional promise) is dramatized in his life and left open at the end of Kings (Jehoiachin).

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**

- **Empire scale**: Maximalist (Provan and others) see substantial 10th-cent. BCE regional power (trade, building projects); minimalist (many critical scholars) see Jerusalem as a modest highland chiefdom with later idealization. Archaeology (Khirbet Qeiyafa, etc.) is debated.
- **Temple historicity and date**: 1 Kgs 6 gives a precise date (480 years after Exodus); critical views often push construction later or see the description as idealized.
- **Wisdom authorship**: Literary convention honoring Solomon as patron; modern scholarship sees multiple hands and periods for Proverbs, Qohelet, Song.
- **Foreign wives and idolatry**: Historical kernel or theological construct to explain the division? The text presents real sin with real (deferred) consequences.
- **Chronicles vs Kings**: The more positive Chronicler portrait raises questions of selective retelling vs. historical reliability.

**Alter's Literary Reading**

[[robert-alter|Robert Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) argues that the
true conclusion of the Books of Samuel is [[1-2-kings|1 Kings]] 1–2 — the
[[succession-narrative|succession]] of Solomon — which later redactors detached to preface
the reign. On his reading Solomon's accession is secured by the coordinated maneuvering of
[[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] and [[nathan|Nathan]] over a senile [[david|David]], the text leaving
pointedly open whether David ever truly promised Solomon the throne. Alter's signature example
of light [[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]] editing is the **seam in 1 Kings 2**:
David's high-minded, Torah-saturated charge to "keep what the LORD enjoins" is grafted onto an
otherwise cold political testament instructing Solomon to kill [[joab|Joab]] and Shimei — the
"wisdom" enjoined being "not the wisdom of the Torah of Moses but rather the wisdom of a
Talleyrand," which Solomon promptly exercises. (Recorded alongside the confessional and
historical-critical readings per the Contradiction Protocol.)

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**

Provan (NIBC) reads Solomon's reign as the hinge that reveals the book's didactic purpose: the story teaches that YHWH alone is God and that covenant fidelity is non-negotiable even for the wisest. The canonical excursus traces Solomon into the wisdom corpus and NT. Leithart (Brazos) highlights the typological depth (Joshua-sapiential, new creation temple, qahal ecclesiology) and the surprising transfer of the Davidic task. In Jewish tradition the Temple builder and wise judge dominate liturgy and midrash. Christian preaching uses the wisdom request, the temple prayer, and the fall as warnings and types. Islamic tradition celebrates the prophet-king who commands the unseen world and receives the submission of foreign queens. Modern scholarship debates the "Solomonic enlightenment" and the political apologetic function of the narrative. The figure remains central to discussions of wisdom, power, idolatry, and the limits of human achievement under the covenant.

## Provan NIBC reingest (2026-07-18)

*Attributed to [[iain-provan|Iain W. Provan]], [[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|*1 and 2 Kings* (NIBC, 1995)]]. Digests R1–R2, §18.*

- **Succession and charge (§1–2):** Solomon’s rise is realpolitik + public anointing; David’s charge binds **Deuteronomic law** to **conditional** dynasty language (2:1–4) alongside ruthless settling of Joab/Shimei—wisdom and violence intertwined.
- **Ambiguous wisdom (§3–7):** Early high-place worship, Egyptian marriage, and horse/chariot buildup (Deut 17 “time bomb”) cloud the Gibeon gift; forced labor (*mas*) and district system fund glory under a cloud.
- **Temple / Name (§8–14):** Temple hosts the Name without containing God; 6:11–13 ties presence to obedience; palace years (13) vs temple years (7) as narrative critique.
- **Prayer as heart (§13):** Dual register—conditional dynasty (8:23–26) and universal sin → forgiveness (8:31–51); major petition = exile/restoration; “grace always underpins law.”
- **Apostasy and lamp (§16–17):** Foreign wives, high places, deferred judgment; one tribe for David’s lamp; division **“not forever”** (11:39)—fatherly discipline (2 Sam 7:14), not Saul-like eternal rejection.
- **§18 Canonical excursus:** Blessing exceeds virtue; messianic type (greater than Solomon; Wisdom of God); anti-anxiety foil of “splendor”; temple superseded yet load-bearing for heavenly-temple theology (Heb./Rev.).
