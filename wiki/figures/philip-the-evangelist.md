# Philip the Evangelist

Philip the evangelist is one of the Seven appointed in Acts 6:5 and the missionary whose
Samaritan and Ethiopian-eunuch episodes (Acts 8) [[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats as a
"bridge" advancing the gospel toward the Gentile mission. Bock calls him "a model of a
Christian missionary."

## Mismatch Flag — NOT Philip the Apostle

This Philip is **distinct from Philip the apostle of the Twelve**. He is "Philip the
evangelist… one of the Seven" (Acts 21:8), first appointed among the Seven Hellenist table-
servers in Acts 6:5. Do not conflate him with the apostle Philip named in the lists of the
Twelve.

## Biographical Overview

Following the persecution that scattered the Jerusalem believers (Acts 8:1b–4), Philip
carries the word into [[samaria|Samaria]] and then, by angelic and Spirit direction, to the
[[ethiopian-eunuch|Ethiopian eunuch]] on the Gaza road. Bock frames the Philip cycle as
fulfilling "Jesus's postresurrection commission (Acts 1:8), on its initial journey 'for
all who are far away.'" Those who respond, Bock notes, "are not Jews but those on the edge
of Judaism, namely, Samaritans and an Ethiopian." Acts later shows Philip settled at
[[caesarea|Caesarea]] with four prophesying daughters, still there when Paul passes through
"twenty years later" (Acts 21:8–9).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 6:5** — appointed one of the Seven to oversee the daily distribution.
- **Acts 8:5–25** — proclaims the Christ in a Samaritan city; his exorcisms and healings
  "draw people into considering the message" (Bock), producing the first mention of joy
  (χαρά) in Acts; his converts include [[simon-magus|Simon Magus]]. Peter and John are
  later sent from Jerusalem, after which the Spirit is given.
- **Acts 8:26–40** — directed first by "an angel of the Lord" and then by "the Spirit,"
  Philip meets the Ethiopian eunuch, expounds Isaiah 53, "gospels Jesus" (Acts 8:35), and
  baptizes him; the Spirit then "catches him up" (ἁρπάζω), and he preaches from Azotus to
  Caesarea.
- **Acts 21:8–9** — hosts Paul at Caesarea; has four unmarried prophesying daughters.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock reads Philip's Samaritan mission as the gospel's first controlled step beyond
Judaism — Samaritans occupying, on Bock's preferred option, a "halfway" status between
Jews and Gentiles, so that the episode approaches but does not yet constitute the
[[cornelius|Cornelius]] "breakthrough." He rejects Haenchen's claim that the subsequent
apostolic visit "minimizes" Philip; his parallel is that "as Jesus affirmed but carried
John the Baptist's ministry further, so Philip's work is carried further by the apostles."
In the eunuch episode Bock stresses that baptism is here "performed by a non-apostle" with
no laying-on-of-hands required (contrast Samaria), and that Philip "gospeled Jesus"
"beginning from this Scripture" — recalling Luke 24:27, 44–47. With Witherington, Bock
notes the account's parallelism to the Emmaus road (a journey out of Jerusalem, an
initiating question, Scripture central, Jesus the topic, the messenger's disappearance).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Philip is the narrative agent by whom the Acts 1:8 program moves outward into Samaria and
to "the end of the earth" (which, per Witherington, is how ancients regarded Ethiopia).
His ministry demonstrates, on Bock's reading, that evangelism and baptism proceed through
non-apostolic hands, while the extraordinary Samaritan Spirit-delay is (for Bock) a
"special legitimization" of a new region's inclusion rather than a normative pattern.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock defends the historicity of the eunuch episode against Haenchen, who doubts it "chiefly
because of the supernatural activity," countering that "The Acts of the Apostles could just
as well be named the Acts of God." With Pesch, Bock treats the episode as "an important
intermediate stop in the journey to Gentile inclusion." The identification of the Samaritan
city is disputed (Justin Martyr suggests Gitta; Bruce and Pesch suggest Shechem or
Sebaste; Bock: "Shechem is the best candidate").
