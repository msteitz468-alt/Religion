# Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – 50 CE)

Philo was the most important Jewish philosopher and biblical exegete of the Hellenistic period. A member of a wealthy and prominent Alexandrian Jewish family, he combined deep loyalty to the Torah and Jewish tradition with a sophisticated appropriation of Middle Platonic philosophy. His massive allegorical commentaries on the Pentateuch and his philosophical treatises made him the principal conduit through which Greek metaphysical concepts (especially the Logos as divine intermediary) entered early Christian theology.

## Biographical Overview

Born into a leading Jewish family in Alexandria, Egypt, Philo received both a thorough Jewish education and the standard Greek *paideia* (including philosophy). He lived his entire life in Alexandria except for a diplomatic mission to the emperor Caligula in Rome (c. 40 CE) on behalf of the Alexandrian Jewish community during a period of anti-Jewish violence (described in *Against Flaccus* and *Embassy to Gaius*).

Philo was a contemporary of Jesus and the earliest Christian movement, though there is no evidence he knew of them. His works were preserved almost entirely by Christian scribes and became foundational for Christian intellectual life, especially in Alexandria.

## Primary Source Appearances

Philo's own writings are the primary sources (nearly 50 treatises survive, many in the original Greek, some only in Armenian). He does not appear as a character in the biblical texts themselves but as a major interpreter of them. Key works include:
- The *Allegorical Commentary* on Genesis (and other Pentateuchal books).
- The *Exposition of the Law* (more accessible treatises including *On the Creation of the World*, *On Abraham*, *On Joseph*, etc.).
- Philosophical essays (*On the Eternity of the World*, *On Providence*, etc.).
- Historical and apologetic writings defending the Jewish community and the dignity of the Jewish law.

Early Christian authors (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, Ambrose, Augustine indirectly) quote or allude to him extensively, often without naming him, treating his exegesis as a valuable resource for Christian reading of the Old Testament.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Jewish**: Philo was largely forgotten in rabbinic Judaism (which developed in a more Hebrew/Aramaic, Palestine-centered direction after 70 CE). Some medieval and later Jewish thinkers (e.g., Azariah dei Rossi in the 16th century) rediscovered him. In modern times he has been reclaimed as a major voice of Hellenistic/Diaspora Judaism and a pioneer of philosophical interpretation of Scripture. His allegorical method is sometimes compared (and contrasted) with midrashic and mystical traditions.

**Christian**: Enormous and mostly positive influence, especially in the Alexandrian tradition. Clement and Origen explicitly or implicitly use Philonic allegory and Logos doctrine. The identification of the Johannine Logos with the pre-existent Christ was facilitated by Philo's prior development of a personal yet subordinate mediating Logos. Later Christian Platonists (including the Cappadocians and the Dionysian tradition) continued to draw on the philosophical vocabulary he helped popularize. At the same time, Christian writers had to correct or go beyond Philo on key points (the Logos is not a "second God" but fully God; the Logos becomes incarnate; creation is *ex nihilo* rather than from pre-existing matter).

**Critical / modern scholarship**: Philo is studied as the outstanding representative of Middle Platonism applied to a revealed religion. Scholars debate the exact relationship between his Logos and the Fourth Gospel (striking parallels, but John's Logos is incarnate and personal in a stronger sense). His political and apologetic writings are important sources for first-century Alexandrian Jewish life and Roman-Jewish relations.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Philo's central theological achievement was to present the God of Israel as the transcendent, simple, perfect Being of Middle Platonic philosophy while maintaining that this God had revealed himself in the Torah and acted in history. The Logos functions as:
- The "image of God" through whom the world was created (echoing Genesis 1 and Proverbs 8).
- The intermediary that allows the utterly transcendent God to relate to the material world without compromising divine simplicity or immutability.
- The "firstborn son," the "second God," or the divine reason immanent in the cosmos.

His allegorical method allowed him to find Platonic metaphysics (the ascent of the soul, the Forms as thoughts in the mind of God or the Logos, the distinction between the sensible and intelligible worlds) already present in the plain text of Scripture. This model of philosophical exegesis was enormously productive for Christian theology and spirituality.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Philo is a well-attested historical figure. His embassy to Caligula is independently confirmed by Josephus. Questions concern:
- The precise nature of his "orthodoxy" as a Jew (he combines philosophical universalism with firm particular attachment to the law, temple, and people of Israel).
- The degree of originality versus dependence on earlier (lost) Alexandrian Jewish exegesis.
- The relationship of his Logos doctrine to later Christian developments: is John dependent on Philo, or do both draw on a common Hellenistic Jewish milieu? Most scholars see influence (direct or indirect) on early Christian thought without simple dependence.
- Whether his allegorical method distorts the plain sense of Scripture or legitimately uncovers deeper meanings already intended by the divine author (a debate that continues in Christian hermeneutics).

Stead treats Philo as the key bridge in the philosophical background to patristic theology without overstating direct literary dependence.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Philo is the fountainhead of the allegorical/philosophical tradition of biblical interpretation that runs through Origen (who wrote the first great Christian commentaries on John and the Song using similar methods), the Cappadocians, Ambrose (who transmitted much Philonic material to the Latin West), and beyond. His conceptual distinctions (transcendent God vs. mediating Logos; literal vs. allegorical senses) shaped the way Christians read the Old Testament as containing hidden philosophical and Christological truth.

In the wiki he is the essential precursor for the [[logos-christology|Logos Christology]] concept and for understanding the Middle Platonic background that Stead analyzes in *Philosophy in Christian Antiquity*, Chapter 6. His influence is also visible in the development of allegorical readings of the Song of Songs (already documented via Origen in the wiki).

**Harry Austryn Wolfson’s analysis** (*The Philosophy of the Church Fathers*, 1956): Wolfson positions Philo as the indispensable foundation and "prolegomenon" for the entire "philosophy of the Church Fathers." The Fathers, like Philo, assumed Greek philosophers had discovered by reason truths presented in Scripture as revelations, and thus recast Christian beliefs into a philosophy—producing a "Christian version of Greek philosophy" parallel to Philo’s Jewish version. Key continuities traced by Wolfson: Philo’s philosophical allegory (with terms like "type," "shadow," "parable") combined with NT non-philosophical midrashic senses (predictive/adventual/eschatological, moral, preexistential); classifications and terms (corporeal/psychical/spiritual or mystical) developed from Philo through Clement and Origen (with Antiochians deviating); Philo’s eight scriptural presuppositions (existence/unity of God, creation, providence, revelation of the Law [incl. Oral Law], existence of ideas) embodied in the Christian Rule of Faith; subordination of philosophy as "handmaiden of Scripture"; the Logos as mediating "image"/"firstborn" (transcendent God relating to world without compromising simplicity); ideas as in the mind/Logos (Philonic interpretation of Plato often followed); single/double faith theories building on Philonic attitudes toward philosophy vs. revelation. Wolfson reconstructs the Fathers’ solutions to the "three and one" (Trinity: relative unity of rule or substratum/ousia/hypostasis/homoousios) and "two and one" (Incarnation: physical union analogies, especially "predominance") as adaptations of the philosophical one-and-many problem, with Philo providing the starting conceptual toolkit. (See also Wolfson’s treatment of lingering identifications of Spirit with preexistent Christ, eternal generation debates, and heresies arising from the same difficulties.)
