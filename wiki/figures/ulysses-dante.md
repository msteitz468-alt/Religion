# Ulysses (in Dante)

The Homeric hero as Dante reimagines him in [[inferno|Inferno]] 26, among the
**false counsellors** of the eighth circle, wrapped with Diomedes in a single
cloven flame — punished for the Trojan Horse, the luring of Achilles to war, and
the theft of the Palladium.

## Dante's Ulysses

Kirkpatrick emphasizes that Dante knew no Homer directly; his Ulysses is built from
Latin sources (Cicero, Seneca, Horace, Ovid) that cast the figure as "low cunning."
The famous **last-voyage speech** (26:90–142) — in which Ulysses, leaving Circe and
overriding every duty to son, father, and Penelope out of "my long desire, /
burning to understand how this world works," sails his crew past the Pillars of
Hercules into the unknown southern ocean — has, Kirkpatrick notes, **no known
source precedent**: it appears to be Dante's invention. The crew is shipwrecked at
the foot of the mountain of [[purgatorio|Purgatory]], "as pleased Another's will."

His rallying cry — "You were not made to live as mindless brutes, / but go in
search of virtue and true knowledge" (26:118–120) — is at once magnificent and
condemned: the very appetite for knowledge that Dante prizes becomes, untethered
from humility and providence, a "mad flight" (*folle volo*). The episode is alluded
to more often in later cantos than any other.

## Significance

Ulysses is the great foil to Dante-the-pilgrim, who also crosses into the southern
hemisphere but under grace. His daring transgression frames the poem's central
question of how the desire for knowledge can be both the highest human calling and,
misdirected, a form of fraud. Modern readers (Tennyson, Primo Levi) have returned
to this Ulysses repeatedly.
