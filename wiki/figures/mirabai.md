# Mirabai

**Mirabai** (c. 1450–1525 CE) is the most celebrated female bhakti saint of North India — a Rajput
princess who refused the constraints of royal marriage to live as a devotee of [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]].
Her poems are the most widely quoted of any North Indian saint, yet relatively few were anthologized
in her lifetime, likely because they mocked both marriage and asceticism, leaving her few institutional
allies.

## Biographical Overview

Mirabai was born into a Rajput family of high standing. In the earliest version of her life story,
she was forced to marry a king's son but preferred the company of wandering mendicants and devotees
of Krishna; the king (either her husband or her father-in-law, according to variant traditions) made
several attempts on her life, all of which she survived through Krishna's intervention. She eventually
left her marriage to join the community of Krishna devotees.

Later retellings — including the popular *Amar Chitra Katha* comic book — sanitized her story: her
husband's brother (not the husband) becomes the villain; the husband conveniently dies soon after the
marriage, and Mirabai is recast as "an ideal Hindu wife." These later versions reveal the discomfort
with her actual message, which subverts both the wifely ideal and the ascetic ideal.

She is associated with the holy sites of Vrindavan (scene of Krishna's youth) and Dwarka, where she
reportedly ended her days, some accounts claiming she dissolved into the image of Krishna in the temple.

## Theological Significance in the Bhakti Tradition

Mirabai exemplifies the Sant tradition's **inversion of gendered social norms** through devotion.
Unlike the Āḷvār saint Āṇṭāḷ or the Virashaiva saint [[mahadevyyakka|Mahadevyyakka]] (who
addressed metaphysical entities as domestic oppressors), Mirabai directed her subversion specifically
at the *institution of marriage itself*:

- She treats Krishna (whom she calls **Mohan**, "the Deluder") as her true husband
- The mother-in-law — the symbol of patriarchal domestic authority — appears in her poems as the
  obstacle to divine love, not as a symbol of illusion (as in Mahadevyyakka) but as a literal domestic
  captor
- Her "eyes sold into someone else's hands" (Krishna's) is at once erotic, mystical, and a frank
  statement that her loyalty cannot be commanded by human authority

This makes her theologically distinctive within the Sant tradition: she did not go nirguṇa (formless
devotion) like [[kabir|Kabīr]] but remained intensely saguṇa, passionately focused on the personal
form of Krishna as lover.

## Selected Poems

**"The Deluder"** (translated by A. K. Ramanujan):

> My eyes are greedy. They're beyond turning back. / They stare straight ahead, friend, straight
> ahead, / coveting and coveting still more. / . . . Abandoning my beautiful veil and the modesty /
> that guards my family's honor; showing my face. / Mother-in-law, sister-in-law: day and night they
> monitor, / lecturing me about it all and lecturing once again. / Yet my quick giddy eyes will brook
> no hindrance. / They're sold into someone else's hands.

**"The Bhil Woman"** (a retelling of a story also told by Valmiki, Tulsidas, and Kabir):

> The Bhil woman tasted them, plum after plum, / and finally found one she could offer him. / What
> kind of genteel breeding was this? / And hers was no ravishing beauty. / Her family was poor, her
> caste quite low, / her clothes a matter of rags. / Yet Ram took that fruit — that touched, spoiled
> fruit — / For he knew that it stood for her love.

Mirabai's version adds the woman's *tasting* of the fruit before offering it — a detail that renders
explicit what Valmiki, Tulsidas, and Kabir leave implicit: that the low-caste woman's direct, untutored
devotion supersedes Brahmin ritual purity. "What sort of a Veda could she have learned?" Mirabai
asks — making caste and textual knowledge irrelevant to the love of God.

## Position Within North Indian Bhakti

Mirabai straddles the Sant and Saguna streams of North Indian bhakti:

- **Sant connections**: anti-caste, vernacular composition, subversion of institutional religion
- **Saguna bhakti**: unlike nirguṇa Sants (Kabir), she is intensely devoted to the *form* of Krishna
- **Female agency**: one of a very small number of medieval women poets whose voice survives as
  personally, not devotionally, female

She is also a historical figure of what Doniger calls the **"anti-wife"** archetype — women in
religious literature who exemplify devotion by resisting the normative wifely role. This contrasts
with the many women in Mughal-period hagiography who *perform* wifely sacrifice (suttee) as the
ultimate devotional act.

## Reception and Influence

- Mirabai's poems are performed across North India at religious gatherings (bhajan sessions) to the
  present day; she is probably the most culturally present woman saint in the Hindu tradition
- She is claimed by Rajasthani tradition as a Rajput noblewoman and by devotional tradition as a
  primary model of *madhurya* (sweet/erotic) devotion to Krishna
- She is sometimes associated with the [[virashaiva|Virashaiva]] figure of Mahadevyyakka as a
  parallel case of female saints who inverted domestic obligation for devotion — though she was
  Vaishnava, not Shaiva
