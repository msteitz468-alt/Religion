Boaz is the wealthy, godly landowner of Bethlehem who notices the Moabite gleaner Ruth, protects and provides for her, praises her *ḥesed*, and publicly redeems the land and takes her as wife "to perpetuate the name of the dead," becoming the father of Obed and an ancestor of David.

## Biographical Overview

A relative (*môd aʿ*) of Elimelech, "a worthy man" (*ʾîš gibbôr ḥayil*) of the clan of Elimelech from Bethlehem. Ruth happens to glean in his field during the barley harvest. Boaz arrives from Bethlehem, greets the reapers "The LORD be with you," and they reply "The LORD bless you." He notices the young Moabite woman, asks about her, and is told she is the one who returned with Naomi from Moab and has been gleaning since morning without rest. He approaches her, addresses her as "my daughter," urges her to stay in his field, instructs the young men not to touch her, and tells her to drink from the water the young men have drawn. At mealtime he invites her to eat with the reapers, serves her roasted grain, and secretly instructs the reapers to leave extra grain for her. Ruth returns with an ephah of barley; Naomi recognizes that the man who took notice of her is "one of our redeemers." Later, at the threshing floor, Ruth comes at midnight, uncovers his feet, and lies down. Boaz wakes, learns who she is, and praises her: "May you be blessed by the LORD, my daughter. You have made this last kindness greater than the first in that you have not gone after young men, whether poor or rich. And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you ask, for all my fellow townsmen know that you are a worthy woman" (3:10–11). He notes a nearer redeemer but promises to act if the man declines. The next morning at the town gate, before the elders and the people, Boaz publicly presents the case of Naomi's land and Ruth the Moabite, the nearer redeemer declines (lest he impair his own inheritance), and Boaz declares before witnesses that he has acquired the land and Ruth "to perpetuate the name of the dead in his inheritance, so that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brothers and from the gate of his native place" (4:10). The people and elders bless him: "May the LORD make the woman who is coming into your house like Rachel and Leah, who together built up the house of Israel... may your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, because of the offspring that the LORD will give you by this young woman." Boaz takes Ruth as wife; she bears Obed. The genealogy lists him as son of Salmon, father of Obed, grandfather of Jesse, great-grandfather of [[david|David]] (4:18–22; Matt 1:5).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Ruth 2**: Field encounter; protective instructions; generous provision at mealtime and through the reapers; Ruth returns with abundance.
- **Ruth 3**: Threshing-floor night; praise of Ruth's *ḥesed* and worth; promise to redeem or yield to the nearer kinsman; sends her home with six measures of barley.
- **Ruth 4**: Gate redemption scene (public, witnessed, legal); marriage to Ruth; birth of Obed; the people's blessing.
- **Matthew 1:5**: Named in the genealogy as husband of Ruth and father of Obed.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Boaz is the model *gibbôr ḥayil* (worthy man) who practices *ḥesed* publicly and privately, protects the vulnerable, and redeems the name and land of the dead. The gate scene is a paradigm of just procedure and communal witness. Rabbinic tradition sometimes links him to the judge Ibzan or sees his generosity as fulfilling the spirit of the law beyond its letter. The blessing "like Rachel and Leah... like the house of Perez" ties him to the matriarchs and the Judahite line.

**Christianity**: Boaz is the kinsman-redeemer who foreshadows Christ (redemption of lost inheritance, taking the outsider as bride, perpetuating the name of the dead). His protection of Ruth, praise of her character over her status, and public commitment at the gate are read as models of godly manhood, justice, and costly loyalty. The story is used for ethics of care for the foreigner and widow, for the inclusion of Gentiles, and for the providence that works through ordinary human faithfulness. In the genealogy he stands in the line that leads to David and Jesus; the "worthy woman" language echoes Proverbs 31.

**Critical and social-scientific**: Boaz is studied as a man of status who uses his power to protect and elevate rather than exploit. Honor-shame readings (Lau) note his public affirmation of Ruth's worth ("all my fellow townsmen know that you are a worthy woman") as restoration of honor to the outsider. The gate scene is analyzed for legal custom, communal consensus, and the economics of redemption vs. levirate. Some see the narrative as subtly critical of the nearer kinsman's self-interest.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Boaz is the human agent of divine restoration. He "happens" to be the field owner (2:3); he notices the foreigner; he goes beyond the minimum of the gleaning law (leaving extra, inviting to table, protecting from harassment); he praises *ḥesed* when he sees it and matches it with his own; he acts publicly and legally at the gate so that the redemption is secure and witnessed. Theologically he shows that *ḥesed* is not only the desperate loyalty of the widow but also the responsible action of the man who has resources and standing. The narrative contrasts him with the nearer redeemer who declines ("lest I impair my own inheritance") — Boaz is willing to "impair" or share for the sake of the name and the living. His redemption makes possible the birth that fills Naomi's emptiness and continues the line to David. In the larger canon he stands as the faithful Israelite through whom the promise to Abraham (blessing to the nations) and to David begins to take concrete form via a Moabite.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Social status and power**: Was Boaz's generosity purely altruistic or also strategic (securing a good name, acquiring land)? The text presents both his public virtue and his genuine praise of Ruth.
- **Legal mechanics**: The relationship between land redemption (Lev 25) and levirate marriage (Deut 25) in this case; whether Ruth or Naomi is the primary legal party; the nearer kinsman's refusal and its legal basis.
- **Threshing-floor propriety**: Cultural custom or risky breach? The text and Boaz's response treat it as honorable initiative within the bounds of what the community will recognize as *ḥesed*.
- **Genealogy and historicity**: The compressed genealogy (Salmon → Boaz → Obed → Jesse → David) raises questions of gaps or telescoping; traditional readings take it as reliable lineage.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Lau (NICOT) presents Boaz as the worthy man whose honor-shame sensitivity and *ḥesed* practice restore the vulnerable and advance the missional purpose of God. The story is a staple for preaching on godly leadership, protection of the marginalized, and the beauty of ordinary faithfulness. In Jewish tradition the gate scene and the communal blessing model justice and hope. Christian typological readings have long seen the kinsman-redeemer as pointing to Christ; the ethical force (care for the widow/foreigner, public justice, costly loyalty) remains primary. The figure is central to discussions of *ḥesed* ethics, redemption, and the surprising ways the Davidic line is preserved and extended.
