# Shoghi Effendi

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Biographical Overview

Shoghi Effendi Rabbani was the eldest son of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's daughter, Diya'iyyih Khánum (d. 1951), and Mírzá Hádí Shírází (d. 1955), a member of the extended family of the Báb's mother (the Afnáns). He was born in 'Akká on 1 March 1897, the eldest of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's thirteen grandchildren, and named in the latter's will as his successor while he was still a child (lines 2432–2438).

He was described as a boisterous but sensitive child, enormously attached to his grandfather. He was educated first at home with the other children of the household, then at Catholic schools in Haifa and Beirut (where he was very unhappy), and then at the Syrian Protestant College (predecessor of the American University in Beirut, which he enjoyed), spending summer holidays as one of his grandfather's assistants. He gained an arts degree from the college in 1918 and became 'Abdu'l-Bahá's chief secretary. In 1920 he went to Balliol College, Oxford, where he studied political science and economics and sought to perfect his English for translating Bahá'í literature. He was still in the midst of his studies when summoned to return to Haifa at the news of his grandfather's death (lines 2440–2452).

In 1937 he married Mary Maxwell (b. 1910), the only daughter of two North American Bahá'ís. The couple had no children; Rúhiyyih Khánum, as she was titled, became his helpmate and constant companion until his passing in 1957 (lines 2557–2560). During the 1940s a conflict developed between Shoghi Effendi and many members of his extended family — his brother, sisters, cousins and aunts — partly because of their contacts, including marriage, with Covenant-breaking members of Bahá'u'lláh's extended family; all were eventually excommunicated (lines 2561–2565).

Shoghi Effendi died unexpectedly on 4 November 1957 during a visit to London and was buried there (lines 2800–2801). He had produced over 17,500 letters (by him or his secretary) by that date (line 2531).

## Appointment as Guardian

The terms of 'Abdu'l-Bahá's will were explicit: Shoghi Effendi was to be *Guardian of the Cause of God* (Válí amru'lláh — a title immediately reminiscent of Shí'í Imáms). He was the "Centre of the Cause" and the "sign of God" on earth. All were to show "their obedience, submissiveness and subordination" to him. He was also to be head of the (as yet unelected) Universal House of Justice. Obedience to him was equated with obedience to God; opposition to him with opposition to God. If worthy, the first-born of his lineal descendants should succeed him as Guardian. The original line of succession indicated in Bahá'u'lláh's Book of Covenant was set aside because 'Abdu'l-Bahá's half-brother Muhammad-'Alí had been "cut off from the Holy Tree" as a result of his misdeeds (lines 2453–2467).

## Style of Leadership

Shoghi Effendi was a young man of twenty-four when he assumed the Guardianship — Western-educated and Western in dress apart from a black fez. He signed his letters to the Bahá'ís "your true brother" and referred to the institution of the Guardianship rather than his own personal role. Unlike 'Abdu'l-Bahá, he never journeyed abroad to visit Bahá'í communities. He distanced himself from local Palestinian notability and concentrated his energies on the worldwide direction of the Faith (lines 2484–2496).

He faced a mass of work and nervous exhaustion in his early years, repeatedly collapsing and taking extended absences from Haifa. He later adjusted to a pattern of unremitting hard work in Haifa interspersed with summer breaks in Europe — in the early years usually the Swiss Alps — and on two occasions travelled through Africa from south to north (1929 and 1940) (lines 2524–2548).

## Challenges to His Authority

Beyond Muhammad-'Alí's unsuccessful post-1921 campaign, the only serious challenges came in the late 1920s and early 1930s in the United States. Ruth White, an American journalist, mounted a sustained campaign claiming Shoghi Effendi had forged 'Abdu'l-Bahá's will; she attracted a small number of Western supporters, particularly in Germany, but Eastern Bahá'ís found her claims spurious. Ahmad Sohrab objected to the policies of the new American Bahá'í assembly and founded his own organization (the New History Society / the International Caravan of East and West). Neither gained many followers (lines 2504–2523).

## Writings and Translations

Shoghi Effendi had a great love of the English language (together with a mild Oxford accent) and was as comfortable in English as in Persian and Arabic; he also spoke French. The vast majority of his writings are letters (mostly in Persian or English). By the date of the source, fifteen volumes of English-language letters had been published: collections of more or less routine letters to the American, Canadian, Alaskan, British, German, Indian, Australian and New Zealand Bahá'ís, together with monograph letters on the "World Order of Bahá'u'lláh", the teaching of the Bahá'í Faith (*The Advent of Divine Justice*), and Bahá'u'lláh's proclamation to the rulers (*The Promised Day Is Come*) (lines 2572–2593).

### Major Letters and Monographs

- **"The Dispensation of Bahá'u'lláh"** (8 February 1934): a major doctrinal statement delineating the religious "stations" of the central figures of the Bahá'í Faith (Bahá'u'lláh, the Báb, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá) and the role of the Guardianship and the Universal House of Justice as primary institutions of the administrative order.
- **"The Goal of a New World Order"** (28 November 1931) and **"The Unfoldment of World Civilization"** (11 March 1936): place the worsening economic and political situation of the 1930s in the context of Bahá'u'lláh's summons to establish the Most Great Peace, outline the guiding principles of Bahá'u'lláh's world order, and refer to an "age of transition" heralding a new world civilization.
- **"The Faith of Bahá'u'lláh"** (1947): addressed to the United Nations Palestine Committee, outlining the fundamentals of the Bahá'í Faith.
- ***God Passes By*** (1944): his one book — a lengthy history of the Faith.

(Lines 2577–2629.)

### Translations

A few short pieces appeared in print in the 1920s, along with a collaborative rendering of Bahá'u'lláh's *The Hidden Words*. Between 1931 and 1941 he published four volumes of Bahá'u'lláh's writings, together with a translation of Nabíl's history of the Babis under the English title *The Dawn-Breakers* (1932). Many other passages translated from Bahá'í scripture appeared in his letters (lines 2630–2637).

## Interpretive Authority

As Shoghi Effendi himself emphasized, 'Abdu'l-Bahá had conferred upon the Guardianship the function of "interpreter of the word of God." Guardians were empowered "to reveal the purport and disclose the implications of the utterances of Bahá'u'lláh and of 'Abdu'l-Bahá." These interpretations were authoritative and binding (lines 2605–2610).

## Development of the Administrative Order

See also: [[bahai-administrative-order|Bahá'í Administrative Order]]

Elements of the Bahá'í administrative system had been described or established by Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá, but it was only during Shoghi Effendi's Guardianship that a complete system emerged, replacing more personalized and informal patterns of local leadership (lines 2640–2645).

### First Period (1920s): Spiritual Assemblies

The basic elements were laid out in 1922 and 1923. All Bahá'í groups with at least nine adults (aged twenty-one and above) were called upon to form local spiritual assemblies, each superintending all Bahá'í activities in its locality. National spiritual assemblies were to be elected by delegate conventions wherever there was a sufficient number of Bahá'ís. Both local and national assemblies were to establish their own funds and committees. Membership rolls, enrolment cards, credential cards, and regular "nineteen day feasts" were introduced. Wherever possible, assemblies secured legal identity enabling them to own property (lines 2647–2675).

### Second Period (1950s): Three New Institutions

1. **The International Bahá'í Council** (1950/1–63): initially appointed, later elected; charged with assisting Shoghi Effendi in Haifa and seen as the precursor of the Universal House of Justice.
2. **The Hands of the Cause of God** (revived 1951 as a functioning group): responsible for assisting national spiritual assemblies and protecting the Faith; initially twelve, increased to twenty-seven by 1957, on all continents.
3. **The Auxiliary Boards** (established 1954 and 1957): members acting as "deputies, assistants and advisers" to the Hands, organized on a continental level — one Board for propagation (1954), one for protection (1957).

All these institutions included both men and women (lines 2686–2706).

## Systematic Teaching Plans

The most important Bahá'í community for planning purposes was the United States and Canada. The first systematic teaching plan assigned to it was a **Seven Year Plan (1937–44)**, in which Bahá'ís were called upon to settle pioneers in all American states, Canadian provinces, and Latin American republics lacking Bahá'ís, and to continue construction of the Bahá'í House of Worship at Wilmette, Chicago (lines 2727–2733).

A **second Seven Year Plan (1946–53)** directed Americans to promote expansion throughout the Americas and re-establish the Faith in Europe after World War II, complete the Wilmette Temple, establish an independent Canadian national spiritual assembly, and form two large regional assemblies (for South America and for Central America and the Antilles). Other national assemblies were also given goals, including Iranian and Egyptian Bahá'ís establishing groups in the Middle East and North Africa, Indians settling pioneers in South-East Asia, and British Bahá'ís co-ordinating an Africa project (1951–3) (lines 2735–2756).

In 1953, all twelve national assemblies then in existence embarked on a ten-year **Global Crusade (Ten Year Crusade)** designed by Shoghi Effendi to secure establishment of the Faith throughout the world. Designated goals were in many cases exceeded (lines 2758–2764).

## The Bahá'í World Centre

The development of the Bahá'í World Centre in the Haifa–'Akká area was largely the work of Shoghi Effendi. This included:
- Obtaining the mansion of Bahá'u'lláh at Bahjí in 1929 and extensive renovation; during the 1950s securing legal possession of surrounding lands and creating gardens with ornamental lighting.
- Renovating the house in which Bahá'u'lláh spent most of his years in 'Akká, and obtaining possession of other associated sites.
- In Haifa, extending the shrine complex in which the Báb and 'Abdu'l-Bahá were interred (1929), and having an elaborate golden-domed superstructure built (1948–53).
- Purchasing surrounding lands and creating gardens with ornamental lighting.
- Constructing a Parthenon-like International Archives building to house Bahá'í relics and scriptures (1955–7).

(Lines 2780–2798.)

## The Interregnum: Hands of the Cause, 1957–63

Shoghi Effendi died unexpectedly on 4 November 1957 during a visit to London and was buried there. Two weeks later the Hands of the Cause met in their first conclave in Haifa. A search of Shoghi Effendi's sealed room revealed no will; he had not appointed a successor nor left guidance on what should be done (lines 2800–2805).

In his last general letter to the Bahá'ís (October 1957), he had referred to the Hands as "the Chief Stewards of Bahá'u'lláh's embryonic World Commonwealth" invested with "the dual function of guarding over the security, and of ensuring the propagation of [the] Faith" (*Messages* 127). This provided the legal basis for the Hands to assume headship of the Faith (lines 2823–2829).

The Hands retained this headship until 1963, when on their instructions the elections for the first Universal House of Justice were held. The Hands debarred themselves from election (lines 2830–2832). The appointed International Bahá'í Council had meanwhile been replaced by an elected council (1961); electors for both the International Council (disbanded 1963) and the Universal House of Justice were the members of all national spiritual assemblies (lines 2833–2837).

The one significant exception to the Bahá'í world's acceptance of the Hands' custodial role was Charles Mason Remey, one of the most senior American Hands, who proclaimed himself second Guardian in 1960 and attracted a small but widely spread following. The rest of the Hands and the overwhelming majority of the Bahá'í world rejected his claims; Remey and his followers were declared Covenant-breakers. After Remey's death in 1974, his followers split into several mutually antagonistic groups; a small remnant remains active mainly in the United States (lines 2842–2851).
