## Biographical Overview

Droṇa was the son of the brahmin sage Bharadvāja. After completing his study of the Vedas and Vedāṅgas, he devoted himself to the art of archery and became a master. While students together in Bharadvāja's hermitage, Droṇa had formed a close friendship with Drupada, the son of the king of Pañcāla; in boyish enthusiasm Drupada would promise Droṇa half his kingdom when he became king (lines 1598–1605).

After completing his studies, Droṇa married the sister of Kṛpācārya (the first teacher of the Pāṇḍava and Kaurava princes). A son, Aśvatthāmā, was born to them. Droṇa loved his family and, for their sake, began to desire wealth — something he had never cared for before. He first went to Paraśurāma, who was distributing his riches among brahmins, but arrived too late; Paraśurāma had already given away everything and was about to retire to the forest. Instead, Paraśurāma offered to teach Droṇa the use of weapons, of which he was the supreme master. Droṇa joyfully agreed and became an unrivalled master of the military art (lines 1606–1617).

Droṇa then went to Drupada to claim the friendship and aid he had been promised, but found the king wholly transformed by power and wealth. Drupada, drunk with sovereignty, told Droṇa contemptuously that friendship can exist only between equals, that a wandering brahmin beggar could not be the friend of a throned king, and had him expelled from the palace. Droṇa departed with blazing wrath and a silent vow of revenge (lines 1618–1633).

He went next to Hastinapura and stayed quietly in the house of his brother-in-law Kṛpācārya. One day he witnessed the Pāṇḍava and Kaurava princes helplessly gathered around a well into which Yudhiṣṭhira's ring and their ball had fallen. Droṇa retrieved both — the ball by firing a chain of grass-blades as arrows, the ring with a single arrow — and when the astonished princes asked who he was, he sent them to Bhīṣma to find out. Bhīṣma recognised from the description that it was Droṇa, received him with special honour, and employed him as military preceptor to both the Pāṇḍavas and the Kauravas (lines 1638–1668).

## Primary Source Appearances

**Chapter XII (lines 1597–1695):** The primary account of Droṇa's origin, his friendship and falling-out with Drupada, his arrival at Hastinapura, his appointment as royal preceptor, and his revenge on Drupada. This chapter also introduces Aśvatthāmā as his son and records the births of Dhṛṣṭadyumna and Draupadī as consequences of Drupada's ritual revenge against Droṇa.

**Chapter X (lines 1407–1408):** Passing reference: before Droṇa, the princes had learned the practice of arms from Kṛpācārya.

**Chapter XI (lines 1463–1464):** The Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas learned the practice of arms first from Kṛpācārya and later from Droṇa; a public exhibition of their proficiency is arranged.

**Chapter XIII (lines 1739–1740):** Duryodhana tells Dhritarāṣṭra that Aśvatthāmā is devoted to him, which means Droṇa and Kṛpa will also take his side.

**Chapter XVII (lines 2343–2354):** At the council following Draupadī's svayamvara, Droṇa advises Dhritarāṣṭra to invite the Pāṇḍavas back and give them half the kingdom — the same counsel as Bhīṣma. Karṇa attacks Droṇa for this advice; Droṇa's eyes fill with anger and he warns that the Kauravas will meet destruction if they do not follow the counsel of Bhīṣma and himself.

**Chapter XXI (line 2764):** Sisupala, in his denunciation at the Rājasūya, notes that Droṇa (as teacher) would have been a more appropriate recipient of first honour than Kṛṣṇa.

**Chapter XXIV (line 3065):** Droṇa is seated in the dice hall, knows the game will end viciously, and watches unhappily unable to prevent it.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Droṇa's narrative function is dual. As preceptor, he is the instrument by which both the Pāṇḍavas and the Kauravas attain martial excellence — he is the common teacher whose pupils will destroy each other at Kurukṣetra. His personal story, however, is one of wounded honour: the friendship with Drupada that collapses into mutual hatred gives rise to the two figures — Dhṛṣṭadyumna and [[draupadi|Draupadī]] — who will be central to the war. Drupada's sacrificial rites produce Dhṛṣṭadyumna, destined to slay Droṇa, and Draupadī, whose humiliation in the dice game is among the war's proximate causes.

Rajagopalachari presents Droṇa's revenge on Drupada as just but insufficient: "hate is never extinguished by retaliation, and few things are harder to bear than the pangs of wounded vanity" (line 1685). Droṇa thinks the matter settled; Drupada's hatred only intensifies. This irony — that a proportionate and even generous revenge (giving back half the kingdom) simply cannot extinguish a grievance rooted in pride — is the chapter's stated moral.

The episode of Ekalavya (not in this line range — belongs to another agent/scope) is traditionally associated with Droṇa's demand for the highest "guru-dakṣiṇā" (teacher's fee), but Rajagopalachari's account in these chapters does not treat it; any reference to it should be checked against the relevant line range before attribution.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari treats Droṇa as a legendary figure within the epic tradition. No historical-critical commentary is offered.
