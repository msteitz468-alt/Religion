# Tertullian

## Biographical Overview

Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus was born in Carthage (North Africa, modern Tunisia) c.155 CE and died after 220 CE. He is the first major Christian writer to produce a substantial body of work in Latin, earning the title "father of Latin theology." Jerome records that he was the son of a centurion, trained as a lawyer, and converted to Christianity in adulthood — though these details come from a single later source and cannot be independently confirmed.

His conversion was thorough and his engagement with Roman culture turned polemical: he used his legal training to frame Christian truth-claims as a matter of proper judicial procedure and to attack both pagan persecution and heretical corruption. His style is brilliant, caustic, and polemical — he coined numerous Latin theological terms (including *trinitas*, *substantia*, *persona* in their Christian theological senses) that shaped the entire subsequent Latin theological tradition.

In his later career he was attracted to Montanism — the "New Prophecy" movement of Phrygia — and eventually identified with a Montanist faction called the "Tertullianists" in Carthage, though he never formally broke with catholic Christianity by his own account. Augustine reports that this faction died out in his own day (late 4th c.) and its members rejoined the catholic church.

## Primary Works

### Apologetic

- ***Apologeticus* (c.197)**: His masterpiece. Written to Roman provincial governors, it defends Christianity against charges of immorality, atheism, and political disloyalty. Argues: Christians are law-abiding; their God is not a new invention; the persecution is unjust since no evidence of wrongdoing is required for condemnation. Famous line: "the blood of Christians is seed" (*semen est sanguis Christianorum*).
- ***To the Nations* (*Ad Nationes*, c.197)**: Similar to the *Apologeticus* but addressed to the general public.
- ***Ad Martyres***: Short address to imprisoned Christians awaiting martyrdom.

### Anti-Heretical

- ***Prescription against Heretics* (*De Praescriptione Haereticorum*, c.200)**: Tertullian's most distinctive and influential work. Adapts the Roman legal concept of *praescriptio* (preliminary objection that precludes litigation) to argue that heretics have no right to appeal to the Scriptures, because the Scriptures belong to the Church that holds the apostolic tradition. Contains the classic formulation of the [[rule-of-faith|rule of faith]] (*praescr.* 13).
- ***Against Marcion* (*Adversus Marcionem*, 5 vols., c.207-212)**: The most sustained anti-Marcionite work in the patristic period. Books 1-2 argue against Marcion's dualistic theology; Books 3-5 offer a verse-by-verse refutation of Marcion's edited Gospel and Pauline letters.
- ***Against Valentinians***: Summary and refutation of Valentinian gnosticism.
- ***Against Praxeas***: Anti-Modalist; contains the clearest pre-Nicene articulation of Trinitarian theology (one *substantia*, three *personae*).

### Moral/Disciplinary

- ***On Baptism*, *On Prayer*, *On Repentance***: Practical theological works.
- ***On the Shows*, *On the Crown*, *On Idolatry***: Rigorist works against Christian participation in Roman public life.
- ***On the Veiling of Virgins*, *On Monogamy*, *On Fasting***: Later, increasingly Montanist-influenced works advocating stricter sexual and ascetic discipline.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Catholic Tradition

Tertullian is recognized as one of the most important Latin Church Fathers, though never formally canonized or declared a Doctor of the Church (due to his Montanist phase). His Trinitarian vocabulary (*trinitas*, *persona*, *substantia*) became standard in Latin theology and was formalized at Nicaea and Chalcedon. The *Apologeticus* is a monument of Latin prose and apologetics. The *Prescription against Heretics* formulated the principle that Scripture belongs to the Church — a foundational Catholic argument.

### Protestant Tradition

Tertullian's appeal to *tradition* and the Church's custodianship of Scripture was contested by Protestant reformers who used Scripture against tradition. Yet his emphasis on the rule of faith as a summary of what Scripture teaches — rather than as an independent authority — is sometimes read as proto-Protestant. His Trinitarian formulations are shared by Catholic and Protestant orthodoxy alike.

### Montanist Legacy

Tertullian's Montanist phase is treated as a regrettable deviation by most traditions. However, scholars (de Labriolle, Heine) argue his Montanist writings do not abandon orthodox Christology or Trinitarianism — they simply push for greater charismatic openness and stricter moral discipline. His "Montanism" was more a shift in emphasis than a doctrinal break.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The *Praescriptio* Argument

Tertullian's legal contribution to theology: heretics have no *standing* (*locus standi*) to dispute Scripture, because the Church had the Scriptures before the heretics existed, and it is the custodian of the apostolic tradition. The argument runs:
1. The apostles planted churches across the world
2. Those churches preserved the apostolic teaching
3. The rule of faith is the apostolic teaching's summary
4. Any group that contradicts the rule of faith is not appealing to the same apostolic teaching — they are using someone else's property

This anticipates later Catholic and Anglican appeals to *traditio* as the proper context for scriptural interpretation.

### Trinitarian Vocabulary

Tertullian coined or fixed the Latin technical terminology of Trinitarian theology: *trinitas* (trinity), *substantia* (substance/being), *persona* (person), *unitas substantiae* (unity of substance), *distinctio personarum* (distinction of persons). His formulation — "one substance, three persons" — was essentially the framework ratified at Constantinople (381 CE) and Chalcedon (451 CE) for Trinitarian and Christological definition.

### The Blood of Martyrs

Tertullian's observation that persecution makes Christianity grow was a strategic apologetic argument, not mere bravado: the willingness to die for a belief demonstrated its sincerity and attracted observers. He turned the logic of persecution against the persecutors: the more you kill, the more we multiply.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Legal training**: The tradition that Tertullian was a lawyer or legal professional is plausible given the rhetorical style but cannot be confirmed from independent sources.

**Montanist break**: The degree to which Tertullian "broke" with orthodox Christianity is debated. Some scholars (Barnes) argue he gradually separated; others (Dunn) argue he maintained dual identity. The "Tertullianist" sect Augustine mentions may have been a minor local phenomenon rather than a formal schism.

**Authorship disputes**: The *Apologeticus* and the *Prescription* are universally attributed; some shorter works are debated.

## Influence on Commentary Tradition

Tertullian's influence is pervasive but often invisible because his vocabulary became the standard. Cyprian of Carthage, who followed him by a generation, reportedly called him "the Master." Augustine engaged extensively with his works. The entire Latin apologetic and anti-heretical tradition builds on his framework. His rigorism influenced later ascetic movements and the donatist controversy.

## Coining the Trinity ([[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]])

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) credits Tertullian
as the **first Christian author to use the word [[trinity|Trinity]]** (*trinitas*), in the course
of refuting **[[modalism|modalism]]** (*Against Praxeas*) — arguing that John 10:30 ("I and the
Father *are* one") uses a plural verb and that one cannot be father and son to oneself. Yet his
Trinity is graded ("the Father is greater than the Son"), a position later judged heretical: "Theology,
in these early years … could not stand still." He also wrote the five-volume *Against Marcion*, the
chief source on [[marcion|Marcion]]'s [[docetism|docetism]].

## Tradition and the Rule of Faith (Kelly)

[[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|Kelly]] (ch. II) makes Tertullian, with Irenaeus, a central witness to the patristic relation of Scripture and tradition. In the *De praescriptione haereticorum* he argued the futility of disputing Scripture with heretics who "twisted its plain meaning," and so preferred the *[[rule-of-faith|regula fidei]]* as the standard of debate — not as a separate source, but as "the intrinsic shape and pattern of the revelation itself," the key to right exegesis. He extended the word "tradition" to long-established customs (triple immersion at baptism, the sign of the cross), an innovation in usage, while insisting "no secret tradition existed" and that authentic doctrine is marked by [[apostolic-succession|apostolic foundation]] and the churches' "otherwise inexplicable unanimity."
