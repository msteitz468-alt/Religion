# Abu Bakr al-Siddiq

**Abu Bakr ibn Abi Quhafa** (c. 573–634 CE), known as **al-Siddiq** (the Truthful), was the
Prophet [[muhammad|Muhammad]]'s closest companion, the first adult free male to accept Islam,
and the first caliph (*khalifa*) after Muhammad's death (r. 632–634 CE). He is one of the most
frequently cited figures in [[sahih-al-bukhari]], appearing as both a subject of prophetic praise
and as a transmitter of hadiths.

## Biographical Overview

Abu Bakr was a Meccan merchant of the Quraysh tribe, approximately two years older than Muhammad.
His birth name is often given as Abd Allah (or Abd al-Ka'ba before Islam). He was a close friend
of Muhammad before the first revelation and the first adult male to accept Islam, though the
traditional accounts credit Khadija (the Prophet's wife) as the very first Muslim.

- **Early Islam**: Among the very first converts. In Bukhari's account (Book 57, No. 12): "I saw
  Allah's Apostle and there was none with him but five slaves, two women and Abu Bakr" — among
  the extremely small original group.
- **Daughter Aisha**: Father of [[aisha|Aisha bint Abi Bakr]], the Prophet's wife and one of the
  most prolific transmitters of hadith.
- **Death**: r. 632–634 CE; died and was buried next to the Prophet in Medina, at Aisha's request.

## Primary Source Appearances — Sahih al-Bukhari

*Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, Vol. 4–5, Books 51–58. M. Muhsin Khan trans.*

### Title "al-Siddiq"

The title *al-Siddiq* (the Confirmer / the Truthful) is conferred explicitly in the Prophet's
statement when Uhud mountain shook (Book 57, Nos. 24, 35, 46, 49, narrated Anas):
> "Be firm, O Uhud! For on you there are no more than a Prophet, a Siddiq and two martyrs."

The Prophet also defended Abu Bakr's title against a challenger: "Allah sent me (as a Prophet)
to you but you said, 'You are telling a lie,' while Abu Bakr said, 'He has said the truth,'
and consoled me with himself and his money" (Book 57, No. 13, narrated Abu Ad-Darda).

### The Khalil Statements (Closest Companion)

Multiple hadiths record the Prophet's statement — in various transmissions — that he would have
taken Abu Bakr as his *Khalil* (bosom friend in the divine love-friendship sense) if not for
the fact that Allah is his Khalil:

> "The person who has favored me most of all both with his company and wealth, is Abu Bakr. If
> I were to take a Khalil other than my Lord, I would have taken Abu Bakr as such, but (what
> relates us) is the Islamic brotherhood and friendliness. All the gates of the Mosque should be
> closed except the gate of Abu Bakr." (Book 57, No. 6; paralleled in Nos. 8, 9, 10, 11)

The exclusive open gate is interpreted as symbolic of Abu Bakr's unique spiritual access to the
Prophet.

### The Hijra — Cave of Thawr

Abu Bakr accompanied the Prophet on the flight from Mecca to Medina (622 CE), hiding with him
in the Cave of Thawr while Quraysh pursers searched nearby:

> **Book 57, No. 5** (Abu Bakr): "I said to the Prophet while I was in the Cave, 'If any of
> them should look under his feet, he would see us.' He said, 'O Abu Bakr! What do you think
> of two (persons) the third of whom is Allah?'"

This corresponds to Quran 9:40 ("He being the second of two… Allah sent down His Sakinah upon
him"). The Hijra narrative in Book 58 (Nos. 245–259) provides extended detail: Abu Bakr's
earlier attempted migration to Ethiopia, his patron Ibn Ad-Daghina's endorsement of Abu Bakr's
character ("you help the destitute, earn their livings, keep good relations with your kith and
kin, help the weak and poor, entertain guests generously"), Abu Bakr's milking a shepherd's
sheep for the Prophet on the road, and Suraqa bin Malik's pursuit (and miraculous defeat).

### At the Prophet's Death — Steadfastness

Abu Bakr's most decisive moment comes at the Prophet's death (Book 57, No. 19, narrated Aisha):
> Abu Bakr said: "Whoever worshipped Muhammad, then Muhammad is dead, but whoever worshipped
> Allah, then Allah is Alive and shall never die." He then recited Q 3:144.

Aisha comments: "Allah benefited the people by their two speeches" (referring to Abu Bakr's
and then Umar's). Abu Bakr then argued at the Saqifa of Bani Saida for the Quraysh's
succession claim over the Ansar's, offering Umar or Abu Ubayda for leadership. Umar took Abu
Bakr's hand and pledged allegiance, and the people followed.

### Ranking of Companions

Multiple hadiths establish Abu Bakr as first among the Companions:

- **Ibn Umar** (Book 57, Nos. 7 and 47): "We used to compare the people as to who was better
  during the lifetime of Allah's Apostle. We used to regard Abu Bakr as the best, then Umar,
  and then Uthman."
- **The Prophet's love-ranking** (No. 14, Amr bin Al-As): Asked who the Prophet loved most:
  "Aisha." "Among men?" "Her father (Abu Bakr), then Umar."
- **Ali's own testimony** (No. 20, Muhammad bin Al-Hanafiya): He asked his father Ali, "Who
  are the best people after Allah's Apostle?" Ali said: "Abu Bakr." Then Umar. Ali described
  himself as "only an ordinary person." — Ali himself publicly ranking Abu Bakr first.

### Dream Interpretation — The Caliphate

**No. 16** (Abu Huraira): The Prophet dreamed of drawing water from a well; "Ibn Abi Quhafa
(Abu Bakr) took the bucket from me and brought out one or two buckets of water, and there was
weakness in his drawing the water. May Allah forgive his weakness for him." This is consistently
interpreted as foreshadowing Abu Bakr's brief caliphate and the challenges he faced.

### Called from All Gates of Paradise

**No. 18** (Abu Huraira): The Prophet described the one called "from all (Paradise) gates" as
requiring nothing else. When Abu Bakr asked whether anyone would be so called: "Yes, and I hope
you will be among those, O Abu Bakr."

### Zakat Enforcement

Abu Bakr's decision after the Prophet's death to fight those who withheld Zakat is documented
in Book 24 (No. 536): "By Allah, I would definitely fight against him who severed prayer from
Zakat, for it is the obligation upon the rich." This established Zakat as a state-enforceable
obligation, not merely a personal religious act.

### Fadak — The Inheritance Ruling

Abu Bakr refused Fatima's claim to Fadak and the Prophet's other properties on the basis of the
prophetic hadith "Our property will not be inherited; whatever we leave is Sadaqa." See
[[fadak-inheritance]] for the full dispute.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

In Sunni Islam, Abu Bakr is the paradigmatic Companion — the man closest to the Prophet, the
first to believe, the steadfast caliph who preserved Islam through the *Ridda* (apostasy) wars.
His willingness to fight Zakat-refusers is considered the act that preserved the institutional
structure of Islam after the Prophet's death.

In Shia tradition, his refusal of Fatima's Fadak claim and his assumption of the caliphate (seen
as legitimately belonging to Ali) make him a more contested figure, though Shia sources do not
generally condemn him as harshly as Umar.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The Sunni hadith corpus is narrated largely through chains that privilege Abu Bakr's companion
networks (his daughter Aisha, Ibn Umar, etc.). The Shia reading of the same events emphasizes
different narrators and arrives at different conclusions about the significance of Abu Bakr's
actions at the Saqifa and in the Fadak ruling. See [[fadak-inheritance]] and the [[sunni-islam]]
/ [[shia-islam]] pages for the traditions' respective accounts.
