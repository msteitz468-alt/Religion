# Rāma

**Rāma** (also Rāmacandra) is the seventh avatar of **Viṣṇu** and the hero of the
**[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]**. He is the model of the ideal man (*maryādā puruṣottama*), the perfect son,
husband, and king — and simultaneously a figure whose moral imperfections the text labors to paper over,
with only partial success.

## Biographical Overview

Rāma is the eldest son of King Daśaratha of Ayodhyā (born of Queen Kausalyā). His divine birth results
from a **horse sacrifice** (*aśvamedha*): Viṣṇu becomes incarnate in Rāma (and partially in his three
half-brothers) in order to kill the ogre-king Rāvaṇa, who has a boon preventing any divine being from
killing him — but foolishly forgot to specify "humans." Rāma must therefore be fully mortal (and largely
unaware of his own divinity) in order to defeat Rāvaṇa.

He trains under the sage Viśvāmitra, kills various ogres (including the ogress Ṭāṭakā), and wins
**[[sita|Sītā]]** by lifting Janaka's great bow. His exile (fourteen years) is engineered by his
stepmother Kaikeyī. In exile he kills the ogre Rāvaṇa after Rāvaṇa abducts Sītā. After returning
to Ayodhyā and ruling in the idealized "Rāma-rāj," he banishes Sītā twice — once after the fire ordeal
and once while she is pregnant — and eventually performs a horse sacrifice during which Sītā enters the
earth permanently. Rāma's return to heaven as Viṣṇu closes the epic.

## Avatar Dynamics

Rāma "is a god in the making": unlike Krishna (who is fully self-aware of his divinity), Rāma must
repeatedly be reminded by gods that he is Viṣṇu. He says of himself, "I think of myself as a man,
as Rāma the son of King Daśaratha." His divinity increases in the centuries *after* Valmiki, as
later tradition works to erase his imperfections — while Sītā (an ex-goddess) descends from divine
to human on the same trajectory Rāma is climbing. They meet like people on adjacent escalators,
"Rāma on the way up, Sītā on the way down."

## Moral Imperfections

The Rāmāyaṇa shows a Rāma who:
- **Kills Vālin (the monkey)** by shooting him in the back while he is fighting his brother Sugrīva —
  a dharmic violation the dying Vālin explicitly protests. Rāma defends himself: "Even sages go
  hunting. After all, you're just a monkey."
- **Doubts Sītā publicly** before the fire ordeal, saying "What man of good family could take back a
  woman who had lived in the house of another man?" — despite knowing she is chaste.
- **Banishes Sītā** twice, each time citing public opinion over personal knowledge of her innocence.
- **Beheads Śambuka**, a Śūdra ascetic, for performing tapas above his caste station — enforcing the
  caste system in his capacity as avatar of the cosmic order.
- **Taunts Śūrpaṇakhā** cruelly before Lakṣmaṇa mutilates her — behavior that triggers the entire war.

**Rāma's relationship to his father.** A recurring anxiety: Daśaratha was destroyed by lust for
Kaikeyī. Rāma repeatedly distances himself from this — "Sex is much more potent than either artha
or dharma… What man, even an idiot like father, would give up a good son like me for a pretty woman?"
— and then, paradoxically, banishes the wife he loves because he fears what people will say. "Politics
is driving Rāma to make a sexual and religious mistake; public concerns make him banish the wife he
loves. Rāma banishes Sītā as Daśaratha has banished Rāma."

## The Unconscious Dimension: Rāma and the Monkeys

The parallel monkey plot is, in Doniger's reading, Rāma's dream-work. His unconscious anger at his
half-brother and father — anger that his role as "perfect son" forbids him to express — is projected
onto Sugrīva and Vālin. Sugrīva (the "deposed" monkey king) mirrors Rāma's self-image; Vālin (the
"usurper") is the screen onto which Rāma's displaced resentment is vented. "In the magical world of
the monkey forest, Rāma's unconscious mind is set free."

The parallel extends to Lakṣmaṇa: the text's overdetermined insistence that Lakṣmaṇa never desired
Sītā, that he had "not yet experienced the joys of a wife's company" — it "doth protest too much."
When Rāma must "kill" Lakṣmaṇa near the end of the epic, he solves the problem by banishing him
instead (since separation from Rāma would be as bad as death for Lakṣmaṇa). The suppressed desire
had already been acted out on Vālin.

## Theological Significance

Rāma is the paradigm of Vaiṣṇava avatāra theology: God becomes fully human to work within the world's
rules. His mortality is not a limitation but the mechanism of cosmic justice — only a human can kill
Rāvaṇa. His ignorance of his own divinity is structurally necessary and, for devotional Hinduism,
an occasion for compassion rather than doctrinal difficulty.

In later bhakti, his name ("Rāma") becomes a mantra: the dying Maricha (an ogre) becomes so terrified
of Rāma that he thinks of him constantly — an early instance of "hate-love" (*dveṣa-bhakti*), which
Doniger notes "foreshadows the importance of the name of Rāma in later bhakti."

## Reception across Traditions

The Rāmāyaṇa has been retold across South and Southeast Asia, in dozens of languages and hundreds of
versions. Regional traditions have refocused the text — making Rāvaṇa the hero (South Indian perspectives),
emphasizing Sītā's divinity, or (as in a later Sanskrit-Jaina retelling) exonerating Drona's treatment
of Ekalavya by analogy. In 2002 political Hinduism invoked Rāma's governance of Ayodhyā as justification
for the site of the Babri Masjid mosque demolition.

Doniger: "Sītā and Rāma continue to shape attitudes to women and to political conflict in India to
this day" — and "alas, it has proved all too capable of doing" so in violent directions when used to
"rally religious fanatics."

## William Buck's Retelling: The Dharma Wheel (Lines 13566–16313)

### The Coronation and the Return

After fourteen years of exile and the war of Lanka, Rama returns to [[ayodhya|Ayodhyā]] on the flying
chariot Pushpaka. Bharata restores Rama's sandals to his feet and returns the kingdom: "A child cannot
easily pick up and carry the burden set down by a man. We have survived only by the power of your
sandals. Take this land back, my king" (l. 14579–14582). The coronation is elaborate: Vasishtha pours
waters from all four seas over Rama's head "and made him King and Lord of Earth; the Solar King of Fair
Ayodhya, the Kosala Lord, and Sita was his one Queen with him" (l. 14696–14698).

### Rama-raj: The Ideal Reign

"So passed the first day of Rama's long reign of eleven thousand years" (l. 14721). Buck describes
the reign in explicitly utopian terms:

> "Rama was strong enough to support all men, and gentle as the new Moon's beams. Fame and Wealth never
> left him. When he was king men were long in life, and lived surrounded by their children and
> grandchildren… The old never had to make funerals for the young. There was rain and fertile Earth;
> indeed, the Earth became bountiful. Peace and Rama ruled as friends together, and bad things did not
> happen. Men grew kind and fearless. Everyone had about him a certain air and look of good fortune."
> (l. 14726–14735)

The reign also had a quality of natural communication: "many creatures and created things could speak
to men sometimes. When deer ate the crops the Kosalas would speak with the deer kings in goodwill…
If one wished to hear stories he could speak with an old sword about ancient battles, or hear the old
tales told by the trees and stones" (l. 14787–14793). See → [[rama-raj|Rāma-rāj]].

### The Second Banishment of Sita

After nearly ten thousand years of reign, Sita is pregnant and asks to visit the Ganges retreats.
Rama gives his permission "with great love and happiness" — and in the same passage we read: "And with
those kind and innocent words sorrow again came into Rama's life" (l. 14808–14809).

That night Rama asks a minister what his people say of him. The minister Bhadra eventually reveals:
"They say — The King desires Sita although Ravana touched her. How could he forget she lived with
another?" (l. 14843–14845). Rama meets Lakshmana privately; his face is "pale, his hands trembled
like leaves in a wind, the lights of his eyes were gone as though clouded over" (l. 14851–14853).
Lakshmana protests: "Fire himself proved her innocent. She is fired gold, poured into golden fire!"
(l. 14862–14863). But Rama insists: "Kings cannot afford blame. Ill fame is evil to kings; they above
all men must be beyond reproach" (l. 14865–14866). He orders Lakshmana to take Sita to the forest and
abandon her there.

Lakshmana says: "Surely a king is remote and lonely, and very far from reason. We cannot speak to you…"
(l. 14882–14883). Rama responds: "Each person can be told what he will understand of the nature of the
world, and no more than that — for the rest, take my word" (l. 14884–14886). Lakshmana obeys, framing
his obedience as dharmic submission to a mystery beyond his understanding.

### Grief and the Golden Statue

Rama rules the remaining years with a golden statue of Sita beside him: "I have made a golden statue
of Sita that will stand beside me" (l. 15207–15208). He cannot bear to speak of her but cannot let
her go. When Valmiki asks him to receive Sita back before all his people at the festival, Rama weeps:
"Tears flowed down Rama's face from his green eyes. 'Rise, Poet'" (l. 15620–15621).

### Encounter with Kusha and Lava

Rama listens to Kusa and Lava sing the Ramayana every day for a year: "The longer I hear those two
boys sing the more I love to listen; the longer I look on them the more I want to see them" (l. 15571–15573).
He recognizes them at the end: "Those twin brothers are my two sons" (l. 15585). He praises Valmiki
for the poem in terms that reveal how deeply he received the story of his own life — "I shall never
forget those verses" (l. 15602).

### Sita's Departure and Rama's Response

When Sita enters the earth before Rama and all his assembled guests, Rama says: "I am King and Lord of
Earth, but she has taken my wife away before my very eyes. I will never meet Sita again while I live
as a man" — and "still he was smiling" (l. 15770–15772). The smile is the text's most complex moment:
Rama receives even this loss within a dharmic equanimity he cannot fully explain to those around him.
After the festival he sits with Janaka and they drink together until Rama falls asleep. "Rama, why am
I here? What am I doing here? Can you tell me why?" (l. 15789–15790). Janaka: "Whatever you have done,
you did it to serve the good Dharma Law" (l. 15791–15792).

### The Visit of Time and Lakshmana's Death

A thousand years after the festival, Time comes disguised as a hermit and delivers Brahma's message:
"You killed Ravana. Your promised time is full. Oh Narayana, Lakshmi awaits you… Return to yourself"
(l. 15925–15927). Rama sets a condition: anyone who enters the room while he speaks with Time must
die. The irascible sage Durvasas threatens to curse all of Kosala unless Lakshmana admits him. Lakshmana
enters to protect the kingdom and says to Rama, "You must die for entering here" (l. 15951). Rama
tells him: "Lakshmana, you must die for entering here" (l. 15951). Lakshmana walks to the Sarayu
River and dies voluntarily, stopping his breathing: "The luminous person within Lakshmana's heart…
rose step by step up along his backbone, seeking flight out the crown of his head" (l. 15985–15991).
Indra takes his soul to heaven.

Rama says farewell: "Farewell, my brother" (l. 15972). Lakshmana makes no answer.

### Rama's Final Departure

Rama tells Vasishtha: "I'm going all the way on through to my own home now, I'm going back around by
going forward, and I'll be again as I was before being born into this world" (l. 16043–16045). He walks
north to the Sarayu River with all of Ayodhya following — Bharata, Satrughna, monkeys, bears, Naga
serpents, hermits, all the creatures of the world. "Lakshmi of the Lotus walked on his right; Mother
Earth went patiently on his left" (l. 16096–16097). Rama gives Jambavan and Hanuman each a gift and
a blessing. Then: "Trust the True! The King took a broad jump and went first in through the water. He
went on through to the other side, winning all time past and all to come" (l. 16222–16226).

Heaven receives him instantly: "Narayana was back in his heaven, with Lakshmi an instant later. The
four dark hands picked up again the shell and the lotus, the discus and the mace" (l. 16272–16279).

### Rama's Divinity — Buck's Handling

Throughout this section, Rama consistently declines to acknowledge his own divinity even when gods
name him directly. At the reunion after Lanka, both Agni and Indra address him as "Narayana," but
Rama says "I am Rama, a mortal man" (l. 13883). Even at the end, when Time delivers Brahma's message
naming him Narayana, Rama says only "I remember all of you" (l. 15930) — an acknowledgment, not a
theological assertion. Buck's Rama inhabits his mortality with complete conviction; his divinity is
real but perpetually deferred.

Shiva's final action in the text is characteristic of Buck's style: "Shiva the great god divided this
romance into three parts, one for heaven, one for Earth, and one for the underworlds. He had one
section left, and divided that into three. He had one verse left, and divided that as well. Shiva had
one word left, that he kept for himself — the word Rama — as being the essence and heart of the whole
thing" (l. 16016–16021).
