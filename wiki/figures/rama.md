# Rāma

**Rāma** (also Rāmacandra) is the seventh avatar of **Viṣṇu** and the hero of the
**[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]**. He is the model of the ideal man (*maryādā puruṣottama*), the perfect son,
husband, and king — and simultaneously a figure whose moral imperfections the text labors to paper over,
with only partial success.

## Biographical Overview

Rāma is the eldest son of King Daśaratha of Ayodhyā (born of Queen Kausalyā). His divine birth results
from a **horse sacrifice** (*aśvamedha*): Viṣṇu becomes incarnate in Rāma (and partially in his three
half-brothers) in order to kill the ogre-king Rāvaṇa, who has a boon preventing any divine being from
killing him — but foolishly forgot to specify "humans." Rāma must therefore be fully mortal (and largely
unaware of his own divinity) in order to defeat Rāvaṇa.

He trains under the sage Viśvāmitra, kills various ogres (including the ogress Ṭāṭakā), and wins
**[[sita|Sītā]]** by lifting Janaka's great bow. His exile (fourteen years) is engineered by his
stepmother Kaikeyī. In exile he kills the ogre Rāvaṇa after Rāvaṇa abducts Sītā. After returning
to Ayodhyā and ruling in the idealized "Rāma-rāj," he banishes Sītā twice — once after the fire ordeal
and once while she is pregnant — and eventually performs a horse sacrifice during which Sītā enters the
earth permanently. Rāma's return to heaven as Viṣṇu closes the epic.

## Avatar Dynamics

Rāma "is a god in the making": unlike Krishna (who is fully self-aware of his divinity), Rāma must
repeatedly be reminded by gods that he is Viṣṇu. He says of himself, "I think of myself as a man,
as Rāma the son of King Daśaratha." His divinity increases in the centuries *after* Valmiki, as
later tradition works to erase his imperfections — while Sītā (an ex-goddess) descends from divine
to human on the same trajectory Rāma is climbing. They meet like people on adjacent escalators,
"Rāma on the way up, Sītā on the way down."

## Moral Imperfections

The Rāmāyaṇa shows a Rāma who:
- **Kills Vālin (the monkey)** by shooting him in the back while he is fighting his brother Sugrīva —
  a dharmic violation the dying Vālin explicitly protests. Rāma defends himself: "Even sages go
  hunting. After all, you're just a monkey."
- **Doubts Sītā publicly** before the fire ordeal, saying "What man of good family could take back a
  woman who had lived in the house of another man?" — despite knowing she is chaste.
- **Banishes Sītā** twice, each time citing public opinion over personal knowledge of her innocence.
- **Beheads Śambuka**, a Śūdra ascetic, for performing tapas above his caste station — enforcing the
  caste system in his capacity as avatar of the cosmic order.
- **Taunts Śūrpaṇakhā** cruelly before Lakṣmaṇa mutilates her — behavior that triggers the entire war.

**Rāma's relationship to his father.** A recurring anxiety: Daśaratha was destroyed by lust for
Kaikeyī. Rāma repeatedly distances himself from this — "Sex is much more potent than either artha
or dharma… What man, even an idiot like father, would give up a good son like me for a pretty woman?"
— and then, paradoxically, banishes the wife he loves because he fears what people will say. "Politics
is driving Rāma to make a sexual and religious mistake; public concerns make him banish the wife he
loves. Rāma banishes Sītā as Daśaratha has banished Rāma."

## The Unconscious Dimension: Rāma and the Monkeys

The parallel monkey plot is, in Doniger's reading, Rāma's dream-work. His unconscious anger at his
half-brother and father — anger that his role as "perfect son" forbids him to express — is projected
onto Sugrīva and Vālin. Sugrīva (the "deposed" monkey king) mirrors Rāma's self-image; Vālin (the
"usurper") is the screen onto which Rāma's displaced resentment is vented. "In the magical world of
the monkey forest, Rāma's unconscious mind is set free."

The parallel extends to Lakṣmaṇa: the text's overdetermined insistence that Lakṣmaṇa never desired
Sītā, that he had "not yet experienced the joys of a wife's company" — it "doth protest too much."
When Rāma must "kill" Lakṣmaṇa near the end of the epic, he solves the problem by banishing him
instead (since separation from Rāma would be as bad as death for Lakṣmaṇa). The suppressed desire
had already been acted out on Vālin.

## Theological Significance

Rāma is the paradigm of Vaiṣṇava avatāra theology: God becomes fully human to work within the world's
rules. His mortality is not a limitation but the mechanism of cosmic justice — only a human can kill
Rāvaṇa. His ignorance of his own divinity is structurally necessary and, for devotional Hinduism,
an occasion for compassion rather than doctrinal difficulty.

In later bhakti, his name ("Rāma") becomes a mantra: the dying Maricha (an ogre) becomes so terrified
of Rāma that he thinks of him constantly — an early instance of "hate-love" (*dveṣa-bhakti*), which
Doniger notes "foreshadows the importance of the name of Rāma in later bhakti."

## Reception across Traditions

The Rāmāyaṇa has been retold across South and Southeast Asia, in dozens of languages and hundreds of
versions. Regional traditions have refocused the text — making Rāvaṇa the hero (South Indian perspectives),
emphasizing Sītā's divinity, or (as in a later Sanskrit-Jaina retelling) exonerating Drona's treatment
of Ekalavya by analogy. In 2002 political Hinduism invoked Rāma's governance of Ayodhyā as justification
for the site of the Babri Masjid mosque demolition.

Doniger: "Sītā and Rāma continue to shape attitudes to women and to political conflict in India to
this day" — and "alas, it has proved all too capable of doing" so in violent directions when used to
"rally religious fanatics."
