# Noah

## Biographical Overview
Noah is the tenth and last of the pre-flood Patriarchs. According to the Genesis narrative, he was the only righteous man of his generation, chosen by God to survive the global deluge in an ark, preserving human and animal life. Following the flood, he becomes the recipient of the Noahic Covenant, marked by the rainbow, and the progenitor of the post-flood nations.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Genesis 6-9**: The primary narrative of the building of the ark, the flood, the covenant, and his later drunkenness.
- **1 Peter 3:19-21**: Noah's salvation through the floodwaters is presented typologically as a precursor to Christian baptism. 
- **2 Peter 2:5**: Describes Noah as a "herald of righteousness" who was preserved while the world of the ungodly was destroyed.
- **Hebrews 11:7**: Highlights his faith in building the ark based on things "not yet seen."
- **1 Enoch**: Expands heavily on the flood narrative, placing it in the context of the judgment of the fallen Watchers.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Noah functions as the ultimate archetype of rescue in the midst of cosmic judgment. In 1 and 2 Peter, he is invoked to assure suffering Christians that God is able to preserve the righteous while condemning the wicked. The flood is not merely an act of destruction but an act of cosmic cleansing, forming the typological basis for baptism in early Christian thought. As a "herald of righteousness" (2 Peter), Noah models the faithful witness to a hostile world. Whether the deluge he survived was global or regional is treated separately under [[flood-universality|the Flood's scope]].

## Tradition-Specific Reception
**Christianity**: In addition to his role in Hebrews and the Petrine epistles, early Christian tradition read the ark as a type of the Church, within which one is saved from the waters of judgment.

**Kabbalah (the Zohar)**: The [[zohar|Zohar]]'s Parashat Noaḥ reads the Flood narrative at the **Sod** level as a cosmic-sexual drama, and treats Noah himself with notable ambivalence:

- **Limited, "this-worldly" righteousness**: The Zohar dwells on the qualifying phrase "Noah was righteous *in his generations*" (Gen 6:9). His righteousness was relative and incomplete — sufficient to save himself but not his generation. Unlike [[abraham|Abraham]], who pleaded for Sodom, Noah did not intercede for the doomed world; he built his ark and was silent. The Zohar (drawing on rabbinic ambivalence) faults him for this passivity. Some passages contrast him unfavorably with Moses, who placed himself between God's wrath and Israel.
- **The Flood as the failure of the divine union**: The generation of the Flood sinned chiefly through sexual corruption — "all flesh had corrupted its way" (Gen 6:12), read as the wasting of seed and improper union (see [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]]). Their sin disrupted the cosmic union of the upper and lower waters (Yesod and [[shekhinah|Malkhut/Shekhinah]]). The punishment mirrors the crime: corrupted waters below are answered by the unleashing of the waters above and below in the Flood.
- **Noah hidden from the Destroyer**: The ark is read as a refuge in which Noah is concealed from the "Destroyer" / Angel of Death (a face of the Sitra Aḥra) who walks abroad once permission to destroy has been granted — the same principle as the blood on the doorposts at Passover ("when the Destroyer is given license, it does not distinguish between righteous and wicked"). Entering the ark is entering the protection of the Shekhinah / the supernal Tevah ("ark/word").
- **The post-Flood world incomplete**: The renewed world after the Flood remains defective; the *zuhama* (the serpent's filth from [[adam|Adam]]'s sin) persists in humanity. The full repair (*tikkun*) awaits Sinai and ultimately the Messiah. Noah's drunkenness and exposure (Gen 9:20–27) figure this incompleteness — a righteous man who plants a vineyard and falls into disorder, re-enacting in miniature the unguarded nakedness that opens the way to the Other Side.

This portrait flags a **contradiction** with the Christian reception above: where 1–2 Peter and Hebrews exalt Noah as the unambiguous "herald of righteousness" and exemplar of saving faith, the Zoharic (and underlying rabbinic) reading qualifies his righteousness sharply. The two are recorded side by side without adjudication.
