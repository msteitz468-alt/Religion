# Umar ibn al-Khattab

**Umar ibn al-Khattab** (c. 584–644 CE), known as **al-Faruq** (the Distinguisher), was the
second caliph of Islam (r. 634–644 CE) and one of the most prominent figures in [[sahih-al-bukhari]].
He is the transmitter of the opening hadith of the entire collection — the *niyyah* (intention)
teaching — and is portrayed throughout as a figure of exceptional strength, insight, and
eschatological significance.

## Biographical Overview

Umar was a Meccan merchant of the Quraysh tribe who initially violently opposed Islam; his
conversion is presented as a pivotal turning point for the early Muslim community. Before
converting, he tied and persecuted Muslims (Book 58, No. 202: Said bin Zaid recalls being tied
by Umar before Umar himself had converted). After converting, his effect was immediate:
> "We have been powerful since Umar embraced Islam." (Book 57, No. 33)

He served as the first caliph for two years under [[abu-bakr]] and succeeded him as second caliph.
He was assassinated by a Persian slave (Abu Lu'lu'a) while leading the Fajr prayer in 644 CE.

## Primary Source Appearances — Sahih al-Bukhari

*Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, Vol. 4–5, Books 51–58. M. Muhsin Khan trans.*

### The Niyyah Hadith — Opening the Collection

Umar transmits the hadith that opens [[sahih-al-bukhari]] and the entire Sunni hadith tradition:
> "The reward of deeds depends upon intentions and every person will get the reward according to
> what he has intended." (Vol. 1, Book 1, No. 1)

This is the most quoted hadith in Islamic ethics and the hermeneutical key to Islamic practice —
that intention (*niyyah*) determines the religious validity of any act.

### Conversion and Its Effects (Book 57–58)

**Book 57, No. 33** (Abdullah): "We have been powerful since Umar embraced Islam." This
signals the communal change his conversion effected in the early Muslims' ability to practice
openly.

**Book 58, Nos. 204–205**: The conversion account — the pagans gathered around his home
threatening violence; Al-As bin Wail (a pre-Islamic ally) extended protection publicly so
the crowd dispersed.

**Book 57, No. 32** (Sad bin Abi Waqqas): "Quraishi women used to lower their voices and veil
from Umar when he passed." The Prophet said to him:
> "Never does Satan find you going on a way, but he takes another way other than yours."

This is one of the key hadith about Umar's moral force: Satan himself avoids Umar's path.

### The Muhaddath — Inspired Without Prophethood

**Book 57, No. 38** (Abu Huraira):
> "Among the nations before you there used to be people who were inspired (though they were not
> prophets). And if there is any of such persons amongst my followers, it is Umar."

The concept of *muhaddath* (one whose intuitions prove true as if divinely guided) is applied
specifically to Umar — the highest praise short of prophethood.

### Dreams Foreshadowing the Caliphate

**Book 57, Nos. 25 / 31** (Ibn Umar): The Prophet dreamed of drawing water from a well;
Abu Bakr drew "one or two buckets with weakness"; then Umar:
> "Umar bin Al-Khattab took the bucket and it turned into a very large bucket. I have never
> seen any powerful man among the people doing so perfectly as Umar."
Interpreted consistently as foreshadowing the strength of Umar's caliphate.

**Book 57, No. 40** (Abu Said): The Prophet dreamed of people in shirts; Umar's shirt was so
long he dragged it — interpreted as "Religion": Umar embodies the fullness of the religion.

**Book 57, No. 30** (Hamza's father): A dream in which milk was given to Umar, interpreted as
"Knowledge."

**Book 57, No. 28 / 29** (Jabir / Abu Huraira): The Prophet dreamed of a palace in Paradise
and was told it was for Umar, but refrained from entering "out of respect for Umar's *ghira*
(jealous self-respect)." Umar wept: "How dare I think of my ghira being offended by you?"

### "The Door" — Umar's Eschatological Role

**Book 57, No. 786** (Hudhaifa): Umar is identified as "the door" between the Muslim community
and coming *fitn* (civil strife / afflictions):
> "There is a door between you and the afflictions, and that person (i.e. Umar) is the door."
When asked if the door would be broken or opened: "It will be broken." Hudhaifa's interlocutor
understood: if the door was broken (i.e., Umar was killed), the afflictions could never again
be shut out.

### Grouped Eternally with the Prophet and Abu Bakr

**Book 57, Nos. 26 and 34** (Ibn Abbas, narrated at Umar's deathbed): Ali stood over Umar and said:
> "I always hoped that Allah will keep you with your two companions, for I often heard Allah's
> Apostle saying, 'I, Abu Bakr and Umar were (somewhere); I, Abu Bakr and Umar did (something);
> I, Abu Bakr and Umar set out.'"

This triple grouping — "I, Abu Bakr and Umar" — occurs repeatedly in prophetic speech and is
read in Sunni tradition as a permanent divine grouping. Ali's eulogy thus endorses this reading.

### Umar's Death and Burial (Book 57, No. 50)

Stabbed by Abu Lu'lu'a while leading Fajr prayer. Before dying, Umar:
- Designated a consultative council (*shura*) of six: Ali, Uthman, Az-Zubair, Talha, Sad, and
  Abdur-Rahman bin Auf — to choose the next caliph.
- Gave final counsel to protect the Ansar, early emigrants, Arab bedouin, and dhimmis.
- Sought permission from Aisha to be buried next to the Prophet and Abu Bakr — granted.

The extended deathbed narrative (No. 50) is one of the most developed character portraits in
Books 57–58.

### Umar's Instructions Regarding Dhimmis

**Book 52, No. 287** (at his deathbed): Among Umar's final instructions:
> "I urge him (i.e. the new Caliph) to take care of those non-Muslims who are under the
> protection of Allah and His Apostle in that he should observe the convention agreed upon with
> them, and fight on their behalf (to secure their safety) and he should not over-tax them beyond
> their capability."

### Umar and the Waqf at Khaibar

Umar received agricultural land at Khaibar and asked the Prophet's advice. The Prophet told him:
"If you wish you can keep it as an endowment to be used for charitable purposes." Umar made it a
perpetual *waqf* — beneficiaries to include the poor, kinsmen, slaves to be freed, Jihad fighters,
guests, and travelers (Book 51, Nos. 26, 33–34). The Khaibar waqf is a foundational precedent
in Islamic endowment law.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

In Sunni Islam, Umar is the paradigm of Islamic governance and moral strength. The repeated
prophetic endorsements (Satan avoids his path, he is the *muhaddath* of this community, he
embodies Religion itself) place him as the practical executor of the Prophet's vision. His
assassination is read as the opening of the *fitna* era that has never fully closed.

In Shia tradition, Umar is a more contested figure — his alliance with Abu Bakr at the Saqifa
and his role in the caliphate succession are seen as steps that displaced Ali from his rightful
position. The Shia reading does not appear in Bukhari's framing but is documented in the Shia
Four Books.
