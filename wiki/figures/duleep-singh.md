# Maharaja Duleep Singh

The youngest son of [[ranjit-singh|Maharaja Ranjit Singh]] and the last sovereign of the
Sikh kingdom of [[lahore|Lahore]]. His reign — begun at about age seven and ended at eleven
with the British annexation of the [[punjab|Punjab]] — frames the entire collapse of the
empire his father built, narrated by Khushwant Singh in *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2.

## Biographical Overview

Born September 1837, youngest son of Ranjit Singh; his mother was **Rani Jindan** (Jind Kaur),
daughter of an officer in charge of the royal kennels, married to Ranjit Singh in 1835 (KS Vol. 2,
~p.20). He was proclaimed maharaja after the assassination of his half-brother Sher Singh in
September 1843, with Hira Singh Dogra as chief minister, and reigned as a child under successive
regents amid the army *pañcāyat*'s ascendancy.

On **29 March 1849** he formally handed over the **Koh-i-noor diamond** and stepped down from the
throne as the Punjab was annexed; a veteran's lament at the army's surrender — "*Āj Ranjīt Singh mar
gayā* — today Ranjit Singh has died" (KS ~p.92) — became the emblem of the dynasty's extinction. He
was later removed to British India and England, converted to Christianity, and died in exile in 1893.

## Regency: Rani Jindan (Jind Kaur)

His mother dominated the page Khushwant Singh devotes to the regency. After the murders of Hira Singh
and his adviser Jalla (December 1844) she assumed court functions, assisted by her brother Jawahar
Singh (vazir) and Raja Lal Singh. Singh records both her compromised position — the widely-rumoured
liaison with Lal Singh, and the charge that she, Lal Singh, and Tej Singh "were in communication with
the British and willing to sell the Punjab provided their lives and jagirs were secured" (KS ~p.51) —
**and** her transformation into a popular heroine: "The more restrictions and dishonour the British
heaped on Jindan, the more she became a heroine in the eyes of the people" (KS ~p.72). The resident
Currie admitted "she was the mother of all the Khalsa" (KS ~p.83). Pensioned and confined after the
Treaty of Bhairowal (1846), then deported to Benares (May 1848) — a removal Singh treats as a trigger
of the **Second Anglo-Sikh War**. Her protest to the resident: "Instead of being secretly king of the
country, why don't you declare yourself so?" (KS ~p.72)

## The Succession Crisis (1839–1843)

Singh narrates the "fratricidal" disintegration of Ranjit Singh's house through Duleep Singh's
predecessors — see [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] for the full sequence: **Kharak Singh** (the
"block-head" opium-eater), **Nao Nihal Singh** (killed when a fort archway collapsed at his father's
cremation, 5 November 1840 — Singh weighs but rejects the charge that Dhian Singh Dogra contrived
it), **Chand Kaur** (the regent *malikā mukaddas*, later murdered), and **Sher Singh** (the
"handsome and well-meaning dandy," assassinated September 1843). The **Dogra faction** — Dhian
Singh, Gulab Singh, Hira Singh — runs through every transition as, in Singh's reading, a fifth column
ultimately rewarded by the British with [[punjab|Kashmir]].

## Significance

Duleep Singh's deposition closes Vol. 1's story of Sikh political ascendancy. Singh's framing of the
boy-king — sovereign in name while regents, Dogras, and a British resident emptied the throne of power
— makes him the human figure of the [[annexation-of-the-punjab|annexation]], and the *sau sākhī*
prophecies later attached to his hoped-for return (with Russian help, in the 1880s) a recurring motif
of Sikh political longing.
