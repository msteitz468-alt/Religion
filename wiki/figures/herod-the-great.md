# Herod the Great

## Biographical Overview

**Herod the Great** was the Rome-appointed **king of Judea (37–4 BCE)**, founder of the
Herodian dynasty. He was of **[[idumeans|Idumean]]** descent — his people the forcibly
Judaized **[[edomites|Edomites]]** of the region south of Judea — a fact repeatedly thrown
against him as making him a **"half-Jew"** and an illegitimate "king of the Jews." Appointed
by the Roman Senate and installed with Roman arms, he ruled as a capable, ruthless **client
king**, navigating the civil wars of the late Republic (shifting from Antony to Octavian) to
hold his throne for over three decades. His later years were marked by dynastic paranoia and
the execution of his wife Mariamne and several of his own sons.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Matthew 2**: the dominant New Testament portrait — the **Magi** seek "the one born king
  of the Jews," and a troubled Herod, after consulting the chief priests about Bethlehem,
  orders the **massacre of the infants** there, prompting the **flight into Egypt**. His
  death (Matt. 2.19) allows the holy family's return.
- **Luke 1.5**: dates John the Baptist's and Jesus' nativity stories "in the days of Herod,
  king of Judea."
- **Josephus**: the indispensable historical source, depicting Herod's wars, building
  program, court intrigues, and brutality.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: an ambivalent-to-hostile memory — a magnificent **builder of the Temple** yet a
  cruel, Rome-serving usurper of dubious lineage; later rabbinic tradition is largely
  negative. His **Idumean ("Edomite") origin** feeds the older typology in which "Edom"
  becomes a cipher for oppressive **Rome** (see [[edomites]]).
- **Christian**: the archetype of the **tyrant threatened by the true King** — the
  Matthean massacre of the innocents makes him a paschal-Moses foil (Pharaoh ordering the
  killing of male infants), and a fixture of Nativity liturgy and art.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

In Matthew, Herod embodies **worldly kingship in mortal opposition to the kingdom of God** —
the reigning "king of the Jews" who tries to destroy the newborn "king of the Jews." His
Idumean blood and Roman sponsorship sharpen the irony and link the Gospel scene to the long
biblical history of Edom as Israel's kin-enemy.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Herod's reign, building works (the vastly expanded **Jerusalem Temple**, Caesarea Maritima,
Masada, the Herodium, Sebaste at [[samaria|Samaria]]) are richly attested archaeologically
and in Josephus. The **massacre of the innocents**, however, is recorded **only in Matthew**
and is widely regarded by critical scholars as a theological construction (echoing the Moses
infancy narrative) rather than an independently documented event, though it is consistent
with Herod's attested ruthlessness. His death is fixed to **4 BCE**, a key datum for dating
the nativity.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Herod anchors discussion of the **political world of the New Testament** (client kingship,
the Herodian dynasty — Antipas, Agrippa) and of Matthew's **typological** method (Jesus as
the new Moses). Related: [[idumeans]], [[edomites]], [[jerusalem]], [[samaria]],
[[pharisees]], [[persians]].
