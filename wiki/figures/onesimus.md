# Onesimus

Onesimus is the central figure in Paul's shortest letter, the runaway slave of Philemon who has become a Christian ("my child, whom I have begotten in my imprisonment," Phlm 10) and is now returning to his master with Paul's appeal for reconciliation and brotherly treatment. McKnight (NICNT) presents him as the test case for the gospel's power to transform social relationships, with enduring relevance for modern slaveries.

## Biographical Overview

A slave (δοῦλος) belonging to Philemon, a wealthy Christian in Colossae who hosted a house church (Phlm 2; cf. Col 4:9 "one of you"). He ran away (or was sent on an errand and did not return), encountered Paul in prison (Rome or Ephesus), was converted, and became "useful" (Ὀνήσιμος means "useful" or "profitable") to Paul in ministry. Paul sends him back with the letter, describing him as "my very heart" (v. 12) and asking Philemon to receive him "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (v. 16), "both in the flesh and in the Lord."

Paul offers to repay any debt or wrong Onesimus may have caused (v. 18-19), using promissory note language. The letter is to be read publicly in the house church, creating social pressure for the right response. Tradition (later) identifies him with the Onesimus who became bishop of Ephesus (Ignatius, *To the Ephesians* 1-2), though this is debated.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Philemon**: The entire letter is an appeal on his behalf. Paul presents him three times (vv. 8-11, 12-16, 17-21) in escalating rhetorical appeals. He is the "test case" for Christian brotherhood transcending social status.
- **Colossians 4:9**: Sent with Tychicus; "the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you." Confirms his Colossian origin and Christian identity. The two letters were likely delivered together.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Early Church**: The letter was preserved and read, showing the church valued its message of reconciliation. Later identification with the Ephesian bishop (Ignatius) elevated him. Used in debates over slavery (e.g., by abolitionists in the 19th century as evidence that the gospel undermines the institution).
- **Patristic/Medieval**: Seen as example of Paul's pastoral heart and the power of the gospel to make "brothers" out of slaves and masters. Used in discussions of manumission and Christian ethics of slavery.
- **Reformation and Modern**: Luther saw it as a model of Christian love and intercession ("I am willing to be your slave"). Modern commentators (McKnight) emphasize its radical social implications: the slave is to be received as a brother, with all economic and social consequences. Not explicit abolition, but the logic of the gospel makes chattel slavery incompatible with Christian community.
- **Critical Scholarship**: Debates over whether Paul expects legal manumission or only "spiritual" brotherhood within the church. Some see the letter as conservative (Paul returns the slave); others as subversive (public pressure + "brother" language + repayment offer effectively emancipates).
- **Contemporary**: Central text in discussions of modern slavery, human trafficking, racial reconciliation, and economic justice. McKnight explicitly asks what Paul would say to the church about "slaveries of all sorts" today.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Onesimus is the living embodiment of the gospel's power to reverse social realities. Once "useless" (perhaps through flight or theft), he is now "useful" (the meaning of his name) to Paul and, Paul argues, to Philemon "forever" (v. 15). The letter is a "presentation" of Onesimus: Paul presents the slave as a brother, using every lever (love, partnership, usefulness, providence, his own "heart," repayment, obedience, eschatological hope).

Theologically, the letter shows that in Christ there is neither slave nor free (Gal 3:28 applied concretely). Reconciliation is not merely vertical (with God) but horizontal (with one another), and it has social and economic teeth. Philemon is asked to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself (v. 17). The public reading in the house church makes the appeal a community matter.

Narratively, the letter is a masterpiece of persuasion. Paul never directly commands manumission but creates a situation in which continued enslavement would be unthinkable for a Christian master who values his partnership with Paul and his own reputation in the church.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The offense**: Was Onesimus a runaway thief? A slave sent on business who overstayed? The letter implies a "wrong" or "debt" (v. 18) but focuses on reconciliation rather than punishment.
- **Manumission or not?** Paul does not say "free him." Some argue he expects only Christian treatment within slavery; others (McKnight, many moderns) argue the language ("more than a slave, a beloved brother," "receive him as you would receive me," public reading) makes legal freedom the only consistent response. The letter's preservation and use in abolitionist arguments support the latter reading.
- **Providence**: "Perhaps this is why he was parted from you for a while" (v. 15) — Paul sees God's hand even in the flight.
- **Relationship to Colossians**: The link (Tychicus, "one of you") confirms historicity and shows the letter was not purely private.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

- **McKnight (Philemon)**: The letter deserves "emancipation" from its common pairing with Colossians because it has a distinctive message for racism and slaveries of all sorts. Onesimus is the test case: the gospel creates brothers where society sees only property. Modern application is direct and urgent.
- **Broader**: Used by 19th-century abolitionists (e.g., against the claim that the Bible supports slavery). In contemporary scholarship, a key text for "gospel and social ethics," "reconciliation," and "empire-critical" readings. Challenges both conservative readings that downplay social implications and radical readings that see Paul as insufficiently revolutionary.
- **Pastoral**: Model of intercession, persuasive (not coercive) authority, and the cost of reconciliation (Paul offers to pay the debt).
