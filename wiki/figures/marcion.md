# Marcion of Sinope

## Biographical Overview

Marcion was born in Sinope on the Black Sea coast (modern Sinop, Turkey), the son of a bishop according to some ancient sources. He became wealthy — probably as a ship-owner — and arrived in Rome c.140-144 CE. He made a large monetary donation to the Roman church (reportedly 200,000 sesterces), was welcomed, and then presented his theological system to the leadership. When it was rejected, he was excommunicated, his donation returned, and he founded his own church community.

The Marcionite church spread rapidly throughout the Roman world in the second century — by Tertullian's time (late 2nd c.) it was present from the Euphrates to Britain, and Tertullian names it the most widespread heterodox movement. [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]] reportedly called him "the firstborn of Satan" on meeting him. [[irenaeus|Irenaeus]], [[tertullian|Tertullian]], [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr]], Hippolytus, and Origen all wrote against him.

Marcion's own writings do not survive except in quotation by his opponents. His primary work, the *Antitheses* (*Antitheseis*), contrasted the God of the OT with the God revealed by Jesus — it is known only from Tertullian's extensive refutation in *Against Marcion*.

## Primary Works and Canon

### *Antitheses*

Marcion's theological treatise arguing that the God of the OT and the Father of Jesus Christ are two different gods:
- The OT God is the Demiurge (creator and judge) — just, harsh, changeable, violent, the god of the Jews
- The Father revealed by Jesus is a previously unknown, purely loving, merciful God with no connection to the material world
- These two gods are in fundamental opposition, hence *antitheses*

Tertullian preserves numerous examples of the antitheses: OT commands "eye for eye," Jesus commands love of enemies; OT God commands destruction of enemies, Jesus says love your enemies; OT God is jealous, Jesus' God has no wrath.

### Marcion's Canon (*Evangelikon* + *Apostolikon*)

Marcion produced the first known closed Christian canon:
- **Evangelikon**: A single Gospel — an edited version of what scholars generally identify as the Gospel of Luke, without the birth narrative (which Marcion rejected as proving Jesus had a material birth), without some OT citations, and with other modifications that removed connections to the OT God
- **Apostolikon**: A collection of 10 Pauline letters (excluding the Pastoral Epistles — 1-2 Timothy, Titus — and Hebrews), edited to remove what Marcion saw as pro-OT interpolations; Galatians placed first (because of its anti-Judaizing content)

The question of whether Marcion's Gospel is a reduction of canonical Luke or whether both derive from a common source (the "Proto-Luke" hypothesis) is debated; most scholars accept that Marcion's Gospel is a reduction of canonical Luke rather than an independent older source.

### Theological Content

Marcion was not a gnostic in the technical sense — he did not posit elaborate mythological cosmologies or Valentinian-style pleroma speculations. His theology was simpler:
1. Two gods: the just OT Demiurge and the loving NT Father
2. Docetic Christology: Christ did not have a real material body; his appearance on earth was a phantasm (*phantasia*) not a real birth
3. Rejection of the OT: it reliably describes the Demiurge, who is not the Father of Jesus; therefore it has no place in Christian teaching
4. Rejection of creation: the material world is the Demiurge's domain; the Father has no interest in it
5. Asceticism: rejection of marriage and procreation (why populate the Demiurge's world?); strict dietary rules

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Proto-Orthodox / Catholic / Protestant

Marcion is the canonical example of heresy for reasons beyond his explicit conclusions. What made him maximally threatening:
1. He was using Paul — specifically Galatians and the anti-law passages — as his primary text; his reading was sophisticated
2. He produced a *written canon* — forcing the question of which texts are authoritative
3. He attracted a large following — Tertullian says his churches "filled the world"

The orthodox response (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Justin, Origen) shaped Christian theology in lasting ways: the insistence on the unity of the two Testaments, the bodily reality of the incarnation, and the canon of both Gospel plurality and a Pauline letter collection were all sharpened in direct response to Marcion.

### Modern Scholarship

Adolf von Harnack (1921) produced the definitive modern study of Marcion, arguing that Marcion was a "consistent Paulinist" who took Paul's anti-law polemic to its logical conclusion. Harnack was sympathetic: he thought Marcion identified something genuinely Pauline that the church had domesticated. Many later scholars (Knox, Blackman) followed Harnack's reading.

The revisionist response (Räisänen, Wilson, Kruger) argues that Marcion *misread* Paul: Paul rejected *works of the law* as a basis for justification, not the OT God, not creation, and not the material world. Paul's God is unambiguously the OT God (*Rom 4*; *11:28-29*; *15:7-13*). Marcion's "Paulinism" is a selective distortion.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The Canon Question

Marcion's importance for [[nt-canon-formation|NT canon formation]] is complex:
1. **Traditional view** (Harnack): Marcion's canon forced orthodox Christians to assemble their own canon in response — the "Marcionite catalyst" hypothesis
2. **Kruger's counter**: Marcion *reduced* a pre-existing larger corpus; the orthodox reaction was not "we need a canon" but "you've mutilated our scriptures." Tertullian and Irenaeus both write as if Marcion corrupted an existing deposit. The Apostolic Fathers (Polycarp, Ignatius, 1 Clement) already treat NT texts as Scripture before Marcion arrived in Rome.

On the current wiki's reading (following Kruger): Marcion did not create the canon question, but his explicit, written reduction clarified and accelerated the process of formal canonical lists. He is a *terminus post quem* for the pre-existence of a recognized corpus of Christian texts.

### The Paul Question

Marcion's selective use of Paul is itself revealing: he found in Paul's letters a resource for an anti-OT theology and took it to extremes Paul himself would have rejected. The need to distinguish "Paulinism" from "Marcionism" — i.e., to read Paul *within* the context of his Jewish heritage and OT theology — was a lasting contribution of the anti-Marcionite controversy to canonical hermeneutics.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Marcion's Gospel**: The precise textual relationship between Marcion's Gospel and canonical Luke is debated. The dominant view (Harnack, Fitzmyer, Evans) is that Marcion reduced canonical Luke. BeDuhn and Klinghardt argue Marcion may preserve an earlier form; most scholars find the cumulative weight of evidence favors Lucan priority.

**Marcion and gnosticism**: Ancient sources (Irenaeus, Hippolytus) sometimes grouped Marcion with gnostics. Modern scholars (Blackman, May) distinguish him sharply: he lacked the mythological cosmologies, the gnosis-salvation model, and the hierarchical pleroma. He is better classified as a *radical dualist* than a *gnostic*.

## Influence on Commentary Tradition

The Marcionite controversy permanently shaped:
- The insistence on the *unity* of OT and NT in Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant theology
- The anti-Marcionite prologues to the Pauline letters (still in some manuscripts)
- The development of explicit canonical lists — the Muratorian Fragment's explicit "in/out" language
- The hermeneutical principle that individual NT texts (especially Paul's anti-law polemic) must be read within the canon's broader context
