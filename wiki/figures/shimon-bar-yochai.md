# Shim'on bar Yoḥai (Rashbi)

A historical 2nd-century CE Tanna — one of the most important sages of the Mishnaic period and a leading disciple of Rabbi Akiva — who in later [[kabbalah|Kabbalistic]] tradition became the central protagonist and attributed author of the [[zohar|Zohar]]. There is thus a sharp gap between the **historical Rashbi** of rabbinic literature and the **legendary Rashbi** of the Zohar: the former a rigorous halakhist and exegete, the latter a transcendent mystic and revealer of cosmic secrets. The historicity and dating of his life are reasonably secure; his connection to the Zohar is the subject of a major controversy (see [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]]).

---

## Biographical Overview

**Historical figure (rabbinic sources)**: Shim'on bar Yoḥai was a fourth/fifth-generation Tanna active in the Galilee in the mid-2nd century CE, after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE) and the Hadrianic persecutions. He was among the foremost students of Rabbi Akiva and is one of the most frequently cited authorities in the Mishnah and related tannaitic literature. He founded an academy at Tekoa in the Galilee. His halakhic method was known for seeking the reason (*ta'am*) behind the commandments and applying it.

**The cave legend** (*Shabbat* 33b–34a): The most famous narrative about Rashbi — and the seed from which his mystical reputation grew. Having spoken critically of Roman rule, he was sentenced to death and fled with his son Eleazar to a cave, where they hid for **twelve years** (some versions thirteen), sustained by a carob tree and a spring, studying Torah buried in sand. When they emerged, Rashbi's gaze was so charged with holy intensity that it incinerated whatever he looked upon; a heavenly voice sent them back for another twelve months to temper him. This legend established Rashbi as a figure of extraordinary spiritual power and divine intimacy — the natural candidate, centuries later, to be cast as the supreme mystic.

**Death and Lag ba-Omer**: Tradition places his death on Lag ba-Omer (the 33rd day of the Omer), commemorated by mass pilgrimage to his reputed tomb at **Meron** in the Galilee — one of the largest annual gatherings in Jewish life. The Zohar's account of his death — the *Idra Zuta* (“Small Assembly”), in main-body **Ha’azinu** (Pritzker Vol. IX, Matt; ~3:287b–296b) — portrays it as the supreme moment of revelation. In this intimate gathering of the Holy Lamp (*Botsina Qaddisha*) with his closest Companions, the most recondite secrets of the Godhead are disclosed; Rashbi’s soul departs in the midst of the discourse. The *Idra Zuta* is at once literary masterpiece, theosophic summit, and theurgic event: the mystical circle itself enacts and unveils the inner divine processes as the master’s soul ascends. **[CORRECTION 2026-07-18]** Earlier wiki notes wrongly placed *Idra Zuta* in Hecker Vol. XI; that volume’s Rashbi appearance is primarily the **Zohar on Song of Songs** (Rashbi–Elijah dialogue), not the deathbed *Idra*.

### *Idra Rabba* (Great Assembly) — Pritzker Vol. VIII standards reingest 2026-07-18

*Source: [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol8|Matt Pritzker Vol VIII]]. Body ~3:127b–145a (within Naso). Complements *Idra Zuta* (Vol. IX Ha’azinu deathbed) without equating the two assemblies.*

In the literary Zohar, Rashbi convenes ten Companions at the **threshing chamber** — “wearing coats of mail and lances” — to array enhancements of the divine face and beard so that flow from **Arikh Anpin** (Elongated / Compassionate Countenance) may soothe aroused **Ze’eir Anpin** (Small / Short-Tempered Countenance) and save the world. Pillars of the assembly: Shim’on, El’azar, Abba. Thirteen beard enhancements of AA (gates of Compassion; Micah 7:18–20) and full ZA arrayal (skull, brains, eyes, nose, nine beard enhancements, Female form-within-form) are spoken into existence by the Companions’ mouths.

**Three Companions die** before leaving the chamber — Rabbi Yose son of Ya’akov, Rabbi Ḥizkiyah, and Rabbi Yeisa: souls **depart by a kiss** while sweetened by the revealed words; angels carry them in the canopy; 250 worlds trembled. **Ten entered and seven emerged.** A heavenly frame presents the deaths as perfection of desire (they had previously “entered and not emerged”), not punishment. Elijah is absent (rescuing Rav Hamnuna Sava). Rashbi is happy; Abba is sad until eased by balsam vision. Matt preface frames the Idra as reaction against Maimonidean incorporeality while indebted to it (e.g. eyes as providence) — apparatus framing.

See [[zohar|The Zohar]] Structure of Volume VIII; [[sefirot|Ten Sefirot]]; [[daniel-matt|Daniel C. Matt]].

### *Idra Zuta* (Small Assembly) — Pritzker Vol. IX standards reingest 2026-07-18

*Source: [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol9|Matt Pritzker Vol IX]]. Body ~3:287b–296b zone within Ha’azinu. (Not a Vol. XI composition — Hecker Vol. XI inventory correction 2026-07-18.)*

In the house of Rabbi Shim’on, with El’azar, Abba, and the Companions, the Holy Lamp **arranges his affairs to depart from the world**. He reveals secrets not disclosed before, “in the presence of Shekhinah,” building on *Idra Rabba* and adding Divine Masculine–Feminine arrayal (Holy Ancient One; Ze’eir; half-body; Yesod union — graphic body theurgy recorded in digest J).

**Death as wedding (*hillula*)**: As he departs he assumes the role of Divine Male uniting with Shekhinah; a celestial voice announces the wedding celebration; the corpse smiles; he is gathered (Dan 12:13). Comparison: Shim’on among Companions as Moses among prophets — model for revealing wisdom before death. Complements cave legend and Meron Lag ba-Omer reception without equating historical Tanna and literary protagonist.

## Primary Source Appearances

**In rabbinic literature**: ubiquitous as "Rabbi Shim'on" in halakhic and aggadic contexts across the Mishnah, Tosefta, both Talmuds, and the tannaitic midrashim (the *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai* and *Sifrei* are associated with his school).

**In the Zohar (Volume I and throughout)**: Rashbi is the towering central character — addressed as **Botsina Qaddisha** ("the Holy Lamp") — around whom the Companions (*Ḥavrayya*) gather. The literary frame of the Zohar consists of Rashbi and his circle wandering the Galilee, expounding Torah secrets to one another "on the way," in caves, in fields, and at night. The Companions include his son **Rabbi Eleazar**, **Rabbi Abba** (the scribe of the circle), **Rabbi Yose**, **Rabbi Ḥiyya**, and others. When Rashbi expounds the deepest mysteries, the natural world responds — birds fall silent, the Companions weep, the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] is present. His authority within the Zohar is nearly oracular: "In the days of Rabbi Shim'on, a person would say to his fellow, 'Open your mouth and let your words illumine.'"

## Zoharic portrait (Pritzker Volume I; reingest 2026-07-18)

Grounded in Matt's *Zohar* Pritzker Edition, Volume I **Haqdamah**, and Arthur Green’s introduction to the same volume. Literary-protagonist vs historical-sage framing is Green’s (and the critical tradition Matt’s edition assumes); Haqdamah roles are claims of the Zohar’s own narrative voice.

### Holy Lamp (*Botsina Qaddisha*)
In the Haqdamah, Rabbi Ḥiyya acclaims him: “The Holy Lamp who has illumined the world… O Rabbi Shim’on, radiance of the lamp, radiance of the worlds…”; “Rabbi Shim’on has not decayed in you!” Messiah’s vision honors his Torah ascending in hundreds of lights; the Messiah seals academies’ Torah and visits Shim’on’s academy.

### Haqdamah roles
1. **Elijah and the *Mi–elleh* secret.** Shim’on silences El’azar so “the concealed mystery on high… may be revealed,” then the theogony: Concealed of all Concealed → single point (*Mi*, Who) → *elleh* (these) → name *Elohim*. The Golden Calf’s “These (*Elleh*) are your gods” is the same mystery inverted.
2. **Academy / Companions.** Innovated-Torah theurgy: “every word innovated in Torah by one engaged in Torah fashions one heaven”; partnership reading *ammi* → *immi*, “with Me.”
3. **Bridal-night host.** “Rabbi Shim’on was sitting engaged in Torah on the night when the Bride is joined with Her Husband” (~1:8a). Companions adorn Assembly of Israel with Torah–Prophets–Writings and “mysteries of wisdom: these are Her adornments and finery.”
4. **Commandments sequence.** Fourteen *mitsvot* mapped onto the Genesis creation narrative — from awe of YHVH as “first commandment of all” / “gate through which one enters faith” through love, *Shema*, knowing YHVH is *ha-Elohim*, Torah study, procreation, circumcision (Elijah’s chair), convert, poor, tefillin, tithes, first fruits, son-redemption, and Sabbath/havdalah.

### Cave emergence (Haqdamah narrative)
Within the Vol. I introduction the Zohar stages cave emergence with El’azar (moldy sores; “Happy is my share that you see me so”) — literary echo of *Shabbat* 33b–34a redeployed as Zoharic frame.

### Literary protagonist vs historical sage (Green / critical framing)
[[arthur-green|Arthur Green]] frames the tales of “Rabbi Shim’on and his disciples, wandering about the Galilee a thousand years before the Zohar was written” as “**clearly a work of fiction**,” while allowing that “a very real mystical brotherhood underlies the Zohar” in thirteenth-century Castile rather than second-century Galilee. Green cites Scholem’s “mystical novel” suggestion. Master and disciples “represent wandering Israel” and the exile of the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]. These are **Green’s (and the scholarly tradition’s) positions**, not the Zohar’s self-presentation. See [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]].

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Rabbinic tradition**: Rashbi is a great halakhist and aggadist, remembered for the cave legend and for sharp sayings, but not as a systematic mystic. His portrait is human-scaled.

**Kabbalistic tradition**: From the late 13th century, Rashbi is transformed into the supreme mystagogue — the one to whom the secrets of the [[sefirot|sefirot]], the divine union, and the inner life of God were entrusted, and through whom they are revealed. The Zohar makes him a near-messianic figure whose generation will not see another like him. The annual Meron pilgrimage and the bonfires of Lag ba-Omer are expressions of this veneration.

**Critical scholarship**: distinguishes sharply between the historical Tanna and the literary character. The teachings the Zohar places in Rashbi's mouth are, on the critical consensus, the composition of [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] and his circle in 13th-century Castile (see [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]]). The historical Rashbi had no connection to the doctrines of the sefirot, which did not exist in the 2nd century.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within the Zohar's literary-theological world, Rashbi functions as:
- The **axis of revelation** — the one human in his generation through whom the supernal secrets descend; his very existence sustains the world ("a single righteous one is the foundation of the world," tied to Yesod/Tsaddiq)
- The **earthly counterpart of Moses** — as [[moses|Moses]] received the revealed Torah at Sinai, Rashbi receives and transmits the hidden Torah (Sod); the Zohar is implicitly the esoteric complement to the Pentateuch
- A **catalyst of the divine union** — his Torah discourse and that of the Companions enacts theurgy below, promoting the union of Tif'eret and the Shekhinah above

---

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- The historical existence and 2nd-century floruit of Rashbi are not in dispute; he is well attested in datable rabbinic sources.
- What is disputed is his authorship of the Zohar — the central question treated at [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]]. The traditional view affirms it; the critical consensus denies it, assigning the Zohar to Moses de León's circle.
- The cave legend's details (twelve years, the incinerating gaze) are aggadic and not treated by critical historians as biography.

---

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

As the attributed author of the Zohar, Rashbi stands — in the eyes of the tradition — at the headwaters of the entire Kabbalistic commentary tradition: Cordovero's and Luria's Zohar commentaries, the whole Safedian and Ḥasidic enterprise, and the liturgical veneration of Lag ba-Omer all flow from his figure. He is, in effect, the patron saint of Jewish mysticism. The historical reality — that this vast influence rests on a 13th-century pseudepigraphic attribution — is itself one of the most remarkable facts in the history of religious literature.
