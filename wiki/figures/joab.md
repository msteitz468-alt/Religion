# Joab

**Joab son of Zeruiah** (David's sister or half-sister, so David's nephew) is the commander
of [[david|David's]] army through nearly the whole of his reign — his most effective and
most ruthless instrument. Indispensable in war and indispensable in the dirty work of
power, Joab repeatedly serves David's interests by means David cannot publicly own, and is
finally executed, on David's deathbed instructions, at the start of [[solomon|Solomon's]]
reign.

## Biographical Overview and Primary Appearances

- **Commander and avenger** — Joab leads David's forces from the war with the house of Saul
  onward. He murders [[abner|Abner]] at the gate of Hebron in revenge for the death of his
  brother Asahel (and to remove a rival), a killing David loudly disclaims (2 Sam 3).
- **The death of Uriah** — Joab carries out David's order to abandon [[uriah|Uriah]] to
  death at the siege of Rabbah (2 Sam 11), making him David's accomplice in murder.
- **Restoring Absalom** — Joab engineers, through the **wise woman of Tekoa's** parable,
  the recall of the banished [[absalom|Absalom]] (2 Sam 14).
- **Killing Absalom** — During Absalom's revolt, Joab kills the prince caught in the
  terebinth **in direct defiance of David's order** to deal gently with him, and then
  brusquely rebukes the king for indulging his grief at the army's expense (2 Sam 18–19).
- **Killing Amasa** — When David appoints Amasa over the army in Joab's place, Joab murders
  him with a stealthy sword thrust to the belly during a feigned greeting (2 Sam 20).
- **The succession** — Joab backs **Adonijah** rather than Solomon (1 Kings 1); on his
  deathbed David charges Solomon not to let Joab's "gray head go down in peace to Sheol"
  (1 Kings 2). Solomon has him killed as he clings to the horns of the altar.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Joab embodies the **violence on which the throne actually rests** — the gap between David's
public piety and the bloodshed that secures his power. The recurrence of his treacherous
killings (Abner, Amasa) builds the motif of **spilled blood** that the narrative cannot wash
from David's house. He is also the rare figure who speaks blunt political truth to David
(the rebuke after Absalom's death), making him a foil to the king's sentiment.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) makes Joab central to his
account of the David story as the "first full-length portrait of a Machiavellian prince."
The image of David's deathbed words — Joab having "spilled the blood of war in peace" and put
that blood "on his belt... and on his sandals" — recalls the stealthy butchering of Amasa and
invokes blood "as material substance and moral symbol throughout the story." David and Joab,
Alter notes, "go back together half a century"; David is "repeatedly dependent on Joab's
resourcefulness and ruthlessness" yet "feels himself to be terribly wronged by his henchman."
When the dying David urges Solomon to act against Joab "in his wisdom," the wisdom in
question "is not the wisdom of the Torah of Moses but rather the wisdom of a Talleyrand" — and
Joab's elimination is plain political housecleaning, lightly draped (by the
[[deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic]] editor) in moral language.

## Reception

Jewish and Christian tradition alike treat Joab as the type of the **over-mighty subject** —
brilliant, loyal in his fashion, and lethally unscrupulous — whose service a ruler cannot do
without and cannot finally tolerate. His killing of Amasa "in peace" became a stock example
in moral and legal discussion of treacherous bloodshed.
