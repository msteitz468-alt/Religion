# Francesca da Rimini

The adulterous noblewoman of [[inferno|Inferno]] 5, the first damned soul who
speaks to [[dante-alighieri|Dante]] at length and the poem's emblem of the lustful.
Wife of Gianciotto Malatesta, she loved his brother Paolo; the husband murdered
both. They drift together on the wind of the second circle, "so light upon the
wind" (5:74).

## In the Divine Comedy

Francesca tells how love overtook them as they read together of Lancelot and
Guinevere: "Love, who so fast brings flame to generous hearts" (5:100); the book and
its author were "Galehault" — the go-between — "and that day we read no further down
those lines" (5:138). Paolo only weeps; Dante faints "as though I were to die."
Kirkpatrick's central crux: Francesca's rhetoric is **morally evasive**, shifting
responsibility onto "Love" and onto the book; "her sin is rather one of moral
apathy and failed intelligence" than lust as such — and her prayer to the
"Sovereign of the Universe" must fail because the God who condemns her is "not her
friend."

## Significance

Francesca dramatizes Dante's refusal to make the damned contemptible: she is
delicate, sympathetic, and articulate — Kirkpatrick compares her to Flaubert's
Madame Bovary — even as the poem exposes the self-deception beneath her sweetness.
The episode founded a long Romantic reception (and figures repeatedly in later art
and music) that often missed Dante's diagnostic edge.
