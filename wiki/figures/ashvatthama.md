Ashvatthama is the son of [[drona|Droṇācārya]] and the last supreme commander of the Kaurava forces. He is the perpetrator of the night massacre of the Pandava camp — the most morally transgressive act in the closing war narrative — driven by grief at his father's death and his pledge to the dying Duryodhana. His story raises the text's most sustained meditation on the chain of retaliatory evil.

## Biographical Overview

Ashvatthama is the son of Dronacharya and the nephew of Kripacharya (Drona married a sister of Kripa). He is one of the most formidable warriors in the Kaurava forces throughout the battle. Duryodhana, in his moments of exasperation with Drona, asserts that Drona's fondness for Ashvatthama explains his leniency toward the Pandavas: "You are like a son to me — Aswatthama himself is not dearer!" (Drona to Duryodhana, Ch. LXXXIII, line 9135). The Glossary identifies him as "Son of Dronacharya and last supreme commander of the Kaurava forces." (line 11523)

## Primary Source Appearances

### During the Battle (Chs. LXXIX–XCI)

Ashvatthama appears repeatedly in the battle chapters in coordination with other Kaurava commanders. He is among the warriors who combine against Abhimanyu in Ch. LXXX (line 8806: "the six great warriors, Drona, Kripa, Karna, Aswathama, Brihatbala and Kritavarma closed upon Abhimanyu"). In Ch. LXXXIX, he protects Jayadratha against Arjuna's assault and fights "fiercely but were all defeated and could not prevent Arjuna from reaching Jayadratha." (lines 9791–9792)

In Ch. XCI (line 9988), after Duhsasana is killed and Bhima drinks his blood, Ashvatthama appeals to Duryodhana earnestly: "Enough of fighting. Let us terminate this disastrous enmity. Beloved friend, make peace with the Pandavas. Stop the battle." Duryodhana refuses.

### Oath to Avenge Duryodhana (Ch. XCIV, lines 10330–10440)

When Ashvatthama learns that Duryodhana lies mortally wounded, "his righteous anger swelled like the sea. The deception, practised by the Pandavas in order to bring about his father's end, had been rankling in his mind." (lines 10332–10334) He goes to Duryodhana and takes an oath to send the Pandavas to Yama's abode that very night. Duryodhana, "transported with joy," immediately orders Ashvatthama installed as Supreme Commander.

That night, resting under a banyan tree with Kripacharya and Kritavarma, Ashvatthama cannot sleep. He watches a large owl attack a flock of sleeping crows one by one, killing them in the dark. The image gives him the idea of a night attack:

> "These wicked Pandavas and the Panchala that killed my father and all their supporters can easily be killed by us, if we surprise them when they are sleeping in their tents at night even as this owl is attacking these blind crows." (lines 10359–10362)

He rationalizes: since the Pandavas achieved their successes through foul play (his father killed through a lie, Karna killed while raising his wheel, Bhima's illegal blow against Duryodhana), there can be nothing wrong in striking them while they sleep. "I am deeply bounden to this bird of prey from whom I have received the teaching." (lines 10363–10364)

Kripacharya objects strenuously: "This can never be. It is wholly wrong. To attack men who have retired to sleep, has never been done before. It would be an unprecedented crime against the laws of kshatriya conduct." (lines 10374–10376) He urges seeking counsel from Dhritarashtra, Gandhari, and Vidura instead.

Ashvatthama rejects this. He argues that the Pandavas themselves destroyed the "barrage of dharma" by killing his father when he had "thrown away all his weapons and had sat down in prayer," by killing Karna while raising his chariot wheel, and by Bhima's illegal blow. "If by killing the sleeping Panchalas, who butchered my great father, I may be doomed to re-birth in the body of a foul bird or of a wriggling worm, I do not care. I seek such a birth!" (lines 10412–10414)

Kripacharya and Kritavarma, unwilling to desert him, ultimately follow. Rajagopalachari comments: "Thus does evil grow! One transgression begets the next and thus evil grows from evil submerging righteousness. Evil flourishes on retaliation." (lines 10422–10424)

### The Night Massacre (Ch. XCIV, lines 10425–10440)

Ashvatthama enters the sleeping Pandava camp. Dhrishtadyumna (who had killed Drona) is found unarmed and asleep. "Aswatthama leapt on the sleeping warrior and, before he could put himself into a posture of defence, cruelly kicked him to death." (lines 10427–10428) All the Panchalas and all the sons of Draupadi are killed one by one in their sleep. Then they set fire to the camp; fleeing soldiers are slaughtered. "We have done our duty," Ashvatthama declares. (line 10438)

### Report to Duryodhana and Aftermath (Ch. XCV, lines 10441–10484)

The three surviving Kauravas reach the dying Duryodhana and report: "All the Panchalas have been slaughtered. The sons of the Pandavas have also been all done to death... There are only seven survivors now on the Pandava side. On our side, Kripacharya, Kritavarma and I remain." (lines 10443–10447) Duryodhana dies happy.

When the Pandavas discover the destruction, Draupadi demands that Ashvatthama be found and punished. The Pandavas search and find him "on the bank of the Ganga, hiding himself behind Vyasa." (lines 10469–10470) Facing the Pandavas and Krishna, Ashvatthama takes up "a blade of grass and charged it with the mantra of destruction and sent it forward saying: 'May this destroy the race of the Pandavas,'" directing it at the womb of Uttara, who carried Abhimanyu's unborn son. Krishna intervenes and saves the child (Parikshit). (lines 10471–10476)

Ashvatthama surrenders: he "prised out the shining jewel which was part of his head and gave it to Bhima, acknowledging his defeat, and went away to the forest." (lines 10479–10480) Bhima presents the jewel to Draupadi, who gives it to Yudhishthira to wear in his crown.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Rajagopalachari presents Ashvatthama's act without exculpation but with attention to its psychological logic. His grief over his father's death — which he regards as murder through a lie — is genuine and drives what he believes is justified revenge. The structure of retaliatory escalation is explicit in the text: the lie about Ashvatthama's death, told to break Drona, triggers the night massacre that kills Draupadi's sons, which in turn triggers Ashvatthama's final desperate weapon against Uttara's unborn child. Each act of adharma generates the next.

Kripacharya's role is important: he provides the voice of normative kshatriya ethics that Ashvatthama explicitly overrides. The fact that Kripacharya ultimately accompanies Ashvatthama anyway — "The sin you are resolved upon, let us share also" (lines 10419–10420) — is itself taken as an illustration of moral contagion.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Ashvatthama's story in this range is primarily the story of retribution becoming atrocity. His appeal to the owl-and-crows as a "teaching" is both practical and symbolic: it is an inversion of kshatriya dharma legitimized by the breakdown of dharma on the other side. The fact that he saves the unborn Parikshit — involuntarily, through Krishna's intervention — ensures the epic can continue: Parikshit becomes the next king and it is to Parikshit's son Janamejaya that Vaisampayana recites the Mahabharata.

The surrender of the jewel from his head (a natural mark of power) to Bhima as submission is the final image: Ashvatthama is not executed but exiled to the forest, carrying his guilt and the permanent loss of his innate power-mark.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The night massacre of sleeping soldiers is the episode most consistently cited in the Mahabharata as the clearest violation of kshatriya ethics. Its perpetrator frames it as legitimate counter-retaliation for prior violations; the text does not endorse this view but does not silently condemn it either — it gives Ashvatthama's reasoning in full and allows Kripacharya's objections to stand unrefuted.

The ethics of Yudhishthira's lie (saying "Ashvatthama the elephant" has been killed, with the second clause drowned out), which precipitates the whole chain, are treated separately at Drona's death and in Ch. XCV's aftermath, where Rajagopalachari calls it "a great sin." The wheels of Yudhishthira's chariot — which had always moved four inches above the earth — come down and touch the ground when he utters the lie: "Yudhishthira, who till then had stood apart from the world so full of untruth, suddenly became of the earth, earthy." (lines 10910–10913)
