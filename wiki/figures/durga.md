# Durga / Chandika

**Chandika** (later usually called **Durga**) is the paradigmatic independent warrior goddess of
[[hinduism|Hinduism]] — a tooth goddess, not a consort — and the first goddess to receive a full
mythological cycle in Sanskrit literature. Her foundational text is the **"Glorification of the
Goddess"** (*Devīmāhātmya*), a long poem interpolated into the **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** between the
fifth and seventh centuries CE, though the goddess herself is visible on Kushana coins as early as
the second century CE. Doniger calls the *Devīmāhātmya* the goddess's "crossover text, from unknown
rituals and local traditions to a pan-Indian Sanskrit text."

## Primary Source Appearances

**Mahābhārata precursors:** References to the seven or eight "Little Mothers" (*Mātrikās*), the
Great Kali, and the goddess of Death/Night who appears before the massacre (MB 10.8.64) — these
are "tantalizingly brief," not full mythology.

**Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa — "Glorification of the Goddess" (*Devīmāhātmya*):** The foundational text.
The outer frame: a sage narrates the myth to a king who has lost his kingdom and a Vaishya who has
lost his family — each asks the goddess for what he wants (kingdom vs. spiritual knowledge), and
she grants both.

## The Myth of Killing the Buffalo (*Mahiṣa-mardana*)

The gods, defeated by the antigods led by **Mahiṣa** ("Buffalo"), emit their energies (*tejas*) —
which coalesce into the goddess Chandika. The gods also give her their weapons and ornaments. Her
mount is a lion.

Mahiṣa, attacking her, shifts shapes: buffalo → lion → man with sword → elephant → buffalo again.
She drinks a divine drink ("her eyes shone red"), kicks him on the neck, and as the anthropomorphic
antigod comes halfway out of the buffalo's open mouth, she cuts off his head. "The final moment in
this story is a scene particularly beloved of artists" — the lion chomping on the buffalo's head while
the goddess beheads the man emerging from the buffalo's neck.

**Ritual connection:** Convincingly linked to the ritual sacrifice of a buffalo, documented across
India to this day and associated with a sect of [[draupadi|Draupadī]]. The goddess, like Draupadī,
drinks blood at her cult's sacrifice.

## The Killing of Shumbha

In the battle that follows, Chandika is courted by the antigod Shumbha, who falls in love with her.
She refuses marriage ("I will only marry someone who vanquishes me in battle"). In the subsequent
battle, the *shaktis* of all the gods — taking their gods' exact forms, weapons, and ornaments —
come out to aid her. Even Chandika emits her own shakti "howling like a hundred jackals." After
absorbing all the gods' shaktis into her breasts, she kills Shumbha. This passage, says Doniger,
"stands out as a feminist moment framed by earlier and later texts that deny the shakti her
independence."

## Tooth Goddess vs. Breast Goddess

Doniger invokes A. K. Ramanujan's distinction: **"goddesses of the tooth"** (unmarried, fierce,
killing, celibate mothers) vs. **"goddesses of the breast"** (wives, subservient to husbands, fertile
but chaste). Chandika is the paradigmatic tooth goddess. She is:

- **Definitively unmarried** — Shiva is her messenger, not her husband
- **Independent of any male god** — her shakti belongs to no one but herself
- **A killer** — not a mother; her "children" are all creatures

The shakti theology of the *Devīmāhātmya*: Chandika is not the *shakti* of a god — she *is* her
own shakti, and absorbs the shaktis of all gods. This sets her apart from **[[parvati|Parvati]]**
(Shiva's shakti) and the consort goddesses.

## Shakti Theology

**Shakti** (creative power, related to *śak*, "to be able") generally means either:
- The power that a *male god has* (e.g., Shiva's shakti), often personified as his wife; or
- Something that an independent goddess *is*

Chandika is the second type — "a shakti who is her own shakti." The Brahmin filter on the
*Devīmāhātmya* tries to subordinate her: she is said to be "created from the gods' energies"
(not self-generated). But the narrative itself shows her absorbing and transcending all divine
power. Later Tantric texts will identify her more fully with *mahāmāyā* (the Great Illusion).

## Worship and Reception

**Why now (5th–7th c. CE)?** Doniger: "At some moment the critical mass of Devi worship forced the
Brahmin custodians of Sanskrit narratives to acknowledge it." Kushana coins depict her; merchants and
kings worshipped her; the Puranic frame makes this explicit. Her cult was likely spread via royal
bhakti patronage, as was Shaiva bhakti.

**Link to folk and tribal religion:** "Some of the stories may have come from villages or tribal
cultures where the goddess had been worshiped for centuries; early in her history she may have been
associated with the periphery of society." The "Brahmin filter" extracted a toll as her stories
crossed into Sanskrit: her power is said to derive from the male gods' *tejas*.

**"Goddess feminism" warning:** Doniger: "The pious hope of goddess feminists, and others, that the
worship of goddesses is Good for Women is dashed by observations of India." The power recognized in
Chandika does not translate into social power for real women — often the reverse (the more powerful
the goddess, the more threatening women seem, and therefore the more controlled). The exception:
women *possessed by* fierce goddesses, thereby becoming shaktis themselves.

## Relationship to Parvati and Kali

Chandika is formally independent, but later mythology begins to link her to Parvati's cluster. The
splitting of **[[parvati|Parvati]]** into the golden Gauri and the dark Kali (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa,
probably later Gupta period) shows the consolidation of the many tooth-goddess figures (Chandika, Kali,
Vindhyavāsinī, Durgā) under a single mythological umbrella. Over centuries, "Durgā" became the
dominant name for all tooth-goddess manifestations.

**Draupadī connection:** The buffalo-sacrifice ritual is specifically linked to a sect of
[[draupadi|Draupadī]] (see that page). Draupadī, who is also born from fire (*ayonijā*), was "a
goddess from the start" per the Puranas.

## Key Controversies

- **Independence vs. subordination**: Is Chandika genuinely independent (absorbing all divine power)
  or created from the gods (the Brahmin frame)? The *Devīmāhātmya* encodes both simultaneously.
- **Tooth goddess vs. consort**: Later mythological development collapsed many independent goddesses
  into the "wives of Shiva" system — tracing a historical loss of women's religious autonomy.
- **Goddess worship and women's status**: The inverse correlation between the power of the Goddess in
  texts and the actual political-economic power of women. See [[shakti|Shakti]] concept page.
