# Papias of Hierapolis

## Biographical Overview

**Papias of Hierapolis** was bishop of Hierapolis in the Lycus Valley in Asia Minor (modern Pamukkale, Denizli province, Turkey) in the early 2nd century CE. He is one of the most important figures in the history of early Christianity not because of his surviving writings (only fragments remain) but because of the traditions he preserved — traditions about the authorship of the Gospels of Mark and Matthew that remain central to NT scholarship nearly two millennia after he wrote them down.

**Dating**: Eusebius places Papias' floruit in the reign of Trajan (98–117 CE). He was a companion of [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]], and both are described by Eusebius as "the most ancient" witnesses of the Asian church. Irenaeus, who knew Polycarp personally, says Papias was "a hearer of John and companion of Polycarp, a man of the old time" (*Against Heresies* 5.33.4). This places him in a direct chain: (John →) Papias ← → Polycarp → Irenaeus.

**John the Elder**: The most debated biographical question. Was Papias a "hearer" of John the Apostle (the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve) or of "John the Elder" (a different disciple of Jesus who lived in Ephesus)? Papias himself distinguishes the two in the preface to his *Expositions* (see [[fragments-of-papias|Fragments of Papias]], Fragment 3.3–4) — listing both "John" among the past-tense apostles and "the elder John" among those still speaking in the present tense. Eusebius uses this to argue that Papias heard only the Elder, not the Apostle. See [[revelation-date-authorship|Revelation: Date and Authorship]] for the implications.

**Character**: Eusebius famously described Papias as "a man of very little intelligence (*sphodra smikros tōn noun*)" — a polemical dismissal motivated by Papias' literal millennialism, which Eusebius found both theologically embarrassing and dangerous (it had misled "even Irenaeus"). Modern scholars recognize this for what it is: Eusebius discrediting a source whose traditionalist, literal approach to apocalyptic texts ran counter to the allegorizing interpretation that the Alexandrian and later Eusebian tradition preferred. Papias was not unintelligent — he was a careful collector of elder-traditions who committed to a literal reading that later theological tastes repudiated.

**Philip in Hierapolis**: Papias reports that Philip the apostle (probably Philip the Evangelist of Acts 21:8–9 rather than the apostle) lived with his daughters in Hierapolis. This is significant for the history of Hierapolis as an early Christian center and for the Philip-daughters tradition.

## The *Expositions of the Sayings of the Lord*

Papias' major work was a five-volume treatise titled *Kyriakōn logiōn exēgēsis* — "Exposition of the Sayings/Oracles of the Lord." The work is completely lost except for the fragments preserved in Eusebius and others. From the fragments we can reconstruct:

- A preface explaining his method: preferring "the living voice" (oral tradition from those who had known the elders) to "books" (Fragment 3.3–4)
- Traditions about Mark as Peter's interpreter (Fragment 3.15)
- Traditions about Matthew's "Hebrew logia" (Fragment 3.16)
- Millennial traditions about the abundance of the kingdom (Fragment 3.11–12)
- The Barsabbas/poison miracle (Fragment 6)
- The woman accused before the Lord (Fragment 23, related to the *pericope adulterae*)
- Various other elder-traditions about the apostles, including Philip's daughters' accounts

**"The living voice"**: Papias' methodological preference for oral tradition over written texts is one of the most important statements about the early 2nd-century understanding of textual authority. He valued direct contact with those who had known the apostles above any written Gospel, however authoritative. This does not mean he rejected the Gospels — he clearly knew them — but that the oral chain from eyewitnesses to elders to informants to himself held higher epistemological weight.

## The Gospel Traditions (Fragment 3.15–16)

The two most discussed passages in the entire corpus of the Apostolic Fathers are Papias' traditions about Mark and Matthew.

### Mark as Peter's Interpreter

"Mark, having become Peter's interpreter (*hermēneutēs*), wrote down accurately (*akribōs*) everything he remembered, though not in order (*ou mentoi taxei*)..." — Fragment 3.15

This tradition, which Papias attributes to an unnamed "elder," does three things:
1. Establishes the apostolic authority of the Gospel of Mark through its connection to Peter
2. Explains Mark's lack of systematic order (its "non-sequential" character compared to Matthew/Luke) as a feature, not a flaw
3. Locates Mark in a position of secondary eyewitness — not a direct follower of Jesus, but an accurate transmitter of Peter's firsthand testimony

Whether this tradition is historically reliable, and what exactly "not in order" (*ou mentoi taxei*) means — whether chronological, topical, or rhetorical — has generated enormous scholarly literature. It remains the primary patristic evidence for Mark's Petrine connections.

### Matthew's Hebrew Logia

"Matthew composed the oracles (*logia*) in the Hebrew language (*Hebraïdi dialektōi*), and each person interpreted (*hermēneusen*) them as best he could." — Fragment 3.16

The *logia* ("oracles") is the crux: does Papias mean the canonical Gospel of Matthew, an earlier sayings-source, or OT testimonies? Given that the canonical Gospel of Matthew shows no clear signs of being translated from a Semitic original, most scholars conclude Papias refers to a different document — possibly a saying-source that preceded or was later incorporated into canonical Matthew, or a source that Papias knew but that is no longer extant.

## Hermeneutical Method

Papias is best described as a **tradent** — a collector and transmitter of oral tradition — rather than as a commentator or theologian in the systematic sense. His hermeneutical commitment is to the oral chain of custody: the "living voice" of those who had known the apostles is the authoritative source of interpretation.

His literal millennialism is the most visible expression of his hermeneutical approach: where later interpreters (Origen, Eusebius) read the messianic abundance passages as spiritual allegory, Papias read them as literal predictions of material kingdom conditions. Irenaeus adopted this literalism from Papias, and it became one strand of the Asian apocalyptic tradition.

## Controversies

**The Two Johns**: Did Papias know and transmit from John the Apostle or John the Elder? The answer shapes how one evaluates Papias as a witness to the apostolic age — if he knew the Elder (not the Apostle), his claim to apostolic proximity is one step more remote. See [[revelation-date-authorship|Revelation: Date and Authorship]].

**John and James martyred by Jews?**: Fragment 5 (from Philip of Side) attributes to Papias the statement that "John the Theologian and James his brother were killed by Jews." If authentic, this would contradict the dominant tradition that John the Apostle died peacefully in Ephesus. The source (Philip of Side) is unreliable, and the fragment may misrepresent Papias. But if authentic, it would have dramatic implications for the authorship of the Gospel of John and the Letters of John, and would give historical support to Mark 10:38–39.

**Millennialism and Irenaeus**: Papias' literal millennialism is the probable source of Irenaeus' similar position. Eusebius blames Papias for this theological error and uses the millennialism argument to support his claim of Papias' intellectual limitations. The historical reality is that millennialism was widespread in 2nd-century Asia Minor — Papias was not an eccentric but representative of a dominant local tradition.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Papias' direct influence on later interpretation comes through:
1. **The Markan/Petrine tradition**: Every patristic discussion of Mark's authorship and its relationship to Peter flows from or engages with Papias' Fragment 3.15
2. **The Hebrew Matthew tradition**: Every patristic claim about a Hebrew Matthew (Jerome, Origen, Eusebius) traces back to or cites Papias
3. **Irenaeus' millennialism**: Directly transmitted from Papias, by Irenaeus' own acknowledgment
4. **The Two Johns controversy**: Eusebius uses Papias as the launching point for his argument about Revelation's authorship
