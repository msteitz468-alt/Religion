# Ganesha

**Ganesha** (the elephant-headed deity, son of [[parvati|Parvati]]) is among the most widely
recognized figures of [[hinduism|Hinduism]] — the "Lord of Obstacles," invoked at the beginning
of all undertakings, patron of scribes and commerce, and guardian of thresholds. His mythological
origin, as narrated in the Puranas, is entirely unusual: he is created by Parvati *alone*, without
Shiva, and only acquires his elephant head through Shiva's later intervention.

## Origin Myth: Parvati's Parthenogenic Son

Parvati, wanting a faithful doorkeeper who would not admit anyone while she bathed (Shiva's ganas
— his retinue — were undependable), scrapes the **dirt and oil** from her own body, shapes it into
a boy, breathes life into him, and sets him as guardian of her door.

**The beheading.** When [[shiva|Shiva]] returns and tries to enter, Ganesha — loyal to his mother's
command — refuses him entry. Unaware this child is in any sense his, Shiva's ganas fight the boy; he
defeats them all. Shiva himself beheads Ganesha.

**The restoration.** Parvati's grief and rage are cosmic: she threatens to dissolve the universe. The
gods capitulate; Shiva sends his ganas to find the head of *the first creature they encounter that faces
north* (or in some versions: south). They find an elephant. The elephant's head is attached to Ganesha;
Shiva breathes life into him and acknowledges him as a son.

**The "first son" controversy:** In Shaiva Puranas, Skanda (the war-god) is generally the elder son
and Ganesha the younger. In some regional traditions, the order reverses. The elephant head itself is
explained several ways in different Puranas (only one consistent point: the original head had to be
replaced after decapitation).

## Vehicle: The Bandicoot (*Mūṣika*)

Ganesha's *vāhana* (vehicle/mount) is the **bandicoot rat** — an incongruous mount for the pot-bellied
deity. Doniger notes that each god's vehicle "is the thing that embodies the dangerous, transgressive
aspect of the deity's own power" — the rat being a creature of darkness, granaries, and destruction
that the god paradoxically masters and rides. The rat/mouse also encodes Ganesha's role as remover of
obstacles (rats gnaw through obstacles) and as patron of wealth (they infiltrate storehouses).

## Theological Significance

**Lord of Obstacles (both creator and remover).** Ganesha places obstacles in the path of those who
do not honor him and removes them for those who do. This double power — obstacle-placer and
obstacle-remover — makes propitiation of Ganesha the logical first act before any undertaking.

**Patron of scribes and texts.** In one Puranic tradition, Ganesha serves as the scribe who writes
down the Mahābhārata as [[vyasa|Vyasa]] dictates. His broken tusk, in this account, serves as the
writing instrument when the pen breaks. This makes him the god of writing itself.

**Guardian of thresholds.** His origin as a doorkeeper is his mytho-structural role: he governs
entrances, transitions, and beginnings. Images of Ganesha are placed at doorways, in shops, at the
start of manuscripts, and at the head of religious ceremonies.

## Single-Parent Creation and the Shiva–Parvati Dynamic

Doniger emphasizes the symmetry:

- **Parvati creates Ganesha without Shiva** — from her own body, as a replacement for intimacy or
  protection Shiva cannot provide
- **Shiva creates [[skanda|Skanda]] without Parvati** — his seed falls into the Ganges; Skanda is
  born of fire and water, nursed by the Pleiades (*Kṛttikā*)

"Two children, two single parents: the permanently unresolved erotic/ascetic tension between Shiva
and Parvati is encoded in the origin of their children." Neither child was conceived by their union.

## Animal Vehicle and Folk Origins

Doniger suggests Ganesha (like many Puranic deities) may have "come from animals and plants that had
their own cults, outside the pale of the four classes, which were then integrated into the Brahmin
pantheon." Folk elephant-worship at dangerous crossroads or thresholds — places where elephant-gods
already stood as guardians — may predate the elaborate Puranic mythology and account for his enormous
pan-Indian popularity.
