# Silas

## Biographical Overview
A leading member of the Jerusalem church and a prophet. He was selected to accompany Paul and Barnabas to Antioch to deliver the decree of the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). He subsequently joined Paul on his second missionary journey, replacing Barnabas. He was imprisoned with Paul in Philippi and helped found the churches in Thessalonica, Berea, and Corinth. From Bock (Acts), Silas is a trusted Jerusalem representative who becomes a key co-worker in the Gentile mission; the "we passages" include him.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Acts**: The primary narrative of his missionary activities with Paul (referred to as Silas).
- **Pauline Epistles**: Listed as a co-sender (under the Roman name Silvanus) in 1-2 Thessalonians. Mentioned in 2 Cor 1:19 as a fellow preacher in Corinth.
- **1 Peter**: Mentioned as the amanuensis or courier ("by Silvanus, a faithful brother", 1 Pet 5:12).

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Silas/Silvanus serves as a vital link between the Jerusalem church and the Pauline Gentile mission. In the context of the Thessalonian correspondence, commentators like Jeffrey Weima argue that Paul's use of the plural "apostles" in 1 Thess 2:7 likely includes Silvanus (and Timothy), granting them authoritative status. Silvanus's legal entanglement in Thessalonica (along with Paul) prevented his return to the city, leading to Timothy being sent instead. In Acts (Bock), he is part of the team through whom God directs the mission westward.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
Scholars generally accept the identity of Silas in Acts with Silvanus in the Epistles. A minor controversy surrounds his exact role in the composition of 1 Peter (whether he was merely a courier, an amanuensis, or a co-author), and whether he held formal "apostolic" status alongside Paul, as implied in 1 Thess 2:7. Bock supports the historical reliability of his partnership with Paul.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Commentators often highlight Silas as a representative of the harmony between the Jerusalem apostles and Paul's mission, countering the hypothesis of absolute division between Petrine/Jewish and Pauline/Gentile Christianity. From Bock (this batch): His role illustrates the collaborative, multi-ethnic team (Jerusalem prophet + apostle to Gentiles) that God uses to plant churches.
