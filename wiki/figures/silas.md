# Silas

## Biographical Overview
A leading member of the Jerusalem church and a prophet. He was selected to accompany Paul and Barnabas to Antioch to deliver the decree of the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). He subsequently joined Paul on his second missionary journey, replacing Barnabas. He was imprisoned with Paul in Philippi and helped found the churches in Thessalonica, Berea, and Corinth. From Bock (Acts), Silas is a trusted Jerusalem representative who becomes a key co-worker in the Gentile mission; the "we passages" include him.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Acts**: The primary narrative of his missionary activities with Paul (referred to as Silas).
- **Pauline Epistles**: Listed as a co-sender (under the Roman name Silvanus) in 1-2 Thessalonians. Mentioned in 2 Cor 1:19 as a fellow preacher in Corinth.
- **1 Peter**: Named at 5:12 (“Through Silvanus, whom I consider to be your faithful brother, I have
  written…”) — traditional amanuensis or courier (or both); see Jobes below.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Silas/Silvanus serves as a vital link between the Jerusalem church and the Pauline Gentile mission. In the context of the Thessalonian correspondence, [[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]] (*[[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|1–2 Thessalonians, BECNT]]*) treats Silvanus as **cosender** of both letters (1 Thess 1:1; 2 Thess 1:1) with [[paul-apostle|Paul]] and [[timothy|Timothy]], and argues that Paul's plural "apostles" in 1 Thess 2:7 likely includes Silvanus (and Timothy), granting them authoritative status. Weima also coordinates letters and Acts: after the Thessalonica riot, security taken from Jason likely guaranteed that Paul and Silas would leave and not return—so Silvanus's legal entanglement, with Paul's, blocked their return and led to Timothy being sent instead (1 Thess 3:1–5); Weima further allows that, after an Athens reunion, Silas may have been sent toward Macedonia/Philippi (for gifts) while Timothy went back to Thessalonica. In Acts (Bock), he is part of the team through whom God directs the mission westward.

## In 1 Peter, on Jobes’s reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|Jobes, *1 Peter* (BECNT)]]. Claims are Jobes’s.*

[[karen-jobes|Jobes]] identifies Silvanus with the Latinized form of Silas (Luke’s companion name in
Acts 16–18): trusted Jerusalem leader (Acts 15:22), Roman citizen (16:37), coauthor of 1–2
Thessalonians with Paul and Timothy, and “among the well-known core leadership of the first-century
church… later with Peter and Mark in Rome.” On **5:12**, she (with Achtemeier, Clowney, Davids 2002,
Elliott, Grudem, Michaels, et al.) judges *dia Silouanou … egrapsa* “more likely describes the role
of a **courier** than of an amanuensis”: the idiom *graphein dia tinos* is a standard way of naming
a letter-bearer (Acts 15:23; first-/second-century papyri; Ignatius), whereas Rom 16:22 uses
different language for a clear scribe. Even as courier, Silvanus (or Mark) *could* still have served
as amanuensis if needed — that question “must be decided on other grounds.” Traditional
Petrine-lifetime interpreters who assumed Greek “too good for Peter” often defaulted to Silvanus as
amanuensis because he was a Roman citizen; Jobes’s syntax excursus reopens that assumption. Against
pseudonymous-fiction readings of Silvanus (e.g. Boring): a fictional bearer would spoil actual
delivery; choosing Silvanus rather than a better-known Petrine associate would be odd. Delivery
objection (Goppelt: one man cannot cover all 1:1 regions) is answered via Michaels/Cranfield: perhaps
Pontus as Black Sea port of entry, then distribution. Named without further introduction → readers
knew him; “faithful brother” may hint reputation more than intimate personal acquaintance if he
carries the letter. Lifetime co-presence of Peter, Mark, and Silvanus: late 40s/early 50s
(Jerusalem/Antioch after the council) and early–mid 60s (Rome).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
Scholars generally accept the identity of Silas in Acts with Silvanus in the Epistles. A minor controversy surrounds his exact role in the composition of 1 Peter (whether he was merely a courier, an amanuensis, or a co-author), and whether he held formal "apostolic" status alongside Paul, as implied in 1 Thess 2:7. Bock supports the historical reliability of his partnership with Paul. Jobes tilts 5:12 toward **courier** on idiomatic grounds without ruling out amanuensis on other grounds, and uses Silvanus’s known identity as a problem for pure pseudonymy scenarios.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Commentators often highlight Silas as a representative of the harmony between the Jerusalem apostles and Paul's mission, countering the hypothesis of absolute division between Petrine/Jewish and Pauline/Gentile Christianity. From Bock (this batch): His role illustrates the collaborative, multi-ethnic team (Jerusalem prophet + apostle to Gentiles) that God uses to plant churches.

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names.*

Bock introduces Silas as one of the delegates the Jerusalem church chooses to carry the apostolic
letter of the Council to Antioch (15:22), alongside Judas Barsabbas; both are called **prophets**
(15:32), and Bock identifies Silas with the **Silvanus** of Paul's letters (2 Cor. 1:19; 1 Thess.
1:1; 1 Pet. 5:12). When Paul and Barnabas split, Paul chooses Silas as his companion for the
**second missionary journey** (15:40). Bock notes that Silas is, like Paul, "a Roman citizen"
(inferred from 16:37–38): at **Philippi** the two are beaten and jailed, sing hymns at midnight, are
freed by an earthquake that occasions the jailer's conversion, and then invoke their citizenship to
require a public release. Silas continues as Paul's coworker through **Thessalonica** and **Beroea**,
remaining behind in Beroea when Paul is sent ahead to Athens (17:14), and rejoining him for the
**Corinth** ministry (18:5). Bock reads Silas as a trusted Jerusalem representative whose partnership
with Paul embodies the harmony between the mother church and the Gentile mission — telling against
any absolute Jewish/Gentile-Christian divide.
