# Aśoka

**Aśoka** (also spelled Ashoka) was the third emperor of the **Mauryan dynasty** — the greatest empire
the Indian subcontinent had seen to that point — and the first major political figure to leave extensive
**epigraphic records** of his own moral self-examination. He is historically important for transforming
Buddhism from a small sect into a world religion and for providing the earliest evidence of a political
ethic of religious pluralism.

## Historical Overview

Aśoka came to the throne c. 268 BCE and ruled most of the Indian subcontinent. In the **eighth year of
his reign** he conquered **Kalinga** (present Orissa) in a campaign so brutal — 150,000 deported,
100,000 killed, "many times that number" perished — that he afterward claimed revulsion and remorse.
The Kalinga edict is "a most remarkable document, allowing us a glimpse into the mind… of a ruler who
regrets what he regards as a major crime committed in the line of duty."

Doniger qualifies the repentance: "That Ashoka renounced war at this point is perhaps less impressive
than it might seem, given that he now already had most of India under his control." He still warned
"forest tribes" who had not submitted that "he has power even in his remorse, and he asks them to
repent, lest they be killed."

He never discontinued capital punishment and continued to eat meat while progressively reducing the
animals killed in his palace kitchens (from "hundreds of thousands" daily to two peacocks and a deer,
with intent to reduce further).

## The Dhamma Edicts

Aśoka made his thoughts known by having them **engraved on rocks** and later on **stone pillars**
across his empire. All in all, nineteen rock edicts and nine pillar edicts survive, in more than thirty
locations across India, Nepal, Pakistan, and Afghanistan.

His **dhamma** was neither specifically Buddhist (the Pali *dhamma*) nor Hindu *dharma* nor any
philosophical doctrine — "a broader code of behavior, one size fits all": honesty, truthfulness,
compassion, obedience, mercy, benevolence, "few faults and many good deeds." He founded hospitals
for humans and animals, planted roadside trees and mango groves, dug wells, and built rest houses.

His famous pluralism: "Whoever honors his own sect or disparages that of another man, wholly out of
devotion to his own, with a view to showing it in a favorable light, harms his own sect even more
seriously."

He was simultaneously condescending toward women's folk religion: "Women especially perform a variety
of ceremonies, which are trivial and useless." He approved of public spectacle — "elephants, balls of
fire, and other divine forms" — as a means of attracting audiences to dhamma.

## The Pillars

The sandstone pillars (40–50 feet high, weighing up to 50 tons) were quarried at Chunar near Varanasi
and dragged hundreds of miles. The animal capitals (lions symbolizing the Buddha as emperor) show
**Persian influence** — Iranian journeyman carvers came after the fall of the Achaemenids. But "the
bulls and elephants are treated in an unmistakably Indian way, stunningly similar to some of the animals
on the Indus seals." Aśoka adapted existing phallic pillars that had symbolized Indra. Thus Persian
technique + Indian aesthetic + Indra's pillar = the Aśokan column.

## Aśoka and Buddhism

His services to Buddhism proved more enduring than his dhamma program. He held the **Third Buddhist
Council** at Pāṭaliputra (c. 250 BCE) and sent out many missionaries. His patronage transformed
Buddhism from a regional sect to a religion that spread throughout India and beyond. He also patronized
Jainas and Ājīvikas.

## Aśoka and the Mahābhārata

The figure of **Yudhiṣṭhira** (eldest Pāṇḍava) has been read as a literary response to Aśoka:
- Both are victorious kings devastated by the violence of their victory.
- Both are "torn between violence and nonviolence."
- Both eventually "rest for a moment in the compromise position of avoiding cruelty."
- Yudhiṣṭhira's refusal to rule after the war may reflect Aśoka's Kalinga edict or subsequent legends
  about Aśoka's renunciation.
- Aspects of Arjuna's character may also have been created in response to Aśoka.

Doniger: "Both Ashoka and Pushyamitra left their marks on Yudhishthira. All three kings responded,
in different ways, to the challenge of Buddhists and Jainas."

## Later Mythologization

Post-mortem myths about Aśoka included the fantasy that he had **killed his ninety-nine brothers**
to gain the throne and then "visited hell, where he learned how to construct a hell on earth." His
empire of light and dhamma was retrofitted with fiendish tortures. In 2001, Bollywood (director
Santosh Sivan, starring Shahrukh Khan and Kareena Kapoor) depicted a romantic youth Aśoka who
eventually converts to Buddhism — in the last two minutes of a three-hour film featuring "nonstop
slaughter."

## Theological and Historical Significance

Aśoka is the hinge point between the Vedic religion of sacrifice and the post-Vedic, bhakti-oriented
Hinduism: in place of royal hunts, he inaugurated **royal pilgrimage** to Buddhist shrines, substituting
a Hindu/Buddhist virtue for a Hindu vice (addiction to hunting). His pluralistic dhamma anticipates
Gandhi's Hinduism of "essential inclusivity" — and was specifically invoked by Doniger as the
alternative to the ethno-religious nationalism represented by Golwalkar (RSS, 1939).

"It is one of the great pities of human history that Ashoka's program of dhamma died with him."
