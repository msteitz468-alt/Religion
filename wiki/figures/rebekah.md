# Rebekah

**Rebekah** (Hebrew *Rivka*), wife of [[isaac|Isaac]] and mother of the twins [[jacob|Jacob]] and [[esau|Esau]], is the **second matriarch**. Chosen at the well by Abraham's servant through her conspicuous kindness, she becomes the decisive human agent in securing the covenant blessing for the younger son, Jacob — receiving the divine oracle that "the elder shall serve the younger" (Gen 25:23) and engineering the stratagem by which the blind Isaac blesses Jacob (Gen 27). She is the most **active and initiative-taking** of the matriarchs.

---

## Biographical Overview

- **The betrothal at the well** (Gen 24): Abraham's servant, seeking a wife for Isaac, prays for a sign; Rebekah waters his camels, embodying *ḥesed*; she consents to leave her family ("I will go") and marries Isaac.
- **The oracle of the twins** (Gen 25:21–23): barren, then pregnant with struggling twins; YHWH tells her "two nations are in your womb… the elder shall serve the younger."
- **The wife-sister episode at Gerar** (Gen 26): Isaac presents her as his sister, paralleling Abraham and Sarah.
- **Securing the blessing** (Gen 27): overhearing Isaac's plan to bless Esau, she dresses Jacob in Esau's garments and goatskins; Jacob receives the blessing; she sends him to Laban to escape Esau's wrath.

---

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Genesis 24–28** — the betrothal, the oracle, and the stolen blessing.
- **Romans 9:10–13** — Paul cites Rebekah's twins (chosen before birth, "not by works") as the paradigm of unconditional election.

---

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Jewish**: *Rivka Imenu*; praised for her *ḥesed* at the well and her decisiveness; rabbinic Midrash treats her as a prophet who acts on the divine oracle ("the elder shall serve the younger") in arranging the blessing — her "deception" is the execution of God's revealed will. Like Sarah, the blessings of the tent (cloud, lamp, dough) are said to have returned with her.
- **Christian**: the mother of the elect (Jacob) and reprobate (Esau) in Pauline election theology (Rom 9); a model of faith and providential agency.
- **Kabbalistic (the Zohar)** — see below.

---

## Kabbalistic Reception (the Zohar)

The Zohar reads Rebekah within the **sefirotic logic of the patriarchal triad**, chiefly in Ḥayyei Sarah and Toledot (Pritzker II).

- As the wife of [[isaac|Isaac]] (Gevurah/Din, the left side), Rebekah stands in the orbit of **judgment and discernment**; her decisive, even severe action to redirect the blessing from Esau to Jacob enacts the necessary work of *din* (judgment) discriminating between the holy line ([[jacob|Jacob]] = Tif'eret) and the side of [[esau|Esau]] (the [[sitra-ahra|Other Side]]). Her stratagem is not deceit but the operation of discernment that secures the harmonizing center.
- She is one of the **four matriarchs** whom the Zohar aligns with the divine feminine; together with [[sarah|Sarah]], [[rachel|Rachel]], and [[leah|Leah]] she embodies an aspect of the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]'s descent into the world and the propagation of the covenant line.

### Pritzker II reingest anchors (Ḥayyei Sarah; Toledot)

Material below is grounded in Zohar II (Pritzker Vol. II), Parashat Ḥayyei Sarah and Toledot.

**Mild din / rose among thorns (Toledot, 1:136b–137a).** In the Zohar (Pritzker II, 1:136b), when Isaac takes Rebekah he “encompassed North and South, fire and water… when he took Rebekah, who resembled a rainbow: green, white, red.” Against Isaac’s severe Judgment she is mild: “Isaac, severe Judgment; she, mild, like a rose among thorns. If she had not been mild, the world could not have endured the severe Judgment of Isaac” (1:137a). The Zohar extends the rose-among-thorns image toward [[shekhinah|Assembly of Israel]]; Rebekah emerges from severe-Judgment stock (Bethuel/Laban/Paddan) yet is not of that side.

**Betrothal spring (Ḥayyei Sarah, 1:131b–132a).** In the Zohar (Pritzker II, 1:131b–132a), Rebekah “came out” by the Blessed Holy One from wicked townsfolk; at the spring *ha-aynah* (with *he*), Miriam’s well is said to have manifested and “water rose toward her.” Servant and bride meet toward evening — the hour of afternoon prayer — at “a spring of gardens, a well of living waters” (Song 4:15), which the Zohar treats as “all a mystery.”

**Sarah’s tent, lamp, and Shekhinah return (Ḥayyei Sarah, 1:133a).** In the Zohar (Pritzker II, 1:133a): “As long as Sarah existed, [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] never departed from her. A lamp would burn from one Sabbath eve to the next… After she died, that lamp died out; as soon as Rebekah appeared, Shekhinah reappeared and the lamp rekindled.” Rebekah “resembling Sarah in all she did,” so Isaac again sees Sarah’s image and is “comforted after his mother.” Abraham never brings Keturah into that tent; the dwelling and Sarah’s image pass to Isaac with Rebekah alone.

**Left-hand love; fourfold phrase (Ḥayyei Sarah, 1:133a–b).** In the Zohar, “He loved her” is special for Isaac–Rebekah: “Arousal of love of male toward female quickens only on the left” (Song 2:6). The four clauses of Gen 24:67 (*took Rebekah… as his wife… loved her… comforted after his mother*) total four from the aspect of Rebekah.

**Womb war (Toledot, 1:138a).** In the Zohar (Pritzker II, 1:138a), the twins’ jostling is crushing of sides: when Rebekah passed sites of mitsvah, Jacob thrust to exit; at idolatrous sites Esau kicked. Jacob rides the sacred perfect throne / sun with moon; Esau rides the serpent.

**Blessing plot (Toledot, 1:142a–142b).** In the Zohar, [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] reveals Isaac’s summons of Esau to Rebekah, who relays it to Jacob. The plot falls on the eve of Passover, when “the evil impulse must be exterminated and the moon prevail”; the two goat kids intimates the Yom Kippur goats — “one for a supernal rung and one to subdue the rung of Esau” (1:142b). Rebekah dresses Jacob in Esau’s precious garments (won from Nimrod, tracing to Adam); they emit fragrance only when Jacob wears them, “for the beauty of Jacob was the beauty of Adam.” The Zohar frames the stratagem as Eve/Adam inverted: a woman resembling Eve and a man resembling Adam deal cunningly with the serpent and its rider (1:145b–146a).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Rebekah is the matriarch through whom **divine election visibly overrides natural primogeniture**: she carries and acts upon the oracle that subverts the rights of the firstborn, making her the human instrument of the younger-son reversal that structures Genesis. Her initiative at the well (chosen for her kindness) and at the blessing (acting on the oracle) frame her as the matriarch of *decisive agency* — the counterpart to the more passive Isaac.

---

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- Not independently attested; part of the ancestral saga horizon.
- The **ethics of the stolen blessing** is a perennial crux; traditions that read Rebekah as a prophet executing the divine oracle resolve the moral tension differently from those who read it as morally ambiguous human scheming.
- The **wife-sister doublet** (Gen 26) parallels the Abraham/Sarah episodes.
