# Gerry Armstrong

**Gerald "Gerry" Armstrong** was a **Sea Organization member** who served as [[l-ron-hubbard|L. Ron Hubbard]]'s **personal research archivist**, tasked with assembling materials for an authorized biography of Hubbard. His discovery that the official biography contradicted the documents led to his defection and one of the most important legal proceedings in Scientology's history.

## The Archive and Discovery

Armstrong was assigned to gather and organize Hubbard's personal papers for a biography to be written by Omar Garrison. In the process of working through Hubbard's archive at **Gilman Hot Springs** (Gold Base), Armstrong discovered approximately **20 boxes of documents** that staff were preparing to destroy using the facility's "Jaws" industrial shredder. He saved these documents — a decision that would define the rest of his life.

The documents included Hubbard's **letters to his wives**, personal diaries, and military records. Armstrong found that these materials **directly contradicted** the official Church biography on virtually every major claim: military heroism, academic credentials, the nature of Hubbard's pre-Scientology career, and his personal character.

He also discovered that **Messenger logs were buried** on the property — further evidence of document destruction.

## Departure

Armstrong left the Church in **November 1981**. He took copies of documents from the archive with him, claiming later that he feared they would otherwise be destroyed.

## The Armstrong Case

The **Church of Scientology of California v. Gerald Armstrong** case was filed in **August 1982** (Superior Court, Los Angeles County, Case No. C 420153). The Church sought return of the documents and damages.

Armstrong's defense — supported by the documents themselves — argued the documents were evidence of fraud: that Hubbard's biography was fabricated and that the Church was built on systematic deception.

### Judge Breckenridge's Ruling (June 22, 1984)

Judge **Paul Breckenridge's** ruling of **June 22, 1984** became a landmark in Scientology litigation. Key findings:

- Hubbard: **"schizophrenic and paranoid"**
- Hubbard: a **"pathological liar"**
- The Church: **"corrupt"** and engaged in "a combination of illegal and immoral acts"
- The documents Armstrong had preserved were genuine and significant
- Armstrong's conduct was justified

The ruling is one of the most cited judicial assessments of Scientology and Hubbard in the fair-game literature.

### Settlement

In **December 1986**, Armstrong settled the case for **$800,000** from the Church. The settlement included confidentiality provisions. Armstrong has subsequently disputed the enforceability of those provisions on First Amendment grounds, leading to ongoing litigation.

## Fair Game Target

Armstrong's testimony in the Armstrong case — describing the organization's deceptive practices and the authentic content of the archive — made him a continuing **Fair Game** target. He testified in multiple subsequent proceedings, including the English custody case (Justice Latey, 1984) and the Wollersheim case.

He was subjected to surveillance, harassment, and multiple legal actions by the Church after the settlement, on the grounds that he had violated his confidentiality agreement. He has lived in Canada in recent decades.

## Significance

Armstrong's case is central to Scientology scholarship because:
1. He preserved primary documents that otherwise might have been destroyed
2. His testimony was the evidentiary foundation for Breckenridge's findings
3. The Church's response to him is one of the most extensively documented Fair Game operations
4. He embodies the paradox of the movement: someone who dedicated years of genuine service and was then targeted for honesty

Armstrong himself has argued (per Atack's account) that **"freedom of religion without freedom to discuss religion and religious experience is impossible"** — a formulation that has been cited in subsequent cases addressing whether Scientology's Fair Game policy is compatible with religious status.
