# James son of Zebedee

James son of Zebedee, brother of [[john-apostle|John]] and one of the Twelve, is the first
apostle to be martyred: [[herod-agrippa-i|Herod Agrippa I]] has him "killed by the sword"
(Acts 12:2, c. AD 44). [[darrell-bock|Bock]] explicitly flags his distinctness from James
the Lord's brother.

## Mismatch Flag — NOT James the Just

Bock states plainly that this James is **"not the Lord's brother but John's brother"**
(commenting on Acts 12:2). This figure is therefore **distinct from [[james-the-just|James
the Just]]**, the Lord's brother, who first appears at Acts 12:17 ("tell these things to
James"), leads the Jerusalem church, and is martyred c. AD 62 by Annas II (Josephus,
*Ant.* 20.9.1). Do not merge the two Jameses.

## Biographical Overview

James is a son of Zebedee and the brother of the apostle John; the two are among the Twelve.
Bock notes that Mark 10:39 had predicted death for "the sons of Zebedee," and cites Eusebius
(*Eccl. Hist.* 2.9.2–3) among the traditional witnesses. In [[acts|Acts]] James appears only
at the moment of his death.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 12:1–2** — during Herod Agrippa I's persecution of the church, James "the brother
  of John" is put to death "by the sword" (μαχαίρῃ).

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock reads James as "the first apostle to suffer martyrdom… the second martyr in Acts,
after Stephen." He judges James was "probably beheaded 'in the Roman manner'" (*m. Sanh.*
7.3), and, following Hengel and Schwemer, connects the execution to a king's use of the
royal *ius gladii*. Bock cites Fitzmyer's observation that "no effort is made to
reconstitute the Twelve after James's death," which Fitzmyer takes to imply a belief that
the Twelve "would be reestablished in the resurrection."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Within Bock's contrast-driven reading of Acts 12, James's martyrdom (the persecution's
success against one apostle) sets off against Peter's miraculous deliverance (its failure
against another) and against Herod's own judgment. James thus stands at the head of the
apostolic martyr tradition in Acts, fulfilling the Markan prediction of the cup the sons of
Zebedee would drink.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock treats the execution as historically grounded within Herod Agrippa I's well-attested
reign (the king's death is datable to c. March AD 44 against Josephus). The chief
interpretive pitfall Bock guards against is confusion with James the Lord's brother — a
distinction he draws explicitly.
