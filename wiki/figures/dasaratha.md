# Daśaratha

**Daśaratha** ("he who can drive his chariot in ten directions") is the solar-dynasty (*sūrya-vaṃśa*) king of [[ayodhya|Ayodhyā]], father of [[rama|Rāma]], [[bharata|Bharata]], Lakṣmaṇa, and Śatrughna. In Buck's retelling he is the frame character to whom Viśvāmitra addresses the long mythological history of [[ravana|Rāvaṇa]] and the demons — "Listen, Majesty" (line 1630) — and his story is the hinge on which the divine incarnation of [[vishnu|Viṣṇu]] turns. His tragedy is a king of impeccable external dharma destroyed by a husband's oath.

## Biographical Overview

### Old Age and Desire for Sons

In Buck's text, Daśaratha is explicitly "sixty thousand years old" when the events of the epic begin, and has no son to inherit his kingdom (lines 1279–1280). He calls his priest Vasiṣṭha and says: "I always long for a son. I can find no happiness without him, therefore make a sacrifice with fire, to please the gods" (lines 1280–1282). Vasiṣṭha promises him sons "after your own heart."

### The Sacrifice and the Births

Vasiṣṭha arranges the sacrificial rites by the [[ayodhya|Sarayu River]]. He sings mantras invoking first Indra, and then [[vishnu|Nārāyaṇa]] himself. When Nārāyaṇa's divine weapons fall to earth (his discus, mace, and shell-trumpet drop in sequence), a giant black man rises from the sacrificial flames (lines 1483–1496). He wore crimson, his body covered with dark lion's hair, his belt a bowstring, his palms marked with thunder-signs and the soles of his feet with wheels; his eyes were yellow like a tiger's. He handed Daśaratha a steaming golden bowl of rice cooked with milk and sweetened with sugar, saying: "Feed this to your wives, it will bring you sons" (line 1495). The giant vanished; Vasiṣṭha walked three times around the fire.

Daśaratha distributed the sacred rice to his three queens: half to his first wife **Kausalyā**, then half of the remainder to his second wife **Sumitrā**, then half of what remained to the youngest wife **Kaikeyī**, and finally all the rest back to Sumitrā again (lines 1510–1516).

A year later, on the ninth day of spring, **Rāma was first born to Kausalyā**. Later the same day **Bharata was born to Kaikeyī**; then **Sumitrā bore Lakshmana and Śatrughna** (lines 1517–1519). Daśaratha lit their birth-fires in jars of earth and named them on the eleventh day of their lives; "the Kosalas danced in the streets and rang all the temple bells" (lines 1520–1522).

### The Kings Who Come to His Court: Viśvāmitra's Visit

When the four princes were sixteen years old, the recluse Viśvāmitra came to Kosala (line 1554). He told Daśaratha that Rākṣasas had been polluting his sacrificial fires with flesh and gore, breaking his waterjars, warping his ladles, and extinguishing his fires — even though his rites were faultless (lines 1591–1600). He asked for Rāma to come kill two demons.

Daśaratha was anguished: "After living through sixty thousand long years, in my old age have I at last gained a son. How can you take a tender child, my firstborn son, and lead him to Death?" (lines 2626–2629). Viśvāmitra threatened to withdraw his blessing. Vasiṣṭha urged the King to keep his word. Daśaratha relented: "Take Rama and his brother Lakshmana" (line 2644). He held his two sons and "smelled the hair on the crowns of their heads" — they were still "round-faced children" — but when they armed themselves expertly "the King smiled then, and felt no more grief for their going" (lines 2650–2655).

Viśvāmitra narrated to Daśaratha the full mythological history of Rāvaṇa's origins — from Sukesa the first Rākṣasa through the building of Laṅkā, the boon of invulnerability, and the Battle of Heaven — explicitly addressing these events to the King as background: "Listen, Majesty—" (line 1630); "Oh Dasaratha, the hooded serpents of the underground are hard to frighten" (lines 2403–2405); "so began the Battle of Heaven, a year before your four sons' births" (line 2543).

### The Coronation Plan and Its Undoing

After Rāma and Sītā had been married twelve years and Rāma had gradually taken over the kingdom's work, Daśaratha felt he had "enough of kingdom" (line 3067). He called the Kosala Council and asked their approval to install Rāma as king. The council cheered; Sumantra the charioteer gave a long speech praising Rāma's qualities. Vasiṣṭha identified a rare auspicious conjunction of stars lasting only one day.

Daśaratha told Rāma of troubling dreams — "for many nights have I dreamt of stars falling to the ground in daylight with terror and noise. The region round my star of life is invaded by fatal planets and my Death, I think, rushes swiftly at me" (lines 3156–3159). He said all his debts to gods, teachers, ancestors, brahmanas, and himself were paid: "All are paid. I will rule no longer" (line 3171).

### The Two Boons and the Breaking

Kaikeyi's maidservant Manthara stirred Kaikeyī's jealousy by telling her Rāma's coronation would reduce her to a slave of Kausalyā. Manthara reminded Kaikeyī of a long-ago event: Daśaratha had entered the gods' war against the Asuras of drought; Kaikeyī drove his sky-chariot, saved his life, and he promised her **two wishes**, which she had saved unclaimed (lines 3293–3302). Kaikeyī ran to the palace anger-room — an iron-doored, lightless chamber — locked herself in, broke her pearls, tore her clothes and screamed (lines 3332–3335).

Daśaratha shattered the door with one stroke of one hand and found her lying "amid her broken pearls and flowers" (lines 3346–3352). He swore to her: "I am the King of Kings in this world, all the land that lies in sunlight is mine" and pledged to grant anything she asked (lines 3354–3372). She asked for the two wishes: **make Bharata king and not Rāma; send Rāma to the forest for fourteen years** (lines 3373–3374).

Daśaratha said: "I give what you ask" and then: "I am a stag facing a tigress, a snake held within a charmed circle" (lines 3378–3385). He cursed Kaikeyī: "let this carnal Queen fall to damnation; let her spend the rest of her life in dread of the next world!" (lines 3400–3402).

Rāma accepted the exile without protest: "Dasaratha never takes back, never fails to carry out what he has once spoken. Mother, I am like my father, trust me, have no fear" (lines 3433–3435).

## Primary Source Appearances

Buck's prose retelling presents Daśaratha across three main narrative arcs in these lines:

1. **The sacrifice and sons' births** (lines 1279–1522): the aging king who desires sons, the elaborate ritual through which Viṣṇu descends as four princes, and the distribution of the sacred rice that determines which queen bears which son.
2. **The reluctant father** (lines 2619–2655): his grief at giving up Rāma to Viśvāmitra; the arms he held his sons in before letting them go.
3. **The coronation and boons crisis** (lines 3059–3502): his desire to see Rāma rule while he still lives; his pride and then devastation when Kaikeyī invokes her boons; his helplessness before his own oath.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The Boons as Dharmic Trap

Daśaratha's tragedy is a dharmic paradox: a king whose every public action is exemplary — righteous rule, generous sacrifice, wise governance — is destroyed by a private oath made at a moment of personal vulnerability. The boons were earned honestly (Kaikeyī *did* save his life in battle) and promised freely. A Solar King cannot break his given word: "Dasaratha never takes back, never fails to carry out what he has once spoken" (line 3433). Rāma himself cites this as the reason he accepts exile.

The text makes Daśaratha explicitly compare himself to a trapped animal — "a stag facing a tigress, a snake held within a charmed circle" (lines 3384–3385) — acknowledging his own helplessness not as weakness but as the consequence of his virtue. A lesser king might break the boon; his integrity becomes the mechanism of his ruin.

### The King as Vehicle for Divine Descent

Daśaratha is the human instrument through which [[vishnu|Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa]] enters the world. His desire for sons, his willingness to hold the horse sacrifice, his readiness to receive the golden bowl — all are conditions that Brahma and Indra's plan requires. The text makes clear that his longing for sons is cosmically timed: Indra goes to Brahma, Brahma sends Indra to Nārāyaṇa, Nārāyaṇa agrees to descend, and *then* Vasiṣṭha's sacrifice is effective. Daśaratha acts; the gods complete. The king of Ayodhyā is not merely a father but the hospitable vessel chosen by heaven for the avatar.

### Yuga Context

Viśvāmitra contextualizes Daśaratha's reign within the doctrine of the yugas: "we are living in the second age of the world, and the quarter part of Virtue has now died among men" (lines 1603–1605). Daśaratha's righteousness is real but exists within a declining world — which is precisely why Viṣṇu must descend into it.

## Death and its Significance (Buck's Retelling, lines 3501–5730)

### Blindness and the Deathbed Confession

After Rāma's chariot passes through Ayodhyā's gate, Daśaratha rushes to the gate too late. He presses his ear to the ground to hear the horses. When the sound stops, he falls. His sight follows Rāma and "does not yet return" — he lies blind in Kausalyā's darkened room, fanned by Kausalyā and tended by Sumitrā (lines 4596–4636). Sumitrā says plainly: "The King is blind" (line 4600). When Sumantra returns with word of Rāma, Daśaratha whispers his first questions: "What did he do? Where is he?" (line 4641). He tries to rise but trembles: "Charioteer, if I have ever done any good thing, drive me to Rāma!" (lines 4671–4672).

Lying in the dark, Daśaratha wakes from a troubled sleep and confesses to Kausalyā the origin of all his misfortune. As a young prince, proud of his ability to **shoot by sound alone**, he hunted one evening by the Sarayu in flood. He heard what he took to be an elephant drinking and loosed an arrow. The arrow struck a young man filling a waterpot — the only son of blind forest ascetics (lines 4696–4729). The young man died in Daśaratha's arms. Daśaratha then carried water to the blind parents and confessed. The father, though capable of cursing Daśaratha to destruction ("I can pull down Indra himself if he knowingly hurts a harmless forest man," line 4766), acknowledged the accident. He asked only one thing: burn them all on one pyre with their son. Before dying, his prophetic warning: "Be careful in the future . . . Will you not one day find your own death over your own lost son? Will you come by this same fate?" (lines 4784–4787). Daśaratha burned all three (lines 4788–4791).

Now on his deathbed, Daśaratha recognizes the karmic return: "That whole crime now comes home to me. If Rāma could touch me I might live, but I have no other help . . . Rāma . . . Where are you? . . . Rāma . . ." (lines 4793–4803).

### Yama's Coming

"Yama the Death Lord softly touched the locked doors of the Ayodhya palace and they flew open for him. Death holding his maces and bars in his hands entered unseen . . . going gently to the King, casting sleep over Kausalyā. Lord Yama reached into Daśaratha's breast and took out his mortal soul. Round the soul he put the diamond-wire silver noose of death and slipped tight the knot, then he took him away" (lines 4804–4811).

The narrator's epitaph: "Two fires of grief past and present had dried away the life of the Ayodhya king. When that night was sometime passed he all unnoticed breathed no more, and departed this life. So did he die, the far-famed King Daśaratha, a warrior with no enemies alive" (lines 4812–4817).

### Funeral and Heaven

Sumantra places the King's body in an iron vat of oil and sends three swift riders to summon Bharata from Kekaya (lines 4853–4855). Bharata performs the funeral "in Rāma's name" (line 5069); the pyre is lit from Ayodhyā's sacred flame. "That burning freed Daśaratha's spirit. It let him come out from Yama's shadowy land and get from the Moon his heavenly body, and in celestial robes take his rightful place in heaven" (lines 5077–5081). Later, at the Ganga crossing on the march to find Rāma, Bharata offers water and "in heaven Daśaratha's spirit was bright-shining with pleasure" (lines 5182–5183).

### The Karmic Structure of the Death

The death is explicitly framed as **karmic return**: the bow-skill that killed the blind man's son returns as the grief that kills the father who loses his own son. The parents' prophetic curse ("Will you not one day find your own death over your own lost son?") is not supernatural punishment but the natural fruition of a deed: "as a man's deeds are good or evil so are the events which follow them, and which the man must face in their time" (lines 4681–4682 — Daśaratha's own words). The man who could "shoot by sound" in the dark dies unable to see in the light: his blindness literalizes the moral insight that desire obscured throughout his life.
