# Ambedkar

**Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar** (1891–1956 CE) — "Babasaheb" — was a Dalit lawyer and thinker who
drafted the Indian Constitution, led the movement for Dalit rights, and organized the mass conversion
of five million Dalits to Buddhism in 1956. His fundamental disagreement with [[gandhi|Gandhi]]
over caste reform, and his final act of leaving Hinduism altogether, make him one of the most
significant figures in the modern history of Indian religion.

## Biographical Overview

Ambedkar was born into the Mahar caste in Maharashtra — one of the "Untouchable" communities
traditionally regarded as ritually polluting by caste Hindus. He received a doctorate from
Columbia University and another from the London School of Economics, making him among the most
highly educated Indians of his generation. He served as India's first law minister and was the
principal architect of the Constitution of India (1950).

He converted to Buddhism on October 14, 1956, along with approximately five hundred thousand
followers, in Nagpur — six weeks before his death on December 6, 1956.

## Conflict with Gandhi

Both Gandhi and Ambedkar agreed that Untouchability had to be abolished. They disagreed on the
means and on caste:

- **Gandhi** held that caste could be reformed from within Hinduism; he supported Untouchability's
  abolition but not the abolition of the caste system as such
- **Ambedkar** held that caste and Untouchability were inseparable; that a religion that regarded
  basic class injustice as *eternal* could not reform it; and that the only genuine liberation was
  to leave Hinduism

Their confrontation crystallized in the **Poona Pact (1932)**: Ambedkar sought a separate
electorate for Dalit communities; Gandhi fasted against it; Ambedkar, rather than watch Gandhi die,
signed the pact and accepted reserved seats within the general Hindu electorate instead. He never
forgave Gandhi for using moral coercion to override Dalit political autonomy.

Gandhi's famous question to Ambedkar — "How can I call this land my own homeland and this religion
my own wherein we are treated worse than cats and dogs, wherein we cannot get water to drink?" —
was Ambedkar's, not Gandhi's; Doniger quotes Ambedkar: "Gandhiji, I have no homeland."

## Rejection of Hinduism and Conversion to Buddhism

Ambedkar reasoned that because Hindus viewed their tradition as *eternal*, they regarded its class
injustice and Untouchability as eternal too — and impossible to eradicate from within. He first
resisted conversion movements; then he concluded it was unavoidable.

He chose **Buddhism** because:
- It rejected caste from its founding texts
- It offered a universally accessible soteriology not tied to birth
- He translated the Buddhist concept of individual suffering (*dukkha*) into his own awareness of
  **social suffering**

Ambedkar discarded much of traditional Buddhism and inserted in its place his own doctrine of social
activism. Notably, he rejected the story of the Four Sights (the young Siddhartha confined in a
palace until, going outside, he saw a sick man, an old man, a dead man, and a renouncer): "It does
not appeal to reason that a twenty-nine-year-old man would not have been exposed to death by then."

## Political Legacy: Reservations and Dalits

Ambedkar fought for the recognition of Scheduled Castes and their entitlement to affirmative action
(called "reservation" in India). The 1980 Mandal Commission, which reserved nearly half of all
government and educational places for underprivileged castes, stands on the foundation of his work.

The legacy is contested and complex:
- Mandal effectively inverted Sanskritization: some Brahmins burned themselves to death in protest
- Castes not particularly disadvantaged managed to get reclassified as Scheduled to access the
  new opportunities
- Dalits who converted to Buddhism faced a dilemma: Buddhist identity meant losing Scheduled Caste
  status (and its benefits) unless they continued to declare themselves Hindu on official forms

One convert described the dilemma: "My father became a Buddhist in honor of Ambedkar but could not
say so openly. I became a Buddhist too, but only orally, because on the forms you have to write
down Scheduled Caste. If you are a Buddhist, you can't get the scholarship. But I am proud to follow
Ambedkar. Being Scheduled Caste causes inferiority in our minds. To be Buddhist, it makes me feel free!"
