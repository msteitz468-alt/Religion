# Bildad the Shuhite

The second of [[job|Job]]'s three comforters in the [[book-of-job|book of Job]], and the friend who
grounds his case in the accumulated teaching of the fathers. He speaks second in each cycle. His
third speech is the shortest in the book and is the pivot of the third-cycle problem.

## Biographical Overview

Bildad is introduced with the other two comforters at 2:11 and identified by his home rather than by
genealogy.

[[john-hartley|Hartley]] on the name and place: *"Bildad, possibly meaning 'son of Hadad,' lived in
Shuah. Akkadian documents mention a district Sūḫu, located on the Middle Euphrates River, below the
mouth of the Khabur River"* ([p. 85–86], on 2:11). He also reports a competing placement: *"Pope
thinks that the biblical genealogies place Shuah farther to the south, since it is connected with
Dedan and Sheba."* Hartley does not adjudicate between the Euphratean and the southern location. See
[[uz|Uz]] for the book's wider geography.

On relative age Hartley resists the ranking schemes: the friends are *"mature and responsible elders
with whom a few years makes little difference,"* and the speaking order reflects *"status and
eloquence rather than age"* ([p. 165]).

## Primary Source Appearances

Bildad speaks three times. Hartley records 43 and 41 lines for the first two; the third, as
transmitted at 25:1–6, runs only 10.

- **First speech (8:1–22).** Thesis, tradition-argument, nature-analogies, and a promise of
  restoration. Hartley: *"Bildad's thesis is that all God's ways are just (v. 3). This conviction
  rests solidly on the teaching of the fathers (vv. 8–10) and on the ways of nature (vv. 11–19)…
  In this regard Bildad is a champion of 'old-time' religion"* ([p. 155]).
- **Second speech (18:1–21).** A complaint against Job (vv. 2–4) and then an unbroken discourse on
  the fate of the wicked (vv. 5–21).
- **Third speech.** Transmitted as 25:1–6; Hartley reconstructs it as **25:1–6 + 27:13–23** (see
  below).

### The appeal to the fathers

The tradition-argument at 8:8–10 is Bildad's characteristic move, and Hartley states both its
premise and its polemical edge: *"The wisdom of the forefathers is prized over an individual's
experience, for a single life is too short to afford a personal perspective on the highest wisdom…
With these words Bildad judges Job's challenge of traditional wisdom as invalid and presumptuous"*
([p. 158]). Hartley contrasts this with the epistemic methods of the other two friends —
[[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz]] appeals to a visionary experience, [[zophar-the-naamathite|Zophar]]
to deductive reasoning from cherished propositions.

### The fate of the wicked (ch. 18)

Hartley notes first that Bildad has reversed himself: *"Whereas in his former speech he emphasized
the possibility that Job might have a bright future, because the righteous are blessed, he now
details the horrid fate that befalls the ungodly. He paints a bleak picture without a single bright
stroke"* ([p. 272]). He catalogues the image-set: *"a sheikh's lamp (vv. 5–6), numerous traps and
snares (vv. 8–10), mythopoetic images of Sheol (vv. 11–14), a curse executed against a defeated foe
(v. 15), and the death of a stalwart tree (v. 16)."*

Hartley reads the chapter as marking the collapse of dialogue itself: *"this speech lacks any hymnic
praise to God and any exhortation to Job. Interchange between the parties is also missing. It
appears that Bildad has abandoned dialoguing with Job; here he delivers only a diatribe on the wicked
man's demise."* The six trapping-terms of 18:8–10 he glosses as a minefield — *"It is impossible for
any wicked person to escape the heavenly trapper"* — and the *"firstborn of death"* of 18:13 as
plague, adding that the description is aimed at Job's actual body: *"For Bildad, Job's emaciated body
is the convicting evidence of his wrongdoing."*

## Tradition-Specific Reception

The ingested source is a modern critical-evangelical commentary and carries no survey of rabbinic,
patristic, or Islamic reception of Bildad. This wiki's ingested sources therefore contain no material
on Bildad's reception outside the book itself and modern scholarship.

Within the book, Bildad is condemned with the other two at 42:7. Hartley: *"Yahweh holds all of them
responsible for the implications of their teachings and exhortations to Job"* ([p. 538]), and the
charge is heavier than the one laid against Job — *"the friends have spoken falsely in their attempt
to defend God"* ([p. 539]). Bildad, with Eliphaz and Zophar, then obeys and submits to Job's
intercession.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### The character's position

In the text Bildad argues that God never perverts justice and that retribution is therefore
exceptionless and operative in the present. Hartley's paraphrase of the character: *"Without any
exceptions, the righteous are blessed and the wicked are punished. Any apparent exception is
momentary."* At 8:3 the stakes are total — Hartley glosses the character's logic: *"any accusation
that God is perverting justice is tantamount to accusing him of acting demonically. If the charge is
true, then God is not God. Such a deity would be a devil, unworthy of a person's devotion."*

Bildad applies this immediately to Job's dead children, and Hartley judges him for it: *"Bildad
proves to be too insensitive to Job's anguish to avoid using his children as an illustration of God's
ways"* ([p. 156]).

Hartley identifies a cosmological premise underneath: *"For Bildad the moral order is so integrally
related to the natural order that asking for a change in the moral law — Job asking God to acquit him
without his repenting — is the same as asking for a remarkable event in nature. But it is beyond his
imagination that God would so alter the universe to redeem one man"* ([p. 274], on 18:4).

### Hartley's verdict

Hartley's sharpest characterization of retribution theology anywhere in the commentary is delivered
on Bildad's first speech:

> *"After renouncing Job's complaint that God is treating him unjustly, Bildad instructs Job about
> the certainty of double retribution… In Bildad's opinion it is unequivocally true that God does
> not pervert justice. **He thus equates justice with double retribution.**"*
>
> *"**A prisoner of tradition, Bildad refuses to allow any experience, particularly Job's, to temper
> his doctrine. Rather, experience must always be interpreted in the light of known precepts. There
> can be no exceptions to the precept, for an exception would challenge not only this precept but
> also the doctrine that God is just, the very cornerstone of orthodox theology. Bildad's error lies
> in building his entire theology on the premises of the doctrine of retribution, for earthly
> retribution is not the backbone of divine justice, as the book of Job itself demonstrates.**"*
> ([p. 164])

The final clause is Hartley's own theological verdict and should not be read back into any character
in the book. See [[doctrine-of-retribution|the doctrine of retribution]] and [[theodicy|theodicy]].

Hartley's pastoral indictment is separate and blunter: *"Sadly he fails to stand with his friend
during the time of his greatest need"* ([p. 165]). Unlike Eliphaz and Zophar, Bildad is not said by
Hartley to take [[satan|the Satan]]'s side of the wager — that verdict is reserved for the other two.

Hartley also notices a literary irony in Bildad's mouth: the friends unwittingly forecast the
epilogue. *"Through Bildad's words the author is hinting at Job's restoration as unfolded in the
epilogue (42:10–17)… **The friends will be caught by surprise at the fulfillment of their promises
without Job's following the conditions they recommend**"* ([p. 157], on 8:7).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

### The third speech and the third cycle

Bildad's transmitted third speech (25:1–6) is only 10 lines, lacks an introduction and a conclusion,
and is the crux of the third-cycle problem. Hartley diagnoses the damage as transmissional rather
than authorial — *"It is quite obvious that this cycle has suffered severely early in its
transmission"* ([p. 323]) — and proposes a deliberately minimal repair: **Bildad's third speech =
25:1–6 + 27:13–23**, with 26:1–14 + 27:1–12 left to Job, no third speech for Zophar at all, and ch.
28 removed from the dialogue entirely. Exactly one block moves. His stated grounds are that 27:13–23
*"is closer to the comforters' type of discussion than to Job's"* and *"offers a direct rebuttal to
the ideas Job was struggling with in ch. 24"*; he concedes the join is rough and posits further lost
lines without reconstructing them. This wiki records the reconstruction as Hartley's position among
others and does not adjudicate it — see [[job-third-cycle-integrity|the third-cycle integrity
controversy]].

### Location of Shuah

Hartley reports two placements — a Middle Euphrates district *Sūḫu* attested in Akkadian documents,
and Pope's southern location near [Dedan] and [Sheba] — without deciding between them.

Hartley offers no position on Bildad's historicity; the book's patriarchal setting is treated as an
authorial and antiquarian choice.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Bildad's thesis is one of the three positions the book must dismantle, and Hartley tracks its echoes
carefully. Job concedes and reframes it at 9:2 (*"The immediate antecedent of the pronoun* it *is
Bildad's thesis that 'God does not pervert justice' (8:3)"*), challenges it at 9:22–24 and 14:3, and
refuses at 14:21 the corollary that a wicked man is punished through his children's misfortunes.
Bildad's own rebuttal at 18:16 targets Job's hope in the felled tree of 14:7, a thread Hartley traces
across three speakers.

Most strikingly, Hartley notes that Bildad's thesis reappears in [[elihu|Elihu]]'s mouth: on 34:12,
*"The second line is a reiteration of Bildad's thesis… This teaching means that whatever misfortune
a person experiences is justly deserved. There are no exceptions, for God makes no mistakes"*
([p. 454]) — which sits in tension with Hartley's broader claim that Elihu departs from the friends.
That tension is recorded on Elihu's page.
