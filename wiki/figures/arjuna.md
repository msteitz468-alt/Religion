Arjuna is the third Pandava, son of Pandu and Kunti (by the god Indra according to tradition), and is portrayed throughout the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] as the preeminent archer and warrior of his age. The stretch of narrative from the war preparations through the first nine days of the Kurukshetra battle (Chs. XLIX–LXXIII in [[c-rajagopalachari|Rajagopalachari]]'s retelling) places him at the center of the epic's moral and martial action.

## Biographical Overview

Arjuna is the archer whose skills are unmatched among his contemporaries. Three figures alone on the Kaurava side could stand against him "with any chance of success — the grandsire Bhishma, Drona and Karna" (Ch. LXV). His famous bow is the Gandiva, described repeatedly as the source of an apparently inexhaustible stream of arrows. His chariot's flag displays Hanuman (Ch. LXVII). He has at least two sons mentioned in this range: Iravan (by a Naga wife, killed on the eighth day) and [[abhimanyu|Abhimanyu]] (by Subhadra, Krishna's sister).

His defining relationship in the war is with [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]], whom he chose as his charioteer — a choice whose theological significance is explored in the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]].

## Primary Source Appearances

**Chapter XLIX — The Choice of Krishna as Charioteer.** Both Arjuna and Duryodhana arrive at Dwaraka on the same day seeking Krishna's support. Krishna woke to see Arjuna standing at the foot of his bed (Duryodhana seated at the head); he offered the choice to Arjuna first as the younger. The choice was between Krishna himself, unarmed, and the great Narayana army. Arjuna chose Krishna without hesitation: "I would be content if you are with us, though you may wield no weapon." When Krishna asked why he had chosen so unwisely, Arjuna answered that he wanted to win glory even like Krishna's, believing that with Krishna driving his chariot he could achieve victory single-handed. "I have desired this for long and you have today fulfilled my wish." (Ch. XLIX)

**Chapter LIII — Arjuna's Words to Sanjaya.** Sanjaya reported Arjuna's declaration to the Kaurava court: "Krishna and I are going to destroy Duryodhana and his followers, root and branch. Make no mistake about it. The Gandiva is impatient for war. My bow-string is throbbing even without my stretching it, and from my quiver, arrows keep peeping out impatiently, demanding when?" He claimed that not even Indra and the gods could defeat him and Krishna combined. (Ch. LIV)

**Chapter LVII — Pandava Generalissimo.** When Yudhishthira sought opinion on who should command the Pandava forces, Arjuna named Dhrishtadyumna: "born to bring about Drona's end... He is the only man fitted to be our commander." Krishna endorsed Arjuna's choice and Dhrishtadyumna was anointed Supreme Commander. (Ch. LVII)

**Chapter LX — Dismissal of Rukma.** When Rukma arrived at Kurukshetra offering to help any sector the Pandavas wished, "Arjuna laughed" and said: "We are not afraid of the size of the enemy forces. We have no need of your help and do not particularly desire it. You may either go away or stay on, just as you like." (Ch. LX)

**Chapter LXII — The Bhagavad Gita.** When Arjuna saw men arrayed on both sides for mutual slaughter, "he was deeply agitated and Krishna spoke to him in order to quell his agitation and remove his doubts. Krishna's exhortation to Arjuna at this juncture is the Bhagavad Gita." (Ch. LXII — see Notes for Main Thread below)

**Chapter LXIII — Seeking Benediction.** When Yudhishthira doffed his armour before battle and walked unarmed toward the Kaurava lines, Arjuna was among the first confused: "Dhananjaya too was perplexed and he jumped down from his chariot and ran to Yudhishthira." He asked Yudhishthira what he was doing. (Ch. LXIII)

**Days One through Nine of the Battle (Chs. LXIV–LXXII):**

- *Day 1*: Arjuna fights Bhishma directly; Bhishma's chariot was all-destructive on the Pandava side. (Ch. LXIV)
- *Day 2*: On Krishna's urging, Arjuna drove straight at Bhishma. His prowess on that day was so remarkable that his chariot "flashed hither and thither sundering hostile ranks like forked lightning." Arjuna's deeds of valour caused great loss to the Kauravas. (Ch. LXV)
- *Day 3*: The key episode of the day: when the Pandava army was being routed by Bhishma's afternoon attack, Arjuna fought but held back his full strength out of "too much regard for his great grandsire." Krishna, frustrated, "dropped the reins, took up his discus and jumped down from the chariot and dashed forward towards Bhishma." Bhishma rejoiced and invited death at Krishna's hands. Arjuna jumped down, overtook Krishna "with great difficulty," and entreated him to return: "Do not lose your patience with me. Desist and I promise not to flinch." Having persuaded Krishna to remount, Arjuna "attacked the Kaurava forces furiously and thousands were slain by him." (Ch. LXVI)
- *Day 4*: Seeing Abhimanyu surrounded by five Kaurava warriors, "Arjuna saw this combined attack on his son and, with a wrathful lion-roar joined his son whereat the tempo of fighting flared up." (Ch. LXVII)
- *Day 5*: "Arjuna killed thousands of warriors that evening. The soldiers, despatched against him by Duryodhana, perished like moths in the fire." (Ch. LXVIII)
- *Day 7*: "Son of Drona and Bhishma," Arjuna said to Krishna, "drive the car towards Bhishma." All five Pandavas joined him, but Bhishma held his own until sunset. (Ch. LXX)
- *Day 8*: Arjuna's son Iravan was killed on the eighth day. On hearing this, Arjuna "broke down completely" and lamented: "What are we doing all this wretched destruction upon one another for? Just for the sake of property." He said he continued fighting only because men would otherwise think him a coward. "When I see the dead warriors lying on the field, my heart is filled with unbearable anguish." (Ch. LXXI)
- *Day 9*: Arjuna's heart was "not in the fight. Unwillingly and in great distress of mind, he proceeded to the combat." His arrows repeatedly shattered Bhishma's bow, but with insufficient force. Krishna again jumped down with his discus. Again Arjuna jumped down and ran after Krishna, casting both arms around him, pleading: "Do not break your pledge. You have promised not to use weapons in this battle. This is my work. I shall not fail." He took Krishna back and resumed the battle. The day ended with the Pandava forces "handled roughly" as the sun set. (Ch. LXXII)

**Chapter LXXIII — The Passing of Bhishma (Day 10).** Keeping Sikhandin in front, Arjuna attacked Bhishma from behind, aiming arrows at the "weak points in Bhishma's armour." Bhishma smiled as the arrows came and recognized them as Arjuna's: "these cannot be Sikhandin's, for they burn my flesh as the crab's young ones tear their mother's body." Bhishma fell from his chariot under the dense arrows. When Bhishma asked for a warrior's cushion, "Arjuna took three arrows from out of his quiver and so placed them that the grandsire's head found support on their points." Bhishma praised the deed. When Bhishma requested water, "Arjuna raised his bow, and drawing it to the ear, shot a shaft down into the earth near the grandsire on his right side. Up the opening made by the arrow, there gushed a stream of pure sweet water to the very lips of the dying man." (Ch. LXXIII)

**Days 11–12 (Chs. LXXVI–LXXVIII — under Drona's command).** Drona's plan to capture Yudhishthira alive could only work "so long as Dhananjaya is nearby" — Drona explicitly stated that removing Arjuna from Yudhishthira's side was the prerequisite. On the twelfth day, the samsaptakas (a sworn suicide squad of Trigarta warriors) challenged Arjuna and drew him to the southern flank. Arjuna destroyed them in a fierce battle. Returning, Arjuna encountered and killed the veteran elephant-warrior Bhagadatta: "Bhagadatta, take your last look at the world and prepare for death!" When a Vaishnava-mantra-charged elephant goad threatened Arjuna, Krishna interposed himself; Arjuna protested that Krishna had agreed to be charioteer only. Arjuna then killed Bhagadatta's elephant with an arrow through the head, and slew Bhagadatta with a crescent-headed shaft. He also defeated Sakuni's brothers Vrisha and Achala. (Ch. LXXVIII)

**Chapter LXXXI — A Father's Grief.** Returning from fighting the samsaptakas on the thirteenth day, Arjuna had a premonition of disaster: "my mind is not at ease. My mouth feels parched and my heart is troubled with a great presentiment of loss." He noticed the unusual silence and the averted eyes of the soldiers, and his first thought was of Abhimanyu: "I did not teach him how to make his way out of that formation. Has he been slain indeed?" When the mournful silence confirmed his fears, Arjuna "burst into heart-broken lamentation" and fell in a swoon. On recovery, he took an oath: "Before sunset tomorrow, I shall slay this Jayadratha who caused my son's death." He twanged the Gandiva string; Krishna blew the Panchajanya conch. (Ch. LXXXI)

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Arjuna occupies a structural position unique in the epic: he is both the supreme warrior and the figure in whom the moral crisis of the war is concentrated. His reluctance to kill the grandsire and teachers, his lamentation over Iravan, and his crisis before battle (leading to the Gita) each exemplify the tension between kshatriya [[dharma]] and natural human attachment. Rajagopalachari frames the Bhagavad Gita as arising from this crisis — "Krishna's exhortation to Arjuna at this juncture is the Bhagavad Gita which is enshrined in millions of hearts as the Word of God, and is acknowledged by all as one of the supreme treasures of human literature. Its gospel of devotion to duty, without attachment or desire of reward, has shown the way of life for all men." (Ch. LXII)

His choice of the unarmed Krishna over an entire army is treated as central to the epic's theology: by choosing the Lord himself rather than his instruments of power, Arjuna receives the teaching that ultimately matters more than military force.

His repeated restraint of Krishna from entering the battle — twice on days three and nine — dramatizes the paradox of a devotee whose love for his god imposes a constraint even on god's action in the world.
