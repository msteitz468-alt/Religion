# Phinehas

## Biographical Overview

Phinehas son of Eleazar son of Aaron appears in the wilderness narrative as the priest who stops the Baal-Peor plague by killing Zimri (Israelite) and Kozbi (Midianite) (Num 25:6–13). Yahweh grants him a **covenant of peace** / everlasting high-priestly line. He later appears with the Midian war (Num 31), at the Transjordan altar crisis (Josh 22), and in later biblical and Second Temple reception as the paradigm of **zeal**.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Numbers 25:1–18** — Baal-Peor: sexual immorality and idolatry; Zimri brings Kozbi into the camp; Phinehas’s spear; plague of 24,000 checked; covenant of peace / everlasting priesthood; Midianite trickery as ground for ch. 31.
- **Numbers 31** — priestly role in Midian war (with sacred vessels/trumpets in Ashley’s reading of the campaign).
- **Joshua 22; 24:33** — mediation in the altar dispute; burial notice for Eleazar (and Phinehas’s association with the high-priestly line).
- **Judges 20:28** — Phinehas serves before the ark in the Benjamin war narrative.
- **Psalm 106:30–31** — “Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was checked… it was counted to him as righteousness.”
- **Chronicles / Ezra** — genealogical high-priestly line through Phinehas.
- **Sirach 45:23–24; 1 Maccabees 2:26, 54; 4 Maccabees 18:12** — zeal as enduring interpretive key (Mattathias models Phinehas).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Evangelical / Ashley (NICOT Numbers)

[[timothy-ashley|Ashley]] accepts that two stories were amalgamated (general fornication/idolatry + specific Zimri–Kozbi–Phinehas) but refuses speculative reconstruction of independent forms; combined final-form point: **priesthood and people must both be pure**. He follows Wenham’s outline for the final form: sin/plague/end (vv. 1–9); outcome for Phinehas (10–15); judgment on Midian (16–18).

- Name: Egyptian *pi-nḥas*, “the dark-skinned one.”
- Act turns aside wrath as Aaron’s incense *kipper* did in Num 17:13–15 (Eng. 16:46–48); possibly not Eleazar because high-priest corpse-contact rules are stricter.
- Yahweh did not command this specific act (his command was sterner) but **approved** it; zeal (*qinʾâ*) is Yahweh’s own jealousy for his honor.
- Pattern for Samuel’s slaying of Agag (1 Sam 15) and Mattathias at the altar (1 Macc 2).
- **Covenant of peace** (*bᵉrîtî šālôm*): bond of obligation; material principle *šālôm* (wholeness); formal principle “covenant of everlasting priesthood” (v. 13) taken as synonymous—not eliminating Ithamar’s line from general priesthood but securing **high** priesthood through Phinehas’s descendants.
- *Kipper* here: mediatorial work; the “offering” is the deaths of Zimri and Kozbi rather than animal substitute.
- Notes unresolved 1 Cor 10:8 (23,000 vs 24,000) without forced harmonization.
- Eli’s naming a son Phinehas may seek to legitimate the Shiloh priesthood (Ashley).

### Jewish and Second Temple

Zeal for Yahweh’s honor; Maccabean self-identification with Phinehas; Sirach praise; rabbinic expansions of the qubbâ episode (including traditions of piercing through the sex organs—“punishment fits the crime,” reported by Ashley as later Jewish interpretation).

### Kabbalistic (Zohar)

Pritzker Vol. IX (Pineḥas) densifies *berit shalom*, *yod*, and Nadab–Abihu **gilgul** into Phinehas—Sod layer distinct from Ashley’s historical-grammatical reading. See [[numbers|Numbers]] Zoharic section and [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol9]].

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Phinehas is the book of Numbers’ climactic model of **zealous covenant fidelity** that preserves the community when leadership and people are both compromised. He bridges priestly succession (Eleazar → Phinehas line) and the new generation’s preparation for the land. In Christian reception he is both exemplar of holy jealousy and a difficult text for ethics of religious violence—Ashley neither softens the narrative nor generalizes it into a standing warrant for private violence; he places it as exceptional, divinely approved zeal patterned later by Samuel and Mattathias.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- Amalgamation of two Peor stories vs single composition (Ashley: amalgamation likely; independent forms unknowable).
- Ethics of the killing and of Midian *ḥērem* (ch. 31) in modern reception.
- Relation of “covenant of peace” to later high-priestly politics and Eli’s line.
- Zoharic gilgul reading vs plain-sense narrative (not adjudicated here).

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Load-bearing for Ashley’s New Orientation section and Cole’s third wilderness cycle; for Maccabean identity; for Psalm 106’s righteousness credit; for Zoharic Pineḥas theurgy.
