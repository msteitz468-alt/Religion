# Marcellus of Ancyra

Bishop of Ancyra and a staunch defender of the Nicene *[[homoousion]]*, whose unusual theology gave it (in the eyes of the Eusebians) a [[modalism|Sabellian]] sense, making him their lasting "bogy." Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. IX.

## Teaching

Grounding his theology on the Bible and apostolic tradition (and rejecting the authority of the Fathers), Marcellus held God to be "an indivisible Monad," a single *prosopon*. Before all ages the Logos was "in God as His immanent reason, identical with Him… as a man's reason is with himself." He condemned the Origenist idea of the Logos as a distinct *hypostasis* as threatening polytheism. At creation and incarnation the Monad "expands" into a dyad, and with the outpouring of the Spirit a triad; after the judgment the Logos will be "reabsorbed in the Monad," and "the kingdom of Christ will end."

Kelly judges him not strictly Sabellian but closer to the [[oikonomia|economic Trinitarianism]] of Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Tertullian. His teaching is the reason anti-Nicene and later catholic creeds added the clause "**of Whose reign there will be no end.**" His pupil **Photinus** of Sirmium pushed it toward [[adoptionism|adoptionist]] Christology.

## Ayres on Marcellus (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

For Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) Marcellus heads one of
the four early-fourth-century **theological trajectories** — the "Marcellan" theology, the
"theologians of the undivided Monad" — and is the trajectory's main surviving witness. Ayres
treats "Sabellian" as a polemical label rather than an accurate description: the persistent
condemnation of Marcellus (and his pupil [[photinus|Photinus]]) by the "Eusebians" is what
drove the anti-Marcellan clauses of the **Dedication creed** (Antioch 341) and the Fourth
Antiochene creed ("whose kingdom endures indissolubly"), and Marcellus's presence (with
[[athanasius|Athanasius]]) is why Eastern bishops refused to sit at **Serdica (343)**. His
condemnation forms part of the polemical machinery by which the broad "[[arian-controversy|Arian]]"
category was constructed — see [[pro-nicene-theology]].
