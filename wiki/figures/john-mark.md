# John Mark

John Mark is the son of Mary of Jerusalem, the missionary assistant whose departure from the
first journey later precipitates the [[paul-apostle|Paul]]–[[barnabas|Barnabas]] split, and,
by later tradition, the author of the Gospel of [[mark|Mark]]. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] notes
that he is called John (a Jewish name), Mark (a Roman name), or John Mark.

## Biographical Overview

John Mark is the son of Mary of Jerusalem (a distinct figure from [[mary|Mary the mother of
Jesus]]), whose house — where believers gather to pray during [[peter-apostle|Peter]]'s
imprisonment (Acts 12:12) — serves as a house-church.
Bock describes this Mary as "a woman of some means," likely a widow, and notes that the setting
"confirms Acts 4 giving was voluntary." Mark is a relative of Barnabas (Col. 4:10, outside
the digest scope) and, on Bock's account of Acts, is recruited into the missionary work
before withdrawing partway through the first journey.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 12:12** — Peter, freed from prison, goes to "the house of Mary, the mother of John
  Mark," where the church is praying.
- **Acts 12:25** — Barnabas and Saul return, "bringing with them John, the one called Mark."
- **Acts 13:5** — serves as assistant on the first missionary journey.
- **Acts 13:13** — leaves the party early, departing back to Jerusalem.
- **Acts 15:37–39** — his readmission is the point of the sharp disagreement that splits
  Paul and Barnabas, who takes Mark and sails to Cyprus.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock reads Acts 12:25 as the seam that reconnects the Pauline strand of the narrative (Saul
last placed at Tarsus, 9:30, and fetched to Antioch, 11:25) with the coming first mission,
introducing Mark as the assistant who will join it. On the traditional authorship of the
Gospel, Bock's discussion here is confined to Mark's role in Acts; the identification of
this John Mark as the evangelist behind the Gospel of Mark is recorded as a **reception
claim** in the tradition rather than a datum of the Acts text.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Mark functions as a narrative pivot: his household is the gathering-place of the praying
church at the height of Herodian persecution (Acts 12), and his later defection becomes the
occasion by which the single Barnabas–Saul team divides into two mission efforts. Bock's
treatment keeps the focus on his connective role between the Jerusalem and Antioch phases
of the story.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The identification of this John Mark with the author of the second Gospel is a
long-standing **traditional attribution**, held in the church's reception history rather
than asserted by Acts itself. The digest scope (Acts 9:32–12:25) does not treat the
Gospel-authorship question directly; the claim is noted here as attributed tradition, not
established in wiki voice.

## In 1 Peter 5:13, on Jobes’s reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|Jobes, *1 Peter* (BECNT)]]. Claims are Jobes’s.*

At the letter’s close, greetings come from “she in Babylon chosen with you and **Mark, my son**”
(5:13). [[karen-jobes|Jobes]] takes Mark as **most likely John Mark** (Acts 12:12, 25; 15:37, 39) —
not Peter’s literal son. The traditional identification of Mark as Peter’s **interpreter**
(Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 3.39.15) mitigates the objection that pairing “the church and Mark, my son”
is harder than “my wife and Mark, my son” (against Bigg’s wife theory for the feminine *hē en
Babylōni*). Jobes also notes that Peter, Mark, and Silvanus share two plausible lifetime windows
together (early 50s after the Jerusalem Council; early–mid 60s in Rome). Indirect Markan associations
with Isa 53 are “what might be expected if mediated by Peter,” the only NT writer who (on her
account) explicitly and extensively identifies Jesus with the Suffering Servant (see
[[servant-songs|Servant Songs]]).
