Naomi is the widow of Elimelech who loses husband and both sons in Moab, returns empty to Bethlehem, and is restored through the *ḥesed* of her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth and the redemption of Boaz, becoming the nurse of Obed and the one whose name is "established" in Israel.

## Biographical Overview

Wife of Elimelech of Bethlehem-judah, mother of Mahlon and Chilion. The family migrates to Moab because of famine; Elimelech dies, then the two sons marry Moabite women (Orpah and Ruth) and also die, leaving Naomi with two daughters-in-law and no male heir or provider. Hearing that YHWH has visited his people with food, she sets out to return to Bethlehem. She urges both daughters-in-law to turn back ("Go, return each of you to her mother's house. May the LORD deal kindly with you..."). Orpah kisses her and returns; Ruth clings and makes the famous vow of loyalty. Naomi arrives in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest and tells the townswomen, "Do not call me Naomi ['pleasant']; call me Mara ['bitter'], for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty" (1:20–21). She accepts Ruth's plan to glean and later initiates the threshing-floor scheme for permanent security ("My daughter, should I not seek rest for you, that it may be well with you?" 3:1). After Boaz redeems and marries Ruth, Naomi receives the child Obed; the women of the neighborhood say to her, "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer, and may his name be renowned in Israel! He shall be to you a restorer of life and a nourisher of your old age, for your daughter-in-law who loves you, who is more to you than seven sons, has given birth to him" (4:14–15). Naomi takes the child to her breast and becomes his nurse. The women name him Obed ("servant/worshiper"); he is the father of Jesse, father of [[david|David]].

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Ruth 1**: Loss in Moab; decision to return; dialogue with Orpah and Ruth; arrival and lament in Bethlehem ("Mara").
- **Ruth 2**: Accepts Ruth's gleaning plan; recognizes Boaz as kinsman-redeemer after the field encounter; debrief with Ruth.
- **Ruth 3**: Initiates the threshing-floor plan for Ruth's "rest"; debrief after Ruth returns with grain and promise.
- **Ruth 4**: Receives the child; the women's chorus of blessing and restoration; takes Obed as nurse; the genealogy implies the continuation of her line through Ruth.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Naomi is the mother-in-law who receives the ultimate *ḥesed* from a foreign daughter-in-law. The book is read as a model of loyalty across boundaries and of divine reversal of emptiness to fullness. Rabbinic tradition sometimes emphasizes Naomi's wisdom in guiding Ruth and the reward of her suffering. The name change (Naomi/Mara) and restoration are noted as paradigms of lament and reversal.

**Christianity**: Naomi embodies honest lament ("the Almighty has dealt very bitterly") alongside quiet faith that moves to action. She is the one restored by the loyalty of an outsider; the women's words to her ("restorer of life," "more to you than seven sons") highlight the reversal. In typological readings she can stand for Israel or the synagogue that receives blessing through the Gentile (Ruth/Church). The story of the bitter widow restored is a pastoral text for those who have lost everything. The emphasis on Ruth's love for *Naomi* (not just for Boaz or for God) is a counter to readings that instrumentalize the foreign woman.

**Critical and feminist**: Naomi is read as a complex figure — initially urging the daughters-in-law to leave (pragmatic despair), later strategizing for their future, ultimately the beneficiary of Ruth's initiative. Some see her as complicit in Ruth's risk on the threshing floor; others as the wise elder who knows the customs. The focus on her restoration and the women's chorus has been reclaimed as female community and agency in a patriarchal world. Postcolonial readings note the power dynamics of the returning migrant widow and the foreign daughter-in-law who becomes the means of her survival and honor.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Naomi's arc is the book's emotional and theological center: from "full" to "empty" to "restored." Her lament is the most direct theological speech in the book ("the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me"); the narrative never corrects or rebukes it, yet the story answers it with fullness. She moves from passive grief to active planning (gleaning, threshing floor), modeling that faith can include honest complaint and strategic action. Theologically the story shows that YHWH's *ḥesed* can work through the loyalty of a Moabite and the public justice of a Bethlehemite redeemer to restore what was lost — name, land, future, and even the nursing of a child in old age. Naomi's restoration is not private; the townswomen witness and celebrate it. Her story answers the crisis of the judges period (loss of name, land, and leadership) at the most intimate level: a widow's household is redeemed and the line that will produce David begins.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Legal and social custom**: The precise nature of Naomi's "rest" and the threshing-floor plan (levirate, redemption, or a combination) is debated; whether Naomi had land rights that Boaz redeems is not explicit.
- **Naomi's agency vs. Ruth's**: Some readings see Naomi as the primary strategist; others emphasize Ruth's initiative and Naomi as the beneficiary. The text gives both significant roles.
- **The name "Mara"**: Is it a formal name change or a bitter cry? The narrative continues to call her Naomi; the women never adopt "Mara."
- **The child as "redeemer" for Naomi**: The legal and emotional claim that Obed restores Naomi's life and name is celebrated by the women; modern readers note the surrogate-mother dynamics.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Lau and others highlight Naomi's restoration as the goal of the *ḥesed* chain: Ruth's loyalty to Naomi, Boaz's loyalty to the family name and to Ruth, and the town's recognition that YHWH has not left Naomi without a redeemer. The story is a pastoral and ethical classic for widows, migrants, and those who have experienced loss of "fullness." In Jewish liturgy the book as a whole (read on Shavuot) celebrates the loyalty that brings blessing; Naomi's arc is part of that. Christian readings often pair her lament with the reversal as a pattern of honest prayer and providential answer. Feminist and womanist interpreters celebrate the female solidarity (Naomi-Ruth-townswomen) and the power of women's words to name reality and to bless. The figure remains central to discussions of *ḥesed* ethics, the inclusion of outsiders, and the quiet ways God restores what seems irretrievably lost.
