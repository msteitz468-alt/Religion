# Krishna

**Krishna** is among the most beloved and theologically central figures of [[hinduism|Hinduism]] — the
eighth **avatar** (descent) of Vishnu and, for [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vaishnava]] traditions, the
supreme God in fully personal form.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]]** — as Arjuna's charioteer, Krishna delivers the Gita's teaching and
  reveals his cosmic form (ch. 11); the source of his role as supreme teacher.
- **Mahabharata** — the master statesman and divine guide of the Pandavas.
- **Bhagavata Purana / Harivamsa** — the cycle of his birth, the child who steals butter, the
  flute-playing cowherd of Vrindavan, and his love-play (*rasa-lila*) with the *gopis* — the heart of
  Krishna *bhakti*.

## Theological Significance

Krishna embodies the Hindu doctrine of **avatara** — God descending in form "whenever dharma declines"
(Gita 4.7–8) — and anchors the **bhakti** path of loving devotion. In the Vedanta debate his Gita
self-disclosures are claimed by every school: [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] hears non-dual identity
([[shankara|Śaṅkara]] reads "the Self neither slays nor is slain," 2.19, as Krishna teaching the
**actionless Self**), [[vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Vishishtadvaita]] and [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]] hear a
personal God really distinct from the devotee, and [[kashmir-shaivism|Kashmir Shaivism]]
([[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]]) hears the voice of consciousness (Śiva) calling Arjuna to **recognize
his own identity with the Lord** ([[pratyabhijna|pratyabhijñā]]).

## Reception and Controversies

The relation of the philosopher-statesman Krishna of the Mahabharata to the pastoral, erotic Krishna of
the Puranas is debated (one figure or a fusion?). His historicity is treated as legendary. In modern
devotion (and globally via ISKCON) he is worshipped as the supreme personality of Godhead.

## Doniger's Reading (Modern Critical — *The Hindus*, Chs. 10–11)

**Death of Krishna.** The Mahābhārata narrates Krishna's death with three overlapping stories: (1) a
mortal man wounded by a hunter's arrow; (2) death by old age; (3) a god who voluntarily withdraws
from the world by entering terminal yoga. When the hunter "Old Age" (Jara) mistakes the meditating
Krishna for a deer and shoots him through the sole of his foot, three stories are "told here at once"
— mortality, karma, and voluntary divine exit — "Are traces of one story left ghostlike in another?"

**Earliest external evidence (Heliodorus Pillar, 115 BCE).** Heliodorus of Taxila — a Greek, the son
of a Greek man of Taxila, serving as Greek ambassador to the Śuṅga court — erected a pillar at
Besnagar (near the Buddhist stupas at Sāñcī) topped with Viṣṇu's eagle (Garuḍa) and declared himself
a **Bhāgavata** (devotee of Bhagavān = Kṛṣṇa/Viṣṇu). This is "significant evidence of the conversion
of a non-Indian not to Buddhism but to a new form of Hinduism" — the devotional Vaiṣṇava tradition
already global by the 2nd century BCE.

**Rise of sectarian Hinduism.** Doniger places Krishna's emergence into Sanskrit texts in the context
of the gradual eclipse of the Vedic gods (Indra, Soma, Agni) and the rise of the sectarian gods Viṣṇu
and Śiva: "In 115 BCE, Heliodorus… set up a pillar in Besnagar; Patañjali [5th c. BCE] mentions a
devotee of the son of Vasudeva [Krishna]; these are the early stirrings of communal sects beginning
to supplement, sometimes to replace, the royal and domestic worship of the Vedic gods."

## The Puranic Childhood: Harivamsha and the Bhagavata (c. 450–950 CE)

The Mahābhārata knows Krishna only as an adult. The **Harivamsha** (c. 450 CE), the appendix to
the Mahābhārata, gave him a childhood — derived, Doniger argues, from popular folk stories about a
cowherd village boy, far from the princely warrior of the epic. The Harivamsha joined these two
mythologies with a third: the **family romance** (a term from Freud) — the myth of a child of noble
blood raised among herders until he discovers his real parents. The result is a "doubly doubled" god:
deity pretending to be a prince pretending to be a cowherd.

**Birth of Krishna** (Harivamsha): Kamsa, Krishna's maternal uncle, was told by a prophecy that
the eighth child of his cousin Devaki and Vasudeva would kill him. He killed seven infants; Vishnu
placed himself in the eighth embryo. Simultaneously, the goddess of Sleep (Kali) was born to the
cowherd woman Yashoda. Vasudeva carried infant Krishna to Yashoda and brought Yashoda's daughter
to Devaki; Kamsa dashed the daughter against the floor, but she flew to heaven and became the
eternal goddess Kali (worshiped with animal sacrifice, "for she is fond of flesh"). Krishna grew up
in the cowherd village and killed Kamsa when he came of age.

**Yashoda and the Bhagavata Purana** (c. 950 CE): The tenth book of the Bhagavata develops
Yashoda as a major figure — along with the other cowherd women (**Gopis**) who become Krishna's
foster mothers and cluster around him in adoration. He kills the ogress Putana (who tries to
poison him with her milk but is killed when he sucks out her life's breath), and her body gives off
sweet perfume when burned, "purified by her death at his hands." Yashoda looks into the toddler
Krishna's open mouth and sees the entire universe and herself — a vision of the divine that she
cannot bear. Krishna grants her the boon of forgetting, in infinite compassion.

**The Gopis as lovers**: The Puranas depict the grown Krishna dancing the great circle dance
(*rasa-lila*) with the Gopis in the moonlight — multiplying himself so each Gopi thinks Krishna is
with her alone. The Gopis likewise leave shadow images of themselves in bed with their husbands.
The texts blur the distinction between Gopis as mothers (*vatsalya*, "calf love") and as lovers
(*madhurya*, "honey-sweet love") — "the astounding Hindu ability to combine ideas that other
religions might feel constrained to choose between."

## Radha and the Gita Govinda (c. 1179–1209 CE)

In later Puranas, one Gopi becomes dominant: **Radha**, nearly unknown in Sanskrit mythology
before the seventh century, but from the twelfth century onward the paradigmatic lover of Krishna.
The **Gita Govinda** ("Song of [Krishna] the Cowherd"), by the Bengali poet **Jayadeva**
(court poet of King Laksmanasena of Bengal, c. 1179–1209), is the foundational Sanskrit text of
the Radha-Krishna relationship. Jayadeva's Radha is powerful: "Krishna bends down before her and
puts her feet on his head." The romance of the two adulterous lovers may owe something to Persian
Sufi romances becoming known in India through the Muslim presence at the time.

In the Bhagavata, the Gopis' longing during Krishna's absence is the great theme of *viraha* —
love-in-separation, the paradigmatic bhakti emotion. In later (Tantric-influenced) Puranas, the
worshiper — male or female — identifies specifically with Radha, the one who longs for the absent
god. In the **Brahmavaivarta Purana** (Bengal, c. 15th–16th c.), the grown Radha is put in charge
of the infant Krishna; suddenly he transforms into a gorgeous young man; they make love for days —
then he turns back into a wet, demanding infant.

## The Shadow Sita Is Draupadi (Brahmavaivarta Purana)

In a remarkable intertextual myth (Brahmavaivarta Purana), the **shadow Sita** (*maya-sita*) of
the Ramayana — the false Sita substituted before Ravana's abduction — is reborn as Draupadi.
Fire tells the shadow Sita to go to Pushkar, generate inner heat, and she is reborn; she asks Shiva
for a husband five times over in excitement, and is given five husbands, the five Pandavas. "Fire
gives the shadow Sita a sexual future as Draupadi, who, like the shadow Sita, is born of fire" —
their shared theme of fire-birth attracting Draupadi's story into Sita's. (See also [[sita|Sītā]]
and [[draupadi|Draupadī]].)

## Bengal Vaishnavism: Chaitanya and the Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa Tradition (16th c. CE onward)

In 16th-century Bengal, [[chaitanya|Chaitanya]] (1486–1533) declared himself an **incarnation of
both Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā in one body** — at last allowing the two divine lovers to simultaneously
experience both sides of their union. This gave rise to **Gaudiya Vaishnavism**, the most globally
influential modern form of Krishna devotion.

**Chandidas** (14th c.): Bengali poet who wrote of a married Radha dragged by Krishna's "fatal
flute" away from her husband — "A devoted wife forgets her spouse / To be drawn like a deer,
thirsty and lost." He was a Brahmin who openly declared love for a low-caste washerwoman (Rami),
was dismissed from his position, and was said to have died in protest.

**The svakīyā/parakīyā controversy**: Is Rādhā Krishna's wife (his own) or his mistress (someone
else's)? The Bengal Vaishnava community decided in **1717** that adulterous love (*parakīyā*) is
orthodox — transgression intensifies the love that models the soul's relationship to God.

**The Radhavallabhas** held that Kṛṣṇa was not the supreme deity but the servant of Rādhā — a
remarkable inversion of the standard Vaishnava hierarchy.

In Bengal Vaishnavism, the devotee chooses which *rasa* (relational register) to inhabit with
Kṛṣṇa: mother, lover, servant, or friend. Women — who can be god's lovers and mothers, the most
intimate roles — were considered naturally better suited to bhakti. Male devotees who identified
with the lover role sometimes adopted female dress and simulated menstruation monthly.

## Tulsidas's Treatment: The Happy Ending (c. 1574 CE)

[[tulsidas|Tulsidas]] in the *Ramcaritmanas* addressed the theological problem of Kṛṣṇa's
counterpart (Rāma) by using the **shadow Sita** device: only the illusory Sita endured the fire
ordeal; the real Sita returned from the fire unharmed; Tulsidas omits the Uttarakanda entirely —
his poem ends with Rāma and Sita together, happily ever after. This is not about Kṛṣṇa directly
but shows the same **Vedantic illusionism** being applied across the two great Vaishnava deities
to solve the problem of divine injustice toward devotees.
