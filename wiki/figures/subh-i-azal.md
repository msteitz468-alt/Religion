# Subh-i-Azal (Mírzá Yahyá)

*(PA: "Morn of Eternity"; 1831/32–1912)*

Religious title of Mírzá Yahyá, a younger half-brother of Bahá'u'lláh, son of Mírzá 'Abbás by Kúchik Khánum, one of his concubines (line 4361–4363). He was raised by Bahá'u'lláh following their father's death in 1839 (lines 4366–4368). He leads the [[azalis|Azali]] faction of Bábíism.

## Status within the Bábí Community (Contested)

The Báb bestowed various exalted titles on Azal — "Everlasting Mirror" (*Mir'átu'l-azaliyya*), "Name of Eternity" (*Ismu'l-azal*), and "Fruit of the Bayán" — and in about 1849, despite Azal's youth (then about seventeen), appointed him to "preserve what hath been revealed in the Bayán," calling him "a Great Way of Truth" (lines 4372–4382). 'Abdu'l-Bahá later regarded this appointment as a stratagem to divert attention away from Bahá'u'lláh (lines 4384–4386).

His exact status within the Bábí community following the Báb's execution in 1850 is described as vague, with strong centrifugal forces threatening to tear the movement apart. At some point he became the "recognized chief of the community," but his headship appears to have been to little effect (lines 4390–4397).

## Conflict with Bahá'u'lláh

Azal accompanied Bahá'u'lláh to Baghdad, maintaining a semi-secret existence and secluding himself from most Bábís (lines 4406–4408). In 1856 he ordered the killing of Dayyan, perhaps the most important of those claiming leadership of the fragmented Bábí remnant (lines 4410–4412). Bahá'í sources accuse him of increasing jealousy towards Bahá'u'lláh and of eventually trying to poison him in Edirne (lines 4414–4420).

A definitive separation then occurred, and Bahá'u'lláh made open claim to be He Whom God Shall Make Manifest. The great majority of Bábís eventually became his followers as Bahá'ís, while a small minority continued to acknowledge Azal as their leader (the Azalis) (lines 4421–4428).

Accusations that Azal and Sayyid Muhammad Isfahání made to the Ottoman authorities against Bahá'u'lláh led to a further order of exile: Bahá'u'lláh and most of his companions were sent to Akka, whilst Azal and a few others were sent to Famagusta in Cyprus (lines 4430–4438). His order of exile ended in 1881 when the British acquired Cyprus; he remained on the island as a British pensioner until his death on 29 April 1912 (lines 4438–4441).

## Character and Legacy

Although he continued to write a great deal, Azal was reclusive and does not appear to have maintained close contact with his followers in Iran. In Cyprus he came to be regarded as a Muslim holy man, receiving a Muslim burial, and his grave was regarded as a Muslim shrine (lines 4442–4448). He was never one of the Letters of the Living, contrary to the supposition of E. G. Browne (lines 4449–4452).

## Family

Azal had many wives (sources differ: eleven, twelve, fourteen, or seventeen), including four married in Iran and at least five in Baghdad. He briefly married the second wife of the Báb. There were perhaps twenty-five children in all. Some descendants remained Azalis, a few established contact with Bahá'ís, and some became Christians; in Cyprus, most seem eventually to have become de facto Muslims (lines 4460–4473).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Variant Azali and Bahá'í accounts make it difficult to reconstruct an "objective" account of the dispute between the two groups (line 4453–4458). The exact nature of the Báb's appointment of Azal, and whether it constituted a clear succession, remains contested between the two traditions.
