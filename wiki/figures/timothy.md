# Timothy

## Biographical Overview
A native of Lystra, son of a Jewish mother (Eunice) and Greek father. He joined Paul during the second missionary journey. To avoid offending Jews, Paul circumcised him (Acts 16:1-3). He became Paul's most trusted emissary, sent to churches in Thessalonica, Corinth, and Philippi. Traditional sources suggest he later became bishop of Ephesus. From Bock (Acts) and Yarbrough (Pastorals), Timothy is the faithful delegate who shares the risks of the mission.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Acts**: Describes his joining of Paul's mission and travels.
- **Pauline Epistles**: Frequently listed as a co-sender (e.g., 1-2 Thess, 2 Cor, Phil, Col, Philem). Paul sends him on crucial pastoral missions (1 Thess 3:2; 1 Cor 4:17; Phil 2:19).
- **1-2 Timothy**: The Pastoral Epistles are addressed to him, offering instructions on church governance and combating false teaching.
- **Hebrews**: Mentions his release from prison (Heb 13:23); see Cockerill densification below.

## Cockerill (NICNT) — Hebrews 13:23 (2026-07-18)

Attributed to [[gareth-lee-cockerill|Gareth Lee Cockerill]] ([[cockerill-hebrews-nicnt|Hebrews NICNT]]). Digest R12 (ch. 13 peroration).

At 13:23 the pastor writes: “Know that our brother Timothy has been released, with whom, if he comes soon, I will see you.” Cockerill holds it is **probable** that this is Timothy the companion of Paul. Emphasis falls on “**our brother**” (placed before the name). Crucial authorial claim: **the pastor does not claim to be Paul**. By calling Timothy “our brother” and hoping to see the hearers with Timothy, he identifies himself with the **network of people close to Paul**. That association strengthens his authority as a valid teacher of the “great salvation” (2:3) without a Pauline authorship claim (R12 L4366). Reference to Timothy’s “release” also suggests Timothy may have been imprisoned for his faith (cross-linked with the prayer/conscience unit at 13:18–19) (R12 L4338–4342).

## Hansen (PNTC) — Philippians 2:19–24 (2026-07-18)

Attributed to [[g-walter-hansen|Hansen]] ([[hansen-philippians-pntc]]). Under “Recommendations of Christ-like Servants,” Timothy is the rare co-worker of **equal soul** (*isopsychos*) with **genuine concern** for the Philippians’ welfare, not self-interest — a living contrast to those who seek their own (2:21). Paul hopes to send him soon and to come himself; Timothy is “like a son with a father” in the work of the gospel. Hansen pairs him with [[epaphroditus|Epaphroditus]] as concrete models of the humility and service the [[christ-hymn|Christ hymn]] and 2:1–5 demand. Timothy is also named with Paul in the prescript (1:1) as fellow *servant*, modeling shared servant identity against rivalry.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Timothy exemplifies the faithful apostolic delegate and the passing of the tradition to the next generation of church leadership. In the Thessalonian narrative, [[jeffrey-weima|Jeffrey A. D. Weima]] (*[[weima-1-2-thessalonians-becnt|1–2 Thessalonians, BECNT]]*) treats Timothy as **cosender** of both letters (1 Thess 1:1; 2 Thess 1:1) and as the **emissary** who carries Paul's "apostolic parousia" when Paul cannot return: at 1 Thess 3:2 Paul titles him "brother and coworker of God in the gospel of Christ" (Weima prefers *synergon tou theou* as the hardest reading), stressing authoritative proxy status rather than a mere messenger. Weima argues Timothy was eligible to re-enter Thessalonica because, unlike Paul and [[silas|Silas]], he was not implicated in the politarchs' security arrangement; his report of the church's faith and love (3:6) answered both absence-anxiety and persecution-anxiety and occasioned 1 Thessalonians from Corinth. In Acts (Bock), he is part of the team God uses to plant churches; in the Pastorals (Yarbrough), he is the seasoned veteran entrusted with guarding the deposit.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
The main controversy involves the Pastoral Epistles (1-2 Timothy). Critical scholars argue these are pseudepigraphical works written in Paul's name to a later, idealized Timothy figure to address 2nd-century church structures and proto-Gnosticism, whereas traditionalists maintain they were genuinely written to the historical Timothy by Paul. Yarbrough defends the historical Timothy's extensive experience and "risky service."

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
In commentary traditions regarding church order and pastoral ministry, Timothy is viewed as the prototypical Christian minister or bishop, tasked with guarding the deposit of faith and maintaining congregational discipline.

**Robert W. Yarbrough** underscores the magnitude of Timothy's front-line experience, noting his "dual citizenship" in both the Hellenistic world and the Jewish Diaspora uniquely equipped him to mediate the gospel. Yarbrough vigorously pushes back against readings that characterize Timothy as merely timid or fearful in the Pastoral Epistles; he points out Timothy's repeated willingness to render "risky service in the trenches" (such as remaining in hostile Berea). Yarbrough concludes that few early church figures were as privy to Paul's movements and teachings over the years—even compared to Luke, who functioned more as a chronicler and physician, whereas Timothy actively shared the pastoral mandate and its attendant dangers.

From Bock (Acts, this batch): Timothy's selection and role illustrate the collaborative mission and the trust placed in the next generation.

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names.*

Timothy joins Paul and [[silas|Silas]] at **Lystra** at the start of the second journey (16:1–3),
and Bock treats his **circumcision** as the key Acts text for the law question. Because Timothy has a
Jewish mother (Eunice) and a Greek father, Paul circumcises him "out of sensitivity to the Jews" so
"there will be no side debates about Timothy when they share the gospel." Bock stresses that the
issue is **mixed Jewish-Gentile ancestry, not Gentile inclusion** — a categorically different case
from the unmixed Gentile of Acts 15, and the deliberate contrast with [[titus|Titus]] (an unmixed
Greek who is pointedly *not* circumcised, Gal. 2:3–5). Paul thereby acts in line with the Council's
"attitude of sensitivity" (1 Cor. 9:19–23), and Bock reads the move as "affirming Jewish heritage
alongside Gentile heritage," not "standing up for the law." On the disputed matrilineal principle,
Bock sides with Witherington against Cohen: an uncircumcised son of a Jewish mother "was regarded in
Judaism as an apostate Jew," so the circumcision removes a genuine obstacle. Bock draws the leadership
lesson — "knowing which principles are worth standing up for and which… are not worth elevating… is
a sign of discernment." Timothy then travels as a coworker through the journeys (delivering the
Jerusalem decrees, 16:4) and is sent ahead with Erastus to Macedonia during the Ephesus ministry
(19:22).
