# Nathan

**Nathan** is the court prophet of [[david|David]] and the prophetic voice through whom
three of the pivotal moments of the David story are mediated: the **Davidic Covenant**
(2 Samuel 7), the **confrontation over [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] and [[uriah|Uriah]]** (2
Samuel 12), and the **securing of [[solomon|Solomon's]] succession** (1 Kings 1). He is the
paradigm of the prophet who both legitimates the king and stands in judgment over him.

## Biographical Overview

Nathan appears only as a figure of the royal court in Jerusalem; nothing is recorded of his
origins. He functions as David's resident prophet — the conduit of the divine word into the
machinery of the monarchy — and disappears from the narrative after Solomon's accession.
The Chronicler names a "book of Nathan the prophet" among sources for the reigns of David
and Solomon (1 Chr 29:29; 2 Chr 9:29).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Samuel 7 (the [[davidic-covenant|Davidic Covenant]])** — David proposes to build a
  house (temple) for YHWH; Nathan at first approves, then receives a night oracle reversing
  the plan: David will not build God a house; rather YHWH will build David a "house"
  (dynasty), establishing his throne forever. The foundational text of messianic dynastic
  theology.
- **2 Samuel 12 (the parable and the curse)** — After David's adultery and the murder of
  Uriah, Nathan tells the parable of the **poor man's ewe lamb**, drawing David to condemn
  himself ("the man who has done this deserves to die"), then turns it: **"You are the
  man."** He pronounces the curse on the house of David — "the sword shall never depart from
  your house" — and the death of the child. David's confession ("I have sinned against the
  LORD") is traditionally tied to [[psalms|Psalm 51]].
- **1 Kings 1 (the succession)** — With David senile and Adonijah self-crowning, Nathan and
  Bathsheba coordinate to extract David's confirmation of Solomon as successor and to stage
  Solomon's anointing.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Nathan embodies the principle, central to the [[deuteronomistic-history|Former Prophets]],
that **kingship in Israel is subordinate to the prophetic word**: the same prophet who
delivers the unconditional dynastic promise also delivers the conditional moral judgment.
The "you are the man" scene is the classic biblical instance of prophetic *parrhesia* —
truth spoken to power — and the ewe-lamb parable a model of indirection that traps the
hearer into pronouncing his own verdict.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Robert Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) reads Nathan as a
consummate political and rhetorical operator. The ewe-lamb parable is a calculated juridical
trap; and in 1 Kings 1 Nathan and Bathsheba's tightly coordinated approach to the dying king
— each supplying what the other's speech omits — exemplifies the narrative's mastery of the
"contrastive treatment of two interlocutors" in a single scene. Whether David ever actually
promised the throne to Solomon, or whether Nathan and Bathsheba together *persuade* the
befogged old king that he did, the text leaves pointedly open.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Judaism** — Nathan is counted among the canonical prophets; rabbinic tradition treats
  the "you are the man" rebuke as the model of licit reproof (*tochecha*) and reads David's
  immediate repentance as exemplary.
- **Christianity** — Nathan's oracle of 2 Samuel 7 is read christologically as a promise
  fulfilled in Jesus, the son of David whose kingdom has no end (cf. Luke 1:32–33). A
  distinct **Nathan son of David** (not the prophet) appears in Luke's genealogy (Luke
  3:31).

## Distinguish

Not to be confused with **Nathan son of David** (a son born in Jerusalem, 2 Sam 5:14,
ancestor in Luke's genealogy), nor with the modern scholar [[nathan-wolski|Nathan Wolski]].
