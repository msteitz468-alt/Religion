# Gregory the Great

Pope Gregory I (590–604), the figure with whom [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Pelikan]] closes Volume I — "the last of the church fathers and the first of the popes," and "the significant turning point for the papacy, not only jurisdictionally but also theologically." A monk before his pontificate, he consolidated [[augustine|Augustine's]] legacy into the form in which the Latin Middle Ages received it. Drawn from Pelikan, Vol. I, ch. 7.

## An Augustinian Traditionalist

Gregory called his own writings "chaff" beside the "wheat" of Augustine — "perhaps there has never been an author who owed more to the writings of another." His achievement was less innovation than **definitization**: doctrines Augustine had advanced "tentatively and in passing" Gregory made "something that has to be believed" (*credendus*).
- **[[purgatory|Purgatory]]:** Augustine's tentative belief in purifying punishments after death and the value of prayer for some of the dead became, in Gregory, a matter that "has to be believed."
- **The [[eucharist|sacrifice of the Mass]]:** Gregory stated the sacrificial interpretation "with new definiteness and detail" — "at the very hour of the immolation… the heavens are opened and the choirs of angels are present" — while remaining deliberately vague (repeating Augustinian formulas) on the *mode* of the real presence.

## Conciliar and Papal Authority

Gregory drew the famous parallel: "all the four synods of the holy universal church we receive as we do the four books of the holy Gospels" (Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, Chalcedon) — one and the same revealed truth in both. Yet he qualified [[criteria-of-apostolic-continuity|conciliar authority]] from above: "without the authority and the consent of the apostolic see, none of the matters transacted have any binding force." On the Petrine texts (Matt. 16:18; John 21:17; Luke 22:31) he taught that Peter "holds principality in the church," primacy having "moved from the capital city of the old Israel to the capital city of the world." Pelikan: "for a thousand years after Gregory," the teaching authority of the bishop of Rome was "the fundamental assumption" of Western theology — though the doctrine "was not to achieve complete definition as a dogma until many centuries later."
