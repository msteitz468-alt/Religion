# Beatrice

The woman Dante called Beatrice (identified with Bice Portinari, d. 1290), whom
he first met in 1274; the central beloved of the *Vita nuova* and the
[[divine-comedy|*Commedia*]]. In the poem she is both a real, mourned individual
and an allegorical figure of **revelation and divine grace**.

## In the Divine Comedy

It is Beatrice who, from Heaven, sets the journey in motion (sending
[[virgil|Virgil]] to rescue the lost Dante). She arrives at the summit of
[[purgatorio|Purgatory]] (Purgatorio 30) as Virgil disappears — a moment of
"revelation or conversion." Over two cantos (Purg. 30–31) she lays bare Dante's
sins since her death, chiefly his failure to "rejoice in the existence that she
as God's creature had been granted" and to recognize that God, the "joyous
Maker," destines human beings to happiness. Reduced to an infant's sobs, Dante
cannot even speak the word "yes"; Beatrice reads it on his lips.

She then guides him through [[paradiso|Paradise]], superior to Virgil because
vision rises where language cannot, delivering (e.g. through Piccarda Donati in
Paradiso 3) key discussions of [[freedom|free will]] and divine harmony, before
yielding at the end to Saint Bernard for the final vision.

## Significance

Beatrice represents the joy of redeemed existence — virtue understood not as
constraint but as "a joyous exercise of all our faculties." Through her, Dante
claims to have raised the vernacular love-lyric tradition (the troubadours, the
*dolce stil novo*) to a theological level: the beloved becomes the figure who
points to God.
