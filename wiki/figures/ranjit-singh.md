# Maharaja Ranjit Singh

Founder and ruler of the **Sikh kingdom of the Punjab** (r. 1801–1839), who unified the Sikh *misls*
into a single state styling its government the **"Sarkar Khalsa."** His reign is the high-water mark of
Sikh temporal power between the close of the personal Guruship (1708) and the British annexation of the
Punjab (1849).

*(Brief scaffold from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]], 2026-06-27; this figure is slated for fuller treatment
in the Scope 2 ingest of Khushwant Singh's* A History of the Sikhs.)*

## Significance (from McLeod)

McLeod's *Sikhism* treats Ranjit Singh chiefly as **context** for religious developments: under his
rule the ascetic [[udasi|Udasi]] and [[nirmala|Nirmala]] orders prospered on land grants; and the
"military glories and economic opportunities" of his era are precisely what **Baba Dayal's
[[nirankari|Nirankari]]** and **Balak Singh / Ram Singh's [[namdhari|Namdhari]]** renewal movements
arose to counter, recalling Sikhs to [[nam-simaran|nām simaraṇ]]. Khalsa Sikhs of his kingdom were, in
McLeod's framing, "still members of Hindu society" before the Singh Sabha.

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Chs. 10–18)

Khushwant Singh gives the political narrative McLeod only gestures at.

**Rise and coronation.** Born 13 November 1780 into the **Sukerchakia [[misls|misl]]** (Naudh Singh →
Charhat Singh → Maha Singh → Ranjit), he lost the sight of his left eye to smallpox and inherited his
father's estates at twelve (1792), coming under the decisive influence of his mother-in-law **Sada Kaur**,
head of the Kanhaya misl, "who more than anyone else directed his unbounded energy towards unifying the
Punjab" (Singh, ch. 10). He captured [[lahore|Lahore]] on 7 July 1799 at the citizens' invitation, and on
12 April 1801 "Sahib Singh Bedi daubed Ranjit Singh's forehead with saffron paste and proclaimed him
Maharajah of the Punjab" (ch. 11). Singh stresses the source of the title: "it was given to him by that
mystic entity, the Paṅth Khālsājī. He acknowledged no earthly superior." He struck coin not in his own name
but in Guru Nanak's (*Nānak Śāhī*), styled his government the **Sarkār Khālsājī**, refused a throne, and was
addressed simply "Singh Sāhib."

**A Punjabi, not a Sikh, state.** Singh underlines that "he did not intend to set up a Sikh kingdom but a
Punjabi state in which Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs would be equal before the law" (ch. 11). He kept Muslim
*qādī* courts for the Shariat, participated in Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh festivals alike, and staffed the top
of his administration across communities — prime minister Dhian Singh (a Dogra Hindu), foreign minister
**Fakir Azizuddin** (Muslim), finance minister Dina Nath (Brahmin). His famous gloss on his blindness:
"God wanted me to look upon all religions with one eye, that is why he took away the light from the other"
(ch. 18). He took an hour of the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]] daily yet, Singh notes, was "full of
Brahmanical superstition," resented the anti-Muslim zeal of the [[nihang|Akalis]], and "never in his life
sentenced a man to death."

**Conquests.** He took [[amritsar|Amritsar]] (c. 1803), seizing the great gun Zam Zama and granting the
funds to rebuild the Harimandir "in marble and covered with gold leaf"; then Multan (finally 1818),
**Attock** (1813, "the first victory the Punjabis had ever won over the Afghans"), Kashmir (1819, after the
Battle of Shupaiyan), Peshawar (1819 — "For the first time in eight hundred years the city saw an Indian
conqueror ride through its streets"), and Mankera (1821). He extracted the **Koh-i-noor** from Shah Shuja in
1813. By his death the state ran to over 200,000 square miles with revenue above Rs 3 crore.

**The British and the Sutlej.** Blocked from expanding east of the Sutlej, Ranjit accepted the river as his
boundary in the **[[treaty-of-lahore-1809|Treaty of Amritsar (1809)]]** negotiated with Charles Metcalfe —
"a grievous blow to the Punjabi dream of a unified Punjab," cutting off the cis-Sutlej (Malwa) chiefs under
British protection. He renounced ambitions on Sindh and the sea in the commercial treaty of 1832, and joined
the 1838 Tripartite Treaty to restore Shah Shuja.

**The Europeanized army.** From 1822 the Napoleonic veterans **Jean-Baptiste Ventura** (infantry) and
**Jean-François Allard** (cavalry) trained the *Fauj-i-Khas* in Grande Armée methods, later joined by
Court, Avitabile, and others; Singh notes Ranjit required even his European officers to keep the Khalsa
outward norms (unshorn hair, no beef, no tobacco) and never trusted them — "German, French or English, all
these European bastards are alike."

**Death.** Paralysed in December 1838, he died on 27 June 1839; four ranis and seven slave girls burned on
his pyre, with Brahmins, Sikh granthis, and Muslims praying together. Emily Eden thought him "exactly like a
mouse with grey whiskers and one eye"; Singh's verdict rests on his own boast: "My kingdom… was small, it is
now large; it was scattered, broken, and divided; it is now consolidated."
