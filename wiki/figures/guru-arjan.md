## Biographical Overview

Guru Arjan was the fifth Guru of the Sikhs and is pivotal in Sikh history as the compiler of the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]] and the first martyr Guru. McLeod notes briefly that Arjan was responsible for "a formalized sacred scripture," building on the organizational work of his predecessor [[guru-ram-das|Guru Ram Das]] (McLeod ~L236–238). The masand system of deputies was expanded under Ram Das; Arjan formalized the scriptural canon.

## Compilation of the Adi Granth

McLeod's slice notes that the elder son of Gurditta (grandson of Arjan's brother's line), Dhir Mai, retained physical possession of the original Adi Granth manuscript. When Guru Gobind Singh, following the evacuation of Anandpur, requested the manuscript, Dhir Mai refused. "For this reason the Guru recorded not only large portions of the Dasam Granth but also (from memory) the complete text of the Adi Granth" at Damdama Sahib (McLeod ~L1025–1028). This is a reference back to Arjan's original compilation and its contested custody.

## Martyrdom

Guru Arjan was the first Guru to be martyred, a fact McLeod references when discussing Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution: "The Sikhs had received their second martyr Guru and the second one, like the first, was a victim of the vicious and heartless Mughals" (McLeod ~L377–379).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Arjan's martyrdom established the pattern of Mughal persecution that shapes Sikh historical consciousness. His compilation of the Adi Granth gave the Panth its fixed scriptural canon, making him indispensable to Sikh theological identity.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Adi Granth compiled under Arjan's direction became the eternal Guru after Guru Gobind Singh's death — the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]. Arjan's act of compilation is thus the foundation of the central Sikh doctrine of Guru Granth.

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Ch. 3)

Khushwant Singh supplies the detailed historical narrative. Arjan (fifth Guru, 1563–1606) completed the
**Harimandir** at [[amritsar|Amritsar]], inviting the Qadiri Sufi **Mian Mir** of [[lahore|Lahore]] to lay
its foundation stone, and building it low and open on four sides — "the teaching is for all the four castes."
He instituted the *dasvaṅdh* (tithe) and dug the tank at Taran Taran (1590).

**The compilation.** Singh frames the Granth's compilation as a response to a threat: Arjan's hostile elder
brother **Prithi Chand** had begun a spurious anthology, and "Arjun realized the danger of a spurious
scripture gaining currency." He gathered the hymns of the first three Gurus from Mohan (son of
[[guru-amar-das|Amar Das]]), welcomed compositions of Hindu and Muslim poet-saints, and made the selection
himself, "taken down by Bhai Gurdas" by the Ramsar tank. Akbar, hearing reports of anti-Islamic
content, had the work read to him and was satisfied, offering 51 gold mohurs. "In August 1604 the work was
completed and the Granth Sāhib… was formally installed in the temple at Amritsar. Bhai Buddha was appointed
the first reader or *graṅthī*" (Singh, ch. 3).

**The martyrdom.** Singh cites Jahangir's own diary (*Tuzuk-i-Jehāngīrī*), in which the emperor records a
long-standing resolve "to put an end to this false traffic, or [that] he should be brought into the fold of
Islam." The immediate occasion was Arjan's reception of the rebel prince Khusrau. Arrested, fined, and
refusing to pay or recant, Arjan was tortured at Lahore; the Hindu banker Chandu Shah and Naqshbandi Sufi
Shaikh Ahmed Sirhindi are named as pressures, though Singh notes "there is nothing contemporary on record" of
Chandu's personal vindictiveness. He died on **30 May 1606** when, allowed to bathe in the Ravi during the
torture, "the impact of the cold water proved too much for his fevered body, and the current bore him beyond
the reach of his tormentors." Singh's verdict: "Arjun's blood became the seed of the Sikh church as well as
of the Punjabi nation." (Compare McLeod above, who treats the martyrdom only in passing.)
