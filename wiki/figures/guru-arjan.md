## Biographical Overview

Guru Arjan was the fifth Guru of the Sikhs and is pivotal in Sikh history as the compiler of the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]] and the first martyr Guru. McLeod notes briefly that Arjan was responsible for "a formalized sacred scripture," building on the organizational work of his predecessor [[guru-ram-das|Guru Ram Das]] (McLeod ~L236–238). The masand system of deputies was expanded under Ram Das; Arjan formalized the scriptural canon.

## Compilation of the Adi Granth

McLeod's slice notes that the elder son of Gurditta (grandson of Arjan's brother's line), Dhir Mai, retained physical possession of the original Adi Granth manuscript. When Guru Gobind Singh, following the evacuation of Anandpur, requested the manuscript, Dhir Mai refused. "For this reason the Guru recorded not only large portions of the Dasam Granth but also (from memory) the complete text of the Adi Granth" at Damdama Sahib (McLeod ~L1025–1028). This is a reference back to Arjan's original compilation and its contested custody.

## Martyrdom

Guru Arjan was the first Guru to be martyred, a fact McLeod references when discussing Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution: "The Sikhs had received their second martyr Guru and the second one, like the first, was a victim of the vicious and heartless Mughals" (McLeod ~L377–379).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Arjan's martyrdom established the pattern of Mughal persecution that shapes Sikh historical consciousness. His compilation of the Adi Granth gave the Panth its fixed scriptural canon, making him indispensable to Sikh theological identity.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Adi Granth compiled under Arjan's direction became the eternal Guru after Guru Gobind Singh's death — the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]. Arjan's act of compilation is thus the foundation of the central Sikh doctrine of Guru Granth.
