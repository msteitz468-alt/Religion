**Biographical Overview**

Mani (c. 216–274/277 CE) was the third-century prophet, healer, painter, and court sage who
founded [[manichaeism|Manichaeism]], the dualist universal religion that spread from North
Africa to South China. He was born in **Sasanian Mesopotamia** (Babylonia) and raised from
about the age of four in a **Judaeo-Christian baptist community** — identified with the
**Elchasaites**, who practiced ritual ablutions and observed Jewish-Christian law. His father
**Pattikios** (Patticius) belonged to the same community.

According to the **Cologne Mani Codex** (a tiny Greek parchment biography, the single most
important source for his early life), Mani received his first revelation at about age twelve
and a decisive second one at **twenty-four** — dated to the year Ardashir conquered Hatra and
his son Shapur assumed the diadem. The revelation came from his heavenly **Twin** (the
*Syzygos*, Aramaic *al-Tawm*), a celestial counterpart who disclosed Mani's true identity and
mission and accompanied and reassured him thereafter. Breaking with the baptists — over their
washing rituals and their cultivation of the earth — Mani began a public mission, won the
favor of the **Sasanian court of Shapur I** (for whom he composed the Middle Persian
*Šābuhragān*), and dispatched missionaries westward into the Roman Empire and eastward toward
India. He was eventually imprisoned and died in chains under **Bahram I**, an event his
community commemorated as a Passion (the "Crucifixion of Mani").

**Primary Source Appearances**

- **The Cologne Mani Codex (CMC)** — Greek biography focused on his upbringing among the
  baptists and his break with them; the key witness to the Twin/Syzygos revelation.
- **The Kephalaia** ("Chapters") — Coptic compendia presenting Mani teaching his disciples.
- **The Coptic Manichaean Psalm-Book and Homilies** (Medinet Madi) — liturgical memory of
  Mani's death and exaltation.
- **Mani's own canonical writings** — the Living Gospel, Treasure of Life, Pragmateia, Book of
  Mysteries, Book of Giants, Letters, and Psalms (see [[manichaeism|Manichaeism]] for the
  canon).
- **Heresiological and Islamic accounts** — Augustine, the *Acta Archelai*, and later Arabic
  authors (al-Nadim's *Fihrist*, al-Biruni), which preserve much while caricaturing.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**

**Manichaean**: Mani is the **Paraclete** promised by Jesus (John 14–16), the **Apostle of
Jesus Christ**, and the *Seal of the Prophets* — the culminating figure in a prophetic line
running through Adam, Seth, Enoch, Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus. Uniquely, he wrote his
own scriptures so the revelation could not be corrupted, and he founded an organized church
with a canon, hierarchy (Elect and Hearers), liturgy, and art.

**Zoroastrian**: A dangerous heretic. The Zoroastrian priest **Kirdir** is associated with
the persecution that led to Mani's death; Sasanian orthodoxy treated Manichaeism as a
corruption of the Good Religion.

**Christian**: The arch-heresiarch. Augustine — a Manichaean
Hearer for nine years before his conversion — became Manichaeism's most formidable opponent,
and the Christian West remembered "Manichaean" as a byword for dualist heresy. The *Acta
Archelai* circulated a hostile legendary biography.

**Islamic**: Arabic scholars (Ibn al-Nadim, al-Biruni) preserved valuable, relatively
detailed accounts of Mani and his books; in Islamic usage *zindiq* ("heretic," "dualist")
came to be associated with Manichaeans.

**Chinese**: In the East, Mani was assimilated as a Buddha — "**the Buddha of Light**" — and
Manichaeism presented itself in Buddhist and Daoist dress.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**

Mani's significance is that of a **deliberate, self-conscious founder of a universal
religion** — arguably the first to design one as such, with a fixed scriptural canon, a
missionary program in multiple languages, and a synthesis of the Christian, Zoroastrian, and
Buddhist traditions he saw himself completing. His radical Light/Darkness
[[manichaeism|dualism]] became the foil against which orthodox Christian theology defined the
goodness of creation and evil as *privatio boni*, making Mani a permanent negative reference
point in Western [[theodicy|theodicy]] and the doctrine of [[satan|evil]].

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**

- **Chronology and the sources**: Iain Gardner's *The Founder of Manichaeism* (2020)
  re-examines Mani's biography in light of the modern discoveries (the CMC, the Coptic and
  Kellis papyri, the Iranian Turfan fragments), distinguishing how Mani was *remembered* by
  followers, *caricatured* by opponents, and *reinvented* by changing scholarly fashion.
- **The Elchasaite milieu**: The CMC's identification of Mani's baptist sect with the
  followers of Elchasai reframed him as emerging from within Jewish-Christian baptism rather
  than from "Iranian" religion — a major shift in how his roots are understood.
- **Dates of death**: 274 vs. 276/277 CE are both defended, turning on Sasanian regnal
  chronology.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**

Mani shaped later thought chiefly through the **anti-Manichaean** literature he provoked —
above all Augustine, whose entire account of will, evil, and creation bears the marks of his
nine years as a Hearer and his subsequent repudiation. In the modern study of Gnosticism,
Mani and Manichaeism are treated as the most fully developed Gnostic religion (Kurt Rudolph,
[[rudolph-gnosis-the-nature-and-history-of-gnosticism|*Gnosis*]]).
