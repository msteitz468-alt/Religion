# Srimad Rajacandra

**Śrīmad Rājacandra** (birth name: Raicāndbhāī Mahetā; 1867–1901) was a Gujarati Jain mystic, lay teacher, and spiritual correspondent whose writings — especially the verse treatise *Ātmasiddhi* ("Attainment of the Soul") — exerted a significant influence on modern Jain spirituality and, famously, on Mohandas (later Mahatma) [[gandhi|Gandhi]]. He has been compared to the lay mystic [[kundakunda|Banarsidas]] as a figure who "attempted to point to the truth of Jainism on the basis of direct personal experience" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Biographical Overview

Born in 1867 in **Vavania**, a small Gujarati port, into a family that was part Jain (on his mother's side) and part Vaiṣṇava. The influence of Hinduism coloured his writings and he "often talked about God in the idiom of Vaiṣṇava theism" (Dundas, ch. 9). He claimed to have attained memory of former births at age seven upon witnessing a funeral cremation. By sixteen he had concluded — after extensive reading in Jain and other religious literature — that Jainism was "the best and most true" of available paths despite the imperfections all religions inevitably show. He produced writings on Jain philosophy before age twenty.

**Profession and family life.** Rajacandra was a **jeweller** and a **married man with three children**. He regarded the householder state "both as more arduous than that of the monk and as having been brought about by his own karma so that he could engage more fully with inner experience" (Dundas, ch. 9). He never took ascetic initiation, though towards the end of his career he took a vow of celibacy and partially imitated monastic life.

**Death.** He died in 1901 in **Rājkot**, physically depleted by intense fasting — a photograph from near the end of his life shows him "in an advanced state of physical emaciation, more so than any monk would have normally aspired to" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Primary Source Appearances and Writings

Rajacandra's corpus includes approximately **800 letters** that chart his spiritual development. Key works:

- ***Ātmasiddhi*** ("Attainment of the Soul", 1896): a short verse treatise "written in the course of one night," intended as a clarification of a letter to his close friend Sobhagbhāī adumbrating six principles: the soul exists; the soul is eternal; the soul is the agent; the soul is the experiencer of its actions; the state of deliverance exists; the means of gaining it also exist (Dundas, ch. 9).
- **Correspondence with Gandhi**: three letters survive from his South Africa period (c. 1895), offering extensive advice on basic religious and metaphysical matters, including advice to Gandhi to follow the duties and dietary restrictions of his merchant caste "on the grounds that they were a necessary adjunct to correct moral behaviour" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Doctrinal Positions

Rajacandra was most influenced by **Digambara texts**, particularly [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]]'s writings on the true nature of the soul. He argued that Jainism had declined through preoccupation with sectarianism and sterile rituals "divorced from any understanding of the spiritual teachings of the religion" (Dundas, ch. 9). In a letter of 1887: "To be entirely free in whatever way from attachment and hatred is my religion. … Don't forget that I do not belong to any *gaccha* but only to my soul."

He came to accept image worship as a valid aid to spiritual advancement, despite initial scepticism — distinguishing him from the aniconic reformers [[lonka|Lonka]], [[acarya-bhiksu|Bhikṣu]], and the [[sthanakvasi|Sthānakvāsīs]].

**The experience of realisation (1890):** "I am distinct from everything in all respects. I am only most pure consciousness, most exalted and unthinkable and an unadulterated pure experience-self … I direct my resultant consciousness to my soul. I absorb myself deeply within it" (letter of 1897; Dundas, ch. 9).

**Core six principles** (*Ātmasiddhi*):
1. The soul exists.
2. The soul is eternal.
3. The soul is the agent.
4. The soul is the experiencer of its actions.
5. The state of deliverance exists.
6. The means of gaining it also exist.

His formulation: "On the cessation of the identification of the soul with the body, you are neither the performer of the action nor the experiencer of its result. This is the secret of true religion. By this religion, there is liberation; you are liberation itself. You are infinite insight and knowledge; you are un-disturbable bliss itself" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Jain Reception

Rajacandra is regarded by most Jains as a great teacher, though his exact sectarian affiliation is diffuse (he identified with Digambara doctrine but never took Digambara ascetic initiation). He had a "small coterie of friends and associates" during his lifetime. There are now several **Śrīmad Rājacandra religious centres (*āśram*)** throughout India and in East Africa, Britain, and North America, and "signs that a lay guru lineage is evolving" (Dundas, ch. 9). His emphasis on self-experience "will continue to maintain their appeal and provide a transsectarian brand of Jainism which is not reliant on initiated ascetics" (Dundas, ch. 9). His movement has also merged in part with the [[kanji-svami-panth|Kanji Svami Panth]].

### Gandhi and Rajacandra

The two Gujaratis met in **Bombay in 1891**, just after Gandhi returned from studying in Britain. Gandhi recorded in his autobiography: "no one else ever made on me the impression that [Rājacandra] did. His words went straight home to me. His intellect compelled as great a regard from me as his moral earnestness, and deep down in me was the conviction that he would never willingly lead me astray … In my moments of spiritual crisis, therefore, he was my refuge." Nonetheless Gandhi added: "I could not enthrone him in my heart as my Guru. The throne has remained vacant" (Dundas, ch. 9).

While Gandhi was in South Africa and attracted to Christianity, Rajacandra's letters helped lead him back to Hinduism. Dundas observes that Gandhi's understanding of the nature of religion and non-violence "at times shows a Jain tinge which was possibly imparted to it by his early contact with Rajacandra" (ch. 9) — though Gandhi's sources were diverse and primarily Hindu in formal expression.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The label "Gandhi's guru" — still common in Gujarat — overstates the relationship. Dundas distinguishes carefully: Rajacandra provided spiritual counsel in a crisis but Gandhi explicitly denied he took the formal *guru* role. The precise extent of Rajacandra's influence on Gandhi's doctrine of *ahimsā* remains a matter of scholarly interest.
