## Biographical Overview

Ṛṣabha (also known as Ādinātha, "Lord of the Beginning") is the first [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] of the current cosmic era (*avasarpiṇī*) and "inevitably allotted a great deal of space in the Universal History" (Dundas, p. 21). Unlike subsequent fordmakers, he was born "not, as would be expected, at the very beginning of the *avasarpiṇī*, but near the end of its third spoke" (Dundas, p. 21) — the point at which the human world was beginning to degrade from a primordial golden state.

Up to the time of Ṛṣabha's appearance, the needs of human beings had been satisfied by miraculous wishing trees. As the efficacy of these trees decreased, "society slowly became unstructured and incapable of self-maintenance" (Dundas, p. 21). Ṛṣabha accordingly played a patriarchal, civilisational role before his renunciation: he "inculcating social skills such as the preparation of food, the kindling of fire, agriculture, writing, marriage, an organised system of society and so on" (Dundas, p. 21). He is thereby "unique among the fordmakers in that he is not just a spiritual teacher but a form of culture hero" (Dundas, p. 21–22).

## The Institution of *Dāna*

Ṛṣabha is credited with articulating the vital institution of *dāna* (gift-giving, the giving of alms), "whereby a layman gives alms to an ascetic and through that action gains merit." This was "articulated for the first time when a king, Śreyāṃsa, poured sugarcane juice into Ṛṣabha's cupped hands to break the fordmaker's first fast as a renouncer" (Dundas, p. 22). This foundation story defines the basis of the lay–ascetic relationship in Jainism.

## Connection to Mahāvīra

The Universal History pivots around the two figures of [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]] and Ṛṣabha. Mahāvīra is narratively linked to Ṛṣabha "through having been born as his heretical grandson Māricī" in a previous life (Dundas, p. 21). After a succession of rebirths — including existences as a hellbeing and a lion — Mahāvīra completed his penultimate birth in a heaven before being reborn as the twenty-fourth fordmaker.

The chief *gaṇadhara* Gautama once made a pilgrimage to Mount Aṣṭāpada, "the site of the final deliverance of the first fordmaker, Ṛṣabha" (Dundas, p. 39), as part of an attempt to gain enlightenment.

## Historicity and Jain Claims

Dundas summarises the Jain scholarly argument for Ṛṣabha's historicity: "Jain scholars today often claim that Ṛṣabha's historicity is guaranteed by the fact that his name, which means 'bull', is mentioned frequently in the Ṛgveda, and they also argue that a well-known Vedic hymn (Ṛgveda 10.136) which describes a long-haired sage (*keśin muni*) in fact refers to Ṛṣabha who is frequently depicted iconographically with a mane of hair down his back" (Dundas, p. 39–40).

Dundas offers a counter-reading: "The word 'bull' does appear frequently in the Veda but as an epithet of the god Indra, while Ṛṣabha's name and hair suggest a comparison with the Hindu high god Śiva who rides the bull Nandin, has a topknot or mass of matted hair on his head, is both householder and ascetic and whose mythology was taking shape at the same time as the first, skeletal biography of Ṛṣabha was being written in the Kalpasūtra" (Dundas, p. 40). Dundas concludes: "it seems better to view the story of Ṛṣabha as structurally necessary for the Universal History and to conclude that his reality within the framework of Jain mythology and in the minds of his devotees is the more important factor" (Dundas, p. 40).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

The Universal History (composed by Digambara poet Jinasena, 9th century CE, and his pupil Guṇabhadra; and by the Śvetāmbaras Silāṅka, 9th century CE, and Hemacandra, 12th century CE) gives Ṛṣabha the greatest prominence of any fordmaker after [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]]. He is one of the four fordmakers — alongside Nemi, [[parshvanatha|Pārśva]], and Mahāvīra — who are "by far the most prominent at the beginning of the common era" as attested by the Mathura images (Dundas, p. 40–41).

Within Jain devotional culture, Ṛṣabha/Ādinātha is one of the four fordmakers who receive sustained worship. His role as culture hero and progenitor connects the ascetic ideal with the full range of lay civilisational life, making him a symbol that "simultaneously embodies the realms of ascetic and lay values" (Dundas, p. 39).

The Śvetāmbara Universal History also records that the supposedly eternal and authorless Hindu Veda was in fact "created by Bharata, the first Jain universal emperor of this world era" — a Jain counter-appropriation of brahmanical scripture achieved through Ṛṣabha's son (Dundas, p. 11).
