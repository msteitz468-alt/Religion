**Biographical Overview**: 
Mary is a 1st-century Jewish woman from Nazareth in Galilee, betrothed to Joseph. In the Lukan narrative, she becomes pregnant by the Holy Spirit. She gives birth to Jesus in Bethlehem, raises him in Nazareth, and is present among the early believers in Jerusalem after his ascension. Matthew adds the perspective of Joseph's initial plan to divorce her quietly upon discovering the pregnancy, resolved by angelic dream. She appears at the cross in John and in the upper room in Acts.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Central in the birth narrative (Luke 1-2). Gabriel's Annunciation, visit to Elizabeth, Magnificat (1:46-55), birth in Bethlehem, presentation in temple, brief appearance in ministry (8:19-21), and among disciples in Acts 1:14. Green highlights her as model of faithful response.
- **Gospel of Matthew**: In the infancy narrative (chs. 1-2); betrothed to Joseph, virgin birth, flight to Egypt, return to Nazareth. France emphasizes fulfillment of Isaiah 7:14.
- **Gospel of John**: At the wedding at Cana (2:1-12) and at the cross (19:25-27), where Jesus entrusts her to the Beloved Disciple. Michaels notes her role in the "signs" and the "hour" of glorification.
- **Acts**: Present in the praying community awaiting Pentecost (1:14), alongside the brothers of Jesus.
- **Other**: Implied in Mark 3:31-35 (Jesus redefines family) and 6:3 (named among his siblings).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green's commentary stresses that Mary is introduced almost as an orphan—without family background, betrothed but not yet having inherited Joseph's status—a peasant girl of low social status in the first-century Mediterranean agrarian world of power and privilege. Yet she is "favored" (κεχαριτωμένη) by God. Her Magnificat (1:46-55) serves as the theological thesis statement for Luke's Gospel: a prophetic declaration of social revolution and reversal (lifting the lowly, bringing down the powerful, filling the hungry, sending the rich empty away). Green notes the song's structure parallels Hannah's prayer (1 Sam 2) and anticipates the Nazareth sermon (Luke 4) and the holistic salvation of Luke-Acts.
- **Matthean Narrative (Christianity)**: R. T. France reads Mary within the fulfillment motif. Her virginity fulfills Isaiah 7:14 ("Immanuel"); her role in the genealogy and infancy establishes the Davidic and miraculous origins of the Messiah. Joseph’s righteousness and the angelic resolution highlight God's initiative over human plans.
- **Johannine Narrative (Christianity)**: J. Ramsey Michaels presents Mary at Cana as prompting the first sign, and at the cross as the mother entrusted to the disciple whom Jesus loved—symbolizing the new family of faith formed at the "hour" of glorification.
- **Early Church / Patristic**: Venerated as Theotokos (God-bearer) at Ephesus 431; perpetual virginity developed (leading to kinship debates with James and Jude). Protoevangelium of James expands her childhood and purity.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: Theotokos, Ever-Virgin; central in iconography (e.g., Hodegetria) and liturgy; her fiat as model of synergy with grace.
- **Roman Catholic**: Immaculate Conception (1854), Assumption (1950); Mediatrix and co-redemptrix discussions; perpetual virginity (Hieronymian view of brothers as cousins). **Note**: medieval Catholic theology did not uniformly hold the Immaculate Conception — [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] *denied* it in its later-defined form (see below), a famous case where a pre-1854 Doctor of the Church contradicts what became dogma.
- **Protestant**: Emphasizes her as model of faith and obedience (Magnificat as gospel proclamation); skepticism toward later dogmas of perpetual virginity and intercession; focus on her lowliness and God's reversal.
- **Islam**: Maryam, mother of 'Isa; highly honored (Sura 19, 3:42-47); virgin birth affirmed; but Jesus is prophet, not divine Son.
- **Jewish**: Largely absent or negative in early sources; modern scholarship notes her as representative of faithful Jewish women in the Jesus movement.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Mary is the first to hear and embody the gospel in Luke—her song is the initial NT proclamation of God's kingdom as reversal and mercy. She models the "lowly" whom God elevates and the faithful disciple who ponders events in her heart (Luke 2:19, 51). In the broader canon, she bridges the old and new covenants (Hannah typology), stands at the cross as witness to the new family of God (John), and represents the praying community at Pentecost (Acts). Theologically, she embodies election by grace, prophetic witness, and the inclusion of the marginalized in salvation history.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Virgin birth**: Affirmed in Matthew and Luke; debated in critical scholarship as theological claim vs. historical (some see it as midrashic fulfillment of Isa 7; others as early tradition). France and Green treat it as integral to the evangelists' theological narratives without adjudicating biology.
- **Kinship to James and Jude**: See the Helvidian (full/half brother), Epiphanian (stepbrother), and Hieronymian (cousin) positions—tied to Mariology. Green and Moo lean Helvidian on natural reading of "brother."
- **Role in ministry**: Gospels show limited involvement (questioning in Mark 3); John and Acts elevate her presence. No evidence of leadership role beyond the family circle.
- **Later legends**: Protoevangelium and other apocrypha expand her life; these are pious developments, not historical.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Mary's Magnificat as the first NT gospel proclamation; her low status and Spirit-filled prophecy exemplify Luke's theme that God favors the marginalized and pours out the Spirit on women (Joel 2 fulfillment in Acts 2). The birth narrative's social setting (agrarian power structures) makes her transposition of status paradigmatic.
- **France (Matthew)**: Integrates her into the fulfillment and Davidic narrative; the virgin birth as key to the "new genesis" (1:1) and God's sovereign initiative.
- **Michaels (John)**: At Cana and the cross, she participates in the "signs" and the formation of the believing community at the hour of Jesus' glorification.
- **Bock (Acts)**: Her presence in the upper room underscores continuity between the Jesus story and the Spirit-empowered church; the family of Jesus (including brothers) joins the apostolic community.
- Broader: Patristic (Irenaeus: new Eve), medieval (Bernard of Clairvaux), Reformation (Luther's commentary on Magnificat), and modern feminist/liberation readings (Mary as icon of the oppressed). Cross-tradition comparisons (e.g., with Hannah, or Islamic Maryam) highlight her as a figure of divine favor and reversal.

**Aquinas on the Blessed Virgin (Summa Theologiae III Q27–Q35)**:
[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] treats Mary within his Christology. Key positions:

- **Sanctification, not Immaculate Conception** (Q27): Aquinas holds that Mary **contracted original
  sin** and was sanctified (cleansed of it) by grace **in the womb, *after* her animation** but
  before birth — *not* preserved from contracting it from the first instant of her conception. He
  reasons that universal redemption by Christ requires that all (Christ alone excepted) contract
  original sin, "otherwise the Incarnation would not be necessary for all." The *fomes* of sin (the
  tinder of concupiscence) was **fettered** at her sanctification and entirely removed only at
  Christ's conception. This is **materially different from the dogma defined by Pius IX in 1854**
  (*Ineffabilis Deus*), which holds Mary preserved free from original sin from the first instant —
  the position championed by Duns Scotus (the *praeredemptio*) against the Thomist
  objection. The divergence is a standard example for the **development of doctrine**.
- **Perpetual virginity** (Q28): affirmed *ante partum*, *in partu*, and *post partum*; Helvidius
  named and refuted; the "brethren of the Lord" explained as kinsmen (Jerome's view); Joseph also held
  to have remained a virgin.
- **Espousals** (Q29): a true marriage (consent making it real) though unconsummated; possessing all
  three goods of marriage.
- **Annunciation** (Q30): Mary's consent was sought "in lieu of that of the entire human nature."
- **Mother of God / Theotokos** (Q35): affirmed; its denial (Nestorius, Photinus) declared heretical
  — because the one she bore is the divine Person (see [[hypostatic-union]]). She supplied the matter
  of Christ's body (from her purest blood, per Aristotelian biology), the Holy Spirit being the active
  principle (Q31–32).
