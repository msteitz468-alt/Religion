## Biographical Overview

Guru Gobind Singh was the tenth and final personal Guru of the Sikhs, the only child of [[guru-tegh-bahadur|Guru Tegh Bahadur]]. He was born in Patna, Bihar, in December 1666 while his father was on his eastern tour (McLeod ~L317–318). Muslim sources generally refer to him as Gobind Rai; documents issued by Guru Tegh Bahadur give his name as Gobind Das (McLeod ~L471–477). *Bachitar Nātak*, attributed to the Guru himself, describes a pre-birth sojourn performing austerities on the slopes of Hem Kunt, a purely mythological location in the Himalayas (McLeod ~L487–500).

He received his father's severed head at Makhoval when he was only nine years old. His uncle Kirpal guided him and selected the strategically superior site of Anandpur, where the Guru spent most of his active career (McLeod ~L505–512). He is remembered in Sikh popular art and imagination as "Kalgidhar" (bearer of the plume), always young and upright, armed and dressed in gorgeous raiment, seated on a bluish-grey horse with a white hawk on his left hand (McLeod ~L518–533).

## Early Life and the Shivalik Wars

Guru Gobind Singh developed expertise in arms and literary arts. He hunted and composed religious poetry, including works such as *Jap Sahib* and *Akal Ustat*, as well as Dasam Granth compositions including *Chandī Charitra*, *Chandikī Vār*, and *Chaubis Avatar* (McLeod ~L539–547).

In 1685 the ruler of Sirmur invited him to Paunta on the Jamuna river. In 1688, at the battle of Bhangani, he defeated the invading army of Garhwal and its allies, marking him as "the strongest power among the hill chieftains" (McLeod ~L574–596). He moved his capital to Anandpur in 1689. A subsequent victory at Nadaun aided the Raja of Bilaspur against Mughal forces (McLeod ~L609–619). By 1693 the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb dispatched forces against Anandpur, though they did not attack (McLeod ~L624–636).

## The Founding of the Khalsa (1699)

The founding of the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] on Baisakhi Day, 1699, is "the most important event in Sikh history" (McLeod ~L457). The immediate traditional reason was the aftermath of Tegh Bahadur's execution: the new Guru resolved that "all should bear obvious symbols such that a single Sikh would stand out in a crowd of thousands" (McLeod ~L448–450).

**The event**: The Guru summoned Sikhs to Anandpur armed. At the height of the festivities he suddenly appeared and demanded the head of a Sikh. Five volunteers — Daya Singh (a Khatri from Lahore), Dharam Singh (a Jat), Himmat Singh, Sahib Singh, and Muhakam Singh — came forward. After a dramatic sequence involving a tent and a bloodstained sword, the five emerged alive, thereafter known as the *Panj Piāre* (Cherished Five or Five Beloved) (McLeod ~L720–745).

**The initiation rite**: The next morning the Guru administered the *khande dī pahul* (sword ritual): water was stirred in an iron vessel with a double-edged sword and sweetened with *patashas* cast in by his second wife, Mata Jito. The Guru dispensed the *amrit* (nectar), letting it run off the sword on to the initiate's face five times, pouring it into cupped hands five times, applying it to the eyes five times, and sprinkling it on hair five times. All ate *karah prasad* from the same iron bowl. Many Sikhs hold that the Guru then requested the Panj Piare to administer initiation to him in return (McLeod ~L747–768).

**The Rahit**: The Guru promulgated the Rahit (code of belief and conduct), requiring all Khalsa members to wear the **Five Ks** (*panj kakke* or *panj kakār*) — items each beginning with the letter 'k': *kes* (uncut hair), *kahghā* (comb), *kara* (iron or steel wrist-ring), *kirpan* (sword), and *kachh* (breeches not below the knee). Male members added "Singh" to their names; females added "Kaur" (McLeod ~L769–776).

**Two reasons for the Khalsa**: McLeod identifies two complementary explanations. (1) The traditional reason: to make every Sikh visibly identifiable so he could not hide in danger, enabling them to "smite and destroy the Mughal empire" as "chosen messengers of Akal Purakh" (McLeod ~L793–812). (2) The administrative reason: *Khālsā* was a Mughal administrative term for lands under direct imperial supervision, as opposed to those under provincial governors. The Guru's Khalsa similarly consisted of Sikhs under his direct personal care rather than entrusted to the masands. "In summoning Sikhs to join his Khalsa, the Guru was commanding them to renounce the authority of the masands and attach their allegiance direct to the Guru" — effectively abolishing the masand system (McLeod ~L834–848). The masands were formally condemned, together with the schismatic Minas, Dhir-malias, and Ram-raias, as the *Panj Mel* (Five Reprobate Groups) (McLeod ~L849–851).

**Social consequences**: Brahman and Khatri followers largely refused initiation because it required conspicuous external symbols; Khatris stayed on in reduced numbers as *Sahaj-dhārī* Sikhs. The Panth thus became "even more strongly Jat" in its caste composition (McLeod ~L881–884).

**Theological tension**: The conspicuous external symbols appeared to conflict with Guru Nanak's insistence that "external features must necessarily stand squarely in the way of liberation through the divine Name." The Sikh theological resolution is that "there is only one Guru, successively inhabiting ten human bodies" — what Gobind Singh decreed at the end of the seventeenth century was what Nanak "would have decreed in the same circumstances more than 150 years earlier" (McLeod ~L898–919).

## The Assault on Anandpur and the Retreat

Vazir Khan, Mughal governor of Sirhind, allied with jealous hill rajas and besieged Anandpur from 1701 onward. A famous episode from the 1701 siege involves the warrior Bachitra Singh thrusting a lance into the forehead of an iron-shielded war elephant, turning it on its own army (McLeod ~L941–962).

The decisive siege of 1704 brought the garrison near starvation. On the night of 20–21 December 1704 the Guru broke out of Anandpur. At Chamkaur he and a small group were again besieged; his two elder sons were killed in the fighting (McLeod ~L971–982). The Guru escaped alone through the Mughal cordon. He subsequently learned that his two younger sons, captured by Vazir Khan, had been offered conversion to Islam or death; they defiantly chose death and were "bricked up alive in the city wall" — an incident McLeod notes made "a deep and continuing impression on the Panth" (McLeod ~L988–1004).

At Khidrana (later Muktsar) in Firozpur district, forty Sikhs who had earlier deserted rejoined the Guru and died in battle. The Guru forgave their earlier desertion and acknowledged their renewed allegiance; they are still commemorated as the *Chālīs Mukte* (Forty Liberated) in the Khalsa prayer *Ardās* (McLeod ~L1005–1015).

The Guru spent about a year at Talvandi Sabo (Damdama Sahib, "resting place"), where tradition says he dictated from memory the complete text of the Adi Granth, since Dhir Mai's family refused to surrender the original manuscript (McLeod ~L1022–1028).

## Death and the Termination of the Personal Guruship

Guru Gobind Singh assisted Aurangzeb's son Bahadur Shah in the war of succession, then joined Bahadur Shah's court. At Nander on the Godavari in the Deccan, he was stabbed by a Pathan traditionally linked to Vazir Khan. The wounds were stitched but broke open again, and he died on 7 October 1708 (McLeod ~L1093–1106).

**The final declaration**: Before his death, "the Guru summoned his Sikhs and declared that the line of personal Gurus was now at an end. Thereafter they should regard the functions of the Guru as vested in the Granth (which became the Guru Granth) and the Panth (the Guru Panth)" (McLeod ~L1107–1112).

McLeod presents two accounts of this declaration:
- *Gur Sobhā* (completed c. 1711), written by the poet Sainapati who did not himself join the Khalsa but was a loyal follower, records the Guru saying: "Upon the Khalsa which I have created I shall bestow the succession. The Khalsa is my physical form and I am one with the Khalsa... And my true Guru, boundless and infinite, is the eternal Word" (McLeod ~L1189–1204). McLeod concludes the "balance of probability favours the claim that the Guru actually made the crucial pronouncement" (McLeod ~L1210–1215).
- An alternative theory holds that the tradition arose because the Guru died without a living heir (all four sons had died), and the Khalsa and scripture became the eternal Guru by community consensus (McLeod ~L1139–1155).

**Ambiguities**: McLeod notes unresolved questions: which Granth is meant (Adi Granth alone, or both Adi Granth and Dasam Granth?); and whether the Panth designated is the wider Nanak-panth or the narrower Khalsa Panth (McLeod ~L1121–1127).

## The Dasam Granth

The [[dasam-granth|Dasam Granth]] is traditionally attributed in its entirety to Guru Gobind Singh, much of it composed at Damdama Sahib. In the eighteenth century it shared with the Adi Granth the status of the eternal Guru; meetings of the Sarbat Khalsa were held in the presence of both open scriptures. McLeod notes that the question of the Dasam Granth's authority overlaps directly with the question of Guru Gobind Singh's final declaration (McLeod ~L1223–1244).

## Legacy in Sikh Consciousness

Guru Gobind Singh "looms very large indeed in the consciousness of Sikhs" and "shares with Guru Nanak much the greater part of Sikh tradition, attracting accounts of supreme heroism and anecdotes which display his profound wisdom and common sense" (McLeod ~L1251–1255). He is always depicted as young, armed, and regal — Kalgidhar, bearer of the plume — contrasting with the image of Guru Nanak rapt in profound meditation (McLeod ~L533–537).

## Controversies

- Whether Guru Gobind Singh explicitly declared Guru Granth and Guru Panth as successors or whether this tradition developed posthumously.
- Whether the Dasam Granth shares scripture status with the Adi Granth (see [[dasam-granth|Dasam Granth]] page).
- Whether the Khalsa initiation story records actual events or has been shaped by later tradition; McLeod says "it is most unlikely — indeed impossible — that tradition would be seriously astray on an event so critical for the future of the Panth. Equally, however, it would be strange for tradition to get it all right" (McLeod ~L784–791).
