Isaiah son of Amoz, the eighth-century BCE Jerusalem prophet to whom the book of
[[isaiah|Isaiah]] is attributed. This page concerns the prophet as a figure; for the book,
its composition, and the authorship dispute, see [[isaiah|Isaiah]] and
[[isaiah-authorship|the authorship controversy]].

## Biographical Overview

The primary biographical data come from the book itself and from 2 Kings 18–20 (paralleled
in Isa 36–39). The superscription (Isa 1:1) places his ministry under Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz,
and Hezekiah. Oswalt notes the name means "Yahweh saves" — "One could hardly imagine a more
appropriate name in the light of the overall message of the book" — and that his father Amoz
is not the prophet Amos; the tradition that Amoz was of royal blood (brother of King Amaziah)
is, per Oswalt, "without any objective evidence," though a seal of an "Amoz the scribe" has
been noted without proven connection. His wife is called "the prophetess" (8:3), possibly a
prophetess in her own right (Oswalt). His sons Shear-jashub ("a remnant will return") and
Maher-shalal-hash-baz bore sign-names; Isaiah says "I and the children the LORD has given me
are signs and symbols in Israel" (8:18).

Oswalt reads the inaugural vision "in the year King Uzziah died" (ch. 6) theologically: "it
was only when the lesser king was removed that the greater King could be seen." He allows
that Isaiah may have preached under Uzziah before the ch. 6 experience — chs. 1–5 may
precede the vision's decisive commissioning.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Isaiah 6** — the call vision: the enthroned Holy One, the seraphim and trisagion, "I am
  a man of unclean lips," the coal from the altar, "Here am I, send me," and the hardening
  commission (6:9–13).
- **Isaiah 7–8** — the Syro-Ephraimite crisis: confronting [[ahaz|Ahaz]] at the conduit of
  the upper pool (7:3) with Shear-jashub; the Immanuel sign; Maher-shalal-hash-baz.
- **Isaiah 20** — the three-year sign-act, walking "naked and barefoot" against trust in
  Egypt during the Ashdod revolt (713–711); one of only three precisely dated oracles.
- **Isaiah 36–39 // 2 Kings 18–20** — counselor to [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] during
  [[sennacherib|Sennacherib's]] invasion; oracles of deliverance; the illness episode; the
  rebuke over the Babylonian envoys.
- **2 Chronicles 32:32** — "the deeds of Hezekiah… written in the vision of Isaiah the
  prophet," which Oswalt uses for Isaianic priority of the chs. 36–39 narratives over Kings.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **In Oswalt's evangelical reading (the ingested source)**: the prophet's self-understanding
  is total identification with the divine word — "Isaiah did not claim to speak about God, he
  claimed to speak for God"; his political counsel was "neither anti-Assyrian nor
  pro-Assyrian but pro-God." Oswalt credits Judah's survival of Ahaz's apostasy and
  Hezekiah's faithfulness largely to Isaiah's ministry, and holds that Isaiah wrote for
  future generations: "He was not writing for his own generation, he was writing for the
  future so that the follies of his own time would not be fatal for the nation."
- **Rabbinic and later Jewish tradition** (noted via Oswalt's engagement): the Targum reads
  key oracles (9:6) messianically; medieval Jewish exegesis read 9:6 as a birth hymn for
  Hezekiah — a reading Oswalt rejects on chronological and theological grounds.
- **Christian tradition**: Isaiah as the "evangelical prophet"; the NT quotes the book more
  than all other prophets together (21 direct quotations per Oswalt).

The extra-biblical martyrdom tradition (Isaiah sawn in two under Manasseh, cf. the
*Ascension of Isaiah*) is not treated in the ingested source and is not yet documented here.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

For Oswalt, Isaiah's call experience is the prototype of the whole book's theology: vision
of the Holy One → cleansing → commission, a pattern the nation itself must undergo to become
God's servant. The hardening commission (6:9–13) Oswalt reads not as retrospective
rationalization but as inherent in the inaugural call: Isaiah's task was "not to be
successful in a merely human sense but to be faithful," preserving a pure revelation that
"could, faithfully recorded, save future generations."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- Whether the prophet authored all 66 chapters of the book is the central dispute — see
  [[isaiah-authorship|Authorship of Isaiah]].
- Precisely dated points of his career (Damascus 732, Samaria 721, Ashdod 713–711,
  Sennacherib 701) are wiki-voice history; the extent of his literary activity is disputed.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Isaiah is the most commented-on prophet in both Jewish and Christian traditions; in this
