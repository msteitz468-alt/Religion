# Uriah the Hittite

**Uriah the Hittite** is the loyal soldier — one of [[david|David's]] elite "Thirty"
warriors (2 Sam 23:39) — whose wife [[bathsheba|Bathsheba]] David takes, and whom David then
has killed to cover the resulting pregnancy (2 Samuel 11). His murder is the act that draws
[[nathan|Nathan's]] curse on the house of David and that 1 Kings 15:5 singles out as the one
blot on David's otherwise upright record ("save only in the matter of Uriah the Hittite").

## Biographical Overview

Despite the gentile ethnonym "the Hittite," Uriah bears a **Yahwistic Hebrew name** ("YHWH
is my light") and is fully assimilated into Israel's army and religion — a foreigner more
scrupulously faithful to Israel's covenantal norms than its king. While the army besieges
the Ammonite capital Rabbah, David summons Uriah back from the front, hoping he will sleep
with Bathsheba and so account for her pregnancy. Uriah refuses to "go down to his house" to
eat, drink, and lie with his wife while the ark and the army of Israel are encamped in the
open field — even when David makes him drunk. David then sends him back carrying his own
death warrant: a letter to [[joab|Joab]] ordering that Uriah be stationed at the fiercest
point of the fighting and abandoned, so that he is killed before the walls of Rabbah.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Uriah is the narrative's **moral foil to David**: the outsider whose disciplined loyalty
exposes the king's faithlessness. His refusal to enjoy domestic comfort during a holy war —
the very restraint David should have shown by remaining with his army instead of lingering in
Jerusalem — silently indicts the king. His death converts David's adultery into murder and
triggers the unfolding violence within David's own house ("the sword shall never depart").

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) notes the chilling craft
of the scene in which Uriah unknowingly bears the letter ordering his own death, and the
**dramatic irony** of his speech about not going home "while the Ark and Israel and Judah
dwell in huts" — pious words that condemn the king listening to them. Uriah's blood becomes,
in Alter's reading of the larger story, part of the recurring motif of **spilled blood** that
clings to David's house and resurfaces in [[joab|Joab's]] own career of treacherous killing.

## Reception

- **Judaism** — Uriah's murder is treated as David's gravest sin; some rabbinic readings
  strain to lessen David's guilt (e.g. that Uriah was technically guilty of insubordination
  by disobeying the king, or that soldiers gave conditional divorces), but the tradition
  preserves the episode as the paradigm of royal sin and repentance.
- **Christianity** — Matthew's genealogy pointedly names Bathsheba not by name but as **"the
  wife of Uriah"** (Matt 1:6), keeping the memory of the wronged man embedded in the lineage
  of the Messiah. A "Uriah letter" has become a proverbial term for a message that carries
  instructions for the bearer's own destruction.
