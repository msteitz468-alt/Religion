Josiah, king of Judah (ca. 640–609 BCE), grandson of [Manasseh] and son of Amon, remembered
as the great reforming Davidic king who repaired the temple, promulgated a rediscovered "book of
the law," and centralized worship — and whose death by Egyptian arrows at Megiddo marks, in the
[[1-2-chronicles|Chronicler's]] telling, the effective end of the kingdom of Judah.

## Biographical Overview

Josiah came to the throne as a boy after the assassination of his father Amon. The biblical
sources ([[1-2-kings|2 Kings 22–23]] // [[1-2-chronicles|2 Chronicles 34–35]]) present him as the
most thorough reformer of the Davidic line: he purged idolatry from Judah and into the former
northern territory, repaired the temple, kept a great Passover, and renewed the covenant on the
discovery of the law-book during the temple repairs. He died in 609 BCE confronting Pharaoh Neco
of Egypt at Megiddo. Historical-critical scholarship associates his reform with the composition
or promulgation of Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomistic History; confessional readings take the
found scroll as an existing Mosaic text — recorded here as positions (see Controversies).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Kings 22–23**: the temple repair, Hilkiah's finding of "the book of the law," Huldah's
  oracle, the covenant renewal, the sweeping reform (destruction of the high places and the
  Bethel altar), the great Passover, and Josiah's death at Megiddo.
- **2 Chronicles 34–35**: the Chronicler's parallel — reform beginning earlier (seeking YHWH at
  16, purging at 20), the found law-book, Huldah's oracle, a greatly expanded Passover account,
  and the death at Megiddo after Josiah refuses the word of God spoken through Pharaoh Neco.
- **Jeremiah**: Josiah's reign is the setting of Jeremiah's early ministry; Jeremiah laments his
  death (referenced 2 Chr 35:25).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **[[peter-leithart|Leithart]] (Brazos Chronicles, 2019)**: Josiah is the last **ideal Davidic
  king**, who "walked in the ways of his father David" and is "the last to 'begin' anything in
  Chronicles. After Josiah, things are ending rather than beginning." Leithart reads him as a
  "new Joshua, conquering the land and cleansing idolatry," extending the reform into the north
  (Ephraim, Naphtali, Manasseh). At the covenant renewal Josiah stands "by the pillar," himself
  "a pillar holding up the house," so that "Judah enters covenant by incorporation into the king,
  who is Israel in person" — through ch. 34 he is simply "the king," "a living archetype of sheer
  royalty." His great Passover restores the ark to the temple (Leithart surmises the priests had
  removed it during Manasseh's idolatry) and moves toward the new-covenant note "holy things for
  holy people." His **death is a reverse Passover and a Saul-echo**: Chronicles opened its account
  of the monarchy with Saul "shot by archers" and closes it with another king killed by archers,
  Ahab (also archer-slain) at the center. Josiah, disguised like Ahab, "does not listen" to the
  Gentile Neco who — "like Huram and Queen Sheba before him, like Cyrus after him" — speaks a word
  from God; so "in this reverse Passover, Yahweh's own son is slain" (cf. Zech 12:11). Leithart:
  Josiah's death is "the effective end of the kingdom of Judah"; he is "the last king to be buried
  in Jerusalem" and "really the last king of Judah." (All attributed to Leithart.)
- **Deuteronomistic History (via [[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|Provan]], Kings frame)**: Josiah is
  paired with [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] as the model of costly obedience — "good kings in bad times"
  — whose reform nonetheless cannot avert the judgment set in motion by Manasseh (see the Josiah
  note on the [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] page). Recorded as Provan's position, not collapsed into
  Leithart's.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Josiah is the high-water mark of Davidic reform and, simultaneously, the pivot into the collapse:
Huldah's oracle spares him personally but leaves the wrath on Jerusalem "unquenched." In the
Chronicler's typological shape (per [[peter-leithart|Leithart]]) his death completes the
Saul-inclusio around the monarchy and reframes the reader's expectation — the awaited "new David"
after this last Josiah turns out to be the Gentile [[cyrus|Cyrus]].

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **The "book of the law"**: historical-critical scholarship since De Wette has identified the
  found scroll with an early form of Deuteronomy, making Josiah's reform the setting for the
  Deuteronomistic program; confessional readings (and [[peter-leithart|Leithart]], who favors the
  whole Pentateuch) take it as a recovered Mosaic text. Positions, not adjudicated here.
- **Josiah's death at Megiddo**: the Chronicler adds the Neco confrontation absent from Kings'
  brief notice. [[peter-leithart|Leithart]] rejects Mitchell's view that Josiah dies over Passover
  irregularities, holding "the more traditional view… Josiah dies because he refuses to hear the
  word of Yahweh that comes to him through Neco."

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Josiah anchors debates over the origin of Deuteronomy and the shape of the Deuteronomistic
History, and, in the Chronicler's reception, the typology of the "last king" whose fall opens the
Gentile-imperial phase. Cross-links: [[1-2-chronicles|1–2 Chronicles]], [[1-2-kings|1–2 Kings]],
