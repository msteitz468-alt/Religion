**Biographical Overview**:
Anna was a prophet, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher. After a brief marriage of seven years, she lived as a widow to the advanced age of 84. She never left the temple in Jerusalem, worshipping there with fasting and prayer night and day. Her story appears only in Luke's Gospel; she is presented as an ideal of persistent Jewish piety at the hinge between the old and new covenants.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Anna appears only in Luke 2:36-38, immediately after Simeon's oracle. She arrives at the precise moment of the presentation of Jesus. She gives thanks to God and speaks about the child "to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem." Green notes her as the first person explicitly called a "prophet" (προφῆτις) in the NT.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green's commentary highlights Anna's extraordinary character references: named prophet, constant temple devotion, fasting and prayer "night and day," advanced age as widow (84 years, evoking completeness or symbolic fullness). As a widow, she represents the vulnerable and marginalized whom Luke portrays as true recipients and heralds of the kingdom. Her expectation of the "redemption of Jerusalem" (λύτρωσις) encompasses both spiritual and socio-political deliverance from foreign subjection and renewal of Israel. She is paired with Simeon as male/female prophetic witnesses.
- **Early Church**: Celebrated in the Presentation feast (Hypapante); seen as model of contemplative prayer and prophetic witness. Typological links to OT widows (e.g., Judith, or the persistent widow in Luke 18).
- **Eastern Orthodox / Catholic**: Venerated as saint and prophetess; her role in temple presentation iconography alongside Simeon.
- **Protestant**: Emphasizes her as example of faithful endurance and bold public testimony; model of women's prophetic voice in the church (cf. Joel 2 / Acts 2).
- **Jewish**: No independent tradition; her portrait draws on biblical models of pious widows and prophets.
- **Broader**: In feminist and liberation readings, Anna (with other Lukan women) exemplifies the elevation of the marginalized and the outpouring of the Spirit on all flesh.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Alongside Simeon, Anna acts as an authoritative, prophetic witness to Jesus' identity at the temple—the center of Israel's worship and the geographic heart of Luke-Acts. The deliberate pairing of male and female prophets at the Gospel's opening signals that God's Spirit is poured out on both men and women (direct anticipation of Pentecost and Joel 2). Anna's public proclamation ("to all who were looking for the redemption") models the faithful witness that the disciples (and the church) are called to in Acts. Green stresses that her inclusion is part of Luke's elevation of women's voices as key recipients and proclaimers of divine revelation. Her life of constant prayer and fasting embodies the piety that recognizes the "consolation of Israel" when it arrives.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Historical Anna**: The detail (tribe of Asher, 84 years, specific lifestyle) gives verisimilitude, but the scene is highly stylized and theological. Critical views see it as Lukan composition to balance Simeon and introduce themes of witness and redemption. Conservative readers affirm a historical core while recognizing literary shaping.
- **Age symbolism**: 84 years (7 x 12) may symbolize completeness or the fullness of time; debated whether literal or stylized.
- **Women's prophetic roles**: Anna (with Elizabeth, Mary) raises questions about the place of women in earliest Christianity and Luke's pneumatology.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Anna is not a passive bystander but an active evangelist—speaking about Jesus to all awaiting redemption. Her inclusion is part of Luke's deliberate elevation of women's voices as prophetic. The social location (widow = vulnerable) makes her a paradigm of those on the margins whom God uses. She models the "night and day" prayer that characterizes the early community in Acts.
- Broader: Inspires reflection on persistent prayer (cf. Luke 18:1-8), the role of widows in the early church (1 Tim 5), and the fulfillment of Joel 2. Cross-tradition: highlights Lukan emphasis on inclusive witness (male/female, old/young, marginalized/elite).
