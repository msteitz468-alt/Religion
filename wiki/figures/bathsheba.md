# Bathsheba

**Bathsheba** is the wife of [[uriah|Uriah the Hittite]] whom [[david|David]] takes by
adultery and then, after engineering Uriah's death, marries — the central act in David's
fall (2 Samuel 11–12). She becomes the mother of [[solomon|Solomon]] and, in 1 Kings 1–2,
the **queen mother (*gebirah*)** who secures her son's succession. She is one of the four
women named in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (there called "the wife of Uriah," Matt 1:6).

## Biographical Overview

Daughter of Eliam (possibly the son of David's counselor Ahithophel) and wife of Uriah, one
of David's elite warriors (the "Thirty"). David, remaining in Jerusalem while his army
besieges Rabbah, sees her bathing, sends for her, and lies with her; she conceives. David's
attempts to disguise the paternity fail when the loyal Uriah refuses the comforts of home
while his comrades are in the field, so David has [[joab|Joab]] place Uriah where he will be
killed. David then marries Bathsheba; the child dies as the judgment announced by
[[nathan|Nathan]]. Their second son is Solomon (also called Jedidiah, "beloved of the
LORD"). Decades later Bathsheba and Nathan act together to obtain David's confirmation of
Solomon over the elder Adonijah (1 Kings 1); after David's death she conveys Adonijah's fatal
request for Abishag (1 Kings 2).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **2 Samuel 11** — the adultery and the murder of Uriah: the hinge of the entire David
  story.
- **2 Samuel 12** — Nathan's rebuke; the death of the first child; the birth of Solomon.
- **1 Kings 1–2** — Bathsheba as kingmaker and queen mother.

## Interpretive Cruxes

Bathsheba's **agency and culpability** are sharply disputed. The Hebrew gives her no
reported speech in 2 Samuel 11 and no stated motive; the bathing is plausibly ritual
purification (the text notes she was "purifying herself from her uncleanness"), and the verbs
of the scene are David's. Readings range from **innocent victim** of a king's
power (the dominant modern view, sometimes framed as coercion or assault given the
asymmetry of power) to a knowing participant or even seductress (a strand of later
moralizing and visual tradition). The text itself withholds the inner life that would settle
the question.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) stresses the narrative's
deliberate **reticence**: David must "send intermediaries to discover the identity of the
naked beauty bathing on the rooftop in view of his palace, though she seems to be the
daughter of one of the members of his own elite guard" — a measure of how far the once-knowing
David has become "the king isolated in his palace." Bathsheba is characterized through
withheld interiority; her transformation from silent object of desire to the shrewd,
calculating *gebirah* of 1 Kings is itself part of the story's portrait of how power works.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Judaism** — Rabbinic tradition often mitigates David's guilt (e.g. the legal fiction
  that warriors gave conditional divorces before battle, rendering Bathsheba technically
  unmarried), while still treating the episode as David's great sin and model of repentance.
- **Christianity** — Her inclusion in the genealogy of Jesus (as "the wife of Uriah") is
  read, with the other irregular unions in Matthew 1, as a sign of grace working through
  human sin and the inclusion of outsiders in the messianic line. Psalm 51, the church's
  preeminent penitential psalm, is by superscription tied to David's repentance over her.
