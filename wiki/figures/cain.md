# Cain

## Biographical Overview
Cain is the firstborn son of Adam and Eve. According to Genesis 4, he was a worker of the ground whose offering was rejected by God, while the animal offering of his brother Abel was accepted. Consumed by jealousy, Cain murdered Abel, becoming the first murderer in human history. He was subsequently cursed to be a fugitive and a wanderer, marked by God for protection.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Genesis 4**: The foundational narrative of his birth, sacrifice, murder, and exile.
- **1 John 3:12**: Believers are warned not to be like Cain, "who was of the evil one and murdered his brother," linking his action to evil works and jealousy.
- **Jude 11**: Pronounces "woe" on false teachers who "walk in the way of Cain."
- **Hebrews 11:4**: Contrasts Cain's sacrifice with Abel's, which was offered "by faith."

## Theological and Narrative Significance
In the New Testament, Cain serves as an archetype of false religion, murderous jealousy, and rebellion against God's appointed way. In the Letter of Jude, Cain is the first figure in the "woe triad" (Cain, Balaam, Korah), representing those who willfully choose a path of disobedience and brotherly hatred. He embodies the rejection of grace and the descent into violent apostasy.

## Augustine: Founder of the Earthly City (City of God XV)

For [[augustine|Augustine]], Cain is the **founder of the [[two-cities|earthly city]]** (*civitas
terrena*). The decisive detail is that "it is recorded of Cain that he built a city, but [[abel|Abel]],
being a sojourner, built none" — the earthly city has its seat here below, the heavenly city only
sojourns. As the firstborn (carnal before spiritual, 1 Cor. 15:46), Cain heads the genealogical line of
the earthly city, set against [[seth|Seth]]'s line of the city of God. Augustine draws the parallel with
**Romulus**, who likewise founded a city by fratricide: but where Romulus and Remus quarrelled over the
*glory of ruling* (the earthly city "divided against itself"), Cain's murder of the good Abel "for no
other reason than because [he] was good" illustrates the deeper hatred *between* the two cities. Cain is
also read as a figure of "the Jews who slew Christ," prefigured by Abel — a typological claim Augustine
marks and defers (see [[two-cities]], [[typology]]).
