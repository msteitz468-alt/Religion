# Dante Alighieri

Florentine poet (1265–1321), author of the [[divine-comedy|*Commedia*]]. Both a
**figure** — the protagonist of his own poem, a character undergoing conversion —
and a **commentator/theologian** (see [[dante-alighieri-poet|Dante as poet-theologian]]).

## Biographical Overview

Born in Florence in 1265 into the lower nobility. Married Gemma Donati; four
children. First met Bice Portinari — whom he called [[beatrice|Beatrice]] — in
1274; her death in 1290 prompted the *Vita nuova* and his turn to philosophy and
theology. He became a prominent **White Guelf** in the Guelf–Ghibelline conflict;
when the Black Guelfs took power in 1302, Dante (absent from the city) was
condemned to exile. He never returned, taking refuge in Verona and finally
Ravenna, where he completed the *Commedia* (begun c. 1307) and died in 1321.

## The Author–Character Distinction

A scholarly commonplace distinguishes Dante the **author** from Dante the
**character** within the fiction. Kirkpatrick cautions that the similarity often
matters more than the difference: the poet bequeaths to his timorous, sometimes
comic character the real historical figures he himself encountered, and the
poem is **confessional writing** in the tradition of [[augustine|Augustine's]]
*Confessions*. Dante allows his own name to be spoken in the text only once — by
Beatrice, at the climax of [[purgatorio|Purgatorio]] 30.

## Significance

The journey of the character Dante is a drama of conversion: "almost lost to sin,"
he is sustained and made ready for Heaven through Beatrice. His confession to her
(Purg. 30–31) marks his entry into a "new life," after which the [[paradiso|Paradiso]]
testifies to his spiritual and intellectual adulthood. Among the poem's
innumerable figures, Dante is the one who embodies "the full spectrum of human
possibility."

## Reception

Recognized since the nineteenth century (Ruskin: "the most central man in all the
world"); a model for Eliot, Joyce, Mandelstam, Primo Levi, Borges, Heaney, and
Walcott. See [[divine-comedy|The Divine Comedy]] for the fuller reception history.
