# Vivekananda

**Swami Vivekananda** (born Narendranath Datta, 1863–1902) was the principal disciple of the Bengali
mystic [[ramakrishna|Ramakrishna Paramahamsa]] (1834–1886) and the first systematic Hindu missionary to
the West — the opening figure of what Doniger calls Hindu "reverse colonization" of America.

## Biographical Overview

Born in Calcutta to an educated family, Vivekananda came under the influence of Ramakrishna, a devotee
of the goddess Kali at the Temple of Dakshineswar (north of Calcutta). Ramakrishna's conclusion — "all
religions are true, but the religion of one's own time and place is best for that person" — gave educated
Hindus a framework for defending the popular, non-philosophical aspects of their religion against
Western criticism.

Vivekananda represented Hinduism at the **World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893**, where he
made a powerful impression. He returned to India in 1897 with a small band of Western disciples and
founded the **Ramakrishna Mission**, which established branches in many countries. He returned to the
West several times and died in 1902 at age thirty-nine.

## Theological and Social Positions

Drawing heavily on **Advaita Vedanta**, Vivekananda exported a philosophically reformed Hinduism that
played well to Western audiences seeking a non-dogmatic spirituality. Several of his positions were
distinctly reformist by Indian standards:

- **Anti-caste**: influenced by progressive Western political ideas, he set himself firmly against all
  forms of caste distinction
- **Pro-beef**: he reportedly advised people to eat beef, a striking reversal of the Brahminical norm
  that had become increasingly identified as "Hindu tradition"
- **Practical Vedanta**: reframed Vedanta as a basis for social action (service as worship) rather than
  purely as metaphysical contemplation; this synthesis was later developed into the Ramakrishna Mission's
  educational and relief work

## Role in American Hinduism

Vivekananda is the founding figure of what Doniger calls the **first wave** of Hindu imports to America
— a philosophical, Vedanta-centered Hinduism that shaped the American transcendentalist legacy from
Emerson and Thoreau forward. This was distinct from the second wave (mid-20th c.) centered on Kali,
Tantra, and charismatic gurus.

The **Ramakrishna-Vedanta tradition** also intersected with the **Theosophical Society** (founded by
Helena Blavatsky in New York, 1875; based at Adyar near Madras from 1879), which under **Annie Besant**
(1847–1933) established Theosophical lodges in Europe and the US after Vivekananda had "paved the way."

## Reception

Vivekananda is revered across Hindu traditions as the father of modern Hindu self-presentation to the
West. His synthesis of Vedanta with social action and his anti-caste stance placed him in productive
tension with both orthodox Brahmanism and with later Hindu nationalists (who admired his cultural
assertiveness but not his social liberalism). He is a founding figure of Hindu reform movements globally,
and his trajectory runs directly through to [[iskcon|ISKCON]] and other diaspora movements that trace
their lineage through Chaitanya-influenced bhakti.
