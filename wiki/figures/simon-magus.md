# Simon Magus

## Biographical Overview

Simon of Samaria is attested in the **Acts of the Apostles** (8:9–24) and extensively in 2nd-century heresiological sources (Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Origen) as a Samaritan magician whose encounter with Philip and Peter in Samaria is the earliest named heresy incident in the New Testament narrative. He is widely regarded as the prototype of the "Simon" figure who became the fountainhead of Gnostic thought in later heresiological tradition, though the relationship between the Acts figure and the later gnostic "Simon" tradition is debated.

Justin Martyr's account is our earliest datable patristic reference (*First Apology*, c. 155 CE), approximately a century after Simon's supposed activity.

## Primary Source Appearances

### Acts of the Apostles (8:9–24)

Simon is introduced as a Samaritan who "practiced magic in the city and amazed the people of Samaria, saying that he himself was somebody great," and "all... from the least to the greatest, paid attention to him, saying, 'This man is the power of God that is called Great.'" When Philip preaches and baptizes in Samaria, Simon believes and is baptized. Seeing that the Spirit is conferred by apostolic laying on of hands, Simon offers money to Peter and John for this power — giving rise to the term **simony** (the buying or selling of ecclesiastical office or sacraments). Peter rebukes him sharply: "Your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money... your heart is not right before God. Repent, therefore, of this wickedness of yours." Simon asks for intercession. The narrative ends here; Acts does not say whether Simon repented or persisted.

### Justin Martyr's First Apology (Chs XXVI, LVI)

Justin provides the earliest substantial heresiological account (c. 155 CE):
- Simon is "a native of the village called Gitto" in Samaria.
- He came to Rome under Claudius Caesar (41–54 CE) and "did mighty acts of magic, by virtue of the art of the devils operating in him."
- "He was considered a god, and as a god was honoured by you with a statue, which statue was erected on the river Tiber, between the two bridges, and bore this inscription, in the language of Rome: 'Simoni Deo Sancto,' 'To Simon the Holy God.'"
- "Almost all the Samaritans, and a few even of other nations, worship him, and acknowledge him as the first god."
- He travelled with a woman named **Helena**, "who went about with him at that time, and had formerly been a prostitute," described as "the first idea (*ennoia*) generated by him" — an early attestation of the Simonian theological system.
- Simon's disciple **Menander of Capparetaea** performed magic in Antioch and "persuaded those who adhered to him that they should never die, and even now there are some living who hold this opinion of his."

In Ch. LVI, Justin repeats the Simon-in-Rome claim, treating Simon as a demon-inspired counterfeit christ.

**Important note**: The "Simoni Deo Sancto" inscription has been complicated by an actual inscription discovered in Rome (1574) reading "SEMONI SANCO DEO FIDIO" — a dedication to the Sabine deity Semo Sancus, not to Simon. Most historians conclude Justin (or his source) confused the Semo Sancus inscription with a Simonian cult statue. This is a significant case of patristic error in heresiological polemic.

### Second Apology (Ch. XV)

Justin closes the Second Apology noting "I myself, too, when I was delighting in the doctrines of Plato, and heard the Christians slandered... perceived that it was impossible that they could be living in wickedness and pleasure" — a brief personal note — and then states he "despised the wicked and deceitful doctrine of Simon of my own nation," identifying himself as also being from Samaria and thus having personal cultural proximity to Simonian tradition.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Heresiological Tradition (2nd–4th c.)

Simon became the **archheretica** — the founding figure and prototype of all subsequent heresies — in patristic heresiological literature:
- **Irenaeus** (*Against Heresies* I.23): Simon taught that he was the Father appeared in Samaria, the Son in Judaea, and the Holy Spirit among other nations. Helena = the first thought (*ennoia*) of the Father; she fell from the realm of the aeons into matter and transmigrated through female bodies, including Helen of Troy, before ending as a prostitute in Tyre, where Simon found and redeemed her.
- **Hippolytus** (*Refutation of All Heresies* VI): Provides what claims to be quotations from a Simonian text (*Apophasis Megale* / "Great Declaration"), though the authenticity is debated.
- **Origen**: Notes that Simonians were quite small in number by his own time (3rd c.), though the tradition had made Simon the fountainhead of Gnosticism.

The heresiological portrait of Simon as proto-Gnostic became a template: redeemer figure + fallen feminine consort (*ennoia* / Sophia) + magical power as rival to apostolic authority. Whether this reflects the historical Simon or a 2nd-century construction projected back onto the Acts figure is contested.

### Modern Scholarship

- The identity of the Acts "Simon" with the later Gnostic "Simon" tradition is heavily disputed. Many scholars see the Gnostic Simon as a 2nd-century figure whose myth was secondarily connected to the Acts character.
- The "Simoni Deo Sancto" inscription confusion is widely accepted as a patristic error.
- The *Pseudo-Clementine* literature (3rd–4th c.) contains a novelistic "Simon" who functions as Peter's constant adversary, bearing little relationship to either the Acts figure or the Irenaean Gnostic teacher.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Simon's function in early Christian thought:
1. **Prototype of simony**: His attempt to purchase spiritual power gave a name to clerical corruption for centuries.
2. **Foil for apostolic authority**: In Acts, Peter's rebuke establishes the principle that spiritual gifts cannot be bought or manipulated.
3. **Origin myth of heresy**: In heresiological tradition, Simon is the first in the chain of false teachers — the "father of heresies" — making him theologically crucial to the self-definition of proto-orthodox Christianity.
4. **Samaritan context**: Justin's identification of Simon as "of my own nation" and his noting that Samaritans worship him suggests that some form of Simonian religion survived as a Samaritan sectarian movement into the mid-2nd century.

## Historicity and Controversies

- **The statue inscription**: The Semo Sancus confusion almost certainly means the claim that Romans erected a divine statue to Simon is false as stated — a significant patristic polemic error.
- **The Acts figure**: Historical question whether the Acts narrative reflects a real encounter or is a literary construction to illustrate apostolic authority over magic.
- **Helena**: Entirely unattested outside Simonian sources. May be legendary accretion to the Simon myth.

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names.*

Bock reads Acts 8:9–24 on its own terms and pointedly **resists importing the patristic
Gnostic-founder portrait** back into Luke's text. Simon is a Samaritan practitioner of magic
(*mageia*) who "amazed" the city, claiming to be "somebody great," and whom the people acclaim as
"the great power" — for Bock likely a claim to be an incarnation of the highest God (Jervell),
attested in Samaritan usage of "the great power" as a divine designation, but "not explicitly
gnostic" (with Witherington and Barrett, against Haenchen, who charged that Luke "downgraded Simon
from Gnostic to magician"). When Philip preaches, Simon "believed and was baptized," yet Bock notes
his belief has "no explicit object," unlike the Samaritans who believe Philip's message, and the
verb for his continuing with Philip is "not the normal way of describing discipleship." Seeing the
Spirit conferred through the apostles' hands, Simon offers money for the authority — the episode from
which the term **simony** derives; Bock reads him as "thinking in his old and more Gentile ways."
Peter's rebuke ("May your silver go with you into destruction") is a wish that is also a warning; the
present optative and the call to repentance leave the door open, unlike the immediate judgment on
Ananias and Sapphira. Bock surveys the debate over the **genuineness of Simon's faith** (Barrett: no
less sincere than others; Bruce: "sincere, superficial, and inadequate"; Stählin: not true faith)
and judges the overall impression "primarily negative" — Simon's request that Peter pray for him
reads as dismissive ("Peter can pray, but Simon will not"). For Bock the scene's point is theological:
"the Spirit cannot be bought… Christianity has nothing to do with magic."
