# Count Ugolino della Gherardesca

Pisan nobleman (c. 1220–1289) whom [[dante-alighieri|Dante]] meets frozen in
Antenora, gnawing the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri — the climactic encounter of the
lower [[inferno|Inferno]] (Cantos 32–33). Both are damned for treachery; Ugolino
had surrendered Pisan fortresses, and Ruggieri then used this as a pretext to
imprison him.

## In the Divine Comedy

Ugolino narrates his death (33:1–75): locked with his sons and grandsons in the
tower later called the **Hunger Tower**, the door nailed shut, he watched the boys
starve. When they offered their own flesh — "Father, for us the pain would be far
less / if you would chose to eat us" (33:61–63) — he turned "to stone," and after
all had died he groped blindly over their bodies, "Then hunger proved a greater
power than grief" (33:75). Kirkpatrick notes the deliberate ambiguity of this final
line: the poem leaves open whether Ugolino, at the end, ate his children. The
narration prompts Dante's tirade against Pisa, "newborn Thebes," whose reefs he
wishes would drown its whole people — for even a traitor's children "had no right
to crucify" (33:87).

## Significance

The Ugolino episode is among the poem's most harrowing: a study in how a traitor is
also a wronged father, eliciting horror and pity at once. Like [[farinata-degli-uberti|Farinata]]
and [[francesca-da-rimini|Francesca]], Ugolino shows Dante's refusal to flatten the
damned into mere exempla. The contrapasso — Ugolino eternally devouring his betrayer
— literalizes the cannibalism his story only hints at.
