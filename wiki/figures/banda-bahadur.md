# Banda Bahadur

The military commander who, in the seven years after [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]]'s death,
led the first Sikh seizure of political power in the Punjab — conquering [[sirhind|Sirhind]], striking
his own coin, and convulsing the agrarian order of the south-eastern Punjab before his capture and
execution. This page draws on **Khushwant Singh, *A History of the Sikhs*, Vol. 1, Ch. 6**.

## Biographical Overview (Khushwant Singh)

Born in 1670 at Rajauri (Poonch) of Rajput parents and originally named **Lachman Das**, he joined a
*bairāgī* (mendicant) order early, took the name **Madho Das**, and spent some fifteen years at a
hermitage on the Godavari at [[nanded|Nanded]] (Khushwant Singh, *History of the Sikhs* Vol. 1, p.97–98,
fn.1). There Guru Gobind Singh met him in 1708 and renamed him **Banda** ("the slave"): "The Guru caught
the fanatic gleam in the hermit's eye and felt that in the spare frame of the ascetic smouldered the
Promethean fire" (Singh, p.97). The Guru gave him five arrows from his own quiver, a standard and battle
drum, and *hukamnāmās* commanding the Sikhs to rally to him (p.97–98). Singh notes the ambiguity over
whether Banda was ever formally baptized into the [[khalsa|Khalsa]]: the *Siyār-ul-Mutāḵẖerīn* says he was
a Sikh by persuasion who never cut his hair, and "the distinction between a formally baptized 'Singh' and
a Hindu who… was in close sympathy with the Khalsa, was not very great" at that time (p.98, fn.1).

## The Campaign (Khushwant Singh, Ch. 6)

Marching north from the Deccan, Banda planted the Guru's standard about 35 miles from Delhi and issued a
proclamation of protection against "Mohammedan bigots" and injustice — "like a spark in a highly
inflammable situation" (Singh, p.99–100). He looted Sonepat, took Kaithal, and **stormed Samana**
(26 November 1709), the home town of [[guru-tegh-bahadur|Tegh Bahadur]]'s executioners and the slayers of
Gobind Singh's sons — Singh calls it "the first notable victory to Sikh arms" (p.100–101). The decisive
**Battle of Sirhind** (24 May 1710) destroyed the city and killed its governor **Wazir Khan**,
who had ordered the execution of the Guru's younger sons: "Not a man of the army of Islam escaped with
more than his life" (Khafi Khan, in Singh p.102).

Banda became "virtual master of the territories between the Jumna and the Sutlej," took the title
**Banda Pādśāh** (Emperor), introduced a new calendar dated from the capture of Sirhind, and struck coins
in the names of Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh with the *degh–tegh–fateh* legend (p.102–103). His
headquarters was the hill fort of Mukhlisgarh. The peasant revolt spread over the [[punjab|Majha]] and the
Jullundur Doab: "The Punjab became like a surging sea of free peasantry with only two small islands of
Mughal authority… Lahore and… Kasur" (p.105).

## Reversal, Capture, and Execution

Bahadur Shah mobilized the full Mughal army and proclaimed a *jihad*; by December 1710 the plains were
recovered and Banda was besieged at Mukhlisgarh, breaking out by night (p.106–107). Under Farrukh Siyar,
**Abdus Samad Khan** and his son **Zakarya Khan** were sent to destroy him. Banda made his
final stand above **Gurdaspur** (Nangal), where an eight-month siege starved the garrison
("not one of the besieged garrison had strength left to wield his sword," p.110); he surrendered on
17 December 1715. The captives were paraded to Delhi — "seven hundred bullock carts full of severed heads
and over seven hundred prisoners" — and executed at 100 per day; English eyewitnesses (John Surman and
Edward Stephenson, 10 March 1716) recorded that "not one apostatised from this new formed religion"
(p.111–112). Banda himself was executed on **19 June 1716**, his four-year-old son Ajai Singh killed
before him; he refused conversion to Islam (p.112–113).

## Interpretation (Khushwant Singh)

Singh frames Banda primarily as the leader of an **agrarian revolution**, not an anti-Islamic crusader:
"In seven stormy years Banda changed the class structure of land holdings in the southern half of the
state by liquidating many of the big Muslim zamindar families of Malwa and the Jullundur Doab" (p.113–114).
He treats both the hostile Muslim portrait (an "infernal monster," *Siyār-ul-Mutāḵẖerīn*) and the
exultant anti-Muslim reading of the Sikh historian Gyan Singh as biased (p.114). His seal's
*degh tegh fateh* formula became the model for later Sikh coins and seals (p.103, fn.12) — a thread the
[[misls|misls]] and [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] later inherited.
