# Felix

## Biographical Overview

Antonius Felix was the Roman governor (procurator) of Judea before whom Paul was tried at
[[caesarea|Caesarea]], as narrated in [[acts|Acts]] 24:1–27 (with 23:24–35 preparing his
arrival). [[darrell-bock|Bock]] describes Felix as a freed slave appointed by Claudius, who
ruled roughly AD 52–59/60, married to Drusilla, and presided over a violent and chaotic
administration; Bock cites Tacitus's judgment that Felix wielded royal power "with the
instincts of a slave." Felix is one of the externally attested figures in Acts (Fitzmyer's
list of persons corroborated outside the text).

His full name is disputed in the sources: Tacitus gives Marcus Antonius Felix, Josephus
gives Tiberius Claudius Felix, and Bock notes the question is unresolved (Additional Note on
23:24).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 23:24–35** — Paul is transferred under heavy military escort from Jerusalem to
  Felix at Caesarea, accompanied by [[herod-agrippa-ii|Claudius Lysias]]'s letter; Felix
  agrees to hear the case when the accusers arrive and holds Paul in Herod's praetorium.
- **Acts 24:1–9** — The high priest Ananias and the hired advocate **Tertullus** (a ῥήτωρ,
  better "hired pleader") prosecute Paul, charging him as a "pest," a political agitator, a
  ringleader of the "sect of the Nazoreans," and a temple-desecrator.
- **Acts 24:10–21** — Paul makes his defense (ἀπολογέομαι), framing the dispute as one over
  the resurrection and "the Way."
- **Acts 24:22–26** — Felix, with his wife **Drusilla**, hears Paul on "righteousness,
  self-control, and future judgment," is "alarmed" (ἔμφοβος), and repeatedly sends for Paul
  hoping for a bribe.
- **Acts 24:27** — After two years, Felix, succeeded by [[festus|Porcius Festus]], leaves
  Paul imprisoned "to do the Jews a favor."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's Reading)

Bock treats the Felix trial as a full Roman examination conforming to provincial penal
procedure (citing Sherwin-White and Mommsen, who called it "an exemplary account of
provincial penal procedure extra ordinem"). He identifies the prosecution's charge as "more
political than that in Acts 21:28: it is a charge of sedition" (Jervell), turning on the
label "sect" (αἵρεσις) — which Paul counters with "Way" (ὁδός), insisting the movement is
"not a 'heretical' movement" but the completion of Judaism.

On **Drusilla**, Bock notes she was born in AD 38, the youngest daughter of Herod Agrippa I
and sister of [[herod-agrippa-ii|Agrippa II]], and Felix's third wife, whom Felix had
persuaded to leave her first husband (Josephus, *Ant.* 20.7.2). Bock quotes Bruce that
righteousness, self-control, and future judgment were "three subjects Felix and Drusilla
certainly needed to hear about." On Felix's hope for money (χρήματα, here "bribe" — illegal
under the *Lex Julia de repetundis*), Bock concludes, with Witherington, that "this is not a
flattering portrait of a ruler," noting that Felix never actually issues a judgment and
leaves Paul bound as a favor to the Jewish leadership.

Bock draws the apologetic point that Paul's hearing before Felix makes "an exemplary case
for why Christianity is a threat neither to Rome nor to any state," and speculates (with
Witherington) that Luke may have used the two-year Caesarean detention to research his
Gospel and Acts.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Felix embodies Bock's recurring theme that Roman legal process, however corrupt in its
practitioners, repeatedly shields Paul from the murderous plots of his accusers while
declining to convict him — advancing the narrative toward Rome under the sovereignty of God.
Felix's venality and inaction contrast with Paul's steadfast innocence and set up the appeal
to Caesar under his successor Festus.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Felix is among the figures whose existence and office are corroborated by Roman and Jewish
historians (Tacitus, Josephus), which Bock invokes for the historical credibility of the
Caesarean trial narrative. The unresolved question of his full name (Marcus Antonius vs.
Tiberius Claudius Felix) is flagged but not adjudicated. A text-critical note attaches to
24:6–8a, where the Western text expands the account of the Romans taking Paul from Jewish
hands; the shorter reading (𝔓74, א, A, B) is better attested.
