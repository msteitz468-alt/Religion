# Rabbi Akiba

## Biographical Overview
Rabbi Akiba is one of the foundational figures of Rabbinic Judaism. According to Talmudic tradition (and emphasized by Unterman), Akiba began his life as an illiterate, impoverished shepherd who despised scholars. Inspired by his wife Rachel, who recognized his potential and married him against her wealthy father's wishes, he began studying Torah at the age of 40. He spent 24 years studying in the academies and returned with 24,000 students, becoming the greatest sage of his generation.

He was a central leader in the era following the destruction of the Second Temple. Akiba supported the Bar Kokhba revolt against Rome (132–136 CE), famously declaring Simon bar Kokhba to be the Messiah. Following the revolt's failure, the Romans forbade the teaching of Torah. Akiba defied the decree and was arrested, tortured, and executed, becoming one of the Ten Martyrs.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Mishna and Talmud**: Akiba's legal rulings and hermeneutical methods permeate the Mishna. He is credited with systemizing the oral tradition, organizing it by subject matter—a system that formed the basis for Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi's eventual redaction of the Mishna.
- **Aggadic Narratives**: The Talmud records the harrowing account of his martyrdom. As the Romans flayed his skin with iron combs, Akiba recited the *Shema*, smiling because he was finally able to fulfill the commandment to love God "with all your soul" (even as it is taken from you). He died drawn out on the word *Echad* (One).

## Tradition-Specific Reception
**Judaism**: Rabbi Akiba is celebrated both for his unparalleled intellect and his supreme spiritual devotion. Hermeneutically, he developed a method of exegesis that derived halakha from every seemingly superfluous letter, word, or grammatical anomaly in the Torah (such as the word *et*). A famous Talmudic story (Menachot 29b) describes Moses seeing a vision of Akiba deriving heaps of laws from the "crowns" (tagin) on the Hebrew letters, illustrating Akiba's role in expanding the Oral Law.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
In the narrative of the Talmud, Akiba bridges the crisis of the Temple's destruction and the stabilization of Rabbinic Judaism. He represents the democratization of Torah study (an illiterate shepherd becoming the greatest sage) and the ultimate commitment to the covenant through martyrdom. His systematization of the halakha was the critical step that made the Mishna possible.
