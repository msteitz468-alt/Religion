## Biographical Overview

Hemacandra was born in **1089** in the town of **Dhandhikā** (near what is now Ahmedabad) to parents of the **Modhā merchant caste**. While still a boy he was given to a Śvetāmbara teacher called **Devacandra** and initiated by him, receiving the monastic name **Somacandra**. His "prodigious mastery of various branches of learning, Jain and non-Jain, led to Devacandra designating him as his successor, and in 1108 he was given the honorific title *sūri* and became a teacher in his own right who had authority over a group of monks and entitlement to provide his own exegesis of the scriptures. From that time he was known as Hemacandra" (Dundas, ch. 5).

He died in **1172**. His biographers are principally the thirteenth-century **Prabhacandra** (*Prabhāvakacarita*, PC 22) and the fifteenth-century chronicler **Merutuṅga**, the latter locating Hemacandra in the "broader context of a semi-legendary history of Gujarat."

## Primary Source Appearances

Dundas's account (ch. 5) discusses Hemacandra as the culminating and most historically tangible figure in the Śvetāmbara *prabhāvaka* (exalter of the doctrine) tradition. He is the twenty-second and final subject of Prabhacandra's collection of twenty-two teacher biographies. His literary works are the primary evidence for his significance; the hagiographies also record his role in the Caulukya court.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

### Śvetāmbara Jainism

"Hemacandra is the most tangible of all the exalters of the doctrine. His fame is firmly rooted in a specific historical role with which all Gujarati Jains are familiar and which was in part responsible for establishing Śvetāmbara Jainism as a resilient and self-confident presence in western India."

Hemacandra is presented as a **systematiser and maintainer** of Śvetāmbara tradition: "Hemacandra's role was as the maintainer and systematiser of Śvetāmbara tradition rather than a radical innovator. While there may have been later figures of more penetrating intellect, such as Yaśovijaya in the seventeenth century, few subsequent to Hemacandra were able to match his command of Jain learning." His epithet **"Omniscient One of the Corrupt Age"** encapsulates this encyclopaedic role.

Modern scholars sometimes criticise him "for his lack of originality, but that would be to miss the point about his achievement" (Dundas).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

### Relationship with the Caulukya Court

The "dominant concern in the biographies of Hemacandra is his relationship with **Jayasiṃha Siddharāja** and his successor **Kumārapāla** of the Caulukya dynasty who ruled Gujarat from their capital at **Patan**."

- **Jayasiṃha Siddharāja** (r. 1092–1141): "Militantly aggressive towards his neighbours … also interested in learning and culture"; appointed Hemacandra as court scholar and historian, commissioning a poetic history of the Caulukya family and a **Sanskrit grammar** (the *Siddhahaima*, named after patron and author; "still used by Śvetāmbara ascetics today").

- **Kumārapāla**: the hagiographies describe how Hemacandra hid Kumārapāla from Siddharāja's murderous designs "under a pile of manuscripts" (PC 22.368). Once Kumārapāla returned to claim the throne, Hemacandra "apparently prevail[ed] upon him to run both his life and his kingdom in accord with Jain principles." Hemacandra's *Yogaśāstra* ("Treatise on Behaviour") was "specifically directed at Kumārapāla … designed to show him how to lead the Jain life"; towards the end of his Universal History he "portrays Mahāvīra as predicting Kumārapāla's meeting with himself." As a result, "Kumārapāla is said to have given up eating meat and he also issued edicts banning the slaughter of animals in his kingdom."

Dundas notes scepticism is warranted: Siddharāja "was certainly a Śaiva Hindu" and Kumārapāla's religious stance is likely to have been pluralistic — "allowing Jain moral values to inform … his own conceptual universe and the practical running of his kingdom, while also remaining a devotee of Śiva and participating in Hindu ritual kingship." One Hindu text describes Kumārapāla "re-entering the Hindu fold late in life."

A celebrated episode has Hemacandra summoning **Śiva himself** to attest to the superiority of Jainism before Kumārapāla at Somnath — which Dundas reads as an allusion to "Kumārapāla's partiality for this god."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The story of Hemacandra hiding Kumārapāla from Siddharāja employs "a motif … common … in folk literature throughout the world," and sources differ about Siddharāja's motives, leading Dundas to note that "some … question the historicity of this episode." However, there is "unanimity about the close relationship formed between Hemacandra and Kumārapāla."

The nature of Kumārapāla's Jainism is a genuine historical controversy: Jain sources claim an exclusive devotion, while Hindu sources suggest a more fluid religiosity and eventual return to the Hindu fold.

## Key Works

- **Siddhahaima grammar** (Sanskrit grammar of the Caulukya court; still in Śvetāmbara use)
- **Yogaśāstra** ("Treatise on Behaviour"; directed at Kumārapāla)
- **Universal History** (Hemacandra's version, incorporating a prediction of Kumārapāla's meeting with the monk)

Hemacandra's literary output "embraces all the main branches of Indian learning."

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

A late tradition "related by Hemacandra in the twelfth century" describes the legendary Emperor Samprati (grandson of Aśoka) dispatching monks to "civilise the barbarian south" — showing Hemacandra's role as transmitter of earlier lore about south Indian Jainism. His grammar and his hagiographic prominence in the *Prabhāvakacarita* ensured his centrality in Śvetāmbara self-understanding.
