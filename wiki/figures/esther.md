Esther (Hadassah) is the Jewish orphan who becomes queen of Persia and, with her cousin Mordecai, delivers the Jewish people from genocide planned by Haman, instituting the festival of Purim.

## Biographical Overview

A Jewish girl from the tribe of Benjamin, orphaned and raised by her cousin Mordecai in Susa. Selected in a beauty contest to replace Queen Vashti, she hides her Jewish identity on Mordecai's advice and becomes queen. When Haman (the Agagite, descendant of Amalekite king) obtains a decree to destroy all Jews on 13 Adar, Mordecai urges Esther to intervene: "Who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14). After three days of fasting, Esther risks her life by approaching the king uninvited. She hosts two banquets for the king and Haman. At the second, she reveals her identity and Haman's plot. The king has Haman hanged on the gallows prepared for Mordecai. A second decree allows the Jews to defend themselves; they prevail. Esther and Mordecai institute Purim (14–15 Adar) as an annual commemoration of deliverance, with feasting, sending gifts, and gifts to the poor. The book ends with Mordecai's greatness and the establishment of the festival.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Book of Esther** (entire): The only biblical appearance. The narrative is set entirely in the Persian court; God is never named, yet the story is read as a classic example of hidden providence. Key pericopes: Vashti's refusal (ch. 1), Esther's selection and favor (2), Mordecai's refusal to bow and Haman's plot (3), Mordecai's charge and Esther's decision (4), Esther's first approach and first banquet (5), the king's insomnia and Mordecai's honor (6), second banquet and accusation (7), second decree and Jewish victory (8–9), Purim institution and Mordecai's epilogue (9–10).
- **Additions to Esther** (Greek, deuterocanonical): Prayers of Mordecai and Esther, dreams, and explicit theological interpretation (God named; providence made explicit).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: The scroll (*Megillah*) is read on Purim with great joy, noise-making at Haman's name, costumes, and feasting. Esther is a model of courage, strategic wisdom, and using position for the people. The book is one of the five *Megillot*. Rabbinic discussion (Megillah tractate) explores why it was included in the canon (it "was written by the Holy Spirit"). The hiddenness of God's name is celebrated as a sign that God works even when not obviously present ("hester panim").

**Christianity**: Read as an example of God's providential care for his people even in exile and apparent absence. Esther's courage and intercession are models for advocacy and risk-taking faith. The "for such a time as this" (4:14) is frequently quoted for calling and opportunity. Typologically, some see Esther as a type of the Church or of Mary (intercessor). The reversal (Haman hanged on his own gallows, Jews delivered and exalted) echoes the theme of the reversal of fortunes (cf. Hannah's Song, Mary's Magnificat). The lack of explicit God-language makes it a test case for discerning providence in "secular" history.

**Islam**: Not a major Qur'anic figure; some traditions identify her in stories of Persian/Jewish interaction, but she is not a prophetess in the Islamic canon.

**Critical**: The book is studied as diaspora novella or historical fiction with strong comic and ironic elements (reversals, banquets as structuring device, "they cast Pur, that is, the lot"). Historicity of the characters and exact decree is debated; the 127 provinces, the numbers of the slain, and the rapid turnaround are seen as stylized. The Greek additions are viewed as later theological "corrections" that name God explicitly. Feminist readings highlight Esther's agency and the book's subversion of power structures.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Esther is the supreme biblical example of *hidden providence* in the diaspora. God is never mentioned, yet the coincidences (the king's insomnia, the timing of Mordecai's earlier service, the reversal of the lot) are too precise to be accidental. The book teaches that God's people must act with courage and wisdom even when divine intervention is not obvious; fasting and risk are part of faithful response. The institution of Purim shows that human institutions (feast, gifts, remembrance) can commemorate and perpetuate divine deliverance. Theologically it challenges any view that God only acts in "sacred" history (exodus, temple, prophets); the God of Israel is Lord of the Persian court and of lots and banquets. The comic tone and perfect reversals (Haman's gallows, the Jews' elevation, the second decree) underscore that the God who hides his face is still the one who "turns the lot" and delivers.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Ahasuerus = Xerxes I?** The name and some court details fit; the 127 provinces and the vast numbers are exaggerated for literary effect.
- **Mordecai's age**: If he was exiled with Jeconiah (2:5-6), he would be over 100; critical views see this as literary compression or two different Mordecais.
- **The second decree**: Allowing self-defense is seen by some as historically implausible (a king reversing a genocide decree); others note Persian law's irrevocability (cf. Dan 6) and the need for a counter-decree.
- **Theological absence of God**: The book's "secularity" is either praised as sophisticated theology of providence or seen as a problem later "corrected" by the Greek additions.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Breneman (NAC) and Harrington (NICOT for Ezra-Nehemiah context) read it as a story of identity, courage, and the hidden hand of God in imperial politics. Jewish liturgy and midrash make it a celebration of survival and joy in the face of hatred. Christian preaching uses it for "such a time as this," the call to risk for others, and the discernment of providence in ordinary events. It has shaped diaspora theology (God with his people even when temple and land are distant) and resistance literature. The reversal theme influences readings of the cross and resurrection (the one plotted against is exalted). In scholarship it is a key text for narrative criticism, irony, and the theology of divine hiddenness.
