# Banarsidas

## Biographical Overview

Banarsidas (1586–1643) was a Jain merchant-poet of north India and the best-documented early Jain layman to emerge as a "clearly defined individual" in the historical record (ch. 7). Born in 1586, he was "the son of a Jain merchant of the Srimali caste who may have belonged to the Kharatara Gaccha" (ch. 7). He lived and worked primarily in Agra and north India.

His life falls into three broad phases: an unconventional youth of amorous affairs and learning; a period of conventional lay piety; and a later mystical-philosophical transformation through the [[digambara|Digambara]]-influenced Adhyatmika movement.

## Primary Source: The *Ardhakhathanaka* ("Half a Story")

Banarsidas recorded the events of his life in a Hindi memoir called *Ardhakhathanaka* ("Half a Story"), "the title referring to the fact that he completed it at the age of fifty-five which he describes as being half the normal span of a man's life" (ch. 7). It was "most likely an apologia written for the edification of his close personal associates" (ch. 7). It is the richest surviving account of the daily life of a Jain merchant in seventeenth-century north India, documenting the insecurities of commerce, the vicissitudes of business failure, and the life of lay religious observance.

## Conventional Lay Life

After a youth the Jain community regarded as inappropriate — amorous affairs and scholarly pursuits rather than commerce — Banarsidas "adopted the habits of a pious layman, worshipping the fordmakers, performing the *samayika*, undertaking the dietary restriction of giving up green vegetables and so on" (ch. 7). In his thirties this attitude intensified and he became "particularly attracted to various forms of lay ritual" (ch. 7). He did not, even in this period, apparently have serious dealings with ascetics, although he studied briefly with a Svetambara monk when young.

## The Adhyatmika Crisis

In 1623, Banarsidas became associated with the **Adhyatmika movement**, "a group of like-minded merchants of Agra who were attracted to mystical exploration of the inner self (*adhyatma*) of the kind advocated by Kundakunda and his Digambara successors" (ch. 7). He was persuaded to read a Hindi commentary on Kundakunda's *Samayasara* ("Essence of the Doctrine"). Initially baffled by the two-levels-of-truth approach, he experienced a crisis of faith:

> My studies had the effect of completely shaking my faith in rituals and in everything else in religion which was just a conventional, outward form. I had wandered into a spiritual void, for although I lost faith in form, I was unable to savour spirit. I hovered between earth and heaven, befouling the air like a camel's fart. (ch. 7)

He then renounced his ritual observances:

> I renounced all rituals, giving up every form of ordained, conventional precept I had so long been religiously observing ... I even broke my vow of never eating green vegetables. In fact there was no end to my disillusionment with outward forms. I felt strangely isolated and everything began to seem alien to me. (ch. 7)

The moral collapse that followed was dramatic: "he fell in with three cronies, passing the time with them in performing dubious pranks and mockeries of religion, such as stripping naked in private and pretending to be Digambara monks, and his generally irreverent behaviour led to the dissipation of his credibility as a Jain and a businessman" (ch. 7).

## Resolution and Synthesis

By 1636 a "famous scholar" came to Agra and expounded the *Gommatasara* ("Essence of Gommata"), a Digambara doctrinal digest written by the ninth-century monk Nemicandra, at the request of the Adhyatmika group. This work's account of the fourteen stages of spiritual quality (*[[gunasthana]]*) provided Banarsidas with a framework that reconciled his two phases of religious life: the early ritual observances could be understood as "valid part of the spiritual path as preliminaries which the true seeker after enlightenment could confidently move beyond" (ch. 7). He scorned the conventional Jain view that the higher states had become impossible:

> The poems I wrote now were written in the true light of the spirit. I realised too that in the ultimate sense, there was no contradiction between what I was writing now and what I had written earlier. There had been a dark spot on my soul, a canker on my faith; it now disappeared. I achieved a vision with which I could see everything with the same untroubled eye: everything was equal, nothing high or low. As God is my witness, I had indeed attained a knowledge akin to supreme realisation. (ch. 7)

Banarsidas died in 1643, "leaving behind an important corpus of Hindi poetry" (ch. 7).

## Significance

Banarsidas is "the best-documented early example of a trend within Jainism, albeit not a dominant one, which authorises lay people to take charge of their own spiritual affairs without reference to ascetic influence" (ch. 7). His career illustrates a basic fact of Jain lay life: "the lay person does not gain identity solely through interaction with ascetics and that a satisfactory and fully Jain religious life may be constructed around events and practices in which ascetics play a minimal or non-existent part" (ch. 7).

The neo-Digambara Adhyatmika movement, of which he was the most prominent member, "lingered on for no more than a century, although its principles are still to be found in the Digambara Terapanthi sect" (not to be confused with the Svetambara sect of the same name) (ch. 7).
