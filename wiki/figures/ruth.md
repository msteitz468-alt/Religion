Ruth the Moabite is the widow who leaves her people and gods to cling to Naomi and YHWH, whose bold initiative on the threshing floor secures redemption, and whose place in the Davidic genealogy signals the inclusion of the nations in Israel's royal line.

## Biographical Overview

A Moabite woman married to Mahlon son of Elimelech and Naomi of Bethlehem. After the deaths of her husband, brother-in-law, and father-in-law, she is faced with Naomi's return to Judah. Against Naomi's urging, Ruth refuses to turn back: "Do not urge me to leave you or to return from following you. For where you go I will go, and where you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God. Where you die I will die, and there will I be buried. May the LORD do so to me and more also if anything but death parts me from you" (1:16–17). She arrives in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest, gleans in the field of Boaz (a kinsman of Elimelech), and is noticed for her diligence and loyalty to Naomi. Naomi devises a plan for permanent security; Ruth goes to the threshing floor at night, uncovers Boaz's feet, and asks him to "spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (3:9). Boaz praises her *ḥesed* (loyalty) as greater than her first (to Naomi) because she has not gone after young men. He agrees to redeem but notes a nearer kinsman; the next morning at the town gate the nearer redeemer declines, and Boaz publicly acquires the land and Ruth as wife "to perpetuate the name of the dead in his inheritance." Ruth bears Obed; the women of the town tell Naomi that Ruth "who loves you... is more to you than seven sons." The book closes with the genealogy: Obed father of Jesse, father of [[david|David]] (4:17–22). Matthew 1:5 places her in the line of the Messiah.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Ruth 1**: Widowhood in Moab; decision to return with Naomi; the famous "your God my God" speech; arrival in Bethlehem at harvest.
- **Ruth 2**: Gleaning in Boaz's field; his protective instructions and generous provision; Naomi's recognition that he is a kinsman-redeemer.
- **Ruth 3**: Threshing-floor initiative; Ruth's request for covering/redeeming; Boaz's praise and promise; Naomi's confident waiting.
- **Ruth 4**: Gate redemption scene; Boaz marries Ruth; birth of Obed; the women's chorus to Naomi; closing genealogy to David.
- **Matthew 1:5**: Named in the genealogy of Jesus (with Tamar, Rahab, and "the wife of Uriah") as wife of Boaz and mother of Obed.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Ruth is the paradigmatic convert (*giyoret*) and model of *ḥesed*. The book is read on Shavuot (harvest/Torah-giving festival). Rabbinic tradition emphasizes her voluntary acceptance of Torah and people ("your God my God") and her reward in the Davidic line. Some midrashim expand on her beauty, piety, and the merit that brought the monarchy through a Moabite. The annual lament custom is not attached to her; the focus is on inclusion and loyalty.

**Christianity**: Ruth exemplifies Gentile inclusion, covenant loyalty beyond law, and the providence that works through ordinary human kindness to bring the Messiah. The "your God my God" speech is a classic conversion text. In the genealogy she (with other "irregular" women) shows that God's grace reaches outsiders and that the line of David/Jesus is carried by surprising vessels. Patristic and medieval readers saw her as a type of the Church (Gentile bride) or of the soul's attachment to Christ/Naomi (Israel or the synagogue). Evangelical readings stress *ḥesed* ethics, the kinsman-redeemer as a picture of Christ, and the missional trajectory from Moab to Bethlehem to the nations. The threshing-floor scene is handled with care (bold but chaste initiative within cultural custom).

**Critical and other**: The book is prized as a literary gem and a counter-voice within the canon (peace and loyalty amid Judges violence). Some read it as a protest against post-exilic exclusionary policies (Ezra-Nehemiah); others as a Davidic apology or as folk etiology for the monarchy's Moabite blood. Honor-shame and social-scientific readings (Lau and others) highlight the risk to reputation and the restoration of Naomi's name and land through Ruth's agency. Postcolonial readings lift the marginalized foreign widow as the one through whom blessing flows.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Ruth is the narrative embodiment of *ḥesed* — covenant loyalty that exceeds obligation and takes personal risk for the sake of the other. Her "cling" (*dābaq*, 1:14) to Naomi mirrors the Deuteronomic call to "cling" to YHWH. The book answers the crisis of emptiness (deaths, famine, loss of name and land) with fullness (harvest, marriage, child, restored name, royal line). Providence is hidden but pervasive: "it happened" (2:3) that she gleans in Boaz's field; the timing of arrival at harvest, the nearness of the redeemer, the nearer kinsman's refusal — all serve the larger purpose. Theologically the story shows that the God of Israel is God of the whole earth (cf. Rahab's confession) and that the Davidic promise (and ultimately the messianic) runs through the foreigner who chooses Israel's God and people. Narratively it is the quiet counter to the downward spiral of Judges: where the epilogues show chaos and self-destruction, Ruth shows *ḥesed*, wise initiative, public justice at the gate, and the birth of hope. The closing genealogy makes the "grand narrative" claim: this Moabite widow is great-grandmother of David.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Date of composition**: Monarchic (apology for David's Moabite blood or celebration of *ḥesed* in the united monarchy) vs. post-exilic (protest against or model for inclusion after Ezra-Nehemiah). Linguistic and legal features are debated.
- **Legal background**: Levirate marriage (Deut 25) vs. redemption of land (Lev 25) vs. a blended custom. The gate scene assumes knowledge of redemption procedures; whether the nearer kinsman is legally obligated to marry Ruth is disputed.
- **Threshing-floor night**: Cultural custom of "uncovering the feet" as marriage proposal or bold risk of reputation/virtue? The text presents it as chaste and courageous; modern readers differ on the sexual overtones.
- **Moabite inclusion**: Tension with Deut 23:3–6 (Moabites excluded to the tenth generation) vs. the book's celebration of Ruth. The canonical tension is part of the point: *ḥesed* and election can override strict application.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Lau (NICOT) brings honor-shame, social-identity, and missional lenses: Ruth risks shame and status to practice *ḥesed*; the book models boundary-crossing for the sake of the other and the inclusion of the nations in the royal line. The story is central to Jewish liturgy (Shavuot) and to Christian ethics of loyalty, hospitality, and care for the widow/foreigner. In feminist and womanist readings Ruth and Naomi are agents of their own redemption; Boaz is the enabler rather than the sole hero. The kinsman-redeemer has long been read typologically of Christ (redemption of lost inheritance, taking the outsider as bride). The genealogy keeps the book tethered to the larger Davidic/messianic hope. Modern scholarship values it as a literary and theological masterpiece that subverts expectations about who carries the promise.
