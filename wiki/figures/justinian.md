# Justinian I

Byzantine emperor (527–565) who, beyond his legal codification and reconquests, intervened decisively in doctrine — the imperial architect of the **[[neo-chalcedonianism|Neo-Chalcedonian]]** settlement. Drawn from [[pelikan-emergence-catholic-tradition|Jaroslav Pelikan]] (Vol. I, ch. 5).

## Doctrinal Role

Justinian sought to reconcile the [[oriental-orthodoxy|miaphysite]] East to [[chalcedonian-definition|Chalcedon]] by reading the council in a [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyrilline]] sense. His 551 confession declared God the Logos "one of the Holy Trinity, *homoousios* with God the Father according to divinity, *homoousios* with us according to humanity; passible as to the flesh, and yet the same One is also impassible as to the divinity" — endorsing the [[theopaschism|theopaschite]] formula ("one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh") and targeting the [[nestorianism|theology of the indwelling Logos]].

He drove the condemnation of the **Three Chapters** ([[theodore-of-mopsuestia|Theodore of Mopsuestia]], [[theodoret-of-cyrus|Theodoret]]'s anti-Cyrilline works, and Ibas's letter) and convened the **Second Council of Constantinople (553)**, which ratified his agenda, condemned the Three Chapters (and the name of [[origen|Origen]]), and vindicated Chalcedon "in the tone… given to it by the imperial theology that prevailed." Pelikan's verdict: the christological problem "was not settled at the Second Council of Constantinople much more effectively than it had been at Chalcedon."

## In Dante's Paradiso

Justinian is the single speaker of [[paradiso|Paradiso]] 6 — the only canto voiced
entirely by one soul — met in the sphere of **Mercury** (the blessed who sought
honourable fame). He narrates the providential history of the **Roman Eagle** from
Aeneas through Caesar, Augustus's universal peace (preparing for Christ), and on to
the Guelf–Ghibelline abuse of the imperial sign, framing Dante's own imperial
political theology (cf. *De Monarchia*). Dante has him recount his correction from
**Monophysitism** by Pope Agapetus before his legal codification — "I'd thought of
Christ as single and divine — not truly man" (6:13–14) — though Kirkpatrick notes the
chronology is likely Dante's reinvention. In the following canto Beatrice builds on
Justinian's claim that Titus's sack of Jerusalem was a *just* vengeance to expound the
[[atonement|Atonement]] (a contention Kirkpatrick flags as "highly problematic").
