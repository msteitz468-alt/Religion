# Guru Nanak

Founder of [[sikhism|Sikhism]] and first of the ten Gurus (1469–1539). Born at Talwandi (Nankana
Sahib) in the Punjab; his hymns form the doctrinal core of the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth
Sahib]]. His life is transmitted through the hagiographic **[[janam-sakhis|janam-sākhīs]]**.

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Chs 1, 5.)*

## Biographical Overview

Born 1469 at Talvandi (a Khatri family west of Lahore); joined his sister's household at **Sultanpur**,
took employment with a local lord, and there underwent the religious experience that launched his
preaching. He married and had two sons, **Lakhmi Das** and **Siri Chand**; eventually he founded the
village of **Kartarpur** on the Ravi, where he preached until his death in 1539. He chose his disciple
**Lahina — renamed [[guru-angad|Angad]] — as successor over both sons**, who protested; Siri Chand,
drawn to Nath yogis, is traditionally regarded as founder of the [[udasi|Udasi]] order (agent 1
~L551–983). McLeod stresses that reliable biographical data are thin: the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi
Granth]] hymns yield little biography, and the [[janam-sakhis|janam-sākhīs]] are theologically rich
but historically unreliable.

## Teaching

Nanak's central message is **liberation through the divine Name**, freely open to all regardless of
caste: the discipline of **[[nam-simaran|nām simaraṇ]]** — remembrance of the Name in *satsang* and
*kīrtan* and amid everyday work. His God is **[[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]]**, formless and without
gender, whose self-expression is **[[hukam|hukam]]** (the divine Order); the saved person is the
**[[gurmukh-manmukh|gurmukh]]**, turned toward the Guru, against the self-centred *manmukh* ruled by
**[[haumai|haumai]]**. McLeod situates this within the **[[sant-tradition|Sant tradition]]** (nirguṇa
theology, interior devotion), explicitly **rejecting the popular view that Nanak's teaching is a
Hindu-Muslim syncretism**: his use of Islamic terms (as in the *Var Mājh* shalok, "make mercy your
mosque and devotion your prayer-mat") is rhetorical, addressed to a Muslim audience, while "the actual
content remains that of the Sants" (agent 3 ~L353–418). His *Bābur-vāṇī* hymns on the Mughal invasion
he reads as religious (the fate of the unrighteous), not political.

## Significance and Reception

The founder-Guru and the first of ten through whom "a single flame is transmitted to nine successive
torches" — the doctrine of the **unity of the Guruship**. Whether Nanak is the "founder" of a religion
is, for McLeod, a matter of definition: in his own day the **Nanak-panth** was "one panth among many"
and would have been seen as a Hindu panth; Sikhism's emergence as a distinct system he dates to the
later Singh Sabha (see [[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]] and [[sant-tradition|the Sant question]]).

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Ch. 2)

Khushwant Singh, less reticent than McLeod about biographical specifics, dates Nanak's birth to **15 April
1469** at Talwandi Rai Bhoe (now Nankana Sahib), the son of the accountant Mehta Kalian Das; he places
the mystic experience — three days "drowned" in the river at Sultanpur, returning to declare "There is no
Hindu, there is no Mussalman" — around 1499. He narrates the extensive travels (east to Bengal and Assam,
south possibly to Ceylon, and west to Mecca, Medina, and Baghdad), the witnessing of Babar's sack of Saidpur
(the *Bābar Vānī*), and the settling at [[kartarpur|Kartarpur]] with its daily routine of bath, hymn-singing,
and congregational prayer. Singh systematizes the teaching as strict monotheism (God as *sat*, *niraṅkār*),
the institution of the **Guru** as "the pivot of his religious system," the householder ideal (*grihastha
dharma*) against asceticism, the casteless **[[langar|langar]]**, and the three commandments *kirt karo, nām
japo, vaṅḍ chako* ("work, worship, and give in charity"). Like McLeod he resists pure syncretism — Sikhism
"had features which bore no resemblance to either" Hinduism or Islam — and closes with the couplet remembering
Nanak as "*Hindū kā guru, musalmān kā pīr*."
