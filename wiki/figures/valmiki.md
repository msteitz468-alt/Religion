# Vālmīki

**Vālmīki** (*Adikavi*, "the first poet") is the legendary sage-poet credited with composing the Sanskrit [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]. In Buck's retelling he is simultaneously a character *within* the story he composed — he shelters [[sita|Sītā]], raises Rāma's sons Kuśa and Lava, and teaches them the Rāmāyaṇa — creating the recursive structure in which the poem contains its own origin and its own first performance. The text repeatedly insists: "Before Ramayana there was no poetry on Earth" (line 1048).

## The Legend of Vālmīki as Told in Buck's Retelling

### The Search and the Anthill

As a young man Vālmīki searched the world "seeking open friendship and happiness and hope, and finding none of these he went alone into the empty forest where no man lived, to a spot near where the Tamasa river flows into Ganga" (lines 1049–1052). There he "sat for years without moving, so still that white ants built an anthill over him" (lines 1053–1054). He sat inside the anthill "for thousands of years with only his eyes showing out, trying to find the True, his hands folded and his mind lost in contemplation" (lines 1055–1057).

### Narada's Summons

One cloudy winter's day at noon, the heavenly sage Nārada — described as "the inventor of music, born from Brahma's mind" — flew from heaven and said: "Come out! Help me!" (lines 1058–1060). Vālmīki refused: "It's too cold. Away with the worlds, where a little pleasure costs a lot of pain" (line 1061). Nārada told him about Rāma, ruler in Ayodhyā: brave, gentle, firm in fight, of the Solar race. He described how Sītā was being brought in a chariot and would be abandoned at Ganga's bank — "unless you comfort her she will drown herself in Ganga and kill as well her unborn sons by Rama" (line 1077). Vālmīki asked what she had done wrong. Nārada: "Nothing. Sita is innocent and blameless" (line 1079).

Nārada told Vālmīki that he had already sung a "friend-gathering song" while approaching; hermit families had assembled around him while he sat motionless in the anthill (lines 1087–1094). He urged Vālmīki to act before Sītā drowned herself: "Here they come across Ganga in a boat — will you also give way and forsake Sita too from fear of other people?" (lines 1097–1098). Vālmīki "stood up and broke free out of that hard anthill" (line 1110). He found around him "many houses of hermits and their families, young trees carefully watered, a retreat cleared from the forest" (lines 1111–1112). Four boys ran up to tell him about the weeping woman at Ganga's bank.

### Vālmīki Receives Sītā

Vālmīki ran to Sītā on the riverbank: "Sita, stay here in my hermitage, you have found here your father's house in a foreign land, we will care for you as our daughter" (lines 1119–1121). The hermit wives surrounded Sītā and took her to their homes.

### The Invention of the Shloka

Vālmīki went alone to Ganga to bathe, "washed away the anthill dust," and made new clothes from bark. Resting against a stone, he watched two small white waterbirds in a nearby tree. "The mate bird was singing to his mate when before Valmiki's eyes an arrow hit him, and the little bird fell from the limb" (lines 1131–1133). Dead, blood drops on his feathers. "Heartbroken, the dead bird's mate cried —" (lines 1135–1140).

A bird-hunter came from the forest. Vālmīki's heart was pounding and he cursed the killer:

> *You will find no rest for the long years of Eternity,*
> *For you killed a bird in love and unsuspecting.* (lines 1144–1145)

The hunter died that day. Vālmīki reflected: "Those words I cursed him with make a verse, and that verse could be sung to music" (lines 1151–1152).

For days the verse ran through his mind. On the **fourth day after Sītā's rescue**, Lord Brahmā appeared at Vālmīki's retreat — described as an old man with red skin, white hair, four arms, four faces on one head, holding a ladle, a rosary, a waterpot, and a holy book (lines 1155–1159). Even sitting in Brahmā's presence, Vālmīki could think only of the two waterbirds and the world's lack of mercy (lines 1162–1167). Brahmā said: "by a river, the world's first verse has been born from pity, and love and compassion for a tiny bird has made you a poet. Use your discovery to tell Rama's story, and your verses will defeat Time. As you make your poem, Rama's life will be revealed to you, and no word of yours will be untrue" (lines 1170–1174).

### The Vision in the Water

After Brahmā returned to heaven on his swan-chariot, Vālmīki sat every day facing East on a grass mat: "He held a little water cupped in his hands, and looking down into it he clearly saw Rama and Sita. He saw them move, he heard them talk and laugh. He saw all Rama's life happen within the water; he held the past world in his palm; and part by part he made Ramayana delightful to the ear, pleasing to the mind, and a true happiness to the heart" (lines 1177–1184).

## His Role in the Recursive Frame Structure

### Kusa and Lava

Not many months after Vālmīki began his poem, Sītā's twin sons were born. Vālmīki named them **Kuśa and Lava** (line 1186). They grew to resemble Rāma "as the Moon's reflection resembles the Moon himself" (line 1187). All the hermit women loved them. "As Valmiki composed Ramayana, and as Kusa and Lava grew old enough to learn, he taught it to them by memory" (lines 1193–1194). When they were twelve years old they "knew every foot of it, and sang Ramayana to a lute and a drum, like Gandharvas, the heavenly musicians" (lines 1196–1198).

### The Festival Performance

That year Rāma held a year's celebration in Naimisha Forest. Vālmīki kept Sītā hidden but sent Kuśa and Lava to sing the Rāmāyaṇa in a clearing. "When they started to sing the other business of the festival stopped. They held their hearers motionless. For a year they sang some of Ramayana every morning, and never told their names" (lines 1218–1221). Every day King Rāma came to listen; he "looked long at his two sons he had never seen and wondered who they were, and beside Rama stood a golden statue of Sita, for Rama loved her though he had sent her away" (lines 1221–1224).

The twins began: "We sing a song of kingly fame — Oh Listen . . ." (lines 1226–1228).

## The Outer Frame: Sauti and Saunaka

Buck's text opens with a frame within a frame. **Sauti the storyteller** told the tale of the Rāmāyaṇa to his friend **Saunaka in Naimisha Forest** after finishing the Mahābhārata (lines 1013–1015). Saunaka asked about the monkey Hanumān — who appeared in the Mahābhārata — and about "that story of Rama, which keeps Hanuman alive so long as it is told by men on Earth" (lines 1016–1020). Sauti described the Rāmāyaṇa as "a tale of romance and love and of wild adventure, the very crest-jewel of poetry and a legend of the times and worlds of old. . . . Ramayana is beyond Time" (lines 1021–1026). Rāma ruled the earth for eleven thousand years (line 1030), and "this very Naimisha Forest" was part of his kingdom (lines 1031–1033).

Sauti then identifies the third-age speaker's relation to the story: "We live now in the third age of Time, and Rama lived in the second age of the world" (lines 1041–1042). Vālmīki enters as the composer who gave form to what Sauti is retelling: "Valmiki the Poet put the deeds of Rama into musical verses; he clothed them in the sound of singing" (lines 1044–1047). The narrative thus moves: Vālmīki composes → Kuśa and Lava perform → Sauti retells to Saunaka → the reader hears.

## Hermeneutical and Theological Significance

Vālmīki's transformation from seeker who "found none" of the good things he searched for, to the "first poet" born of compassion for a dying bird, models a specific account of how poetry originates. Brahmā's visit makes explicit that the origin of verse is not craft but pity: "love and compassion for a tiny bird has made you a poet" (line 1171). The verse's content — grief that a bird was killed in the act of love — is ethically prior to its form. This founding story makes the Rāmāyaṇa explicitly a text of grief-transformed-into-beauty, with Sītā's own abandonment (the occasion for Vālmīki's emergence from the anthill) as its structural counterpart: both the origin of the poem and the origin of Vālmīki as a social being are acts of rescue.

The vision in the water (lines 1177–1184) adds an epistemological claim: the poem's truth is guaranteed not by scholarship but by divine vision. Brahmā told him "no word of yours will be untrue" (line 1174).

## Valmiki's Second Emergence: The Later Books (Lines 13566–16313)

### Receiving Sita at the Ganges

In the "Dharma Wheel" section (the Uttara Kanda), Valmiki appears again at the moment Sita is abandoned
by Lakshmana at the Ganga-Tamasa confluence. Narada (having been roused by Vibhishana) tells Lakshmana
that the hermit Valmiki lives "inside an anthill… near where Tamasa flows into Ganga below" (l. 14930–14933).
As Sita stands alone by the river — tempted by the Ganga goddess to enter the water — "a wild shaggy man
ran to her, covered with dust from an anthill, with hair and beard long as eternity" and said: "I am
Valmiki. Make my hermitage your home" (l. 15173–15176). The hermit wives lead her to a new house of
leaves "that had just been created by one of Narada's songs" (l. 15182–15183) — placing Valmiki's
hermitage squarely inside the cosmic poetic order.

### Naming of Kusha and Lava (Repeat with New Detail)

The later narrative confirms and extends the earlier account: born at night in summer, the twins were
brought to Valmiki by midwives; he named them by rubbing the firstborn with kusa grass tips (→ Kusa)
and the second with the cut ends (→ Lava) (l. 15185–15192). "For twelve years Sita and her sons lived
with Valmiki while he composed this Ramayana. He taught Kusa and Lava every verse over the years and
they learned to sing this story to the music of a lute and drum" (l. 15193–15197).

### The Naimisha Festival and the Meeting with Rama

When Satrughna came to Valmiki's hermitage to deliver Rama's festival invitation, Valmiki "talked with
great gratitude of the battle of Lanka, as if it had happened just before his eyes, and not ten thousand
years before" (l. 15234–15236). Satrughna heard Kusa and Lava singing in the dark forest that night
but wisely did not investigate: "There are many mysteries here, and it is not for us to speak of them
at all" (l. 15291–15292).

At the festival, Valmiki's instructions to Kusa and Lava reveal his full pedagogical intention: feed
them mangoes to make their voices and memory permanent; send them to sing near Rama so "he will hear
his name and come"; "each day before you start, first bow to the King as to a father, for he is the
father of all the living people in his land" (l. 15440–15452). The paradox — bow to your father as a
stranger — structures the entire year's performance.

On the last day, after Rama recognizes the twins as his sons and sends Hanuman to fetch Valmiki,
their meeting is the poem's emotional culmination. Valmiki confirms: "Those two boys are your sons Kusa
and Lava. They were born in my retreat" (l. 15593–15594). Rama praises the poem: "You have brought
verse into the world, and now anyone can start a poem, but you have brought yours to a blazing wonderful
finish. You've brought it all together; you did it right!" (l. 15596–15598). Valmiki asks — with
touching candor — "Did you like it, Rama?" Rama: "It is excellent, Valmiki, I say it is excellent. I
shall never forget those verses" (l. 15600–15602).

Valmiki's reply gently corrects Rama's impulse toward divine self-congratulation: "Lord Rama like the
Moon, you are a mortal man. She loves you. The bounty of Earth is not your doing, Majesty" (l. 15613–15614).

### After Sita's Departure

After Sita enters the earth and the festival ends, "Kusa and Lava and the poet Valmiki returned to
their forest home" (l. 15864–15865). Rama rules for a thousand more years. The frame narrator Sauti
notes that Valmiki later "composed the last part of Ramayana" — a thousand years after the festival —
and that Kusa and Lava sang these additional verses as grown men to a forest audience (l. 15459–15477).

The closing frame has Sauti describe Valmiki as "seeking the True before he ever was a poet, and when
it all was done he sang — I'm still myself, I'm me. Here is the True, have no fear: / This is for you,
for you are dear / To me; I'm still myself" (l. 16304–16309).
