# Teacher of Righteousness
**Hebrew**: *Moreh ha-Tzedek* (מורה הצדק)

The founding leader and supreme authority of the Qumran sect, referred to throughout the pesharim and the Damascus Document by the honorific title *Moreh ha-Tzedek* — "Teacher of Righteousness" or "Righteous Teacher." He is never named in any surviving scroll; his identity in historical terms remains a matter of scholarly debate.

## Biographical Overview
The scrolls provide an indirect portrait assembled from references across multiple documents. Vermes synthesizes the following picture:

**Origin**: A **Zadokite priest** (possibly) displaced from the Jerusalem high priesthood when the Hasmonaeans assumed priestly office after the Maccabaean revolt. CD implies the community existed in a leaderless state for "twenty years" before God raised up the Teacher to guide them.

**Divine Commission**: The Teacher's authority rests on a divine revelatory gift: God "made known to him all the mysteries of the words of His servants the Prophets" (1QpHab II:8). This is not scholarly exegesis but prophetic revelation — the Teacher is the authorized interpreter of all Scripture, receiving meanings the prophets themselves did not know.

**Persecution**: The Teacher was attacked by two opponents:
- The **Wicked Priest** (= Jonathan Maccabaeus per Vermes, 153–143 BCE) who confronted him physically "at the house of his exile" on the Day of Atonement, violating the community's calendar-observance
- The **Liar / Spouter of Lies**, a rival teacher within or adjacent to the community who rejected the Teacher's Law-interpretation and formed a rival congregation

**Death**: CD XIX–XX speaks of the period "from the day of the gathering in of the Teacher of the Community until the arising of the Messiah of Aaron and Israel," implying the Teacher has died. The community persists in an interim between Teacher and Messiah.

## Primary Source Appearances

**Damascus Document (CD I)**: "In the age of wrath… He visited them, and He caused a plant root to spring from Israel and Aaron to inherit His land and to prosper on the good things of His earth. And they perceived their iniquity and recognized that they were guilty men, yet for twenty years they were like blind men groping for the way. And God observed their deeds, that they sought Him with a whole heart, and He raised for them a Teacher of Righteousness to guide them in the way of His heart."

**Habakkuk Commentary (1QpHab)**: The Teacher is the one "to whom God made known all the mysteries of the words of His servants the Prophets." The Wicked Priest "pursued the Teacher of Righteousness to the house of his exile… to confuse him… on the Day of Atonement." Salvation comes to those who have "faith in the Teacher of Righteousness."

**Commentary on Psalms (4Q171)**: The Teacher is "the Priest, the Teacher of [Righteousness] whom God chose to stand before Him… to explain all the words of His servants the Prophets." The Wicked Priest "watched the Righteous Man in order to put him to death."

**Thanksgiving Hymns (1QH)**: The Teacher hymns speak in the first person about intense personal suffering, divine protection, founding a community that persecutes him, and divine revelation. The Teacher presents himself as: "a trap for sinners but healing for all who repent of transgression"; as one born into a "covenant" from the womb; as a "father to the sons of mercy"; facing a "city of refuge" being besieged.

**4Q541 (eschatological priestly figure)**: Though formally about an eschatological figure rather than the historical Teacher, this text describes a priestly atoner who is slandered by contemporaries, teaches "according to God's will," and around whom "the people will go astray in his days" — widely seen as a prototype for the historical Teacher (or the reverse: the historical Teacher as the type for the eschatological figure).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Within the Qumran sect**: The Teacher functions as the community's prophet and founding interpreter. His Torah-interpretation (*pesher* readings of Scripture) is authoritative and binding. After his death the community awaits the coming of two Messiahs and a Prophet.

**Modern scholarship (outside the ingested text)**: Various identifications have been proposed — a displaced Zadokite priest following the Maccabaean assumption of the high priesthood; possibly Onias III or Onias IV; left unidentified by Vermes. The Teacher is not identified with any known biblical or historical figure by name in any scroll.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Teacher's role in the community's theology is **hermeneutical**: he is the mediating figure between the prophetic text and the community's experience. Just as Moses mediated the Torah, the Teacher mediates the prophetic interpretation of Torah for the end-time community. "Faith" (*emunah*) in the Teacher (1QpHab VIII) is the condition for salvation — loyalty to his teaching is the mechanism of righteous standing before God.

This creates a functional parallel to the role of Jesus of Nazareth in early Christianity (the authoritative interpreter of Torah whose faithfulness/faith is the condition of justification), though with crucial differences: the Teacher is a priest-interpreter, not a messianic king or divine figure; his death is not interpreted soteriologically (not an atoning sacrifice in the scrolls); and the coming Messiah is a distinct future figure, not a retroactive identification with the Teacher himself.

## Historicity and Controversies
The Teacher of Righteousness is almost universally accepted as a historical figure by modern scholars. The debate focuses on identification:
- Vermes: Zadokite priest, c. 150 BCE; Teacher may have died by Jonathan Maccabaeus's machinations; Wicked Priest = Jonathan Maccabaeus (153–143 BCE)
- Stegemann: The Teacher was the legitimate Zadokite high priest displaced when Jonathan assumed the office in 152 BCE
- **Groningen Hypothesis** (García Martínez): The "Wicked Priest" designation refers not to a single person but to an **office** held by six successive Hasmonean high priests. VanderKam (2010) notes the hypothesis has attractions — it explains inconsistencies between pesher passages — but its implausibility lies in the fact that 1QpHab treats the Wicked Priest in a coherent narrative of one individual's career (rise, corruption, confrontation with the Teacher, defeat, death). Multiple referents would make this narrative incoherent. See [[wicked-priest-identity]].
- The scrolls themselves are deliberately anonymous concerning the Teacher's personal identity; no surviving scroll names him

**VanderKam's more cautious stance**: VanderKam (2010) does not firmly endorse any single identification for the Wicked Priest, noting that Vermes's Jonathan identification is plausible but not the only option, and that the Groningen Hypothesis is a live scholarly debate without a consensus resolution.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
The Teacher figure may underlie several Second Temple and early Christian characterizations:
- **4Q541's eschatological priest**: pre-existing archetype or derivative image
- **The Suffering Servant (Isaiah 52–53)**: Thanksgiving Hymns draw heavily on Servant language; whether the Teacher identified himself with the Servant or vice versa is debated
- **John the Baptist**: Some scholars (not Vermes) have proposed a connection; the scrolls give no direct evidence
- **James the Just**: Eisenman (not endorsed by Vermes) identified the Teacher with James; Vermes rejects this strongly
