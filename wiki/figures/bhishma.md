## Biographical Overview

Bhīṣma was born Devavrata, the eighth son of King Śāntanu and the goddess Gaṅgā. His birth arose from a cosmic curse: the eight Vasus (divine beings) had stolen the sage Vasiṣṭha's divine cow Nandinī at the insistence of one of their wives. Vasiṣṭha cursed all eight Vasus to be born as mortals; the seven who did not steal the cow were to be freed immediately at birth, while Prabhāsa, who had led the theft, was fated to live a long life on earth. The goddess Gaṅgā agreed to be their mother and married Śāntanu on the condition that he never question her actions. She cast the first seven infants into the Ganges at birth (liberating each from the curse), but when Śāntanu finally cried out to stop her from drowning the eighth, she departed, taking the child with her. She returned Devavrata to his father years later, when the boy had already mastered archery (equalling Paraśurāma), the Vedas and Vedānta under Vasiṣṭha, and statecraft under Śukra (lines 640–651).

Śāntanu crowned Devavrata as heir-apparent (yuvarāja). Some years later, the king fell deeply in love with Satyavatī, a fisherwoman, but her father would consent to the marriage only if her son became king instead of Devavrata. Unable to make this promise without dispossessing his own heir, Śāntanu fell into silent anguish. Devavrata discovered the cause, went to the fisherman himself, and voluntarily renounced his claim to the throne. When the fisherman then worried that Devavrata's own future sons might contest the crown, Devavrata made his supreme renunciation: he vowed lifelong celibacy. At the utterance of this "terrible vow," the gods showered flowers on him and the air rang with cries of "Bhīṣma" — one who makes and keeps a terrible vow — and that name replaced Devavrata from that moment (lines 705–726).

Satyavatī bore Śāntanu two sons: Citrangada (killed in battle childless) and Vicitravīrya. Bhīṣma governed the kingdom as regent while Vicitravīrya was a minor, then sought brides for him from among the daughters of the king of Kāśī.

## Primary Source Appearances

**Chapter I (lines 557–652):** Birth and divine origin — Ganga, the Vasus' curse, Śāntanu's marriage and Devavrata's childhood. Establishes Bhīṣma as born of a goddess to fulfil a cosmic purpose, yet fated to live long on earth.

**Chapter II (lines 654–733):** The Bhīṣma-pratigya (terrible vow) — the pivotal scene where Devavrata becomes Bhīṣma. Establishes his renunciation of both kingship and fatherhood as the definitive ethical act of his life. Notes the dynastic chain: Vicitravīrya → Dhritarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu → the Kauravas and the Pāṇḍavas.

**Chapter III (lines 734–847):** Bhīṣma and Ambā — the most morally complex episode of his early life. He forcibly carries off the three daughters of the king of Kāśī (Ambā, Ambikā, Ambālikā) for Vicitravīrya's marriage. Ambā reveals she has already mentally chosen Śalva; Bhīṣma releases her. When Śalva rejects her and Vicitravīrya refuses to marry a woman whose heart belongs to another, Ambā is trapped. She cannot marry Bhīṣma (who must keep his vow) and cannot return to Śalva. After years of baffled seeking for a champion, she wins the grace of Śiva, who promises her revenge in her next birth. She immolates herself, is reborn as Drupada's daughter, eventually undergoes a sex change, and becomes the warrior Śikhaṇḍin. At Kurukṣetra, Arjuna fights screened behind Śikhaṇḍin, because Bhīṣma (knowing Śikhaṇḍin was born female) refuses on grounds of chivalry to fight him. Thus Bhīṣma falls.

**Later chapters (passing references):** Bhīṣma appears consistently throughout the narrative as the family patriarch, counsellor, and moral voice. He appointed Vidura as chief counsellor to Dhritarāṣṭra (Chapter VII). He advised inviting the Pāṇḍavas back and giving them half the kingdom (Chapter XVII). At the Rājasuya, it was Bhīṣma who recommended honouring Kṛṣṇa first (Chapter XXI). He sat in the dice hall knowing the game would end viciously but unable to prevent it (Chapter XXIV).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bhīṣma's vow is the pivotal structural event from which the entire narrative of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] flows. By renouncing both kingship and progeny for his father's sake, he creates the conditions for the weakened, morally compromised succession that eventually tears the family apart. Rajagopalachari frames this as the tragedy of an irreproachable individual whose superlative personal virtue produces catastrophic institutional consequences.

His role in the Ambā story encodes a counterpoint: the vow that is the source of his greatness is simultaneously the instrument of his gravest injustice. He has the power to rectify Ambā's plight (by marrying her himself) but cannot without undoing the supreme act of his character. Rajagopalachari does not moralise; the contradiction is left to stand.

At Kurukṣetra, Bhīṣma's chivalric refusal to fight Śikhaṇḍin (a warrior he knows was born female) is the direct mechanism of his death — his ethical code is the weapon his enemies use against him. As he falls, he identifies the arrows by archer: "This is Arjuna's arrow and not Śikhaṇḍin's" (line 847), which Rajagopalachari presents as characteristic dignity.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari treats all events as part of a legendary tradition; no historical-critical distinction is drawn. The Vasus' cosmic backstory is retold as integral to the epic's theology: Bhīṣma is the long-lived Vasu Prabhāsa on earth, fulfilling his penance.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Within Rajagopalachari's retelling, Bhīṣma is repeatedly cited as the embodiment of dharmic wisdom. Other characters — Dhritarāṣṭra, Vidura, Duryodhana — are judged partly by how they stand in relation to Bhīṣma's counsel. His counsel to give the Pāṇḍavas half the kingdom (Chapter XVII) is presented as the paradigm of right action; Duryodhana's rejection of it as the paradigm of failure.
