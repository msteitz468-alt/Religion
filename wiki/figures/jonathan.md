# Jonathan

**Jonathan**, eldest son of [[saul|Saul]] and heir to Israel's first throne, is the
warrior-prince whose **covenant of love with [[david|David]]** — the man destined to
displace his own dynasty — is one of the most celebrated friendships in Scripture. He dies
with his father and brothers at Mount Gilboa, and David's elegy over him ("your love to me
was wonderful, surpassing the love of women") is among the great poems of the Hebrew Bible.

## Biographical Overview and Primary Appearances

- **The warrior** — Jonathan is the real military hero of Saul's early reign: he strikes the
  Philistine garrison at Geba and, with only his armor-bearer, routs the Philistine outpost
  at Michmash by daring faith ("nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few,"
  1 Sam 14). He is nearly executed under Saul's rash battlefield oath after unknowingly
  eating honey, and is ransomed by the people.
- **Covenant with David** — After David kills Goliath, "the soul of Jonathan was bound to
  the soul of David," and Jonathan makes a covenant with him, giving David his own robe,
  armor, and weapons — a symbolic transfer of royal prerogative (1 Sam 18).
- **Protecting David** — As Saul's hostility hardens, Jonathan repeatedly intercedes for and
  warns David, culminating in the arrow-signal in the field and their tearful parting, and a
  renewed covenant binding their houses (1 Sam 20; 23:16–18, where Jonathan acknowledges
  David will be king).
- **Death** — Jonathan falls with Saul and his brothers at Gilboa (1 Sam 31). David laments
  Saul and Jonathan in the elegy "How are the mighty fallen" (2 Sam 1). David later shows
  covenant loyalty to Jonathan's lame son **Mephibosheth** (2 Sam 9).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jonathan is the figure who, though heir by blood, freely **recognizes and yields to David's
election** — the legitimate prince endorsing the LORD's anointed against his own dynastic
interest. His covenant love both humanizes the transfer of the kingdom from the house of Saul
to David and supplies the narrative's strongest internal witness to David's rightful
kingship. His battlefield faith also contrasts with his father's anxious, oracle-hunting
leadership.

## Alter's Reading

[[robert-alter|Alter]] ([[alter-the-david-story|*The David Story*]]) sets Jonathan within the
narrative's **paired episodes** and its theme of knowledge: Jonathan, like the people, can
read the situation his father cannot. The covenant scenes function, in the narrative's
political logic, partly as David's legitimation — Jonathan's love is genuine and also,
within the story's design, the Saulide heir's own testimony that the kingdom belongs to
David. The intensity of the language of love between the two men, and David's elegy, Alter
treats as part of the writer's willingness to give his characters an inner and emotional life
that exceeds mere political function.

## Reception

- **Judaism** — The friendship of David and Jonathan is cited in the Mishnah (*Avot* 5:16)
  as the model of "love that does not depend on any [transient] thing" and therefore endures.
- **Christianity** — Jonathan's selfless love and his yielding to the anointed king were read
  as exemplary; David's lament has furnished liturgy and music for centuries. The relationship
  has also been the focus of modern debate over whether the language connotes covenant
  friendship or erotic love — a dispute the terse text does not resolve.
