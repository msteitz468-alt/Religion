**Biographical Overview**:
Simeon was a righteous and devout Jewish man living in Jerusalem. According to the Gospel of Luke, it had been revealed to him by the Holy Spirit that he would not see death before he had seen the Lord's Messiah. He is presented as the ideal of expectant Second Temple piety at the moment of fulfillment. No other canonical text mentions him by name.

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Luke**: Simeon appears only in Luke 2:25-35. Led by the Spirit into the temple at the precise moment of Jesus' presentation, he takes the child in his arms, blesses God with the Nunc Dimittis ("Lord, now you are letting your servant depart in peace..."), and delivers a prophetic oracle to Mary about the child's destiny for "the falling and rising of many in Israel" and as "a sign that will be opposed" (2:34-35). Green stresses the Spirit's threefold emphasis (2:25, 26, 27).

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Lukan Narrative (Christianity)**: Joel B. Green's commentary notes that Simeon exemplifies the highest ideals of Second Temple Jewish piety—he is "righteous and devout" (δίκαιος καὶ εὐλαβής) and looks forward to the "consolation of Israel" (παράκλησις). This consolation implies both spiritual redemption and socio-political deliverance from foreign subjection. The repeated emphasis on the Holy Spirit upon him marks him as a prophetic voice bridging the epoch of Israel and the arrival of the Messiah. He is paired with Anna as male/female witnesses.
- **Early Church / Patristic**: Celebrated in the Presentation feast (Hypapante / Candlemas); Nunc Dimittis incorporated into daily liturgy (Compline in West, Vespers in East). Typological reading as the last of the OT righteous who sees the fulfillment.
- **Eastern Orthodox / Catholic**: Venerated as saint; central in Presentation iconography (with Anna, Mary, Joseph, Jesus); Nunc Dimittis as prayer for peaceful death.
- **Protestant**: Emphasizes his expectant faith, the universal scope of the oracle ("light for revelation to the Gentiles"), and the warning of division as characteristic of Jesus' mission.
- **Jewish**: No independent tradition; his portrait draws on biblical models of righteous waiters (e.g., the "consolation" language echoes Isa 40, 49, 61).
- **Broader**: In liberation and postcolonial readings, Simeon's oracle highlights the political dimensions of salvation (redemption from enemies) and the "sign of opposition" as critique of empire and religious establishment.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Simeon's oracle explicitly introduces the universal scope of Luke's salvation narrative at the heart of Israel's worship (the temple). He declares Jesus "a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel" (2:32)—the first explicit universalism in the Gospel, anticipating the mission to the ends of the earth in Acts. The warning to Mary foreshadows the conflict, rejection, and suffering that define Jesus' path (and the church's). Green emphasizes that Simeon sets the theological trajectory: God's redemptive purpose advances through and overcomes human hostility. Paired with Anna, he signals the inclusive outpouring of the Spirit on all (male/female, old/young). The Nunc Dimittis is a prayer of fulfillment and release—"now" the servant can depart in peace because the promises are realized.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Historical Simeon**: Highly stylized scene (Spirit leading at exact moment, poetic oracle). Critical scholarship often views as Lukan theological construction to introduce universalism and conflict themes. Green and conservative readers affirm a historical core of pious expectation at the temple while recognizing literary artistry.
- **"Consolation of Israel"**: Debate over spiritual vs. political dimensions; Green notes both are present in Second Temple expectations.
- **Mary's sword (2:35)**: Interpreted as sorrow at the cross, or as the division Jesus causes (even within families).

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **Green (Luke)**: Simeon sets the trajectory for the entire Gospel—universal salvation, division, and the advance of God's purpose through opposition. The Spirit's role and the temple setting link to Acts (Pentecost, temple witness). The pairing with Anna underscores inclusive witness.
- Broader: Nunc Dimittis as model for Christian hope and peaceful death; inspires reflection on waiting, fulfillment, and the cost of discipleship. Cross-tradition: the "light to the Gentiles" language echoes Isaianic servant songs and anticipates global mission.
