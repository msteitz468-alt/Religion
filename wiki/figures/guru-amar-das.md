# Guru Amar Das

## Biographical Overview

Amar Das was born in the village of **Basarke**, near modern [[amritsar|Amritsar]]. His father was Tej Bhan, a Khatri of the Bhalla sub-caste. He came to know the works of Baba Nanak through family connections: a daughter of Guru Angad had married into his family, and it was reportedly by overhearing his niece Amro (Guru Angad's daughter) singing a shabad that he was led to Guru Angad and converted (McLeod ~L1461–1473).

His birth date is disputed, which makes his exact age at succession uncertain, "but it was obviously an advanced age (probably seventy-three)" (McLeod ~L1477–1479). He held the position of Guru for a "remarkably long period of twenty-two years, eventually dying in 1574 at the age (probably) of ninety-five" (McLeod ~L1479–1481).

**Family**: Married to Mansa Devi. He had two sons, Mohan and Mohari (tradition casts them as opponents of his succession choice), and daughters including Bibi Bhani, who married Jetha Sodhi — who became the fourth Guru [[guru-ram-das|Guru Ram Das]] (McLeod ~L1483–1509).

## Residence at Goindval

Tradition records that Guru Angad instructed Amar Das to take up residence in the new village of **Goindval**, founded by a Khatri named Gobind a short distance down the Beas from Khadur. The village had stalled — "what was erected each day was mysteriously dismantled that same night" — until Amar Das took up residence there. "Gobind was unable to persuade others to stay in it" but after Amar Das moved, building proceeded steadily. Amar Das made Goindval the new centre of the Nanak-panth (McLeod ~L1511–1526).

## Innovations Attributed to the Third Guru

McLeod identifies three distinct varieties of innovation in the period of Guru Amar Das, while cautioning that "the uncertainty associated with these developments should be stressed" and that "measures that are traditionally associated with the period of Guru Amar Das…may actually have longer pedigrees and less specific origins" (McLeod ~L1566–1574).

### 1. Administrative: The Manji System

As the Panth expanded geographically, immediate contact with the Guru became difficult. Guru Amar Das is traditionally credited with establishing the **manji system** of preaching deputies: "The function of the manjis seems to have been exclusively preaching the message of [[nam-simaran|nam simaran]]." This system served as the prototype from which the later masand system developed under [[guru-ram-das|Guru Ram Das]] (McLeod ~L1579–1590).

### 2. The Langar (Communal Kitchen)

Guru Nanak had made clear that caste status could have no bearing on access to the divine Name. Amar Das appears to have borrowed from Sufi practice the institution of **compulsory commensality** (eating together), giving practical expression to Nanak's anti-caste ideal. In Sikh usage this emerged as the **langar** — a kitchen and refectory attached to every gurdwara, serving food to all regardless of caste or creed. "The convention requires men and women of all castes to sit in status-free lines (pahgat) and eat together" on sacred ground. McLeod notes that Nanak and Angad certainly maintained langar, "but the specifically anti-caste character appears to have been an introduction by Guru Amar Das" (McLeod ~L1597–1632).

### 3. Ritual Developments

The third cluster of innovations concerns the developing ritual of the Panth — some of which might seem in tension with Nanak's emphasis on interiority over external ritual:

- Digging a sacred **baoli** (well) in Goindval as a place of pilgrimage for Sikhs
- Introduction of particular **festival days**
- Compiling a collection of hymns (the "Goindval pothis" or "Mohan pothis") that was later a prototype for the Adi Granth
- The **decision to excavate the sacred pool** that marked the founding of [[amritsar|Amritsar]]

McLeod addresses the apparent tension with Nanak's interiority: "The theological answer is that the Guru is one and that decisions made by the third Guru proceed from precisely the same source as attitudes expressed by the first…changed circumstances require fresh decisions" (McLeod ~L1660–1673).

**On scripture compilation**: Amar Das commissioned a preliminary compilation of hymns (the Goindval pothis / Mohan pothis). The fifth Guru, Arjan, is believed to have used this prototype when producing the Adi Granth (McLeod ~L2023–2027). The rationale was ensuring that only **sachhi bani** ("true" hymns) were sung in satsangs, guarding against *kachhi bani* (non-authentic compositions) (McLeod ~L1683–1690).

## The Panth under Amar Das

The Panth that Amar Das received was essentially that of Nanak's time, but "changes were increasingly needed if the Panth was to retain the freshness and vigour of its founding days" (McLeod ~L1457–1458). The transition was from a community of personal converts to one that increasingly included those "born into the faith and had known it since infancy" (McLeod ~L1454–1456). The caste profile remained the same: Jats numerically dominant, Khatris prominent in leadership (McLeod ~L1709–1712).

Under Amar Das the Panth still had pilgrimage (to Goindval), two designated festival days, the baoli well, and the anti-caste langar — but "caste as a marriage convention" was preserved, maintaining links with the wider Hindu community (McLeod ~L1731–1737).

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 1, Ch. 3)

Khushwant Singh foregrounds Amar Das's institution-building from his seat at **Goindwal**: he made the
[[langar|langar]] obligatory before audience — "*pehle paṅgat piche saṅgat*: First sit in a row in the
kitchen, then seek the company of the Guru" — extended the parish system to **twenty-two *manjīs***
served by *masands*, fixed the Baisakh gathering, and pressed social reforms: replacing Sanskrit rites
with Guru-hymns, opposing purdah, encouraging widow remarriage and intercaste alliance, and strictly
forbidding ***satī***. Singh notes that Brahmin resentment of these reforms — and their complaints to
Akbar, who refused to act — marked "the beginning of the oppression of the Sikhs," and records Amar Das's
defence of Punjabi over Sanskrit: Sanskrit "was like water in a well… Punjabi was like rain which fell over
all the land."
