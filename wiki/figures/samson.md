Samson is the last and most complex judge: a Nazirite from birth whose personal story perfectly embodies Israel's national struggle — set apart yet chasing what is foreign, achieving in death what he could never manage in life (successor to [[samuel|Samuel]]'s era).

## Biographical Overview

Born to a barren woman of the tribe of Dan after a divine messenger announces his destiny (13:2–5): he will be a Nazirite to God from the womb (no wine, no unclean food, no razor on his head) and will begin to save Israel from the Philistines. His birth narrative places him in the company of Isaac, Samuel, and John the Baptist. As an adult he demands a Philistine wife ("she is right in my eyes"), kills a lion bare-handed, later eats honey from its carcass (violating his Nazirite vow), poses a riddle at his wedding feast, burns Philistine grain with foxes and torches, and slaughters a thousand with a donkey's jawbone. He is betrayed by Delilah (for money), his hair is shaved while he sleeps, his eyes are gouged out, and he is put to grinding in Gaza. In his final act, brought out to make sport at a Dagon temple feast, he prays for strength one last time, grasps the central pillars, and brings the building down, killing more Philistines in his death than in his life (16:30). He is buried between Zorah and Eshtaol in his father's tomb. The narrator's summary: he had judged Israel twenty years.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 13**: Birth narrative and Nazirite vocation announced.
- **Judges 14–15**: Marriage, riddle, foxes, jawbone, water from the rock at Lehi.
- **Judges 16**: Delilah, the seven locks, Gaza prison, final temple collapse.
- **Hebrews 11:32**: Listed among the faithful who "were made strong out of weakness."

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Samson is a tragic hero and cautionary tale. Rabbinic sources debate whether he kept his Nazirite vow in spirit or only literally (the honey from the lion is a flashpoint). He is praised for his strength and final act of faith but criticized for his lust and lack of self-control. Some midrashim see his story as Israel's story in miniature.

**Christianity**: Classic example of the strong man brought low by weakness for women and of grace in the end (prayer in blindness). The Nazirite vow is linked to holiness and separation (contrast with Israel's failure to separate from the nations). His final prayer and death are read as repentance and a type of Christ's victory through apparent defeat. Hebrews 11 includes him among the faithful "who were made strong out of weakness," emphasizing that his strength was always derivative. Patristic writers sometimes allegorize the lion (Satan or death defeated) and the honey (sweetness of the gospel from the carcass). Modern readings note the satire and the pathos of a blind, broken man achieving what armies could not.

**Critical and feminist**: The story is valued for its folk-tale elements, humor, and tragic structure. Feminist readings highlight the women (the Timnite wife, the prostitute of Gaza, Delilah) as agents or victims in a patriarchal narrative of male desire and betrayal. Postcolonial readings see Samson as a colonized resistor whose body is the site of struggle.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Webb's controlling reading: "Samson's personal story *is* Israel's national story." As Israel was set apart by covenant yet chased foreign gods, Samson is set apart as a Nazirite yet chases Philistine women. He wants to be "as other men"; Israel wants to be "as other nations." The birth narrative (barren mother, divine announcement, destiny) sets up the expectation of a great deliverer; the life delivers a series of personal vendettas that happen to harm the Philistines. The final act is simultaneously Israel's greatest victory and its most complete humiliation: a blind, enslaved, ground-down man, achieving by suicidal collapse what he never achieved by living as the Nazirite he was called to be. Theologically the story testifies that YHWH can use even the most compromised vessel and that deliverance can come through death. In the downward spiral it is the nadir — and the point at which the book turns toward the need for kingship (the epilogues follow immediately).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Nazirite status**: Did he violate the vow by touching the lion's carcass or eating the honey? Traditional answers vary; the text never explicitly condemns him for it until the hair is cut.
- **Moral character**: Hero or anti-hero? The text presents his exploits without moral commentary until the end; readers disagree on whether to admire the strength or lament the waste.
- **Delilah**: Betrayer for money or complex character with her own motives? The text is silent on her inner life.
- **Final prayer**: Repentance and restoration of strength, or merely a cry for revenge? The text says "he called to the LORD" and "the LORD remembered him."

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Webb (NICOT) makes the Samson-as-Israel parallel the key to the whole central section and a profound piece of theological narrative art. The story is a staple of preaching on the dangers of compromise, the cost of lust, and the possibility of a "second chance" (the growing hair). In canonical context it stands at the end of the judges before the monarchy and is read alongside Samuel (another Nazirite figure who succeeds where Samson fails). In art, literature, and opera (Saint-Saëns' *Samson et Dalila*) the Delilah scene and the temple collapse dominate. Modern scholarship has recovered the humor, the tragedy, and the critique of Israel's identity crisis. The "made strong out of weakness" line in Hebrews continues to shape Christian reading of flawed biblical heroes.
