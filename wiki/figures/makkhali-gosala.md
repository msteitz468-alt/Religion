## Biographical Overview

Makkhali Gosāla is "generally regarded as having been the head of an ascetic order known as the Ājīvikas, the 'Followers of the Way of Life'" (Dundas, p. 29). He is one of the most distinctive figures in early Indian religious history, appearing in both Jain and Buddhist sources, and serving in the Jain texts primarily as a foil and adversary of [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]].

The latter portion of his name is explained by the Jain tradition: "Makkhali Gosāla (the latter part of his name supposedly signifies that he was born in a *gosāla* or stable)" (Dundas, p. 29). This etymology, provided by the Jain source, has a polemical quality.

## Primary Source Appearances

**Jain sources**: The fullest account of his life is found in the fifteenth chapter of the Bhagavatī (*Exposition of Explanations*), which "is undoubtedly an interpolation into the larger text, showing a consistency and internal coherence unusual in that loosely structured work" (Dundas, p. 29). He is not mentioned in the earlier strata of Mahāvīra's biography (oldest Ācārāṅga and Sūtrakṛtāṅga).

**The Sayings of the Seers** (*Isibhāsiyāiṃ*; IBh): The most ancient Jain texts juxtapose Mahāvīra and Pārśva "along equal terms with figures from traditions which were to be regarded as Jainism's rivals," including Makkhali Gosāla (Dundas, p. 17). The 'Sayings' "counts Makkhali Gosāla among the authoritative teachers," attributing to him a position on "the virtue of imperturbability in the face of the continued change and modification which were to be seen in the world" (IBh 11; Dundas, pp. 31–32). This is significant: in the oldest surviving Jain text that mentions him, he is not yet an enemy but an acknowledged teacher.

**Buddhist Pali Canon**: Contains references to Makkhali Gosāla, including an account of his doctrine. The Buddhist account credits him with "propounding an elaborate if obscure cosmology and of arguing that fate or destiny (*niyati*) was the central motive force in the universe against which no human effort could have any effect" (Dundas, p. 30). All later accounts of Ājīvika doctrine echo this description.

## His Relationship with Mahāvīra (Jain Account)

According to the Bhagavatī account, "Makkhali Gosāla persuaded Mahāvīra in the second year after his renunciation to accept him as his disciple and the two then wandered together for a period of six years" (Dundas, p. 29). Gosāla proved "an unsatisfactory pupil, both headstrong and jealous of his teacher's ascetic attainments, trying fruitlessly to disprove Mahāvīra's powers of prescience" and had to be rescued by Mahāvīra from a brahman ascetic he had antagonised (Dundas, p. 29).

Eventually, "after gaining a degree of magic power from his association with Mahāvīra, [Gosāla] left his teacher and falsely proclaimed himself to be a spiritual conqueror" (Dundas, p. 29–30). Furious at Mahāvīra's refusal to acknowledge his status, Gosāla "attacked him with a blast of ascetic heat which he released from his body, but such was the adamantine nature of Mahāvīra's physique that it rebounded back upon Makkhali Gosāla who eventually died after confessing that the fordmaker was a true spiritual teacher. Mahāvīra subsequently predicted that Gosāla would eventually attain enlightenment and spiritual release" (Dundas, p. 30).

## The Ājīvika Movement

The Ājīvikas "were undoubtedly a fully fledged ascetic corporation in their own right with a community of lay supporters" (Dundas, p. 30). Ājīvika ascetics were naked. Buddhist sources appear to include Mahāvīra's naked followers in their ranks (but not the clothed followers of [[parshvanatha|Pārśva]]) — suggesting the Ājīvikas and early Mahāvīran Jains were not always clearly distinguished from the outside (Dundas, p. 30). There is evidence of an Ājīvika community still existing in south India as late as the thirteenth century (Dundas, p. 30).

No Ājīvika writings survive, so all knowledge of the movement comes from hostile sources (Jain and Buddhist).

## Doctrine: *Niyati* (Fate)

The doctrine attributed to Gosāla by both Buddhist and Jain sources is that *niyati* (fate or destiny) was "the central motive force in the universe against which no human effort could have any effect" (Dundas, p. 30). This doctrine represents a direct antithesis to the Jain and Buddhist emphasis on karma and the efficacy of individual moral and ascetic effort.

However, Dundas raises a significant critical point: "it seems doubtful whether a doctrine which genuinely advocated the lack of efficacy of individual effort could have formed the basis of a renunciatory path to spiritual liberation" (Dundas, p. 30). The evidence of the 'Sayings of the Seers' suggests that Gosāla "was in fact simply arguing for the virtue of imperturbability in the face of the continued change and modification which were to be seen in the world" (IBh 11; Dundas, p. 31) — a rather different nuance from mechanical fatalism.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

**Polemical distortion**: "The suspicion must be that the Jains and Buddhists deliberately distorted Ājīvika doctrine for their own polemical purposes" (Dundas, p. 30). The Jain account of Gosāla as a failed disciple who used stolen magic to challenge Mahāvīra and died in defeat has an obvious apologetic function.

**Possible historical connection**: "There remains the possibility that Mahāvīra and early Jainism were influenced by Ājīvika teachings. There are, for example, inconsistencies in Jain karma theory which could be explained with reference to what little is known of Ājīvika doctrine" (Dundas, pp. 30–31). The developed biography of Mahāvīra "could not easily dispense with Makkhali Gosāla from its narrative structure because there was a persistent reminiscence of a genuine historical connection between the two" (Dundas, p. 30).

**Status in the 'Sayings of the Seers'**: The early Jain text that juxtaposes Gosāla alongside Mahāvīra, Pārśva, and the Buddha's disciples as an authoritative teacher — before later tradition made him the archenemy — is an important indicator that the hostile portrait in the Bhagavatī represents a later polemical development rather than original Jain memory (Dundas, pp. 17–18, 31).

## Significance

Gosāla represents the clearest case in early Indian religious history of the complex boundary between early Jainism and the other *śramaṇa* movements. His figure illuminates: (a) the difficulty of distinguishing early naked ascetic movements from outside; (b) the apologetic strategies by which Jainism constructed its own identity against rivals; (c) the possibility of doctrinal influence running between traditions later treated as hostile. He also appears in the oldest Jain text to treat him (*Sayings of the Seers*) in a strikingly positive light — a contradiction that the later tradition effectively suppressed.
