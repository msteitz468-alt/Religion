Sennacherib, king of [[assyrians|Assyria]] (705–681 BCE), whose 701 campaign against Judah
is the narrative climax of Isaiah 36–37 (// 2 Kings 18–19) and the historical test case of
the book of [[isaiah|Isaiah]]'s theology of trust.

## Biographical Overview

Acceding at Sargon II's battlefield death in 705, Sennacherib spent his first years
suppressing the empire-wide revolt (Babylon under Merodach-baladan finally subdued;
the city destroyed in 689). His 701 western campaign, reconstructed by Oswalt from the
annals: down the coast from Sidon, victory over the Egyptian army at Eltekeh (~20 miles
west of Jerusalem), then the reduction of [[lachish|Lachish]] — commemorated in his palace
reliefs — while pressing Jerusalem by embassy and blockade. His annals boast that he shut up
[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] "like a bird in a cage," but claim no capture of Jerusalem, and he
"never mounted another major campaign to the west" (Oswalt). He was murdered by his sons
Adrammelek and Sharezer in the temple of Nisroch in 681 (Isa 37:38, matching the Assyrian
account per Oswalt); Esarhaddon succeeded him.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Isaiah 36–37 // 2 Kings 18–19**: the Rabshakeh's speech, the follow-up letter ("Do not
  let your god… deceive you"), Isaiah's oracle against him (37:22–29), the destruction of
  his army (37:36), his withdrawal and later assassination.
- **Assyrian annals and the Lachish reliefs** (apparatus/history via Oswalt and Excursus II
  on royal-annals rhetoric).

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Oswalt (evangelical)**: Sennacherib is the tool that "vaunts itself against the hand"
(cf. 10:5–19). The oracle's hook-and-bit image reduces the world-ruler to a "balky beast."
His letter's direct blasphemy makes the crisis explicitly theological: "Against whom have
you lifted the voice…? Against the Holy One of Israel!" (37:23). Oswalt defends the
historicity of the army's destruction as "the crux of the entire account" — "Cut out this
event as a historical fact… and the whole theological content of the passage falls to the
ground" — reads the Lachish reliefs as compensation for being "deprived of the real prize —
Jerusalem — by God," and rejects the two-campaign (Winckler/Bright) harmonization for lack
of extrabiblical evidence. He suggests a plague as the mechanism, with Herodotus ii.141
(the mice at Pelusium) as a garbled Egyptian memory.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Sennacherib is the book's supreme case of the doctrine Oswalt calls transcendence applied to
history: "the gods are not necessarily on the side of the victors." His unanswered prayer in
Nisroch's temple, set against Hezekiah's answered prayer, closes the narrative's irony.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The fate of the army in 701 (destruction vs legend), one vs two campaigns, the "fourteenth
year" datum, and the priority of the Isaiah vs Kings recensions are all disputed;
Oswalt's positions (historical destruction; one campaign; "24th year" emendation; Isaianic
priority) are recorded on [[isaiah|Isaiah]] and in the source page's Excursus I summary.
