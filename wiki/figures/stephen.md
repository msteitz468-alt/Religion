# Stephen

## Biographical Overview

Stephen is the first named Christian martyr, introduced in [[acts|Acts]] as one of the Seven appointed to oversee the daily distribution to the [[hellenists|Hellenist]] widows (Acts 6:5). On [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s reading, the all-Greek names of the Seven, with Stephen heading the list and described as "full of faith and the Holy Spirit," underscore the Hellenist makeup of this group and the early church's refusal to fragment along ethnic lines. Bock stresses that Stephen "does not include merely serving tables": he preaches and performs great wonders and signs like the apostles (Acts 6:8), showing that ministry and mission were "not limited to the Twelve." Bock notes that grace here is "not just a 'charm'"; Stephen "ministers through the enabling power of grace."

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 6:5** — chosen as one of the Seven, "full of faith and the Holy Spirit," to serve the Hellenist widows.
- **Acts 6:8–15** — works great wonders and signs; disputed with a synagogue of Freedmen (Cyrenians, Alexandrians, Cilicians, Asians); seized on charges of blasphemy against Moses, God, the temple, and the law; false witnesses testify; his face appears "like the face of an angel."
- **Acts 7:1–53** — the longest speech in Acts: a history-recital from Abraham through Joseph, Moses, the tabernacle and temple, ending in the charge that his hearers are "stiff-necked" and "uncircumcised in heart and ears."
- **Acts 7:54–8:1a** — sees the standing [[son-of-man|Son of Man]] at God's right hand; is stoned by a mob; prays for his spirit and for his killers' forgiveness; [[paul-apostle|Saul]] consents to his death.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's reading)

**The arrest and the charges (6:8–15).** [[darrell-bock|Bock]] frames the three charge-topics (following Fitzmyer) as "blasphemy, temple in association with law, and Jesus." Luke labels the witnesses "false" to "underscore the injustice," yet Bock grants that Stephen "holds views about the temple that, although rooted in the OT, alarm those with authority." Citing Schnabel, Bock argues that Hellenists like Stephen "see the eschatological implications of Jesus's coming for the temple and the law more clearly than many other believers do initially." Bock notes Jesus "also said similar things about the temple" (John 2:19; Mark 13:2). The "face of an angel" (6:15), Bock says, is "unique in the NT," suggesting Stephen "reflecting a touch of God's glory" (cf. Exod. 34:29–35) and is "one of Luke's ways of saying that Stephen is innocent."

**The speech (7:1–53).** Bock reads the speech as built around two themes: God "raised up a series of leaders whom the Jews failed to recognize," and Israel "responded inappropriately to God's presence" by falling into idolatry. Bock stresses the controlling claim that "the temple was never designed to confine God but was intended to be a place of worship to him alone." The recital moves from Abraham (God's revelation begins "outside Israel and the temple"), through the rejected-then-vindicated Joseph ("a parallel to Jesus"), to Moses as the rejected deliverer and "prophet like me" (7:37; Deut. 18:18) who is a "'type' or 'pattern' for what Jesus was to do and be." The golden calf is on Bock's reading "the most significant of all the incidents of rebellion," linking Israel's idols as "the work of human hands" to the temple "made with hands."

**Relativizing, not rejecting, the temple (7:44–50).** Bock's central verdict: "Yes, Stephen criticizes the temple, but not for what it is; rather, he is finding fault with how it is viewed." He cites Bruce that Stephen "is not rebuking the temple but the state of mind that tended to emerge about the temple," and Jervell that the critique must sit beside Acts' positive temple statements (2:46; 3:11; 5:20–21). On Bock's reading, quoting Isa. 66:1, this is "a prophetic-like critique of a skewed view of the temple," not a rejection of the temple's existence. Bock argues that everything up through 7:50 "would not be objectionable enough to evoke a violent reaction, because the prophets also said what is said up to here. The controversial point comes next."

**The accusation and the vision (7:51–56).** In the peroratio Stephen turns from "our fathers" to "your fathers," which Bock reads as marking "a split within Israel, based upon response to God," charging his hearers as "stiff-necked" and "uncircumcised in heart and ears" who "always resist the Holy Spirit." Bock notes the three NT-hapax terms here. At the climax Stephen sees "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" (7:56) — the only occurrence of the exact title "Son of Man" outside the Gospels. On why Jesus stands rather than sits (the "sitting" of Ps. 110:1), Bock surveys five views and prefers **View 5**: "the picture of Jesus as vindicating judge is the most likely force," Jesus "arising as judge and receiver / vindicating advocate of Stephen's testimony" (cf. Luke 12:8). Bock adds that finer distinctions among judge, advocate, and opponent "attempt too fine a set of distinctions. All of these ideas belong together."

**Stephen–Jesus parallels.** Bock cites Witherington's list of ten parallels between Stephen and Jesus: both appear in a trial-like setting, suffer false witnesses, mention the temple's destruction, speak of the temple "made with hands," are charged with blasphemy, are asked by the high priest to speak, commit their spirits to God, and ask God to forgive their killers. At 7:59 Stephen addresses "Lord Jesus" to receive his spirit — parallel to Luke 23:46 but, Bock says, with "one major difference": Stephen "explicitly addresses his mediator," which Bock reads as "evidence of a high Christology. Prayer is now made to Jesus."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock reads the martyrdom as the third of three escalating early-Acts trials, "from warning (4:21) to beating (5:40) to death (7:60)," and as the moment when "Jewish Hellenists join the Jewish leadership in hostile opposition." The crowd stones Stephen for blasphemy, Bock explains, because on their view "no one has the right to be at the side of God's heavenly presence." For Bock the episode marks a turning point: "The parting of the ways between Judaism and what became Christianity centered on the status the believers assigned to Jesus." Stephen's death also introduces [[paul-apostle|Saul]] (7:58; 8:1a) — Bock: "Luke introduces the hero of the second half of Acts by noting that he observes and shares in the persecution." Some, Bock notes (citing Larkin), hold that Paul's later conversion "was God's answer to [Stephen's] prayer."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Whether the temple charge was "true."** Bock notes Witherington "challenges the idea that the charges might be true," but Bock says "his case may be slightly overargued," granting "there may be an element of truth in the charge" while the accusers push it "to the limit."
- **Source criticism of the speech.** Bock reviews Dibelius (speech inserted from tradition), Steck (Deuteronomistic critique of Hellenistic Judaism), Barrett (speech "not as explicitly Christian as it would be if Luke had composed it"), and Fitzmyer (a Luke-composed form drawing on Hellenist tradition "possibly from Antioch") — Bock repeatedly calls this skepticism "excessive."
- **Was the stoning a legal execution or a lynching?** Bock treats it as "an example of mob justice, not a formal legal process," "the equivalent of a lynching, since there is no formal verdict," and holds the legality debate is "moot."
- **Why the standing Son of Man?** See the five views above; Bock's preferred reading (vindicating judge) is a position, not a settled fact.
