Delilah is the woman in the Valley of Sorek whom [[samson|Samson]] loves and who, bought by the
lords of the [[philistines|Philistines]], extracts the secret of his strength and delivers him to
be blinded and enslaved ([[judges|Judges]] 16:4–21). Her ethnicity is never stated; the text names
only where she lives and what she is paid.

## Biographical Overview

After Samson carries off the gates of [[gaza|Gaza]], he loves a woman in the Valley of Sorek named
Delilah. The lords of the Philistines come to her and offer eleven hundred pieces of silver each
if she will entice him and learn where his great strength lies and how he may be bound and
subdued (16:5). She asks him directly, and three times he answers falsely: seven fresh bowstrings,
new ropes never used, the seven locks of his head woven into the web of a loom. Each time she
binds him and cries "The Philistines are upon you, Samson!" with men lying in wait in an inner
chamber, and each time he breaks free.

She presses him daily with words until "his soul was vexed to death," and he tells her everything:
no razor has come upon his head, for he has been a Nazirite to God from his mother's womb; if he
were shaved his strength would leave him (16:16–17). Delilah sends for the lords, who come with
the silver in hand. She lulls Samson to sleep on her knees, calls a man to shave off the seven
locks, and begins to torment him; his strength leaves him. He wakes saying he will shake himself
free as before, and does not know that YHWH has left him. The Philistines seize him, **gouge out
his eyes**, bring him down to Gaza, bind him with bronze fetters, and set him grinding at the mill
in the prison (16:19–21). Delilah does not appear again; the narrative does not record her fate.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 16:4–21** — the whole of Delilah's role: the Philistine lords' offer, the three false
  answers and bindings, the daily pressing, the disclosure of the Nazirite vow, the shaving, the
  departure of YHWH, the blinding and enslavement at Gaza.
- **Judges 16:22–31** — the aftermath in which she takes no part: Samson's hair grows, and at the
  feast of Dagon he pulls apart the two pillars, killing more in his death than in his life.

Delilah is not named elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible or the New Testament; Hebrews 11:32 names
Samson without reference to her.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

Reception on Delilah in this collection is **thin**, and deliberately left so here. No ingested
source in this wiki treats Delilah as a subject in her own right; no rabbinic, patristic,
Islamic, or feminist-critical reception on her has been ingested. Later Western art, opera, and
homiletic tradition made her a byword for the seductive betrayer — but this wiki holds no ingested
source documenting that reception, and it is not written up here.

What the collection does record bears on her role in Samson's story rather than on her:

- **[[barry-webb|Webb]] (NICOT, 2012)**: his controlling reading of Samson is that **Samson's
  personal story is Israel's national story** — as Israel was set apart from the nations yet
  chased their gods, Samson is set apart as a Nazirite yet chases Philistine women. On that
  reading Delilah belongs to a sequence (the woman of Timnah, the prostitute at Gaza, Delilah)
  that enacts Israel's own attachment to what it was told to keep separate from.
- Susan Ackerman's summary, quoted by Webb, that Judges is "neither a handbook of patriarchy nor a
  celebration of matriarchy… a book not of 'either/or' but of 'both/and'" is the frame under which
  the wiki records the book's women as both agents and victims. Delilah sits on the agent side.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

The Delilah episode is where Samson's Nazirite consecration is finally surrendered — not seized
from him but told away. The narrator's most devastating line is not the shaving but "he did not
know that the LORD had left him" (16:20): the loss of the hair is the sign, the departure of YHWH
is the substance. The blinding then literalizes what the story has shown all along about a man who
"saw" a woman and demanded her (14:1–2), and the mill in Gaza reverses Israel's deliverance into
Israel's servitude in the person of its deliverer.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Delilah's ethnicity is unstated in the text: she is located in the Valley of Sorek and paid by
Philistine lords, but never called a Philistine. Her motive is given as money and nothing else.
This wiki has ingested no source adjudicating her identity, her historicity, or the long
interpretive argument over whether the narrative condemns her, Samson, or both — and none is
invented here.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Within this collection Delilah's significance is instrumental: she is the point at which the
Nazirite theme of [[judges|Judges]] 13–16 collapses, and therefore a fixture in discussions of the
[[samson|Samson]] cycle's structure and of women's agency in the book. Cross-links:
