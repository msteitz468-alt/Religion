# Jesus

**Biographical Overview**:
The central figure of Christianity, believed by his followers to be the incarnate Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary, who suffered crucifixion under Pontius Pilate, rose from the dead, and ascended. The four canonical Gospels provide the primary narrative portraits of his life, ministry, death, and resurrection, each with distinctive emphases shaped by the evangelists' theological purposes and sources. Outside the Gospels, he is presented as the exalted Lord continuing to act through the Spirit (Acts), the crucified and risen one whose death and resurrection bring justification and new creation (Paul), the eternal High Priest and pioneer of faith (Hebrews), and the slain-yet-reigning Lamb (Revelation).

**Primary Source Appearances**:
- **Gospel of Matthew** (France): Infancy as fulfillment (genealogy, virgin birth, flight to Egypt); Galilean ministry with five major discourses; geographical arc from Galilee to Jerusalem and back; passion and resurrection as vindication of the rejected Messiah. Emphasis on "all this happened to fulfill" (11 formula-quotations) and the sociopolitical Galilee/Jerusalem polarity.
- **Gospel of Mark** (France): "Drama in Three Acts" (Galilee proclamation → journey learning the cross → Jerusalem confrontation). Prologue gives reader privileged knowledge (Son of God, Spirit, battle with evil); christology "in the story" rather than titles alone; paradoxical king who suffers; discipleship re-education in Act Two; empty tomb as open-ended climax.
- **Gospel of Luke** (Green): Theocentric historiography (God's δεῖ and Spirit drive the plot); programmatic Nazareth sermon (Luke 4) announcing Jubilee release and good news to the poor; long Travel Narrative (9:51–19:48) as distinctive block; holistic salvation; women and marginalized as models; Jerusalem as goal; Luke-Acts as one continuous story.
- **Gospel of John** (Michaels): Preamble (1:1–5) on the Light/Word; public ministry as extended cosmic trial; seven signs and "I am" sayings as self-revelation; testimony (martyria) chain as governing framework; glorification at the cross; Farewell Discourses and High Priestly Prayer; resurrection appearances and epilogue extending the witness.
- **Acts** (Bock): The exalted Lord who continues to act (distributing the Spirit, directing the mission, appearing to Paul); speeches (esp. Peter's Pentecost and Paul's) interpret his death/resurrection as fulfillment; the one through whom salvation comes to Jew and Gentile alike; central to the four dominant issues of Luke-Acts.
- **Other NT**: Pauline letters (crucified Messiah as wisdom/power of God, justification, new creation, "in Christ"); Hebrews (eternal Son, Melchizedekian High Priest, pioneer and perfecter); Revelation (slain Lamb who conquers, Alpha and Omega); references in James, Jude, 1-2 Peter as Lord and coming Judge.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**:
- **Early Christianity / Patristic**: The incarnate Logos (John 1), the suffering yet exalted Christ (Paul, Hebrews), the fulfillment of Israel’s Scriptures. Debates over divinity (Arian controversy), two natures (Chalcedon 451), and the meaning of the cross (ransom, satisfaction, exemplar).
- **Eastern Orthodox**: Theosis (participation in divine life) through the incarnate and glorified Christ; iconography of the Pantocrator and the harrowing of Hades; liturgy centered on the risen Lord.
- **Roman Catholic**: Real presence in Eucharist, vicarious satisfaction (Anselm), Mary as Theotokos in relation to the incarnate Son; papal and sacramental mediation grounded in Christ.
- **Protestant (Reformation and Evangelical)**: Sola fide, sola gratia through Christ alone (Luther, Calvin); penal substitution (many); emphasis on personal faith in the finished work of the cross and resurrection. France, Green, Michaels, and Bock represent modern evangelical narrative-critical and historical readings that stress fulfillment, theocentric purpose, testimony, and historical reliability.
- **Critical / Historical Jesus**: Quest for the "real" Jesus behind the portraits (Sanders, Wright, etc.); debates over apocalyptic prophet vs. wisdom teacher, Jewish context, and the resurrection as historical event or interpretation. The four Gospels are seen as faith-portraits rather than neutral biography.
- **Jewish**: Jesus as failed or false messiah, or a righteous teacher (some modern Jewish scholarship); New Testament portraits viewed as supersessionist or polemical.
- **Islamic**: 'Isa, a great prophet and messenger, born of the Virgin Maryam, performer of miracles, but neither divine nor crucified (Quran 4:157; 5:75, 110–120). The Gospels' portraits are seen as corrupted.
- **Other**: In various new religious movements and secular culture, reinterpreted as ethical teacher, revolutionary, or myth.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**:
Jesus is the hinge of salvation history—the fulfillment of Israel's story (Matthew, Luke), the paradoxical revelation of God's kingship through suffering (Mark), the self-revealing Envoy whose "hour" of glorification draws all people (John), and the exalted Lord whose Spirit-empowered mission continues to the ends of the earth (Acts). Theologically, he is the incarnate Son who reveals the Father, the Servant whose death brings atonement and new creation, the High Priest who intercedes, and the coming Judge. Narratively, each evangelist shapes the story to address specific communities: fulfillment and reversal (Matthew/Luke), discipleship in the shadow of the cross (Mark), belief and life through testimony (John), and the universal expansion of the community (Acts). The "christology is in the story" (France on Mark); the plot is driven by God's purpose (Green on Luke); the testimony chain extends to the reader (Michaels on John); God remains the ultimate actor (Bock on Acts).

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**:
- **Birth and virginal conception**: Affirmed in Matthew and Luke; absent from Mark and John. Debated as historical claim, theological midrash (fulfillment of Isa 7), or legend.
- **Ministry length and chronology**: Synoptics suggest one year (single Passover in Mark); John suggests two or three (multiple Passovers, temple cleansing at beginning). France, Green, and Michaels treat the differences as theological shaping rather than contradiction.
- **Miracles and exorcisms**: Central to all four portraits; critical scholarship often demythologizes or explains psychologically; evangelical commentators (France, Green, Bock) defend as integral to the historical Jesus' identity as bearer of God's kingdom/power.
- **Trial, crucifixion, and resurrection**: Universally attested; debates over responsibility (Jewish leaders vs. Romans), the nature of the resurrection (bodily, visionary, or metaphorical), and empty tomb traditions. Bock defends historical core; all four evangelists present it as the vindication that redefines messiahship.
- **Self-understanding**: Did Jesus claim divinity? "Son of Man," "Son of God," "I am" sayings, and temple actions are central. Michaels and others see high Christology rooted in the earliest traditions.
- **Relationship to Judaism**: Fulfillment or supersession? The "new perspective" and post-Holocaust readings emphasize Jesus' Jewishness and the continuity in Luke-Acts and Paul.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**:
- **France (Matthew/Mark)**: Jesus as the fulfillment of the entire OT story and the paradoxical king whose suffering redefines power and discipleship. Narrative christology; sociopolitical reading of Galilee/Jerusalem.
- **Green (Luke)**: Theocentric Jesus—bearer of God's purpose, Spirit-anointed for holistic salvation and Jubilee release. The Travel Narrative and birth narrative make him the agent of reversal for the lowly.
- **Michaels (John)**: Jesus as the unique Envoy whose testimony, signs, and "I am" sayings reveal the Father; the cross as glorification; the testimony chain that includes the reader.
- **Bock (Acts)**: The risen and exalted Jesus who continues to direct the mission, distribute the Spirit, and fulfill God's plan; central to the resolution of Jewish-Gentile and Israel-church questions.
- Broader: Shaped all major Christian doctrines (Trinity, atonement, ecclesiology, eschatology). Modern narrative criticism (France, Green, Michaels) and historical work (Bock) emphasize reading the portraits on their own terms while engaging critical questions. Cross-tradition: Jewish readings stress the failed messiah; Islamic the prophet 'Isa; both engage the canonical portraits critically.
