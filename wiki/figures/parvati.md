# Parvati

**Parvati** ("Daughter of the Mountain") is the paradigmatic **breast goddess** of Hinduism — the
devoted wife, the subservient but powerful shakti of [[shiva|Shiva]], and the foil to the
independent warrior goddesses like [[durga|Chandika/Durga]]. Yet the Puranas simultaneously portray
her as dangerous, capable of splitting into the dark, skull-wearing **Kali**, and as the sole mother
of [[ganesha|Ganesha]] — children who emerge from her body alone, without Shiva. She is the primary
"domesticated" goddess, yet her domestication is perpetually contested.

## Sati: The First Wife

Parvati's previous incarnation is **Sati**, daughter of the Brahmin host Daksha. Sati marries Shiva
against her father's wishes. When Daksha stages a great sacrifice and deliberately excludes Shiva
(an insult of cosmic magnitude), Sati goes to confront her father; he humiliates Shiva publicly.
Sati, unable to tolerate the humiliation of her husband, self-immolates in Daksha's sacrificial fire.

**Clarification on "suttee":** Doniger is careful to note that Sati *is not suttee* — she does not
die on a husband's pyre; her *husband is still alive*. "Sati commits suicide to protest her father's
failure to honor her husband." What she enacts is closer to honor-suicide or righteous self-destruction.
The use of her name for widow-burning (*satī*) is etymologically loose and ideologically loaded — it
appropriates a myth of wifely self-sacrifice in her own name, for her own purposes, and turns it into
a dharmic obligation for widows.

**The aftermath — the Shakti Pithas:** Shiva, grief-stricken, carries Sati's corpse through the
world, unable to let go. The other gods, alarmed by this cosmic wandering, persuade Vishnu to
dismember the corpse with his discus (*chakra*). Everywhere a piece falls to earth becomes a
**Shakti Pīṭha** ("seat of the Goddess") — a pilgrimage site. Tradition counts 51 or 108 sites.
This is the origin myth of the network of goddess shrines across the Indian subcontinent.

## Parvati: The Second Birth, Winning Shiva

Sati is reborn as **Parvati**, daughter of the Himalayan mountain-god. The gods need Shiva to father
a warrior son (Skanda) to defeat the antigod Taraka; but since Sati's death, Shiva is a grieving
ascetic, lost in meditation, generating no seed.

Parvati is sent to serve Shiva, wearing him down through devoted service. Shiva tests her with
apparitions and arguments; she responds calmly. Finally she performs extreme austerities (*tapas*)
herself — becoming a female ascetic in order to win Shiva, a peculiar inversion of the usual
framework. "She gives up the pleasures of the body to get them back."

**Kama burned:** Indra dispatches the god of desire, **Kama** ("Desire"), to shoot Shiva with a
flower-arrow and awaken his eroticism. Shiva, disturbed from samadhi, opens his third eye and
burns Kama to ash. (Later, under pressure from Kama's wife Rati and the gods, he restores Kama
but as incorporeal — *Ananga*, "Without Body.") Eventually, by the sheer weight of her tapas,
Parvati compels Shiva's interest. Their union generates the divine seed needed for Skanda.

## The Splitting of Parvati: Gauri and Kali

**Gauri** ("the golden") and **Kali** ("the black") are the two poles of Parvati. The Puranas narrate
their split in at least two ways:

1. **Shiva teases Parvati** about her dark complexion; hurt, she performs tapas and sheds her dark
   outer skin, which becomes Kaushiki or Kali. Parvati re-emerges as Gauri, the golden.
2. **In battle**, Parvati's anger darkens her face and she becomes Kali directly — a war-form she
   cannot easily re-enter from.

This split is a mythological encoding of the tooth/breast distinction: the same goddess is both the
devoted, golden, breast-goddess wife and the dark, skull-wearing tooth-goddess killer — but these
two cannot occupy the same body simultaneously. "The safe, beneficent Hindu goddess is the domesticated
form of a more dangerous power" (Doniger).

**Kali in the Mahābhārata precursors:** Kali appears briefly in the Mahābhārata as the dark deity
of Death/Night before the Kuru massacre. Her full mythology develops in the Puranas.

## Ganesha: A Son From the Body Alone

Shiva and Parvati are repeatedly absent from each other or in conflict. Parvati forms her son
**[[ganesha|Ganesha]]** from the *dirt and oil* she scrubs from her body — creating him alone,
"parthenogenetically," as a doorkeeper while she bathes.

Shiva, unaware this is his wife's child, beheads Ganesha when the boy blocks him. Parvati's grief
is so great that Shiva must restore the boy — attaching the head of an elephant (the first creature
found, whose head could be cut off and still survive because it faced south). Ganesha thus has no
"father" in the usual sense: he is entirely Parvati's creation.

**Symmetry with Skanda:** Just as Parvati creates Ganesha without Shiva, Shiva creates **Skanda**
without Parvati — his seed falls into the Ganges and Skanda is nursed by the Pleiades. Both children
are products of single-parent creation, which Doniger reads as encoding the perpetually unresolved
erotic/ascetic tension between Shiva and Parvati.

## The Androgyne: Ardhanāriśvara

The most radical resolution of the Shiva–Parvati tension is the **androgyne** form, *Ardhanāriśvara*
("The Lord Who Is Half Woman") — Shiva and Parvati fused into a single body, right half male, left
half female. This is a theological claim: the divine ultimately includes both masculine and feminine
poles; the full godhead is neither purely masculine nor purely feminine. This image is ubiquitous in
Gupta-period sculpture.

Doniger's reading: the androgyne is "not simply a balancing act" — it encodes the problem that the
erotic and the ascetic cannot fully inhabit the same space, and the figure acknowledges both impossibility
and necessity.

## Breast Goddess vs. Tooth Goddess

Parvati is the paradigmatic **breast goddess**: married, faithful, generative, ultimately
subordinate (her shakti "belongs to" Shiva in most accounts). Yet:

- She *competes* with Shiva via her own tapas (she is an ascetic in her own right)
- She generates a child without Shiva (Ganesha)
- Her dark form (Kali) is a tooth goddess in every respect

"The story of the domestication of the wild goddess — making her someone's wife — is a story told
again and again in the Puranas."
