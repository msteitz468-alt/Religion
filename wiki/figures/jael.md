Jael is the Kenite woman who, in fulfillment of Deborah's prophecy, kills the fleeing Canaanite general Sisera with a tent peg while he sleeps in her tent, becoming "most blessed of women" in the Song of Deborah.

## Biographical Overview

Wife of Heber the Kenite (a clan that had separated from the Kenites and made peace with Jabin of Hazor). When Sisera flees the routed army on foot to the tent of Jael (the Kenites being at peace with both sides), she meets him, invites him in, covers him with a rug, gives him milk when he asks for water, and stands guard at the door. While he sleeps from exhaustion, she takes a tent peg and a hammer, goes softly to him, and drives the peg through his temple into the ground. He dies. Barak arrives in pursuit; Jael shows him the body. The Song of Deborah celebrates her: "Most blessed of women be Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite, of tent-dwelling women most blessed. He asked for water and she gave him milk; she brought him curds in a noble's bowl. She put her hand to the tent peg and her right hand to the workmen's mallet; she struck Sisera; she crushed his head; she shattered and pierced his temple. Between her feet he sank, he fell, he lay still; between her feet he sank, he fell; where he sank, there he fell dead" (5:24–27). The Song contrasts this with the scene of Sisera's mother waiting at the window, rationalizing his delay while the ladies reassure her of spoil (5:28–30).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Judges 4:17–22**: Sisera's flight to Jael's tent; her hospitality, the killing, Barak's arrival and confirmation.
- **Judges 5:24–31**: The Song's blessing of Jael; detailed poetic description of the act; contrast with the mother's window scene.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

**Judaism**: Jael is "most blessed of women" for her courage and for delivering Israel. The act is celebrated as heroic; some traditions note the peace treaty with Jabin as context for her bold breach. The milk/curds and the "noble's bowl" are read as ironic hospitality turned lethal. She is sometimes paired with other female deliverers (Deborah, Esther).

**Christianity**: Jael is a type of the unexpected deliverer and of the Church or the soul defeating the enemy through "domestic" means (tent peg as cross or simple faith). The blessing "most blessed of women" is sometimes paralleled with Elizabeth's words to Mary. The story illustrates that God uses the marginalized and the "weak" (a woman, a tent-dweller, a domestic tool) to defeat the mighty (iron chariots, a general). Some patristic readings see the tent peg as a figure of the cross or the word of God piercing the enemy. The contrast with Sisera's mother underscores the reversal of expectations.

**Critical and feminist**: The story is prized for its literary power (the window scene as devastating close) and for subverting gender expectations. Feminist readings celebrate Jael's agency, the deception as survival tactic, and the critique of militaristic "spoil" expectations in the mother's scene. Some note the violation of hospitality norms and debate the ethics of the killing in a "peace" context. Postcolonial readings see the Kenite "in-between" status as key to her ability to act.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jael fulfills Deborah's prophecy exactly and embodies the theme that "the LORD will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman." Her act is the narrative's final reversal: the mighty general who trusted in iron chariots dies by a woman's hand with a wooden peg while sleeping under a rug. The Song makes it sung theology — YHWH's victory through the unexpected, the willing, and the lowly. In the downward spiral of Judges, Jael stands as a high point of effective, courageous action by someone outside the male Israelite leadership structure. The mother's scene (rationalizing, expecting spoil and rape) exposes the dehumanizing logic of the oppressor and the hollowness of their "victory" expectations. Jael's *ḥesed*-like hospitality (milk instead of water) turns lethal for the enemy of God's people.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Hospitality violation**: Did Jael break sacred guest-right, or was the "peace" with Jabin a political fiction that allowed her to choose sides? The text celebrates without reservation.
- **Literal vs. symbolic**: The detailed violence (peg through temple, "between her feet he sank") is graphic; some read it as hyperbolic or as celebrating the reversal of sexual/military power.
- **Kenite alliance**: The historical relationship of Kenites to Israel and to Canaanite powers is studied; Jael's act is seen as choosing the Israelite side despite the treaty.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Webb (NICOT) emphasizes the literary artistry of the Song's close (the window scene as the narrative's ironic masterpiece) and Jael as the surprising instrument of YHWH's victory. The story is central to discussions of women as deliverers, the ethics of deception in wartime, and the reversal motif ("the bows of the mighty are broken"). In art, the "Jael and Sisera" scene (often with the peg and hammer) is a favorite for dramatic reversal. In preaching it illustrates that God uses the unexpected and that courage can be found in unlikely places. Feminist and womanist readings have reclaimed Jael as a powerful agent of justice. The blessing "most blessed of women" echoes in later biblical and liturgical language.
