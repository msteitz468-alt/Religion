# Ananias and Sapphira

> **Disambiguation.** This Ananias (of Jerusalem, Acts 5) is distinct from **Ananias of Damascus**, the disciple who baptizes Saul (Acts 9), and from **Ananias the high priest** before whom Paul is tried (Acts 23). All three are separate figures who happen to share the common name Ananias ("the Lord is gracious").

## Biographical Overview

Ananias ("the Lord is gracious") and his wife Sapphira ("beautiful") are a married couple in the earliest Jerusalem community who sell a piece of property, secretly withhold part of the proceeds, and lay the rest at the apostles' feet as though it were the whole. On [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s reading, they are "the deliberate counter-example to [[barnabas|Barnabas]]," whose wholehearted generosity Luke has just narrated (Acts 4:36–37). Both fall dead when confronted by [[peter-apostle|Peter]] (Acts 5:1–11).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 5:1–6** — Ananias, "with his wife's knowledge," keeps back part of the price; Peter charges that Satan has filled his heart to lie to the Holy Spirit; Ananias falls dead and is carried out and buried.
- **Acts 5:7–11** — Sapphira, unaware, arrives three hours later, repeats the lie, is charged with conspiring "to test the Spirit of the Lord," and falls dead at Peter's feet; "great fear came upon the whole church."

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's reading)

**God, not Peter, is the actor.** [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s central theological point is that "God (not Peter) is the major actor"; the passage teaches accountability and that God knows the heart. Bock insists this is "not 'magical power'": Peter "utters no incantation" (against Conzelmann). While Satan is noted as influencing the couple (Gaventa), Bock stresses this "does not excuse them — responsibility is Ananias's own."

**The crux: lying to the Spirit is lying to God (5:3–4).** Bock reads Peter as a prophet reading the heart. Satan "filled" (πληρόω) Ananias's heart — "the opposite of being filled with the Spirit" at Pentecost (2:4). The key theological claim, in Bock's words: lying to the Holy Spirit "= lying to God" ("You have not lied to men but to God," v. 4), with "implications here for the person of God, since to act against the Spirit is to act against God." Fitzmyer calls it "a lie to koinōnia."

**Voluntary giving, not communism.** Bock emphasizes that the property was theirs to keep: Peter's two questions expect the answer "yes" — while unsold the field remained theirs, and after the sale the proceeds were "in your authority" (ἐξουσίᾳ). So, Bock concludes, "the sin was not withholding money but premeditated deceit" — the couple wanted "to appear more generous than they were," preferring "human praise over faithfulness to God." Bock contrasts this with [[sadducees|Qumran]], where surrender of property was a *required* membership condition (1QS 6.18–23), unlike the church's voluntary arrangement. The word "kept back" (ἐνοσφίσατο) is, Bock notes, the same verb used of **Achan's theft at Jericho** (Josh. 7:1).

**Holiness and accountability of the community.** Bock's synthesis: the sin is serial (pride and deceit leading to lying and to an offense against God); God's usual deferral of judgment is grace; and "the church is not a place of perfect people" (Fernando). The episode, Bock argues, "rebuts the idea that Luke idealizes the church." He compares it to Nadab and Abihu (Lev. 10) as judgment "early in a new community's life."

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock treats this as one of only two detailed judgment miracles in Acts (with the blinding of Elymas, 13:11), a "rule miracle of judgment" that anchors the community's sense that God can and does hold his people accountable. The instant death is "shocking, but that is the point," and (Bock argues) natural-cause explanations fail because they would also have to account for Sapphira. Verse 11 carries the first of 23 uses of ἐκκλησία in Acts — here, Bock notes, still meaning a local gathering, "not yet a technical theological term."

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Historicity of the judgment miracle.** Bock records Fitzmyer as "non-committal," rejecting Lucian and Greco-Roman parallels while expressing no verdict on historicity; critics read the account as legend. Bock himself defends its credibility, arguing that the episode's "uniqueness argues for" it and that OT analogues (Nadab/Abihu; Achan; 1 Kings 14) differ at key points, since here "God acts directly."
- **"Test," not "tempt."** Bock notes that Sapphira is charged with conspiring "to test (πειράσαι) the Spirit of the Lord" — "'test,' not 'tempt' (RSV wrong)" — like Israel testing God in the wilderness (Exod. 17:2).
