# Agabus

Agabus is the Jerusalem prophet who, in [[acts|Acts]], twice delivers Spirit-given
predictions: the Claudian famine (Acts 11:27–28) and, later, [[paul-apostle|Paul]]'s arrest
(Acts 21:10–11). His first appearance marks the first mention of prophets in Acts, on
[[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s reading.

## Biographical Overview

Agabus comes down from Jerusalem to [[antioch|Antioch]] among a group of prophets. Bock
notes his name may derive from Hagab/Hagaba (Ezra 2:46, per Bruce) or be a Syrian name
meaning "love" (per Fitzmyer). Beyond his two oracles the text supplies no biographical
detail.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 11:27–28** — at Antioch, Agabus "foretold by the Spirit" (Bock notes the oracle
  language ἐσήμανεν) "a great famine all over the world," which Luke says "took place in the
  days of Claudius" (emperor AD 41–54). The prophecy prompts the Antiochene relief
  collection carried to Jerusalem by [[barnabas|Barnabas]] and Saul.
- **Acts 21:10–11** — Agabus binds his own hands and feet with Paul's belt, an acted
  prophecy foretelling that Paul will be bound in Jerusalem and handed to the Gentiles.

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock)

Bock treats Agabus's famine oracle as genuine Spirit-prophecy fulfilled in historically
attested famines under Claudius: Bock records that famine struck in Claudius's 1st, 2nd,
4th, 9th, and 11th years, that an inscription (CIG 3973) speaks of a world famine, and that
Josephus notes a Judean famine of AD 44–48. He cites Fitzmyer on the famine's geographic
spread (Egypt, Greece, Rome, Judea) and Riesner, who places the prediction itself in
AD 39–42. For Bock the oracle sets in motion the famine relief that "indicates a complete
reconciliation" between the Antiochene church and Jerusalem.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Agabus embodies the continuing prophetic gift of the [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]] in the
early church and functions narratively to link the churches: his Antioch oracle triggers
Gentile-church material support for Jerusalem ("The Jews brought salvation to the Gentiles,
and the Gentiles reciprocate by bringing material support," on Bock's framing), and his
later belt-binding sign at Caesarea prepares Paul's Jerusalem arrest.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Bock reads the famine prophecy as historically anchored rather than a literary invention,
marshalling the epigraphic (CIG 3973) and Josephan evidence and the datings of Fitzmyer and
Riesner. Whether the resulting famine-relief visit is to be equated with the Galatians
2:1–10 visit is, for Bock, a question that "awaits… Acts 15"; he notes Marshall, Bruce, and
Schnabel equate them, without himself adjudicating here.
