**Biographical Overview**

Elisha son of Shaphat (from Abel-meholah), successor to Elijah in the northern kingdom (reigns of Joram/Jehoram, Jehu, Jehoahaz, Joash, late 9th–early 8th cent. BCE). Called while plowing (1 Kgs 19:19–21); receives the "double portion" of Elijah's spirit at the ascension (2 Kgs 2:9–15). Leader of the "sons of the prophets" (prophetic guild/communities). Performs a concentrated cycle of public miracles — provision, healing, resurrection, military deliverance — during a period of national decline, Aramean wars, and political upheaval (including the anointing of Jehu to destroy the house of Ahab). Dies of illness; a dead man is revived when thrown onto his bones (2 Kgs 13:20–21). More politically engaged and communally embedded than the solitary Elijah.

**Primary Source Appearances**

- **2 Kings 2–9, 13**: Parting of the Jordan (following Elijah), purifying Jericho's waters, widow's oil (provision), Shunammite's son (resurrection), Naaman's leprosy (healing of Aramean general), floating axhead (for a prophet's disciple), blinding the Aramean army at Dothan (military deliverance), siege of Samaria and prophetic oracles, anointing of Jehu (political revolution), death and posthumous miracle.
- **1 Kings 19**: Initial call by Elijah.
- **Luke 4:27**: Jesus cites Elisha's cleansing of Naaman the Syrian as precedent for ministry beyond Israel.
- **Sirach 48:12–14** (deuterocanonical): Praises Elisha's wonders and that "his body prophesied" even in death.

**Tradition-Specific Reception**

**Judaism**: Major prophet who leads the "sons of the prophets," actively involved in the political and social crises of the north. Stories emphasize his compassion (widow, Shunammite), power over nature and death, and role in the Jehu revolution that ended Baal worship in the north (at least officially). Posthumous miracle (2 Kgs 13) underscores that the prophetic word/power continues beyond death.

**Christianity**: Typological foreshadowing of Christ's ministry of compassion, healing, and power over death — especially to outsiders (Naaman the Syrian explicitly cited by Jesus in Luke 4:27). The double portion and succession model prophetic continuity. The raising of the Shunammite's son and the axhead miracle illustrate God's care for the ordinary and the marginalized. In patristic reading, Elisha often prefigures the grace and accessibility of the gospel over against Elijah's more austere judgment.

**Islam**: Al-Yasa' (Elisha), prophet who succeeds Ilyas (Elijah) and continues the call to monotheism (Q 6:86; 38:48). Traditions emphasize his righteousness and the continuation of the prophetic line.

**Theological and Narrative Significance**

In the Deuteronomistic History (Provan), the Elisha cycle is the longest continuous prophetic narrative in Kings and functions as "salvation within judgment." While Elijah is the solitary zealot confronting the great public apostasy on Carmel, Elisha embodies YHWH's ongoing care for his people even as the northern kingdom slides toward the theological verdict of 2 Kgs 17. His miracles are deliberately ordinary and domestic (oil for a widow, a son's life, an axhead for a poor disciple) alongside the spectacular (healing a foreign general, delivering a city from siege). This demonstrates that YHWH's power is not limited to grand theophanies but extends to the daily needs of the faithful and even reaches beyond Israel (Naaman). The political engagement (Jehu anointing) shows the prophetic word shaping national destiny. The posthumous miracle (bones revive a corpse) affirms that the prophetic presence and power outlast the prophet's death — a powerful canonical note. In Leithart's reading of the broader history, the deliberate omission of the entire Elijah/Elisha cycle in Chronicles focuses attention on the Davidic/temple/liturgical line; the northern prophetic witness is real but secondary to the temple-centered "all Israel" ideal.

**Historicity and Interpretive Controversies**

- **Historical prophet**: Generally accepted that a historical Elisha and prophetic guild(s) stand behind the cycle; the political involvement with Jehu has some external corroboration in Assyrian records of the period.
- **Miracle density**: The concentration of wonder stories raises classic questions about legend formation, prophetic guilds as tradents, and the theological shaping of history by the Deuteronomist. Traditional readings affirm the events; critical scholarship often sees theological idealization or etiological elements.
- **Double portion**: Succession motif or literal multiplication of spirit/power? The Jordan parting and the "where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?" test the transfer.
- **Naaman and universalism**: The healing of a foreign military commander (with his request for "two mule-loads of earth") is a key text for God's concern beyond Israel and for the limits of ritual purity.

**Influence on Commentary Traditions**

Provan (NIBC) provides a dedicated excursus on Elisha in canonical context, explicitly noting that the miracle cycle supplies the backdrop for understanding Jesus' acts of compassion and power in the Synoptics (Luke 4:27 is the direct pointer). The stories are read as part of the book's didactic purpose: even in a time of national judgment and decline, YHWH remains active in salvation and provision through his prophets. Leithart and typological interpreters contrast the omission in Chronicles (liturgical Davidic focus) with the vivid northern prophetic witness preserved in Kings. Jewish tradition honors Elisha as a wonder-working leader of the prophetic communities and as part of the chain of tradition. Christian preaching and art frequently pair Elijah/Elisha with John the Baptist/Jesus or use individual miracles (Naaman for Gentile inclusion, axhead for God's care for the small, Shunammite for resurrection hope). The cycle has influenced discussions of prophetic communities, the relationship of miracle to message, and the continuation of charismatic power after the founder's departure. In all traditions the figure models prophetic succession, compassion in crisis, and the reach of God's word even to outsiders.

## Provan NIBC reingest (2026-07-18)

*Attributed to [[iain-provan|Iain W. Provan]], [[provan-1-2-kings-nibc|*1 and 2 Kings* (NIBC)]]. Digest R5, §35–§46.*

- **Humble channels:** Servants, maid, lepers, nurse mediate or receive God’s work; kings are often ignorant, arrogant, or obstructive.
- **Naaman:** Grace to outsider/leper; delayed Jordan cure proves living LORD, not prophetic magic; monotheism; Gehazi the judged insider; **Luke 4:27**.
- **Jehu incomplete:** Ahab + Baal destroyed; **golden calves remain**; still *yāšār* with fourth-generation promise because Baal’s fall outweighs Jeroboam-sins for now.
- **Athaliah / Joash:** Judean “Jezebel” nearly ends the seed; Joash hidden in the temple preserves David’s lamp—“David” survives “Jezebel.”
- **Bones (13:20–21):** Corpse/*šlḵ* // exile/*šlḵ*; residual power; exile-as-tomb with hope if prophetic teaching is held.
- **Abraham for the North (13:23):** Everlasting patriarchal grace inserted; law/grace tension for Israel as for Judah.
- **§46 Canonical excursus:** Elisha = **Joshua to Elijah’s Moses** (life-from-death, foreigner salvation, denser miracles after double portion); dual role (“God saves” / “judgment”); named only in Luke 4:27 but many implicit Gospel parallels; underplayed partly because successors raise “Jesus as John’s successor” anxieties.
