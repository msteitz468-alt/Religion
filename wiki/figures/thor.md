# Thor

**Thor** (Þórr; Anglo-Saxon **Thunor**, whence "Thursday") is the [[norse-religion|Norse]] god of **thunder,
strength, and protection** — wielder of the hammer **Mjölnir**, defender of gods and humans against the
**giants** (the forces of chaos). Red-bearded, immensely strong, and quick-tempered, he was the **most
popularly worshipped** of the gods — the farmer's and common man's protector, whose hammer hallowed weddings
and warded off harm.

## Myths in Detail — the Giant-Slayer

Thor's tales pit his blunt strength against the **giants** and the monsters of chaos:

- **Fishing for Jörmungandr.** Thor went fishing with the giant Hymir and **hooked the world-serpent
  [[jormungandr|Jörmungandr]]** itself; he hauled the monster up and raised his hammer to kill it — but the
  terrified giant cut the line. (At [[ragnarok|Ragnarök]] the two are fated to **kill each other**: Thor
  slays the serpent but dies of its venom after nine steps.)
- **The journey to Útgarða-Loki.** In the giant's stronghold Thor and his companions are set humiliating
  contests they cannot win — Thor cannot drain a drinking-horn, lift a "cat," or out-wrestle an old woman.
  Only afterward does the giant reveal these were **illusions**: the horn's other end was the **sea** (Thor
  drank it down a tide), the cat was **Jörmungandr** (he lifted the world), and the old woman was **old age**
  (whom none can beat). Thor's "failures" were in truth world-shaking feats.
- **Þrymr and the stolen hammer.** When the giant Þrymr stole Mjölnir and demanded **[[freyja|Freyja]]** as
  ransom, Thor had to dress as the **bride** (with Loki as bridesmaid) and go to the wedding — recovering his
  hammer when it was laid in "her" lap to hallow the marriage, and slaughtering the giants (a famously comic
  myth).

## Significance

Thor is the wiki's Norse instance of the **Indo-European storm-god dragon-slayer** ([[indra|Indra]]/[[vritra|
Vritra]], [[zeus|Zeus]]/Typhon; see [[the-combat-myth|the combat myth]]) — the divine **strength that holds
chaos back**. His everyday role as protector (not the aristocratic, treacherous wisdom of [[odin|Odin]]) made
him the **people's god**, and his hammer-amulets were worn defiantly during the Christianization.
