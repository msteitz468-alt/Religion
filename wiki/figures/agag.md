Agag, king of the [[amalekites|Amalekites]], is the man [[saul|Saul]] spares in violation of the
*ḥerem* command and whom [[samuel|Samuel]] then hacks in pieces before YHWH at [[gilgal|Gilgal]].
He appears in a single chapter, and his survival is the specific disobedience on which Saul's
rejection as king turns.

## Biographical Overview

1 Samuel 15 opens with Samuel delivering the *ḥerem* command against Amalek for what Amalek did to
Israel on the way up from Egypt: kill man and woman, infant and nursing child, ox and sheep, camel
and donkey. Saul musters, warns the Kenites to withdraw, and strikes Amalek — but spares Agag their
king, together with the best of the sheep, oxen, and fatlings. The narrator states the sparing of
king and livestock as one act of withholding.

Samuel confronts Saul with the sound of the animals — "What then is this bleating of sheep in my
ears?" — and Saul answers that the people took the best of the devoted things to sacrifice to YHWH.
Samuel's verdict is that to obey is better than sacrifice, that rebellion is as the sin of
divination, and that because Saul has rejected the word of YHWH, YHWH has rejected him from being
king. Saul confesses but asks Samuel to honor him before the elders of his people; as Samuel turns
to go, Saul seizes the skirt of his robe and tears it, and Samuel makes the tearing a sign that the
kingdom has been torn from him and given to a neighbor better than he.

Samuel then calls for Agag. Agag comes to him cheerfully — the Hebrew clause is difficult and has
been read both as confidence and as its opposite — and says, or is said to think, that the
bitterness of death is past. Samuel answers, "As your sword has made women childless, so shall your
mother be childless among women," and hacks Agag in pieces before YHWH at Gilgal. Samuel goes to
Ramah and does not see Saul again until the day of his death; YHWH regrets having made Saul king.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **1 Samuel 15:1–9** — the *ḥerem* command against Amalek; Saul's campaign; the sparing of Agag and
  the best livestock.
- **1 Samuel 15:10–31** — Samuel's confrontation; "obedience is better than sacrifice"; Saul's
  confession and concern for his standing before the elders; the torn robe.
- **1 Samuel 15:32–35** — Agag brought out; the *lex talionis* sentence; the hacking in pieces before
  YHWH at Gilgal; Samuel's withdrawal and YHWH's regret.
- **Numbers 24:7** — Balaam's oracle uses "Agag" as a name whose exaltation the coming Israelite
  king will exceed; whether this is the same figure, a dynastic title, or a variant reading is
  disputed and no identification is asserted here.
- **[[book-of-esther|Esther]] 3:1 and following** — Haman is styled "the Agagite," and Mordecai is
  given a Benjaminite genealogy running back to Kish, Saul's father. The connection is a matter of
  the book's own epithet and genealogy; see Reception.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **[[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] (NICOT 1 Samuel, as recorded on
  [[herem-canaanite-conquest|the ḥerem controversy page]] and [[1-samuel|1 Samuel]])** — Tsumura's
  claims bearing on Agag: **partial *ḥerem* is not *ḥerem***; on 15:9 it is Saul *and the people*
  who spare Agag and the best livestock, and "partial fulfillment is not a possibility" in ban
  logic. **Samuel's killing of Agag completes the ban rather than offering sacrifice**, against
  McCarter's proposal of "sacrificial butchering" on the strength of the phrase "before the LORD"
  and the sacrificial site at Gilgal: for Tsumura the act is categorically *ḥerem*, of the same kind
  as the judgment on [[achan|Achan]] in Joshua 7, and "before the LORD" marks a cultic setting
  without defining the act as sacrifice. He treats Samuel's sentence over Agag as poetic prose in
  two-line parallelism, framed as *lex talionis* at the national level rather than as cruelty. All
  attributed to Tsumura.
- **Esther tradition [reception connection, attributed to the text]** — the book of Esther styles
  Haman "the Agagite" and traces Mordecai to Kish the Benjaminite. Later Jewish reading commonly
  takes the epithet as making the Purim conflict a resumption of the Saul–Agag business, with
  Mordecai as the Benjaminite who finishes what Saul left undone. This wiki records the epithet and
  the genealogy as features of Esther's text, and the resumption reading as a reception move; it
  has not ingested a rabbinic source developing it, and none is cited here.
- No patristic or Islamic treatment of Agag has been ingested; none is asserted.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Agag's function is to make the *ḥerem* command a matter of a single, visible, reversible act. The
livestock could be argued about; a living king could not. His survival is the exhibit by which
Samuel's principle — obedience over sacrifice, rebellion as divination — is enforced, and it is the
hinge of the second and final rejection of Saul (the first, at Gilgal in ch. 13, had denied him a
dynasty; this one rejects him personally).

The execution is not softened by the narrative. Samuel does what Saul would not do, in the cultic
place, in the divine presence, and with a sentence of retributive symmetry pronounced over the man
first. The text presents the prophet completing an unexecuted command, and this wiki records that
without mitigation and without endorsement; the ethical problem the passage poses is treated on
[[herem-canaanite-conquest|the ḥerem controversy page]] rather than resolved here.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

- **Sacrifice or ban.** Whether Samuel's act is sacrificial (McCarter) or the completion of the
  *ḥerem* (Tsumura) is a live dispute; both positions are recorded on
  [[herem-canaanite-conquest|the ḥerem page]] and neither is adjudicated here.
- **Agag's approach.** The Hebrew describing how Agag comes to Samuel, and his words about the
  bitterness of death, are textually and semantically difficult; ancient versions differ, and
  translations diverge on whether he approaches confidently or in dread. No reading is settled here.
- **"Agag" in Numbers 24:7.** Whether the name there refers to this king, functions as an Amalekite
  royal title, or reflects a variant text is disputed.
- **The ethics of the command.** This is the sharpest instance, alongside Joshua, of the *ḥerem*
  problem — divine command, non-combatant killing, and the moral status of obedience. Positions
  (Woudstra's judicial framing, Webb's canonical trajectory, Tsumura's obedience-structure focus)
  are recorded on [[herem-canaanite-conquest|the controversy page]].
- **Historicity.** The Amalekite campaign is not externally attested and the composition history of
  1 Samuel 15 is debated; no verdict is offered.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

Agag is a fixed reference in three ongoing discussions: the meaning and limits of *ḥerem*; the
theology of prophetic authority over kingship in 1 Samuel; and, through Esther's "Agagite," the
biblical construction of Amalek as a trans-historical enemy — a construction with charged modern
deployments that the wiki flags on [[amalekites|the Amalekites page]] rather than repeating.
