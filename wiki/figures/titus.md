# Titus

## Biographical Overview
Titus was an early Christian coworker of Paul, mentioned 14 times in the New Testament (though only twice in the Pastoral Epistles: Titus 1:4 and 2 Timothy 4:10). A native Hellenistic Greek and an uncircumcised Gentile, Titus was brought by Paul and Barnabas to Jerusalem around AD 47 for the pivotal meeting with the apostolic "pillars" (Galatians 2:1-5). There, his uncircumcised status became a flashpoint with "false believers," and Paul's refusal to have him circumcised became a defining moment for the inclusion of Gentiles in the early church without the requirement of Jewish ritual law.

Titus later served as Paul's "partner and coworker" (2 Cor 8:23) in a difficult diplomatic role with the church in Corinth, helping to mend their rocky relationship with Paul and organizing the Jerusalem collection for Judean believers. By the mid-60s, Paul delegated him to the island of Crete to consolidate church planting and appoint elders (Titus 1:5). He later traveled to Dalmatia (2 Tim 4:10).

From Bock (Acts, this batch) and Yarbrough (Pastorals): Titus embodies the uncircumcised Gentile believer fully included in the mission; his role as delegate shows the passing of authority to the next generation.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Galatians 2:1–5**: Accompanies Paul and Barnabas to Jerusalem; serves as a test case for Gentile inclusion without circumcision.
- **2 Corinthians 2, 7, 8**: Functions as Paul's emissary to the Corinthians, delivering a severe letter, comforting Paul with news of their repentance, and managing the relief collection.
- **Titus**: The recipient of Paul's epistle, charged with organizing the churches and appointing elders on the island of Crete.
- **2 Timothy 4:10**: Mentioned briefly as having departed for Dalmatia near the end of Paul's life.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Titus represents the success and fidelity of the uncircumcised Gentile believer in the Pauline mission. While Timothy (who had Jewish heritage) was circumcised for the sake of the mission (Acts 16:3), Titus remained uncircumcised, embodying the theological truth that "not even Titus... was compelled to be circumcised, even though he was a Greek" (Gal 2:3). In his later ministry, his role highlights the transition of authority from the apostles to trusted delegates who were authorized to establish church order and confront false teaching. In Acts (Bock), his early presence with Paul and Barnabas at Jerusalem underscores the inclusive nature of the mission from the start.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Commentators like Robert W. Yarbrough emphasize Titus's extensive experience—nearly two decades of ministry by the time he received the Pastoral Epistle bearing his name. This explains the demanding, shorthand nature of the Epistle to Titus; Paul was not writing to a novice, but to a seasoned veteran with whom he shared a Hellenistic Greek background and a long history of confronting false teachers. From Bock (this batch): Titus illustrates the collaborative team (including uncircumcised Gentiles) through whom God advances the gospel.

## In Acts, on Bock's reading (BECNT)

*Added 2026-07-18 from [[bock-acts-becnt|Bock, *Acts* (BECNT)]]. All interpretive claims are Bock's
or those of scholars he names.*

Titus is **absent from Acts itself**; Bock discusses him only in the Galatians 2 chronology material
that surrounds his exposition of the [[jerusalem-council|Jerusalem Consultation]] (Acts 15) and the
circumcision of [[timothy|Timothy]] (Acts 16:1–3). There Bock makes Titus the load-bearing
**contrast case**: whereas Paul circumcises Timothy (a mixed-ancestry Jew) out of missionary
sensitivity, "Titus, who is Gentile, is a different case and principle. He is not to be circumcised
(Gal. 2:3–5)… there is no mixed-ancestry question, so there is no circumcision." For Bock the two
figures together fix the principle underlying Acts 15 — unmixed Gentiles need not be circumcised —
and answer Barrett's difficulty in reconciling Timothy's circumcision with Galatians. Bock also notes
Williams's use of "the Titus problem" as an objection to equating Acts 11:30 with the Galatians 2
visit, part of Bock's larger discussion of the [[acts-paul-tensions|Acts–Paul relationship]].
