# Apollos

## Biographical Overview

Apollos appears in [[acts|Acts]] 18:24–28 as an Alexandrian Jew who arrived at
[[ephesus|Ephesus]] during Paul's absence and then ministered in Achaia and
[[corinth|Corinth]]. Acts describes him as "an eloquent man, well versed in the
Scriptures" (18:24). On [[darrell-bock|Bock]]'s reading, "Apollos" is likely a short form
of Apollonius, and his Alexandrian origin is significant: Alexandria was noted for its
large Jewish population and its philosophical culture (Philo was from there). Bock takes
"well versed in the Scriptures" to mean that Apollos "understands how the Scriptures make
promises about the Christ" — a competence in messianic exegesis, not merely general
learning.

Bock notes that Apollos knew "only the baptism of John" (18:25), yet argues that Apollos
was already a believer: "His preaching is not inaccurate, merely incomplete." Bock
supports this by appeal to Acts 10:45–46, where one can have the Spirit without (Christian)
baptism, and reads Apollos as "a Christian of sorts" (Williams).

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Acts 18:24–26** — Apollos arrives at Ephesus, an eloquent and scripturally learned Jew,
  "fervent in spirit," teaching accurately about Jesus but knowing only John's baptism.
- **Acts 18:26** — [[paul-apostle|Priscilla and Aquila]] hear him in the synagogue and take
  him aside to explain "the way of God more accurately."
- **Acts 18:27–28** — With a letter of commendation he crosses to Achaia, where he
  "powerfully confuted the Jews," proving from the Scriptures that Jesus is the Christ.
- Cross-referenced by Bock to **1 Corinthians** (Apollos and Paul as builders on the same
  foundation).

## Tradition-Specific Reception (Bock's Reading)

On the disputed phrase **"fervent in spirit"** (ζέων τῷ πνεύματι, 18:25), Bock lays out two
views: (1) that it describes only Apollos's own enthusiasm or emotions — a reading on which
Apollos is not yet regenerate (Larkin), or a Jewish follower of Jesus "but not yet a
Christian" (Schneider); and (2) that it alludes to the work of the Holy Spirit, since the
article is present and the wording parallels Romans 12:11. Bock's verdict: "'fervent in
spirit' is the more likely reading here," so Apollos is a genuine believer whose
instruction was simply incomplete.

Bock stresses that **Priscilla and Aquila** "expounded... the way of God more accurately"
(ἐκτίθημι, "explain carefully," a verb found only in Acts), updating Apollos privately and
sensitively and moving him beyond John's baptism "probably to the gift of the Spirit." The
letter of commendation sent ahead of him to Achaia (cf. Romans 16:1–2; Acts 15:23–29)
functions as an early instance of inter-church recommendation. In Achaia Apollos
"powerfully confuted the Jews" (διακατηλέγχετο, a New Testament hapax meaning "overwhelm in
argument").

Bock **rejects the "Apollos as rival/opponent" thesis** (contra Käsemann, Haenchen),
following Trebilco (2004): 1 Corinthians shows Apollos and Paul building on the same
foundation rather than heading competing factions.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Bock frames the Apollos episode as a backdrop to the Ephesian ministry and as a model of
how an already-committed but incompletely instructed teacher is brought to fuller
understanding through the quiet, corrective work of fellow believers rather than public
rebuke. Apollos also illustrates Bock's larger observation that in Acts there is "no pattern
in how the Spirit comes": Apollos and the later Ephesian disciples of John (19:1–7) occupy
transitional situations that resist a single normative sequence of belief, baptism, and
Spirit-reception.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

The chief interpretive dispute concerns Apollos's spiritual status before his instruction by
Priscilla and Aquila — whether he was a Christian with defective knowledge (Bock, Williams)
or a Jewish enthusiast/John-disciple not yet Christian (Larkin, Schneider). A related dispute
concerns whether Apollos was later a rival to Paul at Corinth; Bock, with Trebilco, denies
this. A text-critical note: the Western text and some later manuscripts place Aquila before
Priscilla at 18:26, but older manuscripts (𝔓74, א, A, B, E, 33) name Priscilla first.
