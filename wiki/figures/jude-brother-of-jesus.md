# Jude (the Brother of Jesus)

Jude is the traditional author of the [[jude|Letter of Jude]] and one of the **brothers of the Lord**
— the younger siblings of Jesus named in Matt. 13:55 and Mark 6:3 (James, Joses/Joseph, Judas/Jude,
Simon). He self-identifies in his letter as "a slave of Jesus Christ and **brother of James**" (Jude
1), anchoring his authority in the honor of his prominent brother [[james-the-just|James]] rather
than in apostleship or the kinship to Jesus directly. This is the wiki's third **figure page**, after
[[james-the-just|James the Just]] and [[peter-apostle|Simon Peter]]; with Jude, the wiki now documents
two of the "brothers of the Lord" and rounds out the **family of Jesus** as a force in the early
Palestinian church. ([[gene-green|Gene L. Green]]'s BECNT *Jude and 2 Peter* is the source ingest; [[green-jude-2peter-becnt|source page]], fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18.)

## Identity and the Name

"Jude" (Greek *Ioudas*; Hebrew *Yĕhûdâ*, Judah) was among the most common Jewish names — Ilan catalogs
179 occurrences in Palestine — owing to its patriarchal and tribal roots; the same Greek name is
rendered in English as Jude, Judas, Judah, and (geographically) Judea. The author distinguishes
himself by the qualifier "brother of James." He must be distinguished from:
- **Judas Iscariot** (the betrayer);
- **Judas/Jude of the Twelve**, "son of James" (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13) — *son*, not *brother*, of a
  James, and so not the author;
- **Jude called Thaddaeus** (Matt. 10:3; Mark 3:18) — identified with the apostle Jude in the Western
  church, and with the brother of the Lord by later conflation.

The early church sometimes confused the brother of the Lord with the apostle Jude, and the Syrian
tradition even conflated him with **Thomas** ("Didymos Judas Thomas"). Green concludes the internal
NT evidence favors identifying the author as the brother of James and Jesus (Matt. 13:55; Mark 6:3).

## Biographical Overview

- **Family**: a younger brother of Jesus (named near the end of the sibling list, perhaps the
  youngest or next-youngest son). Like his brothers, he did **not believe in Jesus** during the
  ministry (John 7:5) but is found with the disciples and Mary before Pentecost (Acts 1:14).
- **Ministry**: the "brothers of the Lord" engaged in itinerant mission and were known alongside the
  apostles (1 Cor. 9:5) — which presupposes Jude acquired competence in Greek.
- **Family status, not dynasty**: James, Jude, and the others are never portrayed as a royal dynasty;
  both James and Jude style themselves "slave" of Jesus Christ (James 1:1; Jude 1). The family kept a
  humble socioeconomic standing.
- **The grandsons before Domitian**: Hegesippus (in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 3.19–20) records that
  Jude's **grandsons** — landed peasants of **Davidic descent** — were interrogated by Domitian as
  potential messianic claimants; they showed their work-calloused hands and their tiny landholding,
  and were dismissed as "simple folk." They later held leadership among the Palestinian churches.
  This is striking evidence both of the family's continuing recognition (the *desposynoi*, "the
  Lord's people") and of its non-dynastic humility.
- **Bishop lists**: some early lists (Apostolic Constitutions; Epiphanius) name a Jude among the early
  bishops of Jerusalem (after James and Simeon), reflecting the church's recognition of Jesus' family
  — though the identifications are tangled.

## The Kinship Question (Three Views)

As with [[james-the-just|James]], how Jude is related to Jesus depends on one's view of Mary:
- **Helvidian** (most Protestants; Green's preference, L1011): Jude is a *full/half-brother*, a son of
  Joseph and Mary — “the most accessible reading” of the nuclear-family language of Matt. 13:55.
- **Epiphanian** (Eastern Orthodox): a *step-brother*, son of Joseph by a prior marriage (preserving
  Mary's perpetual virginity) — Green: “possible but not probable,” and “distinctly theological” (to preserve Mary’s honor).
- **Hieronymian** (Jerome; Roman Catholic): a *cousin* (Mary of Clopas / Alphaeus conflations) — least probable for Green.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Matt. 13:55 / Mark 6:3** — named among Jesus' brothers (basis for the identification).
- **John 7:5; Acts 1:14** — the brothers' unbelief during the ministry, then their presence with the
  disciples after the resurrection.
- **1 Cor. 9:5** — "the brothers of the Lord" as itinerant missionaries.
- **Jude 1** — "Jude, a slave of Jesus Christ and brother of James," the author's self-identification.

## Tradition-Specific Reception

- **Palestinian Jewish Christianity**: Jude belongs, with James, to the *desposynoi* — the relatives
  of Jesus who exercised authoritative, apostle-like (but non-dynastic) leadership in the Palestinian
  churches across the first century. Green reads the Letter of Jude as a primary witness to this
  milieu and its **apocalyptic, midrashic** hermeneutic.
- **Canon history**: doubts about the Letter of Jude in antiquity attached not to Jude's identity but
  to his use of **1 Enoch** and the **Assumption of Moses** (see [[jude-1-enoch-canon|the canon
  controversy]]). Slow acceptance in Syria; honored in Alexandria (Clement wrote a commentary).
- **Western liturgy**: the apostle "Jude" (Thaddaeus) — often conflated with the brother of the Lord
  — became a popular saint (patron of desperate causes), a reception largely independent of the
  letter's exegesis.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Jude embodies, with James, the **family of Jesus** as a recognized authority structure in the early
church — yet one consciously framed in terms of *servanthood*, not dynastic privilege. His letter
gives the clearest NT window into the **apocalyptic and pesher-style exegesis** of Palestinian
Jewish Christianity, and his appeal to "the faith once for all delivered to the saints" (Jude 3)
preserves an early conception of the gospel as a defensible deposit.

From the NT sources in this batch (Gospels + Acts): In the Gospels he is named among the brothers who did not believe during the ministry (John 7:5; Mark 6:3; Matt. 13:55). In Acts 1:14 he is with the disciples and Mary in the upper room after the resurrection. Like James, he transitions from unbelief to leadership in the Palestinian church. Green (Jude/2 Peter) emphasizes his Palestinian Jewish-Christian provenance, the honorable *imitatio* relationship with 2 Peter, and his authoritative use of 1 Enoch as predictive revelation.

**Influence from this batch**: The Gospels and Acts locate Jude within the *desposynoi* (family of Jesus) who exercised non-dynastic, servant-framed authority in the early Palestinian communities. This provides the historical matrix for his letter's apocalyptic, midrashic hermeneutic and its defense of "the faith once for all delivered."
