## Biographical Overview

Kuntī was born Pṛthā, the daughter of Śūra, a worthy scion of the Yādava race and the grandfather of Kṛṣṇa. She was noted for her beauty and virtues from childhood. Because his cousin Kuntibhoja was childless, Śūra gave Pṛthā to him in adoption; from that time she was called Kuntī after her adoptive father (lines 1297–1301).

As a girl, the sage Durvāsa stayed as a guest in her father's house and she served him for a year with great care, patience, and devotion. So pleased was he that he granted her a divine mantra: if she repeated it while invoking any god, that deity would appear to her and bless her with a son equal to him in glory. He gave her the boon because his yogic insight foresaw misfortune awaiting her future husband (lines 1302–1308).

Out of impatient youthful curiosity, Kuntī immediately tested the mantra by invoking the Sun, whom she saw shining in the sky. The sun god appeared and, though Kuntī told him she was an unwedded girl who had merely been testing the boon and begged him to withdraw, he was bound by the mantra and could not leave. He reassured her that no blame would attach to her and that she would regain her virginity after bearing his son. Kuntī conceived and gave birth to Karṇa, who was born with divine armour and earrings, radiant as the sun. After his birth she regained her virginity as promised. Not knowing what to do with the infant and wishing to conceal her transgression, she placed him in a sealed box and set it adrift on a river; a childless charioteer found the box and his wife raised the child — thus Karṇa came to be known as a charioteer's son (lines 1309–1340).

When Kuntibhoja arranged a svayamvara for her, many princes gathered from across Bhāratavarṣa. Kuntī placed the garland on King [[pandu|Pāṇḍu]] of the Bharata race, whose personal splendour eclipsed all other princes; the marriage was solemnised and she accompanied him to his capital Hastinapura. On the advice of Bhīṣma and in accordance with the custom of ensuring progeny, Pāṇḍu also married Mādrī, the sister of the king of Madra (lines 1341–1353).

A curse by a sage whom Pāṇḍu shot while the sage was in the form of a deer condemned Pāṇḍu to die the instant he sought conjugal pleasure. Pāṇḍu retired to the forest with his wives and lived in complete abstinence. When he expressed his grief at being denied progeny, Kuntī confided to him the story of Durvāsa's mantra. At Pāṇḍu's urging, Kuntī used the mantra to invoke the gods and bore three sons: Yudhiṣṭhira (son of Dharma), Bhīma (son of the Wind god Vāyu), and [[arjuna|Arjuna]] (son of Indra). She also shared the mantra with Mādrī, who used it once and bore the twins Nakula and Sahadeva. Thus the five Pāṇḍavas were born in the forest among ascetics (lines 1354–1369).

When Pāṇḍu broke his abstinence in a moment of spring-madness and died instantly, Mādrī immolated herself on his funeral pyre out of remorse, entrusting her sons to Kuntī. The forest sages brought the bereaved Kuntī and the five boys to Hastinapura, placing them in Bhīṣma's care (lines 1369–1386).

## Primary Source Appearances

**Chapter VIII (lines 1296–1354):** Origin, the Durvāsa boon, the birth of Karṇa (including the episode in full), the svayamvara, marriage to Pāṇḍu.

**Chapter IX (lines 1354–1394):** The curse on Pāṇḍu, the disclosure of the mantra, the births of the five Pāṇḍavas, Pāṇḍu's death, Mādrī's immolation, and the return to Hastinapura.

**Chapter XI (lines 1508–1510):** At the princes' public exhibition, Kuntī recognises Karṇa as her firstborn son (she alone knows the truth) and faints; Vidura sends a maidservant to attend her; she revives but stands stupefied, unable to act.

**Chapter XIII–XIV (lines 1784–1920):** Kuntī and her sons are sent to Vāraṇāvata. Vidura's cryptic warning is communicated to her; she shares it with the Pāṇḍavas. On the night of escape from the wax palace, Kuntī arranges the feast to lull the guards; she is carried out through the tunnel by Bhīma on his shoulders. In the forest she collapses from exhaustion (line 1894) and is tended by Bhīma.

**Chapter XV (lines 1923–1930):** In Ekacakra the family lives in brahmin disguise; Kuntī divides the alms brought by her sons, giving half to Bhīma alone (for his great appetite and strength) and sharing the rest among herself and the other four brothers.

**Chapter XV (lines 2005–2053):** When the brahmin landlord's family faces the rakṣasa Bakāsura, Kuntī offers Bhīma as substitute, citing her confidence in his divine-born strength. She also admits her concern that revealing Bhīma's powers would expose the Pāṇḍavas to Duryodhana's spies.

**Chapter XVI (lines 2114–2123):** Kuntī divine-reads her sons' desire to attend Draupadī's svayamvara and gracefully manages the suggestion to leave Ekacakra.

**Chapter XVII (lines 2379–2386):** Vidura comes to her at Drupada's court; she affirms her trust in him and agrees to return to Hastinapura. Called "son of Vicitravīrya" by Kuntī in a form of address meaning he is family (line 2380).

**Chapter XXIV (line 3025):** The chapter on the dice game notes Yudhiṣṭhira's vow not to oppose his kinsmen's wishes — he had vowed this partly because Vyāsa had warned him of coming disasters; the narrator connects this to Kuntī's inadvertent command that the brothers "share all things" (cited at Chapter XVI, line 2248).

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Kuntī occupies the fulcrum between two irreconcilable demands: divine motherhood and social shame. The Karṇa episode establishes her as a woman who made a mistake of youth (testing the boon impulsively), covered it with concealment, and carried that secret through her son's adult life — including the devastating moment at the princes' exhibition when she sees Karṇa and knows him but can say nothing.

Rajagopalachari emphasises her practical worldly wisdom (lines 2121–2123: "Kunti was second to none in worldly wisdom and sagacity and could gracefully divine her sons' thoughts"). This quality recurs: she is the one who sees the wax palace for what it is, who feeds the servants to make them sleep, who negotiates with the brahmin landlord about Bakāsura. She is simultaneously a woman of profound suffering and a decisive agent in her family's survival.

The mantra tradition makes her the vehicle through which five sons of five gods enter the epic; she is thus structurally parallel to Gāndharī, who produces Dhritarāṣṭra's hundred sons through her own boon — the two mothers of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]]'s warring camps.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Rajagopalachari presents all events as legendary. The narrator's footnote at line 1354 explicitly flags that the curse on Pāṇḍu (narrated next) explains why Kuntī used the mantra — an authorial act of sequencing the story for a popular audience rather than a scholarly commentary.
