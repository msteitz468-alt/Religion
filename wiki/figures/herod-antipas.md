# Herod Antipas

## Biographical Overview

Herod Antipas was, in the account [[rt-france|R. T. France]] gives (*The Gospel of Matthew*,
NICNT, 2007), "tetrarch of Galilee and Perea from 4 B.C. to A.D. 39, and thus the ruler of
[[galilee|Galilee]] during Jesus' adult life." France notes that he is mentioned only in Matt
14:1-12 within [[matthew|Matthew's Gospel]].

On titulature France is precise: none of Herod the Great's three sons "(unlike their father) was
officially given the title 'king'" — Archelaus was ethnarch, Antipas and Philip tetrarchs.
Matthew correctly calls Antipas "tetrarch" at 14:1 while using "king" at 14:9, a title France
describes as one "which Antipas wanted and campaigned for… but which Rome never officially
approved for him," and which local usage employed to flatter him (reflected also at Matt 2:22).

France stresses that Antipas's Jewish standing was thin: "how thin was Antipas's supposed
Jewishness (his father, Herod, was Idumean, not really Jewish, and his mother Samaritan)" — which
made him vulnerable to orthodox Jewish criticism, of which [[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]]
was an outspoken and persistent representative. Politically, France describes Antipas as ruling a
geographically split tetrarchy from two palaces: "Antipas maintained palaces both in his new city
of Tiberias in Galilee and at Machaerus," presumably moving between them. He also rebuilt
Sepphoris, four miles from [[nazareth|Nazareth]], as Galilee's capital.

France gives Antipas a small military establishment rather than a Roman one: "there was no Roman
legion stationed in Palestine at this time, but Herod Antipas had a small force of auxiliary
troops," drawn from the non-Jewish population of surrounding areas such as Phoenicia and Syria.

**The marriage to Herodias.** France argues that the marriage was doubly objectionable: "Not only
did the marriage infringe the Mosaic restrictions on the marriage of relatives (Lev 18:16;
20:21), but it had also been achieved only by means of two divorces." Antipas's own divorce of
his first wife, France judges, "would probably have been religiously acceptable to the less
rigorous school of Hillel, but not to the stricter Shammaite party," who allowed divorce only for
the wife's sexual infidelity (see [[divorce|divorce]]). Herodias for her part repudiated her
first husband, which Jewish law forbade; "she had probably invoked Roman law, which did allow a
wife to divorce her husband, but that would not have made it any more acceptable to Jewish
opinion." France reads Herodias as "the dominant personality in the story," and suggests her
daughter's dance "was designed from the beginning" to undermine Antipas's defences.

## Primary Source Appearances

- **Matt 2:22** (indirectly, on Herodian titulature) — the Herodian succession after Herod the
  Great, with Archelaus in Judea and the tetrarchs elsewhere.
- **Matt 4:12** — the arrest of John, which triggers Jesus' withdrawal to Galilee. France reads
  the withdrawal as in part political: as John's "successor" Jesus could not be safe in Antipas's
  Perean territory.
- **Matt 14:1-12** — the identification of Jesus as the risen John, and the flashback narrating
  John's execution.
- **Matt 14:13** — Jesus' further withdrawal, which France takes as evidence that "he regarded
  Antipas as a potential threat to himself."

France reads 14:3-12 as a flashback: "Verses 3-12 are a 'flashback,' needed to explain Antipas's
extraordinary idea that Jesus is the resurrected John." The link between John's death and Jesus'
withdrawal he judges "probably more a literary device than a deliberate chronological link," since
Antipas's identification of Jesus with John must postdate John's death. Matthew ties the
chronologically unconnected incident in by having John's disciples report to Jesus (v. 12).
France further flags a deliberate literary contrast between "this degenerate scene of Antipas's
lavish feast with its sordid and tragic outcome and the wholesome simplicity of the 'feast' which
will follow" in the feeding of the five thousand. He notes that 14:3-12 is "the one substantial
pericope in the gospel after Jesus' adult appearance on the scene in 3:13 which is not directly
concerned with him and his disciples."

Two textual details France draws out. The dancer is called *korasion*, the diminutive used of
Jairus's daughter, and "the term would be appropriate to a younger teenager, and what we know of
the family history supports such an age." And "the 'telling'" by John "was presumably by public
denunciation since John would hardly have had direct access to Antipas before his arrest" — the
imperfect indicating that John "kept on telling him."

## Tradition-Specific Reception

The ingested source is a single modern Protestant critical commentary, and this page carries no
survey of patristic, medieval, or liturgical reception of Antipas. France does record one strand
of later Christian popular development: the dancer "is unnamed in the gospel accounts," and both
the identification as Salome and the "dance of the seven veils" belong to "subsequent popular
elaboration." Beyond this, the commentary as digested supplies no tradition-specific reception
material on Antipas, and this section is deliberately left thin rather than filled from
background knowledge.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

France's reading gives Antipas a structural rather than a merely biographical role. He suggests
Antipas "may well have been concerned about the possible implications of Jesus' announcement of
the coming of a '[[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]]'" — the tetrarch as the political
correlate of the gospel's central proclamation. He classifies Antipas's belief that Jesus is the
risen John as "popular superstition… rather than a worked-out theology of resurrection such as
that of the [[pharisees|Pharisees]]," comparing cross-cultural returning-figure beliefs
([[elijah|Elijah]], Nero, King Arthur, Elvis).

Most importantly, France reads the pericope as prospective as well as retrospective: "this
pericope is not just a flashback but also a foreshadowing of what is to happen to the 'second
John.'" As Jesus continues John's mission and authority, he will share John's fate of unjust
execution. France draws the parallel further: as with John, Jesus' popularity as a prophet
obstructed the authorities' desire to arrest him; "in the case of Jesus the problem of public
support will be overcome by strategy and the recruitment of Judas; in John's case it happens
apparently almost by accident."

Antipas thus stands in France's Galilee/Jerusalem framework as the northern political power whose
menace shapes Jesus' movements — the reason the ministry withdraws, itinerates, and stays out of
Perea (see [[galilee|Galilee]]).

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies

Positions are recorded here as France records them; the wiki does not adjudicate.

- **The date of John's death — evidence explicitly inconclusive.** France states plainly: "There
  is no independent information on the date of John's death." Josephus (*Ant.* 18.116-19) links
  John's death with Aretas's AD 36 invasion of Perea only via popular opinion, not by close
  sequence.
- **Josephus versus Matthew on the motive.** Josephus attributes the imprisonment and execution
  to fear of John's political influence; Matthew names the marriage issue. France harmonizes
  rather than choosing: Josephus's motive "would be consistent with" Matthew's, since Josephus
  also records the political fallout of the Herodias marriage and the divorce that enabled it,
  which provoked a ruinous war with the Nabatean king of Petra. France also cites Josephus
  (*Ant.* 18.119) locating John's imprisonment and execution at Antipas's palace at Machaerus in
  Perea, which he finds consistent with the prison being close to the birthday celebrations.
  Elsewhere France cites Josephus (*Ant.* 18.118) that "Antipas saw the baptizing movement as a
  potential source of sedition."
- **The "Philip" problem — unresolved.** France notes that Josephus names Herodias's first
  husband simply "Herod" and elsewhere makes Herod Philip the husband of Salome. Since "Herod"
  served as both a family and a personal name, confusion is possible, and France leaves the
  matter open: "it is possible that the 'Herod' who was Herodias's first husband also bore the
  name Philip."
- **Antipas's reluctance (v. 5 versus v. 9).** France identifies a tension — v. 5 says Antipas
  wanted to kill John, v. 9 reports his reluctance — and offers two explanations without
  deciding: reluctance about summary execution without trial, or a relic of Mark's explanation
  that Herodias, not Antipas, wanted John dead.
- **The source of the court story — flagged speculative.** France suggests the account may
  reflect popular report, or "it is possible that it derives from people in the Herodian court
  who had links with the Jesus movement" (Luke 8:3; Acts 13:1), and flags this as speculation.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions

France refers the reader to a single monograph for the historical problems of this passage: the
Herod family identifications and related difficulties "are patiently unraveled in an exhaustive
study by H. W. Hoehner." This is the one scholarly authority the digested material records
France naming on Antipas; the ingest supplies no wider survey of the commentary tradition on the
figure, and none is supplied here.

## France on Antipas in Mark (NIGTC Mark, 2002)

*Added 2026-07-18 from the ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], [[france-mark-nigtc|*The Gospel
of Mark* (NIGTC)]]. All interpretive claims are France's unless another scholar is named.*

- **Titulature again**: France notes Mark styles Antipas "basileus" throughout 6:14-29 though
  he was technically only tetrarch (a royal title refused by Augustus; Antipas was deposed
  A.D. 39 while campaigning for it) — reflecting Galilean popular usage "rather than
  constitutional niceties."
- **The move to Galilee (1:14)**: since Galilee remained Antipas's territory, Jesus' move there
  after John's arrest was *not* a tactical escape from the ruler who arrested John but "the
  appropriate way for Jesus, the Galilean, to begin his own independent and public ministry."
- **The execution of John (6:14-29)**: France explains Antipas's alarm via Josephus (*Ant.*
  18.116-19) — John was executed for sedition-potential, and to Antipas "one successful
  'revival' preacher looked very much like another." The sandwiching of John's death within the
  mission of the Twelve shows "what the mission of the kingdom of God can expect from the
  kingdoms of this world." France highlights the Ahab/Jezebel–Elijah pattern behind
  Herodias/Antipas–[[john-the-baptist|John]], and calls the parallel between Antipas's
  ineffectual resistance to Herodias and [[pontius-pilate|Pilate]]'s to the priests (15:1-15)
  "remarkable" — Mark's way of linking the fates of John and Jesus (Antipas's "righteous and
  holy" John echoed in Pilate's implicit view of Jesus). On the historical problems (via
  Hoehner): Herodias's first husband is "Philip" in Mark but "Herod" in Josephus, and Machaerus
  (Josephus's execution site) sits awkwardly with Mark's Galilean guest list — "Mark was
  unaware (or Josephus was misinformed) of the specific location." Against the well-attested
  variant making the dancer Antipas's own daughter "Herodias" (NRSV), France argues narrative
  continuity shows Mark assumed Herodias's daughter (traditionally Salome), αὐτοῦ being an
  early error. He contrasts Antipas's degenerate banquet with the wilderness feeding that
  follows — "a foretaste of the messianic banquet."
- **Antipas as enemy**: France observes that after 6:16 "Mark records little public activity by
  Jesus within Antipas's territories," the narrative resuming with a retreat across the lake —
  suggesting Jesus regarded Antipas as an enemy. The warning against "the yeast of the
  Pharisees and of Herod" (8:15) he reads as focused on those who "constitute a real threat to
  Jesus' life" (the 3:6/12:13 alliance) rather than on any specific connotation of the yeast
  metaphor, which "remains elusive" in Mark. The [[pharisees|Pharisee]]–Herodian coalition of
  3:6 he finds unsurprising once the modern religion/politics separation is discarded (noting
  the plausible suggestion that the Herodians were the Boethusians). At 8:28 he judges
  Antipas's John-redivivus surmise "not so eccentric after all," since all popular assessments
  of Jesus fell within the prophet category; the danger of the divorce question (10:2) was real
  "given John the Baptist's fate under Antipas in Peraea."
- **Endnote (via Hoehner)**: Antipas is "unlikely to have shared Pharisaic views of an
  afterlife"; his "John raised" remark may be irony or Hellenistic superstition about the dead
  haunting their killers — converging with France's "popular superstition" verdict recorded
  above from the Matthew commentary.
