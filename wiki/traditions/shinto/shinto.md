# Shinto

**Chronology:** see the [[shinto-timeline|Timeline of Shinto]] and the [[comparative-timeline|Comparative Timeline of World Religions]].

**Shinto** (神道 *Shintō*, "the way of the **[[kami|kami]]**") is the **indigenous religion of Japan** — a
tradition of reverence for the *kami*, the innumerable sacred powers immanent in nature, ancestors, and
remarkable things. It has **no founder, no fixed creed, no commanding moral code, and (until modern times) no
single scripture or even a name of its own**: the word *Shintō* was coined only to distinguish native
kami-worship from imported **Buddhism**. Shinto is less a system of belief than a **way of acting** — of
**purity, gratitude, and right relationship** with the kami through ritual, festival, and shrine.

## The kami and the world

The cosmos of Shinto is **populated by [[kami|kami]]** — the *yaoyorozu no kami*, the "eight million" gods. A
kami may be a **great deity** (the sun-goddess **[[amaterasu|Amaterasu]]**), a **natural force or place** (a
mountain, waterfall, ancient tree, the wind, rice), an **ancestor or emperor**, or any being or object that
inspires awe (*musubi*, the generative power, runs through all of it; see [[musubi|musubi]]). The kami are
**not omnipotent or wholly transcendent**: they live alongside humans, can be **benevolent or wrathful**, and
must be **honored, thanked, and placated**. There is **no creator from nothing** and **no absolute moral
dualism** of good and evil; the great polarity is rather **purity vs. pollution**.

## Purity, pollution, and ritual

The ethical-ritual heart of Shinto is **[[harae|harae]]** — **purification** — and its opposite **kegare**,
**pollution or defilement** (incurred above all by **death, blood, and disease**). One approaches the kami
**clean**: rinsing mouth and hands at the shrine, undergoing ritual cleansing, casting impurity into running
water. The aim of religious life is *makoto* (sincerity) and the maintenance of **purity and harmony**, not
the overcoming of sin. Worship centers on the **jinja (shrine)**, marked by the **torii** gate, where the kami
is enshrined in a *honden*; on **matsuri** (festivals) that renew the bond of community and kami; and on
**offerings, norito (prayers), and divination**.

## Scripture and the imperial myth

Shinto's nearest thing to scripture is the pair of **8th-century imperial chronicles**, the
**[[kojiki|Kojiki]]** ("Record of Ancient Matters," 712) and the **[[nihongi|Nihon Shoki / Nihongi]]**
("Chronicles of Japan," 720), which record the **age of the gods**: the creation of the islands by
**[[izanagi-and-izanami|Izanagi and Izanami]]**, the birth of **[[amaterasu|Amaterasu]]**, **[[tsukuyomi|Tsukuyomi]]**,
and **[[susanoo|Susanoo]]**, the drama of the **heavenly rock-cave**, Susanoo's slaying of the
**[[yamata-no-orochi|eight-headed serpent]]**, the cession of the land by **[[okuninushi|Ōkuninushi]]**, and the
**descent of [[ninigi|Ninigi]]** — Amaterasu's grandson — whose great-grandson **[[jimmu|Jimmu]]** becomes the
first emperor. These texts ground the **imperial house's claim to descend from the sun-goddess**, the
politically explosive doctrine later harnessed by **[[state-shinto|State Shinto]]**.

## Hermeneutics

Shinto reading runs in two channels: **mythic-ritual** (the Kojiki narratives enacted and renewed in shrine
festival), and **philological-theological**, above all the **Kokugaku ("national learning")** movement —
**Motoori Norinaga**'s monumental 18th-century commentary on the Kojiki — which sought to recover a "pure"
Japanese way beneath Chinese and Buddhist overlay. For over a millennium Shinto was also fused with Buddhism in
**shinbutsu-shūgō** (kami understood as manifestations of buddhas), a fusion forcibly broken only in 1868.

## Map of sub-traditions

- **[[shrine-shinto|Shrine Shinto (Jinja Shintō)]]** — the mainstream: worship of the kami at shrines, the
  ordinary practice of most Japanese.
- **[[state-shinto|State Shinto (Kokka Shintō)]]** — the modern (1868–1945) nationalized cult of emperor and
  nation, since disestablished.
- **[[sect-shinto|Sect Shinto (Kyōha Shintō)]]** — the thirteen 19th-century organized sects (mountain cults,
  faith-healing, new revelations).
- **[[folk-shinto|Folk Shinto (and shinbutsu-shūgō)]]** — popular, domestic, and Buddhist-fused kami practice.
