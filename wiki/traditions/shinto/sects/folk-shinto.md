# Folk Shinto (and shinbutsu-shūgō)

**Folk Shinto** (*Minzoku Shintō*) is the **unorganized popular substratum** of [[shinto|Shinto]] — the
everyday, domestic, and agricultural kami-practice of ordinary people, without theology or priesthood. It is
the **oldest layer** of Japanese religion and the **most thoroughly fused with Buddhism**.

## Popular kami practice

- the **kamidana** ("kami shelf"), the household altar enshrining the **ujigami** (tutelary/ancestral kami) and
  charms (*ofuda*) from shrines such as [[amaterasu|Ise]] and **[[inari|Inari]]**;
- **agricultural rites** for planting and harvest, **rain-making**, and the kami of the **rice field (ta no
  kami)**;
- **life-cycle and seasonal customs** — New Year (*shōgatsu*) visits, the **dōsojin** (roadside guardian
  stones), protective charms, and divination;
- belief in **spirits and the dead** — including the placation of restless or vengeful spirits (*goryō*,
  *onryō*) and ancestral spirits, overlapping with [[folk-shinto|Buddhist]] memorial rites.

## Shinbutsu-shūgō — the Shinto–Buddhist fusion

For over a millennium (roughly **8th–19th centuries**) kami-worship and **Buddhism** were so intertwined that
they formed a single popular religious world, **shinbutsu-shūgō** ("kami-and-buddha fusion"). Its doctrine,
**honji suijaku** ("original ground, manifest trace"), held that the **kami are local manifestations
(*suijaku*) of the buddhas and bodhisattvas (*honji*)** — so **[[amaterasu|Amaterasu]]** could be identified
with the cosmic Buddha **Dainichi (Vairocana)**, and shrines and temples shared precincts and clergy. This
fusion (in forms such as **Ryōbu Shintō**) was **forcibly broken** by the Meiji **shinbutsu bunri** of 1868,
but its imprint on popular practice — Japanese routinely marrying "Shinto-style" and being buried
"Buddhist-style" — endures.
