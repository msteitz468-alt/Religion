# Shrine Shinto (Jinja Shintō)

**Shrine Shinto** (*Jinja Shintō*) is the **mainstream, normative form of [[shinto|Shinto]]** — the ordinary
practice of kami-worship at the tens of thousands of **shrines (jinja)** across Japan, in which most Japanese
participate without exclusive membership. It is **Shinto as lived**: not a creed but a **cycle of reverence,
purity, offering, and festival** centered on the local and great shrines.

## The shrine and its worship

A shrine is **sacred space** set apart by the **torii** gate; the kami is enshrined in the inner **honden**,
often in a sacred object (*shintai* — a mirror, sword, or stone) that is **never seen**. The worshipper
**purifies** (rinsing mouth and hands at the *temizuya*), approaches, **bows, claps, and offers** a coin,
prayer, or rice/sake; priests perform **norito** (ritual prayers), present offerings (*shinsen*), and conduct
**matsuri** (festivals) that bring the kami out in portable shrines (*mikoshi*) through the community. Life-cycle
rites — the **miyamairi** of newborns, weddings, blessings — and seasonal and agricultural festivals bind
families and locality to their **ujigami** (tutelary kami).

## Major shrine networks

- **[[amaterasu|Amaterasu]]** at the **Grand Shrine of Ise** — the spiritual center, rebuilt every 20 years.
- **[[inari|Inari]]** shrines (Fushimi Inari and ~30,000 others) — rice, prosperity, the fox-messengers.
- **[[hachiman|Hachiman]]** shrines — the kami of war and the deified Emperor Ōjin.
- **Izumo Taisha** — the shrine of **[[okuninushi|Ōkuninushi]]**, lord of the unseen.

## Canon and hermeneutics

Shrine Shinto's mythic charter is the **[[kojiki|Kojiki]]** and **[[nihongi|Nihongi]]** age-of-the-gods
narrative, but its mode is **ritual rather than doctrinal**: the myths are **enacted and renewed** in festival
and enshrinement, not expounded as dogma. Since 1946 most shrines are coordinated by the **Association of
Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honchō)**.
