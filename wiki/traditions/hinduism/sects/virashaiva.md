# Virashaiva (Lingayat)

**Virashaivas** ("Shiva's Heroes") — also called **Lingayats** ("People of the Linga") and
**Charanas** ("Wanderers") — are a large South Indian Hindu sect founded by [[basava|Basava]]
(1106–1167) in Karnataka. They were, at their founding, one of the most radical anti-establishment
sectarian movements in Indian history: rejecting stone temples, Brahmin ritual authority, caste, and
the megalomania of the temple-building economy, while insisting that every devotee's body was itself
a moving temple of Shiva.

## Identity and Origins

The Virashaivas emerged in twelfth-century Karnataka at the court of King Bijjala of Kalyana, where
Basava served as a Brahmin minister. Basava's opposition to both institutional religion and caste
hierarchy was radical enough that the sect was persecuted; a legend describes a Pariah boy who
married a Brahmin girl, whose fathers were condemned to death — the Virashaivas rioted and
assassinated the king. Basava himself eventually withdrew from the community he had founded, troubled
by its violence.

The name "Charanas" (Wanderers) evokes their anti-householder, itinerant identity: they prided
themselves on being moving temples, refusing to put down roots.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Virashaivas diverge from normative Shaiva Hinduism in three connected areas:

1. **Temple worship**: Stone temples are rejected entirely. "The rich will make temples for Shiva. /
   What shall I, a poor man, do? / My legs are pillars, the body the shrine, the head a cupola of gold. /
   Things standing shall fall, but the moving ever shall stay." (Basava)
2. **Priestly intermediary**: Initiation is required, but there is no professional Brahmin priest class.
   Every Virashaiva wears a small linga (*istalinga*) on the body and worships it directly.
3. **Caste**: Explicitly rejected. Membership open across caste lines, including Pariahs; the only
   social category recognized is the community of devotees.

Their rejection of the Brahmin imaginary may have been influenced by Muslim missionaries active on
India's west coast at precisely the moment when Virashaiva doctrine was forming. Conversely, the
threat of Islamic iconoclasm may have been one reason for the widespread adoption of portable linga
images over stone temple idols.

## Canon and Scripture

The Virashaivas have no separate scriptural canon distinct from the Puranas. Their primary literary
form is the *vachana* (spoken/utterance poem) — short, vernacular, personal compositions in Kannada
by their saints, including Basava and Mahadevyyakka. These are devotional rather than doctrinal texts.

The **Basava Purana** (13th c. CE, Telugu, by Palkuriki Somanatha) is the earliest full narrative
hagiography of Basava. Doniger describes it as "an extraordinarily violent book": Virashaiva heroes
perpetually decapitate, mutilate, or poison enemies — primarily Brahmins and Jainas — and
sometimes themselves (for lapses in devotional intensity). "Things standing shall fall" extends to
Brahmin and Jaina power structures; the god in the temple murti sneaks out the back door in
humiliation; washer-men, thieves, and Pariahs win out over religious power mongers.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **The body as moving temple**: Every Virashaiva's body is the linga's dwelling place. This makes
  stone temples not just unnecessary but a sign of misplaced attachment: "How can I feel right about
  gods you sell in your need, and gods you bury for fear of thieves?"
- **Istalinga**: The personal linga worn around the neck, the sole object of Virashaiva worship.
- **Union with Shiva**: The only soteriological goal. No other deity, no other ritual, no afterlife
  in any other sense. The devotee becomes Shiva.
- **Left-hand classification**: In the South Indian binary of left-hand vs. right-hand castes
  (artisans/merchants/servants vs. agricultural workers), Virashaivas were classified as left-hand,
  implying their social militancy.

## Hermeneutical Method

Virashaiva devotion is expressed in **vernacular Kannada poetry** — the *vachanas* — rather than
Sanskrit Vedic or Agamic authority. The interpretive tradition is hagiographic: the saint's life
as embodied theology. Doctrinal positions emerge from the stories of the saints rather than from
systematic treatises. This parallels the South Indian Nayanmars' relationship to the Shaiva Agamas:
poetic testimony precedes doctrinal systematization.

## Mahadevyyakka: Female Saint

In the twelfth century, the female Virashaiva saint **Mahadevyyakka** composed Kannada poems that
addressed simultaneously the metaphysics of Maya and the domestic predicament of a woman under
patrilocal authority:

> "I have Maya for mother-in-law; the world for father-in-law; three brothers-in-law, like tigers. . . .
> I will give this working wench the slip and go cuckold my husband with Hara, my Lord."

A. K. Ramanujan reads the husband as symbolic of karma ("the past of the ego's many lives") and
the sister-in-law as *vasana* (the binding memory that clings to karma). The poem uses the vulgar
word for "cuckold" — transgressive language as spiritual declaration. Mahadevyyakka ultimately
left her human husband, wandered naked clothed only in her hair, and died still in her twenties.

Her life provides "a paradigm precisely for atypicality, for the possibility that a woman might shift
the paradigm," though the roles of teacher and erotic partner still map onto male/female respectively.

## Historical Development

Virashaivism spread as a militant lay movement, primarily in Karnataka but with influence into
Andhra Pradesh (the Telugu Basava Purana). Internal violence — directed against Jainas and Brahmins —
was a characteristic feature that Basava himself eventually rejected. Despite this, the movement
survived and evolved into the large Karnataka Lingayat community, now one of the most politically
prominent communities in the region.

**Muslim influence**: Doniger notes that Muslim social customs, unrestricted by caste, may have
deeply influenced Basava's vision of a caste-free community. Whether cause or parallel, the
Virashaiva rejection of the Brahmin imaginary was historically contemporaneous with the growing
Muslim presence in South India.

## Relationship to Other Sects

- **Nayanmars**: Shares the Tamil Shaiva bhakti tradition's emphasis on devotional intensity, the
  body as locus of worship, and saint hagiography — but pushes further against all institutional
  religion.
- **Kapalikas and Pashupatas**: Virashaivas are not Tantric; they reject antinomian transgression
  in favor of ethical purity within devotion to Shiva.
- **Kabir**: A northern parallel — anti-caste, vernacular, resistant to both Hindu and Muslim
  orthodoxy — though Kabir's is nirgun (without qualities) bhakti, while Virashaiva devotion is
  saguna (with a specific form: the linga).
