# Brahmo Samaj

The **Brahmo Samaj** ("Society of God") is a Hindu reform movement founded in Calcutta in 1828 by
[[rammohun-roy|Raja Rammohun Roy]]. It represents the most thoroughgoing adaptation of Hinduism to
Protestant rationalism and Enlightenment deism in the 19th century — while simultaneously
articulating a *Hindu* critique of what Roy saw as the degradation of original Vedic monotheism
by Puranic polytheism.

## Identity and Origins

Roy founded the Brahmo Samaj on the doctrines of the **Upanishads**, presenting it as a return to
the "pure" monotheism of the earliest Hindu texts. The "Brahmo" of the name refers to *Brahman* —
the impersonal Absolute of the Upanishads — not the god Brahma.

The key influences on the founding theology:

- **Upanishadic Vedanta**: the monistic Absolute as the true Hindu God
- **Islamic monotheism**: Roy's study of Islam reinforced his opposition to polytheism
- **18th-century deism and Unitarianism**: a transcendent Creator God reached through reason
- **British Protestant criticism of Hinduism**: absorbed and reframed as internal Hindu self-critique

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Brahmo Samaj **rejected** the following central elements of mainstream Hinduism:

- Image worship (*pūjā*, temple devotion, pilgrimage, festival ritual)
- Polytheism and the devotional Puranas
- Caste discrimination and ritual pollution
- Suttee and child marriage
- The authority of the dharma-shastras (Manu, etc.) as divine law

It **retained**:
- The language of Vedic/Upanishadic Hinduism
- The Brahmin-led religious gathering structure
- Sanskrit scriptural authority (selectively)

Roy himself continued wearing the sacred thread and observing some Brahmin customs — the Brahmo
Samaj was a reform *within* Hinduism, not a departure from it (though critics argued otherwise).

## Canon and Scripture

The Brahmo Samaj elevated the **Upanishads** as the primary scriptural authority and Roy translated
several Upanishads into Bangla (1825). The **Bhagavad Gita** was respected but subordinated to the
Upanishads. The Puranas and devotional texts were generally dismissed.

This canon selection reflected both genuine Vedantic conviction and British Protestant preference:
the British admired the "philosophical" Hinduism of the Upanishads and despised the "idol-worshiping"
Puranic religion. The Brahmo canon *coincided* with what the colonizers considered respectable.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Monotheism**: God is one, transcendent, reachable through reason and prayer
- **Anti-ritualism**: External religious practices (images, temple ritual, caste purity) are obstacles
- **Social ethics**: Abolition of suttee, child marriage, and caste discrimination are religious duties
- **Universal religion**: All major religions, properly understood, converge on the same truth

## Hermeneutical Method

The Brahmo Samaj read scripture **rationalistically**: passages supporting polytheism or social
injustice were dismissed or reinterpreted; the Upanishads were read as proto-monotheism. This is
essentially the **Protestant hermeneutic applied to Hindu texts**: scripture over tradition, reason
over ritual, inner conviction over external compliance.

## Historical Development and Leadership

1. **Rammohun Roy** (1828–1833): founder; died in Bristol while lobbying the British Parliament on
   behalf of Indian reform

2. **Debendranath Tagore** (1817–1905; father of the poet Rabindranath): second leader; treated
   the ideas behind suttee "respectfully" even while opposing the practice; more orthodox than Roy
   on Vedic authority

3. **Keshab Chunder Sen** (leader from 1858): abolished caste *within the Samaj* and admitted women
   as members; pushed further toward universalism and Christian influence; eventually led a schism

4. **Rabindranath Tagore** (1861–1941): Nobel laureate poet; the Brahmo Samaj shaped his deeply
   spiritual but anti-ritual religious sensibility

## Influence and Significance

The Brahmo Samaj produced the Bengali intellectual class that provided much of independent India's
early leadership. Its influence on the Indian reform movements was enormous:

- Provided the ideological framework for the campaign against suttee that led to the 1829 British ban
- Shaped the educational reform movement (support for Western-style education)
- Influenced [[gandhi|Gandhi]] indirectly through the reform tradition it inaugurated
- Created what Doniger calls the **"colonial Stockholm syndrome" variant of Hinduism**: Hindus who
  internalized the British Protestant critique and developed forms of Hinduism shaped by Protestant
  standards while believing they were recovering original Hinduism

## Relationship to Orthodox Hinduism

The Brahmo Samaj was regarded by orthodox Hindus as a heterodox departure from tradition —
essentially a Protestant-Christian movement in Hindu clothing. It was also regarded by many Brahmin
scholars as a betrayal of the rich tradition of Puranic devotion. The orthodox resistance to the
Brahmo Samaj's version of Hinduism eventually gave rise to the competing reform movement of the
[[arya-samaj|Arya Samaj]].
