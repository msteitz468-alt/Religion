# Kashmir Shaivism (Trika / Pratyabhijñā)

> **Sect page.** The non-dual tantric Śaiva tradition of medieval Kashmir, systematized by
> [[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]]. *(Ingested: Abhinavagupta's Gītārtha-saṃgraha.)*

## Identity and Origins

**Kashmir Shaivism** — its philosophical heart called **Trika** ("the triad," of Śiva, Śakti, and the
bound soul) and **Pratyabhijñā** ("Recognition") — is a non-dual [[hinduism|Hindu]] tradition that arose
in Kashmir from the 9th century, beginning with **Vasugupta** and the *Śiva Sūtras*, developed by
Somānanda and **Utpaladeva** (the *Īśvara-pratyabhijñā-kārikā*), and brought to its synthesis by
**[[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]]** (c. 1000) and his disciple Kṣemarāja.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

It is **not a Vedanta school** — its primary scriptures are the **Śaiva Āgamas and Tantras** (the
*Mālinīvijayottara*, the *Vijñāna Bhairava*, the *Śiva Sūtras*), not the [[upanishads|Upanishads]], and
as a Śaiva, Abhinavagupta was "not obliged to comment on the prasthāna-trayī." Yet it engaged the
Vedantic mainstream directly — most notably by producing its own reading of the
**[[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]]** — and defines its non-dualism *against* both Sāṃkhya and Vedanta.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

The true canon is the **Śaiva Tantric corpus**, supplemented by the school's own *śāstra* (Utpaladeva's
and Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā works). Its treatment of the **[[bhagavad-gita|Gita]]** is distinctive:
it uses a **Kashmiri recension** that differs from the standard text, and reads it **esoterically** — so
the Gita appears on this page's `canon_distinctives` as a shared text *re-read*, not a text the school
originated. (The Gita remains `canonical_for: [hinduism]`; Kashmir Shaivism adds only a divergent
*reading*, not a divergent canonical status.)

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Reality is one consciousness — Śiva.** All that exists is Śiva's own self-manifestation through his
  inseparable power, **Śakti**.
- **Absolute freedom (*svātantrya*).** The world is not illusion ([[maya|māyā]]) superimposed by
  ignorance, as in [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]], but the **real** and free self-expression (*ābhāsa*) of
  consciousness — hence a **dynamic** non-dualism against Advaita's **static** witness. This is the
  school's central quarrel with Śaṅkara.
- **The 36 tattvas** map the descent from Śiva to matter; **spanda** ("vibration") names the creative
  pulse of consciousness.
- **Liberation as [[pratyabhijna|recognition]] (*pratyabhijñā*).** Bondage is self-forgetting
  (contraction); freedom is recognizing that one **already is** Śiva — attainable in this life, by grace
  and the descent of power (*śaktipāta*).

## Hermeneutical Method (mandatory)

**Esoteric tantric decoding within a living lineage.** Abhinavagupta comments only on verses with a
**hidden meaning**, reading the Gita's yoga and sacrifice as veiled initiatory practice (breath-control
as guru–disciple transmission; sacrifice as offering into the fire of one-pointedness). He insists the
teaching cannot be grasped without **initiation and practice** in the tradition — knowledge here is
transmission, not text alone. The Gita's secret is held to be "more esoteric than Vedanta" (18.63). This
is the Śaiva instance of the cross-tradition **inner-meaning** hermeneutic ([[allegorical-reading|
allegory / bāṭin / sod]]).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Schools

Vedāntins regard it as outside their fold; it in turn treats Vedanta (and Sāṃkhya) as inadequate to the
Gita's deepest sense. Its **dynamic non-dualism** is the sharp foil to [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita's]]
illusionism — same word ("non-dual"), opposite picture of the world (see
[[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the comparison]]). Recorded without adjudication (Contradiction Protocol).

## Historical Development, Subdivisions, and Influence

Internally it gathered several streams — **Pratyabhijñā** (philosophy), **Krama**, **Kaula**, and
**Spanda** — into Abhinavagupta's Trika synthesis. The tradition declined after the Islamic conquest of
Kashmir (from the 14th c.) but was **revived in the 20th century** (notably by Swami Lakshman Joo) and
has since spread globally. Abhinavagupta's aesthetics (rasa theory) carried its non-dual sensibility into
all of later Indian literary thought.
