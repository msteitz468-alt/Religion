# Quanzhen (Ch'üan-chen / "Integrating Perfection")

**Ch'üan-chen** ("Complete Perfection" or more accurately "Integrating Perfection") is the
dominant surviving northern Chinese Taoist tradition, founded by **Wang Che** (Wang Ch'ung-yang,
1113–1170) and still centered at the **White Cloud Abbey** (*Po-yün kuan*) in Beijing. It
represents the fullest expression of [[biospiritual-cultivation|biospiritual cultivation]] in a
monastic institutional form.

## Identity and Origins

Wang Che was born into a well-to-do gentry family and received a traditional Confucian education
before embarking on the Taoist path. His **"Fifteen Articles on Establishing the Teaching"**
(*Ch'ung-yang li-chiao shih-wu lun*) is the foundational text of the tradition. It teaches that
"spiritual immortality" (*shen-hsien*) can be attained within this life by entering seclusion,
cultivating one's inner nature (*hsing*), and harmonizing it with the realities of one's external
life (*ming*). Wang's goal was not physical immortality but spiritual transformation occurring
during ordinary human life.

Wang Che attracted **seven famous disciples**, including:
- **Sun Pu-erh** (1119–1182): his only female disciple; a founder of one of the tradition's
  branches; associated with "Inner Alchemy for Women" in later Ch'ing-era texts. Her teachings
  survive in verse form.
- **Ch'iu Ch'u-chi** (1148–1227, also Ch'iu Ch'ang-ch'un): the most politically prominent
  disciple; summoned by Genghis Khan to central Asia; negotiated protections for the Chinese
  population; highly influential under Mongol rule.
- **Wang Ch'u-i** (1142–1217): summoned by the Jurchen emperor Shih-tsung in 1187 for instruction
  in "methods of preserving life."

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Ch'üan-chen inherited and synthesized:
- T'ang-era **biospiritual cultivation** and "sitting in forgetfulness" (*tso-wang*) from
  Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen's tradition
- **Inner Alchemy** (*nei-tan*) symbolic framework (already present in older Taoist texts;
  now made central)
- **Buddhist monasticism** as an institutional model (adapted to Taoist terms and values)
- Compatible **Confucian** concepts (moral cultivation, loyalty, filiality) as foundational
  prerequisites — not replacements for Taoist practice

Unlike Ch'an/Zen Buddhism, Ch'üan-chen never embraced "sudden awakening." Wang Che taught
that "to enter the sagely Tao one must first strengthen one's determination for many years."
Gradual, staged cultivation is fundamental.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Ch'üan-chen's canon is essentially the full Taoist canon (*Tao-tsang*). Its founders compiled
the **Hsüan-tu pao-tsang** (1244), the largest Taoist textual collection ever assembled — a
forerunner of the present *Tao-tsang*. Key texts for the tradition:
- *Ch'ung-yang li-chiao shih-wu lun* (Wang Che's "Fifteen Articles")
- *Wu-chen p'ien* ("Folios on Awakening to Reality") by Chang Po-tuan: the premier Inner Alchemy
  classic, though predating Wang Che
- *Tso-wang lun* (Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen): gradual cultivation framework inherited by Ch'üan-chen
- *Ch'ing-ching ching* ("Scripture of Purity and Tranquillity"): recited in Ch'üan-chen daily services

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Hsing/ming integration**: the cultivation of *hsing* ("inner nature" — the spiritual
  dimension) must be harmonized with *ming* ("fate" / "life conditions" — the circumstances
  of one's actual embodied existence). This holistic insistence distinguishes Ch'üan-chen from
  Buddhist models that focus solely on mental transformation.
- **Monastic vocation**: entering seclusion, withdrawing from worldly entanglements, as the
  context within which cultivation can properly proceed. The community developed a full monastic
  institutional framework.
- **Inner Alchemy (*nei-tan*)**: a rich symbolic system for describing the transformation of
  coarser bodily forces into refined spiritual energy; the "elixir" is produced within the
  practitioner's own being.
- **Women's full participation**: Sun Pu-erh's co-foundership established a precedent; 20–40%
  of early clergy were female. Women Taoists remain present in Ch'üan-chen abbeys today.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ch'üan-chen reads Taoist texts through the lens of Inner Alchemy: the symbols of classical texts
(water, fire, dragon, tiger, lead, mercury) are decoded as descriptions of the practitioner's
own inner processes. The *Tao te ching* and *Chuang-tzu* are read as manuals for self-cultivation.
Commentary is transformative practice, not merely intellectual exposition.

## Historical Development

**Jurchen dynasty** (1115–1234): Wang Che emerged under Jurchen rule; Ch'üan-chen attracted
followers across north China, including Jurchen nobles.

**Mongol period**: Ch'iu Ch'u-chi's meeting with Genghis Khan gave Ch'üan-chen imperial
sanction; a Jurchen (Wan-yen Te-ming) became Ch'üan-chen high priest under the Mongols.
The Hsüan-tu pao-tsang was compiled in 1244 by Ch'üan-chen leaders. However, the Mongol
Buddhist-Taoist debates of 1258 damaged Ch'üan-chen's standing and led to the destruction
of some Taoist texts.

**Ming dynasty**: Ch'üan-chen was politically marginalized (the Ming founder disdained "internal
cultivation" Taoism in favor of Cheng-i communal ritual). Ch'üan-chen survived mainly in the
north, maintaining monastic practice.

**Ch'ing dynasty (Lung-men synthesis)**: Wang Ch'ang-yüeh established **[[lung-men|Lung-men
("Dragon Gate")]]** Taoism at the White Cloud Abbey in Beijing in 1656, integrating Wu Shou-yang's
southeastern Inner Alchemy tradition with Ch'üan-chen's monastic framework. This synthesis has
survived as the dominant form of Chinese Taoism to the present day.

Today the **White Cloud Abbey** (*Po-yün kuan*) in Beijing remains the center of Ch'üan-chen /
Lung-men Taoism, operating (with disruptions during the Maoist cultural revolution) as a living
monastery.
