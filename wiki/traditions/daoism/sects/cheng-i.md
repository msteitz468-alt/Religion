# Cheng-i (Orthodox Unity / Zhengyi)

**Cheng-i** ("Orthodox Unity") is the dominant southern Taoist tradition, descending (at
least in self-presentation) from the [[heavenly-masters|Heavenly Masters (*T'ien-shih*)]]
tradition through the Chang family at **Lung-hu shan** ("Dragon-Tiger Mountain") in
Jiangxi province. It is characterized by hereditary priesthood, communal liturgy, talismanic
ritual, and a non-monastic structure. In contemporary China it is centered in Taiwan and
coastal Fujian.

## Identity and Origins

The Cheng-i tradition's narrative of origins is complex and partly fabricated. The Chang family
at Lung-hu shan claimed (from the Sung dynasty onward) to represent an unbroken hereditary
succession from **Chang Tao-ling** (traditional founder of the Heavenly Masters, 144 CE).
[[russell-kirkland|Kirkland]] argues that this claim of unbroken succession is almost certainly
a retroactive Sung-era fabrication: there is no evidence that Lung-hu shan maintained a continuous
organized institution through the T'ang period, and T'ang Taoists showed no sign of regarding
Lung-hu shan as particularly authoritative.

The consolidation of Cheng-i as the dominant southern tradition was the result of:
- Sung imperial recognition of the Chang family at Lung-hu shan alongside the Mao-shan
  Shang-ch'ing center
- Mongol Qubilai Khan's grant of "exclusive authority" to the Lung-hu shan Taoists over all
  southern Chinese ordinations (~13th c.), as a political move to consolidate control
- Ming dynasty intermarriage with the Chang family and entrustment of Tao-tsang compilation
  to a Cheng-i leader (resulting in the 1444/45 Cheng-t'ung Tao-tsang)
- Parallel absorption of new ritual movements (Ch'ing-wei, T'ien-hsin, Shen-hsiao) under
  the Cheng-i umbrella in the Yüan and Ming periods

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Cheng-i claims to represent the oldest and most authoritative strand of organized Taoism
(the T'ien-shih lineage). Kirkland's analysis shows that this claim is problematic — the
actual living T'ien-shih organization dispersed and evolved after Chang Lu's surrender in
215 CE, and the "lineage" was reconstructed centuries later through retroactive genealogy.

What Cheng-i genuinely does preserve are the talismanic and communal liturgical elements of
the T'ien-shih tradition, now formalized into a hereditary priestly system.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Cheng-i priests were responsible for compiling the **Tao-tsang** (Taoist Canon) in its present
form under the Ming emperor's auspices (1444/45). This gives Cheng-i a unique institutional
relationship to the canon — it did not add distinctive texts so much as define what counts as
the Taoist textual corpus.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Hereditary priesthood**: Cheng-i priests receive ordination through the Chang family's
  authority; the "63rd Celestial Master" etc. This contrasts with Ch'üan-chen's meritocratic
  monastic system.
- **Non-monastic**: Cheng-i priests marry, live in ordinary communities, and are called upon
  to perform liturgies for laypersons. The priest is a ritual specialist, not a recluse.
- **Talismanic ritual**: specialized ritual techniques (talismans, petitions to the celestial
  bureaucracy, exorcistic rites) inherited from the T'ien-shih.
- **Communal liturgy**: the *chai* (fasting/retreat) and *chiao* (offering) ceremonies performed
  for communities, rulers, and the welfare of the state. These are the Cheng-i priest's primary
  social function.
- **Absence of women in ordained roles (late period)**: unlike the early T'ien-shih, later
  Cheng-i practice excluded women from ordination. This divergence from the T'ien-shih model
  reflects broader social changes in late imperial China.

## Women and Cheng-i

In contrast to the early T'ien-shih model (which included women as libationers) and [[quanzhen|
Ch'üan-chen]] (which welcomed women in monastic life), the living Cheng-i tradition as recorded
in late imperial and modern times gives women little or no meaningful sacerdotal role.
Kirkland notes this as a loss relative to earlier Taoist practice, not a feature of Taoism
as a whole.

## Decline

The Manchu Ch'ing dynasty (1644–1911) continued recognition of the Cheng-i Celestial Masters
for a period, but the Ch'ien-lung emperor (r. 1736–1796) banished Inner Alchemy practitioners
from court and reduced Cheng-i's influence. By the Tao-kuang period (1821–1850) the title
"Perfected of Orthodox Unity" disappeared from court usage entirely.

The tradition survived primarily in southern coastal China and Taiwan, where Cheng-i priests
continue to perform communal liturgies.
