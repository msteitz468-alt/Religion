# Christian Science

> **Sect page.** Mary Baker Eddy's metaphysical-healing church. *(Eddy's [[science-and-health|Science
> and Health]] ingested.)*

## Identity and Origins

Founded in 1879 by **Mary Baker Eddy** (1821–1910), who, after her own healing, taught that reality is
wholly spiritual and that sickness and sin can be overcome through right understanding of God. Her textbook
**Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures** (1875) is the movement's defining work.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Centers on Jesus and the Bible and calls itself Christian, but its **metaphysics is radically
unorthodox** — closer to the 19th-century **New Thought** milieu than to historic Christianity — so the
creedal churches regard it as outside the faith.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Uses the **Bible** but pairs it with **Science and Health** as jointly authoritative — together the two are
designated the church's **"pastor"** (read aloud in services in place of preaching). Eddy's "Key to the
Scriptures" supplies the **decoding** of biblical language into metaphysical meaning. In `canon_scope`
terms *Science and Health* is `authoritative_for: [christian-science]` (a sub-canonical "interpretive key"
elevated almost to scripture) rather than added canon — a distinct pattern from the Mormon open canon.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**The unreality of matter, evil, sickness, and death** — these are "errors" of mortal mind; God is
infinite **Mind/Spirit/Principle**, and humanity is His spiritual idea. Healing comes through prayer as
**correct understanding** ("Christian Science treatment"), historically in place of medicine — the source
of its best-known public controversies. A largely **non-Trinitarian, non-sacramental** theology.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

Heterodox by every historic measure (denies the bodily/material categories the creeds assume); related to
the broader **New Thought** family (Unity, Religious Science). Recorded without adjudication.

## Influence

Small and declining in membership but culturally visible — notably through the respected newspaper *The
Christian Science Monitor* and longstanding church reading rooms.
