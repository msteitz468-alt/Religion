# Calvinism (Reformed tradition)

> **Sect page.** The Reformed stream of [[protestantism|Protestantism]] associated with
> [[john-calvin|John Calvin]] and his [[institutes-of-the-christian-religion|*Institutes*]]. Built
> from [[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Gordon's biography of the *Institutes*]] (Scope A).

A governing distinction, emphasised throughout Gordon's account: **"Calvinism" is not Calvin.** The
movement is a diverse, protean body that has claimed Calvin's name across four centuries while often
departing substantially from the historical reformer's thought. The popular "Calvinist" composite —
the predestinarian work-ethic moralist of Max Weber, the theocrat, the patron of biblical inerrancy —
"owes more to the Synod of Dort than to the pulpits of Geneva. Calvin never saw a tulip in his life."

## Identity and Origins

Calvinism emerged from the **Reformed wing** of the sixteenth-century Reformation, distinct from the
Lutheran and Anabaptist streams. Its formative network included Calvin in Geneva alongside Huldrych
Zwingli, Heinrich Bullinger (Zurich), Martin Bucer (Strasbourg), and Philip Melanchthon — Gordon
stresses that Calvin worked *within* this network, not as a solitary founder. The
[[institutes-of-the-christian-religion|*Institutes*]] became the tradition's confessional anchor and
"sat in the library of any educated Protestant cleric."

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Calvinism shares the broad Protestant *solas* (scriptura, fide, gratia) and the Protestant biblical
canon with [[protestantism|Protestantism]] generally, but is marked off from the Lutheran stream by
its insistence on **divine sovereignty and double predestination**, its **spiritual (not corporeal)**
account of Christ's presence in the Lord's Supper, and its **presbyterian** ordering of church
discipline. Mutual hostility with Lutherans was sharp in Calvin's lifetime and after.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

No divergence from the Protestant canon: Calvinism uses the 39-book Old Testament (excluding the
deuterocanon) and 27-book New Testament. Its distinctive textual authority is **confessional, not
canonical**: the [[institutes-of-the-christian-religion|*Institutes*]] and later Reformed confessions
(Westminster, the Canons of Dort) function as authoritative standards subordinate to scripture. The
*Institutes* is therefore recorded as `authoritative_for: [calvinism]` on its text page — esteemed
and binding, but explicitly *not* scripture.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Double predestination / unconditional election** — God's eternal choice of elect and reprobate
  apart from merit (see [[predestination|predestination]]). Later codified at the Synod of Dort
  (1618–19) into the "five points" (the retrospective "TULIP"), a systematisation Gordon warns is *not*
  identical with Calvin's own emphasis.
- **Divine sovereignty and [[divine-providence|providence]]** over all things.
- **Covenant** framing of God's dealings with humanity.
- **Union with Christ** and [[justification|justification]] by faith with sanctification as ordered
  fruits of the Spirit.

## Hermeneutical Method

Inherited from [[john-calvin|Calvin]]: grammatical-historical reading disciplined by humanist
philology; *sola scriptura* with Scripture interpreting Scripture; *accommodatio* (God adapts
revelation to human capacity); and the principle that systematic doctrine (the *Institutes*) and
running biblical commentary be read **side by side**.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

Regarded as **arch-heretical by the Catholic Council of Trent**, hostile to and hated by most
Lutherans, and internally divided over double predestination (opposed even by Calvin's friends
Melanchthon and Bullinger, and later by the Arminian Remonstrants whom Dort condemned). Recorded
without adjudication per the Contradiction Protocol.

## Historical Development and Influence

From Geneva the tradition spread into France (Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland and England
(Presbyterianism, Puritanism, the Westminster Assembly), and on to America, South Africa, and Asia.
Its contested modern afterlife — Kuyper's neo-Calvinism, Barth and Brunner, Weber's capitalism thesis,
apartheid theology and its Reformed opponents, and the contemporary "New Calvinism" — is traced in
[[gordon-calvins-institutes-a-biography|Gordon's later chapters]] (Scope C pending).
