# Islamic Feminism

**Islamic feminism** is a reformist interpretive movement within [[islam|Islam]] that seeks
gender justice **from within the Islamic tradition** — grounding equality not in secular or
Western feminism but in the [[quran|Qur'an]] itself, reread free of what it diagnoses as a
fourteen-century **androcentric** overlay. It is not a sect with a separate community or canon
so much as a **hermeneutical school and activist current** cutting across Sunni and Shia
contexts; it is filed here as a *movement* within Islam and cross-references the reformist
[[mutazilism|rationalist]] and modernist streams.

## Identity and Origins

The movement's deeper roots lie in 19th–20th-century **Islamic modernism** (Muhammad Abduh,
Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Muhammad Iqbal) and in the neo-modernist hermeneutics of
[[fazlur-rahman|Fazlur Rahman]]. Its distinctive **gender-as-category** turn arrives with
[[amina-wadud|Amina Wadud]]'s [[wadud-quran-and-woman|*Qur'an and Woman*]] (1992), which Wadud
called part of the **"gender jihad."** Organizationally it is embodied in groups such as the
Malaysian **Sisters in Islam** (founded 1988) and the global **Musawah** movement (2009), and
extended by scholars including **Asma Barlas** (*"Believing Women" in Islam*), **Kecia Ali**,
**Sa'diyya Shaikh**, and **Ziba Mir-Hosseini**.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The movement accepts the Qur'an as the uncreated (or at minimum uncontested) authoritative word
of God and the supreme criterion — but **demotes** much of the inherited apparatus: classical
*fiqh*, large parts of the *hadith* corpus, and the accumulated *tafsir* tradition are read as
products of patriarchal "prior text," human and historically conditioned rather than divine. It
understands itself as **recovery and reform**, not innovation: returning to the Qur'an's own
intent against custom and male consensus.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

No texts are added or removed. The divergence is one of **authority-ranking**: the Qur'an is
elevated as sole final criterion, while hadith and *fiqh* are subordinated and read critically.
This is a re-ranking within the shared canon rather than a new canon — recorded here, with no
change to the Qur'an's `canon_scope`, since the Qur'an remains canonical for the movement.

## Hermeneutical Method (mandatory)

- **Holistic / thematic reading** — the Qur'an as a coherent unity (Wadud's "hermeneutics of
  [[tawhid|tawhid]]"), against the classical **atomistic** verse-by-verse method.
- **The double movement** ([[fazlur-rahman|Fazlur Rahman]]) — universal moral principle
  abstracted from its seventh-century particulars, then re-applied.
- **Naming the "prior text"** — exposing the androcentric assumptions smuggled into ostensibly
  neutral exegesis.
- **Gender as a category of thought**, and **[[tafsir|ijtihad]]** reopened against taqlid
  (imitation of inherited rulings).

## Doctrinal Distinctives

The full moral agency (*khilafah*) and spiritual equality of women; marriage as mutual
partnership (the spouses "raiment for one another," Q 2:187); [[qiwama|*qiwama*]] as a
conditional *responsibility*, not a fixed male rank; the re-reading of
[[quran-4-34-male-authority|Q 4:34]] and the maintenance/inheritance reciprocity; rejection of
the "obedience to the husband" gloss on *qanitat*.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Currents

Traditionalist and Islamist critics regard the movement as **heterodox** and dismiss it as
"Western"/"feminist" importation; it in turn charges them with mistaking *culture* for
*revelation*. The dispute came to a head over [[amina-wadud|Wadud]]'s 2005 mixed-gender
prayer leadership. Both judgments are recorded per the [Contradiction Protocol]; the exegetical
crux is carried on [[quran-4-34-male-authority|the Q 4:34 page]].
