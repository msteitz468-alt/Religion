# Mu'tazilism

> **School page (theological / *kalam* movement).** The great **rationalist** school of Islamic
> speculative theology, which made reason (*'aql*) a criterion of religious truth and bequeathed the
> classic Sunni counter-formation, **Ash'arism**. Its supreme exegetical monument is
> [[al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]]'s [[quran|*al-Kashshaf*]].

## Identity and Origins

The **Mu'tazila** ("those who withdraw / stand apart") arose in 2nd/8th-century Basra, traditionally
traced to **Wasil b. Ata** (d. 131/748), who "withdrew" from the circle of al-Hasan al-Basri over the
status of the grave sinner. They preferred to call themselves **Ahl al-'Adl wa'l-Tawhid** — "the
People of Justice and Unity" — naming the two doctrines (divine **justice** and **unity**) at the
heart of their system. Drawing on the conceptual tools of Hellenistic philosophy, they built the
first systematic Islamic theology (*kalam*).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Mu'tazila shared the [[quran|Qur'an]] and the prophetic *sunna* with all Muslims — **there is no
canon divergence**. What set them apart was a *method* and a set of *doctrines*: the conviction that
where the literal text appears to contradict a rational demonstration, reason must govern and the
text be read figuratively. They briefly became state orthodoxy under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun,
whose **mihna** ("inquisition," from 833) compelled scholars to affirm the **created Qur'an** —
provoking the resistance of [[sunni-islam|Sunni]] traditionalists (Ahmad b. Hanbal) and, ultimately,
a backlash that left Mu'tazilism a minority. The decisive turn came when **al-Ash'ari** (d. 935), a
former Mu'tazilite, broke with the school and forged the via media that became **Sunni orthodoxy**:
God's attributes are real (not bald metaphors), the Qur'an is **uncreated**, and human acts are
*created by God but "acquired" by man*.

## The Five Principles (al-usul al-khamsa)

The school's identity was codified — by Abu al-Hudhayl and refined by later masters down to **al-Qadi
Abd al-Jabbar** (d. 415/1025) — into **five fundamentals**:

1. **Tawhid (Unity)** — the cardinal principle and source of the rest. God is absolutely one and
   absolutely incomparable; he has **no anthropomorphic attributes** (no body, place, direction,
   face, hand, eye) and the divine "attributes" are not entities distinct from his essence. From
   this follows the most explosive corollary: the **Qur'an is created** (*makhluq*), not a coeternal
   uncreated attribute of speech, since an eternal "Word" beside God would compromise his unity. See
   [[tanzih-tashbih|tanzih]] for the radical-transcendence pole this represents.
2. **'Adl (Justice)** — God is necessarily just and does only the good; he cannot will evil or
   command what reason condemns. This entails genuine **human free will** (humans author their own
   acts and are justly rewarded or punished), and notions of divine **grace** (*lutf*), the
   obligation to do the **optimal** (*aslah*) for creatures, and the figurative reading of
   predestinarian-sounding verses ("sealing the hearts," "leading astray").
3. **Al-wa'd wa'l-wa'id (the Promise and the Threat)** — God's promises of reward and threats of
   punishment are unconditionally binding upon himself; the unrepentant grave sinner *will* be
   punished, and there is no arbitrary intercessory reprieve.
4. **Al-manzila bayna al-manzilatayn (the Intermediate Position)** — the founding thesis: the Muslim
   who commits a grave sin (*kabira*) is neither a believer nor an unbeliever but occupies an
   **intermediate station** (a *fasiq*, "reprobate").
5. **Al-amr bi'l-ma'ruf wa'l-nahy 'an al-munkar (Enjoining Right and Forbidding Wrong)** — the active
   social-ethical duty to command good and forbid evil, by hand, tongue, or heart.

## Hermeneutical Method

Mu'tazilite exegesis is the rigorous form of **[[tafsir|*tafsir bi'l-ra'y*]]** (reason-based
interpretation). Its signature move is the **[[allegorical-reading|ta'wil]]** (figurative reading) of
the *mutashabih* (ambiguous/anthropomorphic) verses in light of the rational certainties of *tawhid*
and *'adl*. The classic literary expression is [[al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]]'s [[quran|*al-Kashshaf*]],
which argues the five principles out of the Qur'an's own grammar and rhetoric — so persuasively on the
philological level that even the [[sunni-islam|Sunni]] orthodoxy that rejected its theology preserved
the work in expurgated form (al-Baydawi's *Anwar al-tanzil*; Ibn al-Munayyir's marginal *al-Intisaf*).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

- **Sunni Ash'arism** regards classical Mu'tazilism as **heterodox** — the cautionary example of
  reason overreaching revelation; the polemical label *Qadariyya* (free-willers) is often attached.
- **Hanbali traditionalism** condemned it outright, the *mihna* having made Mu'tazilism the symbol of
  rationalist coercion against the "people of hadith."
- **Zaydi Shia** (in Yemen) and much of **Twelver [[shia-islam|Shia]] kalam** *adopted* the
  Mu'tazilite framework (especially *'adl* and *tawhid*), so the school survives there as normative
  rather than heretical — a textbook case of one community's heresy being another's orthodoxy,
  recorded **without adjudication** (Contradiction Protocol).

## Historical Development, Subdivisions, and Influence

Two great regional schools formed — **Basra** (Abu al-Hudhayl, al-Jubba'i, ultimately Abd al-Jabbar)
and **Baghdad** — with internal disputes (al-Nazzam's atomism and the "leap," debates over the
optimal). After the 11th-century Sunni turn to Ash'arism the Sunni Mu'tazila faded, surviving in
**Khwarazm** (the milieu of al-Zamakhshari) and migrating into **Shia** theology. Its long shadow is
everywhere: it set the agenda of Islamic theology (the created-vs.-uncreated Qur'an, free will,
divine attributes), provoked Ash'arism into existence, and supplies the rationalist pole against which
both Sunni traditionalism and Sufi *batin* defined themselves. Modern reformers have periodically
invoked a "neo-Mu'tazilism" in the name of a more rationalist Islam.
