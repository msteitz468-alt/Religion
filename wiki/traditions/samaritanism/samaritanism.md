# Samaritanism

> **Tradition page.** Filed as its own top-level tradition rather than a sect of Judaism:
> Samaritans understand themselves as the faithful remnant of northern Israel (Ephraim and

## Definition and Self-Understanding

The religion of the Samaritans, an ancient Israelite community centered on
[[mount-gerizim|Mount Gerizim]] above [[shechem|Shechem]]. Samaritans hold that theirs is the
original, uncorrupted Israelite worship, and that Judaism diverged when Eli illegitimately moved
the sanctuary from Gerizim to Shiloh. Coogan's framing — "more derivation than deviation" — notes
the community is best understood as a parallel development from a shared Israelite matrix rather
than a heretical offshoot (correcting the polemical account of 2 Kings 17).

## Canon and Scripture

The defining feature: the **Samaritan Pentateuch** is the *sole* scripture — the five books of
Moses only. Samaritanism **rejects** the Prophets (Nevi'im) and Writings (Ketuvim) that
[[rabbinic-judaism|Rabbinic Judaism]] and Christianity accept as canonical (no Isaiah, no Psalms,
no Esther, etc.). The Samaritan Pentateuch differs from the Masoretic text in ~6,000 readings,
most famously the **tenth commandment** to build an altar on Mount Gerizim and the Deut 27:4
"Gerizim" reading (vs. Masoretic "Ebal") — see [[gerizim-vs-jerusalem]]. On text pages, the
Torah books list `samaritanism` under `canonical_for`; the rest of the Tanakh does not.

## Native Hermeneutical Frameworks

Literal and halachic exegesis of the Pentateuch, read through a Gerizim-centered lens; a distinct
Samaritan halacha (Sabbath, purity, festivals, the Passover sacrifice still performed on Gerizim).

## Doctrinal Distinctives

Four articles of faith: one God; one prophet, Moses; one book, the Torah; one holy place, Mount
Gerizim — plus a day of vengeance/reward and the coming of the **Taheb** (a Moses-like restorer).
No Davidic messianism, no Jerusalem Temple theology.

## Relationship to Judaism and Other Traditions

Judaism and Samaritanism mutually rejected each other's sanctuary and canon; the breach hardens
through the Second Temple period (John Hyrcanus destroyed the Gerizim temple, 128 BCE). The NT
preserves the tension and partly relativizes it ([[gospel-of-john|John 4]]: "neither on this
mountain nor in Jerusalem"; the Good Samaritan). Recorded without adjudication (Contradiction
Protocol).
