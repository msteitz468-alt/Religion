# Bahá'í Faith

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Definition and Self-Understanding

The Bahá'í Faith is an independent world religion founded in mid-nineteenth-century Iran. Its adherents regard it as the latest in a succession of divine dispensations — encompassing Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam — and its prophet-founder, [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]] (1817–1892), as the [[manifestation-of-god|Manifestation of God]] for the present age. Smith stresses that calling the Faith "a Muslim sect" is "as inaccurate as describing second-century Christianity as a Jewish sect" (lines 279–281): while it grew from Shi'i Islam, it asserts its full independence.

The Bahá'í Faith emerged directly from the [[babi|Babi Religion]], a mid-nineteenth-century Iranian movement founded by [[the-bab|the Báb]] (1817–1850). The Bahá'í calendar dates its inception from the Báb's declaration in 1844 (AH 1260); Bahá'ís regard the Báb as a co-founder and full Manifestation of God, not merely a precursor.

## Historical Origin and Development

**1844–1853 — The Babi precursor:** The Báb declared his mission in Shiraz in May 1844. The Babi movement spread rapidly through Shaykhi networks in Iran and evolved from a Shi'i reform tendency into a new religion with an independent scripture (the Bayán) and abrogated Islamic law. After the Báb's execution in 1850 and military suppression of Babi uprisings by 1853, the movement collapsed as an organized force but its adherents continued.

**1853–1863 — Bahá'u'lláh's emergence:** Bahá'u'lláh, exiled from Tehran to Baghdad in 1853, became the pre-eminent Babi leader. He revitalized the community and wrote foundational works including the *Kitáb-i-Íqán* (1862) and the *Hidden Words*. He made his claim to spiritual authority known at the Garden of Ridván (22 April – 3 May 1863) on the eve of his deportation to Istanbul — the event Bahá'ís commemorate as the inauguration of his ministry.

**1863–1892 — The 'Akká exile:** Through Istanbul and Edirne, Bahá'u'lláh was imprisoned in 'Akká (St Jean d'Acre) from 1868. The great majority of surviving Babis accepted him as the fulfilment of the Báb's prophecy. He wrote the *Kitáb-i-Aqdas* (c.1873), his book of holy law, and tablets to world rulers, and died at Bahjí in May 1892.

**1892–1921 — 'Abdu'l-Bahá:** [[abdu-l-baha|'Abdu'l-Bahá]], Bahá'u'lláh's eldest son, served as head of the Faith and Center of the Covenant. Released from Ottoman custody in 1908, he traveled to Egypt, Europe, and North America (1910–1913), vastly expanding the Faith's Western presence. He died in Haifa in November 1921.

**1921–1957 — Shoghi Effendi:** [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] systematized the Bahá'í Administrative Order, launched systematic global teaching plans, and developed the World Centre in Haifa–'Akká.

**1963–present — The Universal House of Justice:** The supreme elected governing body, first elected in 1963, now leads the worldwide community of approximately five million adherents (as of 1995).

## Canon

Bahá'í canon comprises three bodies of authoritative text:

1. **Writings of the Báb** — including the Persian and Arabic *Bayán*, the *Qayyúmu'l-Asmá'*, and numerous tablets; all regarded as divine revelation.
2. **Writings of Bahá'u'lláh** — at least 15,000 "Tablets" including the *Kitáb-i-Aqdas* (holy law), *Kitáb-i-Íqán* (doctrinal), *Hidden Words* (ethical/mystical), and tablets to rulers. All are treated as divine revelation.
3. **Writings of 'Abdu'l-Bahá** — not classed as divine revelation but as authoritative interpretation; together with Shoghi Effendi's translations and letters, constitute the secondary literature.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

The Bahá'í Faith is distinctive in having built-in interpretive authority:

- **Manifestation theology:** Bahá'u'lláh's writings are divine revelation; all prior revelation is also recognized but superseded by his dispensation (see [[progressive-revelation|Progressive Revelation]]).
- **The Covenant:** 'Abdu'l-Bahá was appointed sole authorized interpreter by Bahá'u'lláh; Shoghi Effendi was appointed authoritative interpreter by 'Abdu'l-Bahá. These interpretations are binding. The Universal House of Justice holds legislative authority over matters scripture does not address.
- **No clergy:** There is no priesthood or clerical interpretive class; authoritative interpretation terminated with Shoghi Effendi. The Universal House of Justice may legislate but may not interpret.

## Map of Institutions

See [[bahai-administrative-order|Bahá'í Administrative Order]] for the full institutional structure.

- **Local Spiritual Assemblies** — elected annually by all adult Bahá'ís in a locality.
- **National Spiritual Assemblies** — elected annually by delegates; 56 existed by 1963.
- **Universal House of Justice** — nine members elected quinquennially; supreme authority; first elected 1963.
- **Appointed institutions** — Hands of the Cause, Continental Boards of Counsellors, Auxiliary Boards — advisory and protective functions.

## Scope 2 Notes (lines 2133–4536)

*From [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]], Chapters 8–11, Conclusion, and Chronology.*

### Bahá'í Community Development, 1866–1921

Bahá'u'lláh clearly conceived of his religion as a universal creed destined to spread to the whole world; a major theme in the writings of both Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá was the need to "teach the Cause." Expansion was concentrated in Iran but also reached the Caucasus (1860s), Egypt (1860s), India and Burma (1870s), Russian Turkistán (1880s), and North America and Europe (1890s) (lines 2133–2148).

The establishment of Bahá'í communities in the West bore particular significance as a "major cultural breakthrough, indicating the potential universality of the Bahá'í Cause" (line 2147). The first Western community was established in Chicago in 1894; by 1900, probably well over 1,500 Western Bahá'ís existed. The Bahá'í Faith thereby became a genuinely international movement for the first time (lines 2349–2365).

In Russian Turkistán, the first Bahá'í House of Worship in the world was substantially completed at Ashkhabad by 1907, and the first elected Bahá'í council in the world was established there in 1895–6 (lines 2328–2336).

### Shoghi Effendi's Guardianship and the Administrative Order (1921–1957)

Shoghi Effendi (1897–1957), appointed Guardian in 'Abdu'l-Bahá's will, systematized the [[bahai-administrative-order|Bahá'í Administrative Order]] during his Guardianship. The basic elements were laid out in 1922 and 1923: local spiritual assemblies (nine or more adult Bahá'ís), national spiritual assemblies elected by delegate conventions, and the full apparatus of committees, funds, and membership procedures.

In the 1950s, three additional institutions were created: the International Bahá'í Council (1950/1), the Hands of the Cause (revived 1951), and the Auxiliary Boards (1954, 1957). Systematic national teaching plans began with the first American Seven Year Plan (1937–44), followed by a second (1946–53), and culminating in the Ten Year Crusade (1953–63). Over the span of 1935–1963, local spiritual assemblies worldwide increased from 139 to 4,437, and localities from 1,034 to 14,437 (lines 2765–2774).

See [[shoghi-effendi|Shoghi Effendi]] for the full figure page.

### Universal House of Justice: Establishment and Contemporary Role

The Universal House of Justice was first elected in April 1963 by the members of all fifty-six national spiritual assemblies then in existence and has since acted as the supreme administrative body. It ruled in October 1963 that no further Guardians could be appointed (*Wellspring of Guidance* 11) and thereby assumed permanent headship of the Faith. It has nine members elected quinquennially at an international convention; membership is confined to men per 'Abdu'l-Bahá's instruction (lines 2855–2876).

Five international plans have been launched by the House (as of 1995): Nine Year Plan (1964–73), Five Year Plan (1974–9), Seven Year Plan (1979–86), Six Year Plan (1986–92), and Three Year Plan (1993–6). Between 1963 and 1992, the total number of localities in which Bahá'ís reside increased from 14,000 to over 120,000; local spiritual assemblies from 4,437 to 20,435; and Bahá'í literature had been translated into over 800 languages by 1988 (lines 2987–3008).

The Bahá'í International Community (BIC) was affiliated with the United Nations in 1948; the House has strongly encouraged UN involvement. The BIC gained consultative status with ECOSOC (1970) and UNICEF (1976) and participates in work on literacy, human rights, primary health care, combating racism, and women's issues. The House published *The Promise of World Peace* in 1985, addressed to "the peoples of the world," and *The Prosperity of Humankind* in 1995 (lines 3135–3181).

Houses of Worship have been constructed in: Wilmette (completed 1953), Kampala and Sydney (dedicated 1961), Frankfurt (1964), Panama City (1972), Apia/Western Samoa (1984), and New Delhi (1986). The New Delhi Temple, built in the form of an opening lotus, attracts thousands of visitors every day (lines 3014–3020, 3993–3994).

The seat of the Universal House of Justice in Haifa came into use in 1983. An International Teaching Centre was established 1973; Continental Boards of Counsellors (five Boards, seventy-two members) in 1968 (lines 2965–2974).

### Global Spread and Demographics (as of 1995)

Total Bahá'ís worldwide: approximately 5 million, residing in Bahá'í communities in almost every part of the world (line 3559). Of these:
- Only ~300,000 live in Iran (the community formerly comprising well over 90% of all Bahá'ís until at least the 1950s; now only ~6–7%) (lines 3561–3564).
- By 1992: 31% of localities in South Asia (mainly India), 28% sub-Saharan Africa, 16% Latin America/Caribbean, 10% South-East Asia, 11% West, 1% Islamic heartland (lines 3597–3603).
- India: possibly approaching 2 million Bahá'ís — about two-fifths of the world total, making it the largest single Bahá'í community (lines 3981–3983).
- Sub-Saharan Africa: probably over 1 million Bahá'ís (~one-fifth of world total) (line 3094).
- Latin America: probably ~750,000 Bahá'ís (~16% of world total) (line 4148–4150).

## From Smith, Concise Encyclopedia (2026-06-28)

### Global Expansion: Regional Summaries (lines 12931–16930)

**Russia and Central Asia**: After the 1917 revolution, Bahá'í expansion beyond the expatriate Iranian communities became possible. Central assemblies for Turkistán and the Caucasus were consolidated as a national entity by 1925. Under Communist consolidation, however, restrictions were imposed and the Bahá'í communities of Soviet Asia were crushed during the 1930s. Following liberalization in 1989–91, Bahá'í communities re-emerged and national assemblies were formed in each of the former Soviet republics (1992–4) (lines 12942–12965).

**Turkey**: Early Bahá'í groups consisted largely of expatriate Iranians; a genuinely Turkish Bahá'í community only slowly developed after the establishment of the modern Turkish nation. A national assembly was formed in 1959; Turkey now has the greatest number of local assemblies of any country in the Islamic heartland (lines 12966–12984).

**North America**: By 1900 there were some 1,500 American Bahá'ís in 60 localities in 25 states; by 1916 approximately 3,000. Large-scale enrolments during the late 1960s–early 1970s raised the total to 105,000 by 1973 and 179,000 by 1988. Canada established a separate national assembly in 1948. Of the 27 Hands appointed by Shoghi Effendi (1951–7), thirteen were North Americans; of the first sixteen-member Universal House of Justice, seven were Americans and one was Canadian (lines 13007–13104).

**Europe**: The German Bahá'í community was severely persecuted under the Nazi regime (1937); Bahá'í activities ceased throughout most of Europe during World War II. The European House of Worship in Frankfurt was dedicated in 1964. By the late 1980s, approximately 25,000 Bahá'ís in Europe; by 1992, thirty-four national assemblies (lines 13105–13249).

**Africa**: Systematic Bahá'í teaching began with the Africa Campaign (1951–3). The Ten Year Crusade (1953–63) produced dramatic growth: over 50,000 Bahá'ís in 2,655 localities by 1963; 348 African tribal groups represented; Bahá'í literature translated into 94 African languages. The Bahá'í House of Worship in Kampala dedicated January 1961. By 1992, 5,877 local assemblies and 33,000+ localities; 54 national assemblies (lines 13302–13431).

**Latin America**: Mass teaching, especially among Amerindians, grew the community to an estimated 700,000+ by 1992, with 3,501 local assemblies. The first Latin American House of Worship was dedicated in Panama (1972); six Bahá'í radio stations were established (lines 13432–13594).

**South Asia (India)**: Mass teaching campaigns from the 1960s onward transformed the Indian Bahá'í community from fewer than 400 (1961) to over 2.3 million by 1993 — the world's largest. The Bahá'í House of Worship in New Delhi (completed 1986) attracts more daily visitors than the Taj Mahal. Zonal ('State') Bahá'í Councils were developed from 1986 to devolve administration (lines 13595–13720).

**South East Asia**: Originating with Jamál Effendi and Siyyid Muṣṭafá Rúmí's visit to Burma in 1878. Large-scale enrolments during the Ten Year Crusade: Vietnam (195 local assemblies by 1964), Philippines (150), Malaysia (97), Indonesia (63) (lines 13721–13833).

**Oceania**: Sustained activity began in the 1950s. By 1992, Bahá'ís in over 3,500 localities, 614 local assemblies, 14 national assemblies; significant portions of some island populations are Bahá'í (approaching 20 per cent in Kiribati). The Bahá'í House of Worship in Apia, Western Samoa was dedicated in 1984 (lines 13905–13976).

### Key Theological/Ethical Teachings Documented in This Range

- **God**: God's essence is unknowable; knowledge of God is primarily achieved through the [[manifestation-of-god|Manifestations of God]] who reflect his attributes. Every created thing in the universe is a "sign" of God's sovereignty. Shoghi Effendi described God as one, personal, unknowable, inaccessible, eternal, omniscient, omnipresent, and almighty; "infinitely subtle yet rational"; rejecting immanentist, pantheistic, and anthropomorphic conceptions (lines 15010–15082).

- **[[he-whom-god-shall-make-manifest|He Whom God Shall Make Manifest]]**: The Babi messianic figure promised in the Bayán; identified by Bahá'u'lláh with himself in the 1860s. The Azali objection that the Báb specified a distant future date was countered by Bahá'ís citing the Báb's own indication of "the year nine" (1852–3) as significant (lines 16548–16634).

- **Government**: Bahá'u'lláh emphasized justice in government and praised constitutional monarchy; 'Abdu'l-Bahá called for separation of legislative and executive functions and predicted federalism as the future pattern of government. Bahá'ís are to be loyal, honest, and obedient to government, avoiding sedition and partisan political involvement (lines 15204–15383).

- **Holy war**: The Bahá'í Faith explicitly rejects jihád; Bahá'u'lláh forbade holy war at the time of his 1863 Riḍván declaration. Bahá'ís teach through moral example and peaceful persuasion (lines 16893–16926).

- **[[bahai-holy-days|Holy days]]**: Major observances include Nawrúz (21 March), the Riḍván Festival (21 April–2 May), Declaration of the Báb (23 May), Ascension of Bahá'u'lláh (29 May), Martyrdom of the Báb (9 July), and the Twin Birthdays (20 October and 12 November) (lines 16726–16852).

### Contemporary Challenges: Persecution of Iranian Bahá'ís (1979–present)

The fall of the Sháh and establishment of the Islamic Republic in 1979 ushered in a period of massive persecution. Within a few years, over 200 Bahá'ís had been executed or murdered; hundreds arrested; properties confiscated; graveyards and shrines destroyed; thousands discharged from work and their children dismissed from schools and universities. In 1983, the government banned Bahá'í activities and made membership of spiritual assemblies a criminal offence; all Bahá'í institutions were disbanded. Although varying in intensity, the threat remains real. Some 10,000 Bahá'ís fled and were resettled elsewhere, but the mass of Iranian Bahá'ís remain. Ironically, the persecutions appear to have stiffened Bahá'í resolve globally and increased worldwide public awareness, provoking sympathy and support from individual governments, the European Union, and the UN (lines 3668–3734).
