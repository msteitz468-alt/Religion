# Jainism

Top-level tradition page. Jainism is an ancient Indian **[[sramana|sramana]]** (renouncer)
tradition — like [[buddhism|Buddhism]], a **non-theistic** path with no creator God — built on
radical **[[ahimsa|non-violence]]**, asceticism, and a distinctive realist, pluralist metaphysics.
Its goal is the liberation (*[[moksha|moksha]]*) of the soul (*[[jiva|jiva]]*) from the bondage of
**[[karma|karma]]** (understood by Jains as a subtle *material* substance) and the cycle of
[[samsara|rebirth]]. Followers are *Jains* — "followers of the **Jina**" ("conqueror"), the
honorific for a [[tirthankara|fordmaker]] (*tirthankara*) who has conquered the passions and
crossed the ocean of rebirth. This is the parent node for the Jain sects under
`traditions/jainism/sects/`.

> Per Dundas, Jainism is best understood not as a "Hindu sect" (the old Western and Brahminical
> stereotype) but as an independent tradition with its own canon, cosmology, and soteriology,
> historically interacting with — and defining itself against — both Hinduism and Buddhism.

## The Fordmakers (Tirthankaras)

Jain sacred history centres on **twenty-four [[tirthankara|fordmakers]]** (*tirthankaras*,
"ford-makers" / "crossing-makers") per cosmic half-cycle — omniscient teachers who build the
*tirtha* (the "ford" across the river of rebirth: the fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen,
laywomen). The twenty-fourth and last of the current age is **[[mahavira|Mahavira]]** (c. 5th c.
BCE), the historical founder-figure; his predecessor **[[parshvanatha|Parshva]]** (c. 8th c. BCE)
is also likely historical. The first is the legendary **[[rshabha|Rshabha]]** (Adinatha).

## A Tradition with No Single Shared Canon

Like Buddhism, Jainism fails the single-canon model. The schism between the two great communities
is partly a **canon** dispute:

- The **[[svetambara|Svetambaras]]** ("white-clad") hold a canon of **forty-five Agama texts** in
  [[ardhamagadhi|Ardhamagadhi]] Prakrit (the *angas*, *upangas*, etc.), headed by texts such as the
  [[acaranga-sutra|Acaranga]] and the [[kalpasutra|Kalpasutra]].
- The **[[digambara|Digambaras]]** ("sky-clad," naked) hold that the **original canon was lost**,
  and reject the Svetambara Agama as inauthentic, relying instead on later systematic works (e.g.
  the *Satkhandagama*) and the writings of [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]].
- The one near-universal authoritative text is the **[[tattvartha-sutra|Tattvartha Sutra]]** of
  Umasvati, accepted across both communities.

## Doctrine in Brief (in place of theology)

There is no creator deity; the liberated soul itself becomes the highest reality. Core teachings:

- **Two fundamental categories** — *[[jiva|jiva]]* (sentient soul) and *[[ajiva|ajiva]]* (non-soul),
  elaborated as the **seven (or nine) *tattvas***.
- **[[karma|Karma as matter]]** — a fine material substance that flows into (*asrava*) and binds the
  soul, to be stopped (*samvara*) and shed (*nirjara*) through restraint and austerity.
- **[[anekantavada|Anekantavada]]** — "many-pointedness": reality is many-sided, and every
  statement is perspectival, expressed through **[[syadvada|syadvada]]** and the **sevenfold
  predication**. This is Jainism's signature epistemological doctrine.
- **The [[loka|Jain cosmos]]** — an uncreated, eternal universe of distinct layers, populated by
  souls migrating through the [[samsara|cycle of rebirth]].
- **The fourteen [[gunasthana|stages of quality]]** (*gunasthana*) — the ladder of spiritual
  progress toward [[kevala-jnana|omniscience]] and liberation.

## Ethics and Practice

The **Three Jewels** — right faith, right knowledge, right conduct — frame practice. Ascetics take
the **[[mahavrata|Five Great Vows]]** (non-violence, truth, non-stealing, celibacy, non-possession);
laypeople take their lesser analogues (*anuvrata*). **[[ahimsa|Ahimsa]]** is the supreme principle,
generating strict vegetarianism and elaborate care for life. The voluntary ritual death by fasting,
**[[sallekhana|sallekhana]]**, is the ideal end. Lay devotion centres on image-**[[jain-puja|worship]]**
(*puja*) of the fordmakers — itself contested by the aniconic [[sthanakvasi|Sthanakvasi]] and
[[terapanth|Terapanth]] reform movements.

## Map of Major Sects

- **[[digambara|Digambara]]** ("sky-clad") — monks are naked; hold women cannot attain liberation
  in a female body and that the original canon is lost. Strong historically in south and west India.
- **[[svetambara|Svetambara]]** ("white-clad") — monks and nuns wear white; accept the 45-text
  Agama and the possibility of women's liberation. Subdivided into ascetic lineages (*gacchas*) such
  as the [[kharatara-gaccha|Kharatara]] and [[tapa-gaccha|Tapa Gaccha]].
- **[[sthanakvasi|Sthanakvasi]]** — image-rejecting Svetambara reform movement (from the 15th–17th
  c., via [[lonka|Lonka]]).
- **[[terapanth|Terapanth]]** — image-rejecting movement founded by **[[acarya-bhiksu|Acarya
  Bhiksu]]** (18th c.), notable for its single-acarya centralised organisation.
- **[[kanji-svami-panth|Kanji Svami Panth]]** — modern (20th c.) Digambara-derived movement
  centred on [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]]'s mysticism.

## History and Sacred Geography

Early Jain communities are attested archaeologically at **[[mathura|Mathura]]** (stupa, donative
inscriptions). Jainism flourished under royal patronage in the south (Karnataka under the Gangas and
Rashtrakutas) and west (Gujarat), where the polymath **[[hemacandra|Hemacandra]]** (1089–1172) served the
Caulukya court. Distinctive institutions emerged: the [[digambara|Digambara]] **[[bhattaraka|
bhattaraka]]** (a clothed cleric-administrator presiding over monastic seats), the now-largely-extinct
south-Indian **[[yapaniya|Yapaniya]]** sect, and the [[svetambara|Svetambara]] ascetic lineages
(*gacchas*) that arose in reaction to the settled **[[temple-dwelling-monks|temple-dwelling monks]]**.
Major pilgrimage centres include **[[mount-satrunjaya|Mount Satrunjaya]]** (the great Svetambara hill at
Palitana) and **[[shravana-belgola|Shravana Belgola]]** (the Digambara site of the [[bahubali|Bahubali]]
colossus). On lay piety see the autobiographer **[[banarsidas|Banarsidas]]**.

## Relations with Other Traditions

Jainism defined itself against [[hinduism|Hinduism]] and [[buddhism|Buddhism]]; its *anekantavada*
was deployed both as a tool of tolerance and as a polemical weapon. See
[[jain-views-of-hinduism-and-buddhism|the comparison page]] and the contrast with the materialist
[[carvaka|Carvaka]] and the fellow-sramana Buddhists.
