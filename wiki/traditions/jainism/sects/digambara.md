## Identity and Origins

The Digambaras (literally "sky-clad" or "space-clad") are the sect of Jainism whose male ascetics practice complete nudity. According to the Digambara account of their own origins, they represent the original, undifferentiated Jain community, while the Svetambaras are the descendants of a backsliding group who remained in the north of India during a famine and subsequently adopted the heretical practice of wearing clothes. The Digambara account identifies the famine migration as led by the teacher Bhadrabahu, with the faithful southward-migrating community preserving authentic practice and the northern remnant compromising. The oldest literary version of this Digambara origin story dates to the tenth century CE, and Dundas judges it "of little historical worth, merely serving as indices of sectarian bitterness" (lines 50–55).

The Svetambara counter-account locates the founding of the Digambara sect in the career of an apostate monk called Sivabhiti, who self-initiated after being locked out by his mother-in-law and, inspired by accounts of the now-defunct jinakalpa (the naked 'way of the conquerors' practised in Mahavira's lifetime), arrogantly threw off his robes. This Svetambara story dates from about the fifth century CE (lines 19–36).

Archaeological and inscriptional evidence suggests the truth is more complex: "there was a gradual movement among Jain monks towards a differentiation based on apparel, or the lack of it, rather than any abrupt doctrinal split" (lines 95–98). The earliest fordmaker images from Mathura are naked, and it is not until the fifth century CE that clothed images appear. Even the name "Digambara" took time to gain currency — a late fifth-century inscription from south India still uses the old expression "nirgrantha" ("bondless") for naked monks (lines 99–107).

The Council of Valabhi (453 or 466 CE), which codified the Svetambara scriptural canon, was the likely catalyst for the final hardening of sectarian boundaries, being "exclusively Svetambara, with no naked monks in attendance and indeed no record of it in any Digambara source" (lines 133–140). The Digambaras rejected the authority of this editorial process, and this rejection is the formal point of canonical divergence.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Digambaras share the parent tradition's fundamental metaphysics, ethics, and cosmology. Doctrinally, "there were no great differences between the two sects" (lines 159–160). The Digambara scholar Akalanka (eighth century) in fact "took the lead in sharpening Jainism's logical and epistemological tools in the struggle to controvert its Buddhist and Hindu intellectual opponents" (lines 161–163).

The central point of departure is orthopraxy rather than orthodoxy: "the fact that outward appearance was regarded as an index of proper understanding of the doctrine caused continuing controversy between the Svetambaras and the Digambaras" (lines 165–166). For the Digambaras, nudity is not an archaism but a living spiritual requirement. As Aparajita (eighth century) argues, nudity entails the "abandonment of all possessions and the overcoming of the passions" and manifests "an inner, spiritual purity." The naked monk "imitates the fordmaker" and does not infringe non-violence by harboring insects in dirty clothes (lines 167–182).

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence) — Required Section

The Digambara position on scripture is distinctive and internally paradoxical: they claim that the authentic Jain scriptural tradition has been **entirely lost**, making *agama* "at best a metaphorical designation" (lines 657–659). Their lineage of teachers descending from Mahavira shows ever-diminishing scriptural knowledge; by the time of Dharasena (thirty-third in succession, 683 years after Mahavira), knowledge of only one *anga* survived, and after Dharasena's two pupils, Puspadanta and Bhutabali, even that disappeared (lines 1460–1464).

The two most extensive Digambara sacred texts are:

- **Satkhandagama** ("Scripture of Six Parts"): the oldest Digambara sacred text, attributed to the oral teaching of Dharasena (approximately mid-second century CE), written down by Puspadanta and Bhutabali. Palm-leaf manuscripts were preserved at Mudbidri in Karnataka and treated primarily as sacred objects of veneration (*darsana*) rather than texts for study. Todarmal (eighteenth century) conceded they "were purely for looking at reverentially since owing to the effects of the corrupt world age nobody was capable of understanding them" (lines 789–791). Published only in the early twentieth century after considerable clerical resistance.
- **Kasayapahuda** ("Treatise on the Passions"): composed not long after the *Satkhandagama*, accompanied by an important eighth-century commentary by Virasena.

The Digambaras **reject the Svetambara canon** — specifically the authority of the Council of Valabhi's editorial process — and regard the forty-five-text Svetambara list as a human confection rather than authentic transmission (lines 151–157). However, they hold that their enumeration of the *anga* texts is identical to that of the Svetambaras, "although unsurprisingly they differ with regard to the non-anga texts" (lines 1506–1507). Prominent medieval Digambaras were familiar with the Svetambara canon and cited it on occasion; learned Digambaras in the nineteenth century "accepted the authority of some Svetambara texts, while rejecting others" (lines 1517–1520).

In lieu of a formal canon, the Digambaras use a **quasi-canonical grouping into four *anuyoga*** (exposition) categories, associated with the legendary teacher Raksita: (1) *prathama* (Universal History), (2) *karana* (cosmology), (3) *carana* (monastic behaviour), (4) *dravya* (metaphysics broadly). The ninth-century *Mahapurana* of Jinasena and Gunabhadra holds "a practical significance in Digambara Jainism as a repository of lore and exemplification which outweighs much older texts like the 'Scripture of Six Parts'" (lines 1538–1542).

The **Tattvartha-sutra** of Umasvati is claimed by both Digambaras and Svetambaras (with minor textual differences), and Dundas suggests it "was written at a time before the sectarian traditions had fully crystallised" (lines 1738–1740). Its Digambara commentators include Pujyapada (sixth century), Akalanka (eighth century), and Vidyanandi (ninth century).

The **Five Homages** (*Pancanamaskar*) is accepted by all Jains including Digambaras. Its oldest literary occurrence is at the beginning of the *Satkhandagama*, placed there by Puspadanta (c. second century CE) (lines 1589–1591).

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**Nudity as a soteriological requirement.** The Digambara view, given its fullest expression by Aparajita (eighth century), holds that nudity is not merely a mark of renunciation but the necessary outward sign of complete inner detachment. Two modes of asceticism — the *jinakalpa* (naked, solitary, Mahavira's own way) and the *sthavirakalpa* (robed, communal, suited to the age of decline) — were devised by robed monks to explain away scriptural references to nakedness; the Digambaras reject this compromise (lines 86–94).

**The kevalin does not eat.** The enlightened and omniscient person (*kevalin*) "in his lack of need for nourishment is perhaps a reflex of the naked ascetic who through restricted intake of food by means of his cupped hands alone represents an 'otherness' which sets him apart from the rest of the world" (lines 416–420). This contrasts with the Svetambara view that the kevalin eats normally.

**Alms bowl rejected.** The true ascetic uses only cupped hands as a receptacle for food; using an alms bowl implies attachment to possessions. (This is contested by Yasovijaya's Svetambara argument that the human body itself could then be called a possession; lines 202–212.)

**Women cannot attain liberation in female bodies.** This is the most extensively debated Digambara distinctive. Since ascetic nudity is required for liberation and female nudity is socially impossible, women cannot achieve enlightenment in female form. For Prabhacandra (eleventh century), the fullest Digambara discussant (*NKC* pp. 865–70), women cannot achieve liberation because: (a) they cannot be naked; (b) they cannot achieve any genuine intensity of moral action (though this also means they cannot reach the lowest hell); (c) the social inferiority of women carries over into the ascetic realm. Women must be reborn as men to advance. See [[women-and-liberation-in-jainism]].

**The nineteenth fordmaker was male.** The Digambaras reject the Svetambara claim that Malli was a woman and worship the nineteenth fordmaker as the male Mallinatha (lines 475–475).

**Scriptural loss.** The authentic agama is gone; what remains (the *Satkhandagama* etc.) are human compositions of the early common era, not the words of Mahavira.

## Hermeneutical Method

Digambara hermeneutics is shaped by the conviction that the original agama is lost. As a result, scripture functions less as a text to be read and more as:

1. **A sacred object** (*darsana*): the Mudbidri manuscripts were treated as objects of worship, their content inaccessible and irrelevant to most. The festival of *Sruta Pancami* ("Scripture Fifth") involves taking manuscripts out, worshipping them, and recopying them.
2. **A structure of authority mediated through teacher lineage**: the early Digambara [[kundakunda]] emphasizes that the ascetic monk "deficient in agama does not know his self" and "cannot destroy karma" (*PS* 3.33 and 36), yet this agama is known through lineage, not text.
3. **Classified by function**: the four *anuyoga* categories organize texts by purpose (cosmological, ethical, metaphysical, historical) rather than by a textual canon per se.

Medieval Digambara scholars such as Akalanka also adopted the Svetambara *kalika/utkalika* classification of texts (appropriate-time vs. any-time study), integrating it into Digambara usage (lines 1511–1514).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

**Digambara view of Svetambaras:** Svetambara monks and nuns are relegated "to the status of lay people" by virtue of their wearing of clothes, since clothed monks have not achieved the detachment required for the ascetic state (Aparajita, lines 168–170). The Svetambara origin is traced to apostasy or weak compromise.

**Svetambara view of Digambaras:** The sect traces to the self-initiated apostate Sivabhuti; its nudity is a misguided attempt to revive the long-defunct *jinakalpa*. Svetambara polemicists referred to Sivabhuti's "scurrilous career" for centuries (lines 18–19).

**The Yapaniyas** occupied a middle position: Digambara in appearance but accepting Svetambara scriptures and defending Svetambara views on female liberation. The Digambaras viewed the Yapaniyas as heretics. The Yapaniyas disappear from the record after the fourteenth century (lines 109–131).

**Inter-sectarian social relations** were often bitter in the medieval period, as evidenced by the famous 1125 debate between Vadideva (Svetambara) and Kumudacandra (Digambara) at Patan. Holy-place disputes have been numerous — Dundas estimates some 134 disputes over Jain holy places currently exist in India (lines 389–390). The Digambaras predominate in south India (Karnataka, Maharashtra) and have often felt numerically and financially inferior to migrating Svetambara traders. Nevertheless, large-scale pilgrimages by members of both sects together have occurred in medieval sources (lines 272–278).

## Historical Development

- **Pre-5th century CE:** Gradual differentiation between naked and robed ascetics; no sharp doctrinal break. The interstitial Yapaniya sect existed briefly.
- **c. 453 or 466 CE:** Council of Valabhi codifies the Svetambara canon; Digambaras absent; this marks the formal separation.
- **8th century:** Akalanka sharpens Jain logic against Buddhist and Hindu opponents; Aparajita defends ascetic nudity; Virasena writes important commentary on *Satkhandagama*.
- **11th century:** Prabhacandra produces the fullest Digambara argument against female liberation.
- **18th century:** Todarmal (Jaipur) writes extensively from a Digambara perspective; states that neither low-caste people nor women can attain enlightenment.
- **1896–1922:** Mudbidri manuscripts transcribed and eventually published, over clerical resistance.
- **Modern period:** The Kanji Svami Panth revives a brand of Digambara Jainism (mentioned; details in Chapter 9 of Dundas, outside this slice). Today some 350 Digambara nuns exist, of great learning, but who accept the teaching that their female bodies preclude serious advance toward enlightenment (lines 591–596).

The **Taran Svami Panth** (a small Digambara sub-group in central India) follows Taran Svami (1448–1515), who rejected image worship; their temples contain sacred books instead of images, and worship involves reading texts aloud or paying homage to them (lines 1543–1556).
