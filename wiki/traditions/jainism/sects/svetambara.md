## Identity and Origins

The Svetambaras ("white-clad") are the sect of Jainism whose ascetics, male and female alike, wear white robes. They constitute the numerically dominant Jain community in northwest India — particularly Gujarat and Rajasthan — and their influence historically spread through trading communities.

The Svetambara account of their own origins, dating from about the fifth century CE, traces the origin of the rival Digambara sect to the apostate Sivabhuti, who self-initiated after a domestic quarrel and arrogantly tried to revive the long-defunct *jinakalpa* (naked "way of the conquerors") (lines 19–36). The Digambara counter-account portrays the Svetambaras as descendants of monks who weakly adopted clothing during a northern famine. Both origin stories, Dundas stresses, are "of little historical worth, merely serving as indices of sectarian bitterness" (lines 50–55).

The actual differentiation was gradual. The early Svetambara literature (including the *Acaranga* and *Sthananga*) acknowledges variety in monastic dress: some passages describe nakedness as Mahavira's practice, while others permit limited clothing for reasons of embarrassment or endurance (lines 60–94). Robed monks devised the two-tier categories of *jinakalpa* (naked, now defunct) and *sthavirakalpa* (robed, suited to the current age of decline) to account for these tensions without purging their scriptures.

The **Council of Valabhi** (453 or 466 CE) was the pivotal event: an exclusively Svetambara conclave that redacted the scriptural canon in manuscript form. "The stabilisation of an old scriptural tradition by one section of the community finally confirmed the existence of fully self-conscious Svetambara and Digambara sects" (lines 153–157).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Svetambaras share the fundamental Jain metaphysics, ethics, and soteriological goal with the Digambaras. As a Svetambara hagiography acknowledges, between the two sects "as far as the authority of the fordmakers and the ethical and metaphysical prescriptions of the Jain religion were concerned, there were no disagreements" — the difference is "with regard to behaviour" (lines 224–227).

The Svetambara position is that the current age of decline (*duhsama*) makes the full *jinakalpa* impossible and the *sthavirakalpa* (the "way of the elders," allowing robes) both legitimate and sufficient. The eleventh-century canonical commentator Abhayadeva Siri argues that "all monks are naked metaphorically if they are pure in spirit, while to be lacking clothes and other possessions is pointless if one is still afflicted by delusion. Clothes enhance the religious life, just as food keeps the body going" (lines 187–194).

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence) — Required Section

The Svetambaras maintain a living textual canon, ultimately derived from oral transmission through Mahavira's disciple Sudharman (the last surviving direct disciple) and redacted through three councils (*vacana*, "recitations"):

1. **Pataliputra** (160 years after Mahavira): established that knowledge of the twelve-limbed canon was imperfect; the twelfth limb (*Drstivada*) eventually disappeared, officially reducing the canon to eleven surviving *angas*.
2. **Mathura and Valabhi simultaneously** (827 years after Mahavira): Skandila at Mathura and Nagarjuna at Valabhi produced divergent recensions; the Mathura recension became the official version.
3. **Valabhi** (first half of the 5th century CE): convened by Devarddhi Ganin; the canon was for the first time committed to writing in manuscript form to prevent complete loss (lines 1077–1108).

The **standard Svetambara canon** consists of **forty-five texts** classified into five groups. This list is strictly that of the **Svetambara Murtipujaka Sangha** ("Image-Worshipping Assembly"); the aniconic Svetambara sub-sects ([[sthanakvasi]] and Terapanthi) accept only a **thirty-two-text list**, omitting thirteen texts including the controversial fifth *Chedasutra* (*Mahanisitha*, written in Maharastri rather than Ardhamagadhi and containing references to goddesses and magic spells) and all ten mixed (*prakirnaka*) texts (lines 1330–1354).

For a full enumeration of the forty-five texts see [[jain-agamas]].

The **Kalpasuta** holds a particularly important place in Svetambara life: it is recited publicly at the festival of Paryusan and its manuscript copies are among the most prolifically illuminated in Jain tradition. See [[kalpasutra]].

The **Tattvartha-sutra** of Umasvati is shared with (and claimed by) the Digambaras, though with minor textual differences.

The **Five Homages** (*Pancanamaskar*) is accepted by all Svetambaras. Its first occurrences within Svetambara scripture are the fifth *anga* (where it is expanded to include homage to the alphabet and scripture) and the sixth *upanga* (where the commentators treat its occurrence as interpolation), suggesting it was "virtually unknown to the Svetambara scriptures" in origin and entered through Digambara influence (lines 1593–1599). The [[sthanakvasi]] sect rejects the authority of the second explanatory portion of the mantra (line 1582).

The Svetambara conception of *agama* is also elastic: when the Bombay State government requested scriptural evidence in the mid-twentieth century for the inalienability of Jain temple funds, the Svetambara community produced "commentaries and treatises dating from well into the medieval period, with one source stemming from as recently as the eighteenth century" — "Scripture here is perceived as being any text of reasonable or indeterminate age which is located within the tradition and supports the view of the person citing it" (lines 728–733).

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**The** ***sthavirakalpa*** **as the legitimate current path.** Robed monasticism is not a compromise but the appropriate form of the ascetic life in the age of decline, when the *jinakalpa* (died out with the ganadhara Jambi after Mahavira's death) cannot be revived.

**The kevalin eats.** The omniscient *kevalin* "is unquestionably a human of a highly developed type, but also one who has many of the characteristics, such as hunger and consequent intake of food, of spiritually undeveloped people" (lines 421–425). The alms bowl is a legitimate tool; Yasovijaya (seventeenth century) argues that rejecting it could lead to the destruction of insects attracted by food falling between cupped fingers (lines 202–212).

**Women can attain liberation.** This is the most doctrinally significant Svetambara-distinctive position. The scriptures show female monastics (nuns outnumbered monks 36,000 to 14,000 in Mahavira's *tirtha* according to the *Kalpasuta*) and record women achieving salvation during Mahavira's lifetime. The first person of this world age to achieve liberation was Marudevi, the mother of Rsabha — a woman (lines 459–462). The nineteenth fordmaker **Malli was female**, reborn in that form because of a karmic fault of secret overachievement in austerities in a previous male life (lines 463–474). For the Svetambara argumentative position see [[women-and-liberation-in-jainism]].

**Ardhamagadhi** as the sacred scriptural language, "the dialect in which Mahavira had preached and the language spoken by the gods in heaven" (lines 1014–1016), automatically transformed into all listeners' languages when Mahavira spoke it (*Aup* p. 178). Siddhasena Divakara (c. fifth/sixth centuries CE) was expelled from the community for twelve years for proposing translation into Sanskrit (lines 1052–1062).

**Restrictions on lay reading.** Some Svetambara sects ban lay people from reading sacred texts without monastic supervision; others require an overseeing monk. Dharmasagara (sixteenth century) held that the main cause of rival Jain sects was appeal to scriptural texts alone without the validation of an authenticated teacher lineage (lines 687–703).

## Hermeneutical Method

Svetambara hermeneutics balances two poles:

1. **Teacher lineage and authoritative exegesis:** "the meaning of a scripture could only be activated in conjunction with the authoritative exegesis of the great teachers of the past" (Dharmasagara, lines 699–702). Scripture without the lineage is "a prey to the literalist misinterpretations of heretics."
2. **The canonical text itself**, once fixed at Valabhi, as the authoritative reference: medieval commentators treat the Valabhi text as definitive even where it contains inconsistencies (attributed by Samayasundara to Devarddhi Ganin's "unwillingness or inability to distinguish among the various readings available," *SS* p. 86, line 1108).

Scripture also functions **as spectacle and sacred object**: the *Kalpasuta* recitation at Paryusan involves breakneck Prakrit recitation rendering the text "completely incomprehensible even to those who know Prakrit" while illuminated manuscript pages are displayed to the audience — scripture communicates "through sound and sight" conferring "a particular merit" (lines 889–924). The text is also paraded through streets (days three and four) and its auction of rights to touch silver dream-images is a major communal event (lines 912–919).

Practical canons (ascetic textual curricula spanning ancient Ardhamagadhi texts through medieval Sanskrit and Prakrit works) define a "textual culture within which Jains of differing levels of attainment and status can grasp the varying modalities of their tradition" (lines 750–754). Few texts in these practical canons are from the ancient Ardhamagadhi canon.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

**Svetambara view of Digambaras:** The Digambara sect is traced to Sivabhuti's self-initiated apostasy. Robed Svetambara monks are not to be relegated to lay status; pure spirit matters more than naked bodies. The Svetambara establishment of Gujarat — confirmed through the famous 1125 Patan debate in which Vadideva defeated the Digambara Kumudacandra — is the "official Jainism of Gujarat" (lines 265–270).

**Digambara view of Svetambaras:** Clothed monks and nuns are at best lay persons in terms of their spiritual attainment. The wearing of clothes indicates lingering passion and attachment (lines 168–175).

**Relations between sects:** Large-scale joint pilgrimages have occurred in the medieval period. Currently about 134 disputes over the ownership of Jain holy places exist in India (line 389). Major contested sites include Mount Girnar (Gujarat) and Bahubali Hill (Maharashtra). The Svetambara demographic advantage in Gujarat is clear; Digambaras predominate in the south (Karnataka, Maharashtra). The migration of Svetambara traders from Gujarat and Rajasthan into areas with indigenous Digambara populations has generated significant friction (lines 314–396).

**Internal Svetambara divisions:** The [[sthanakvasi]] and Terapanthi sects are aniconic Svetambara sub-traditions using a thirty-two-text canon, rejecting image worship and certain canonical texts accepted by the Murtipujaka mainstream. A survey of one hundred Terapanthi nuns found over seventy wanting changes in their circumstances (lines 604–606).

## Historical Development

- **Pre-5th century CE:** Gradual differentiation from naked monks; robed asceticism becomes established. Categories of *jinakalpa* and *sthavirakalpa* developed.
- **c. 453 or 466 CE:** Council of Valabhi; Devarddhi Ganin redacts the canon in manuscript form. This is the formative event for Svetambara self-identity.
- **8th century:** Abhayadeva Siri as commentator on the canon; Yasovijaya (seventeenth century) on the alms-bowl debate. Jain libraries established by monks gaining merit through manuscript commissioning.
- **12th century:** Hemacandra, the great Svetambara polymath, credited with collecting manuscripts at Patan (lines 1673–1675).
- **1383 CE:** A monk compiles the oldest Indian manuscript catalogue known, based on the Svetambara libraries in Gujarat (lines 1643–1645).
- **18th–19th centuries:** Breakdown in Svetambara monastic initiation; non-initiated clerics (*yati*) assume functions including Kalpasuta reading; Terapanthi sect formed in the eighteenth century by a monk who broke from the Sthanakvasis (lines 881–888, 1664–1668).
- **19th–20th centuries:** Emergence of printing reduces manuscript importance; Sagarananda Siri (1875–1950) revives scholarly study of the scriptures (line 1429); the "Agamodaya Samiti" series publishes the canon; the "Jaina Agama" series publishes all twenty *prakirnaka* texts to hedge enumeration disputes (lines 1433–1435).
