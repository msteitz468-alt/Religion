# Terapanth

The **Terapanth** ("Thirteen Path" or "Your Path") is a Śvetāmbara aniconic reform sect founded in 1759 by [[acarya-bhiksu|Acarya Bhikṣu]] (Bhikhanji; 1726–1803) in Rajasthan. It is defined by the strict concentration of all monastic authority in a single *ācārya*, a rigorous doctrine of non-violence as self-discipline, and rejection of image worship — and is the best-documented Jain sect in history, owing to its meticulous record-keeping of its founder's life and teachings.

Note: There is also a **Digambara Terapanth** (from the seventeenth century), which rejected the authority of the *bhaṭṭārakas* and some image-related practices; its adherents are now called **Bispanthīs**. The Śvetāmbara Terapanthīs discussed here constitute a distinct movement (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Identity and Origins

Bhikṣu, after eight years as a disciple of the Sthānakvāsī *ācārya* Raghunāthji, concluded in 1759 that current Jain monks had "forgotten the path taught by Mahāvīra" (Dundas, ch. 9). He broke away by severing his commensal relationship (*sambhoga*), reinitiated himself and six monks, and formally constituted the community in 1764.

**Name.** The sect's name can be explained three ways, all based on the Rajasthani word *terā*: (1) "thirteen," referring to an anecdote about thirteen followers being observed in a vacant bazaar shop; (2) "thirteen," referring to the thirteen constituent elements of ascetic practice Bhikṣu sought to restore (five Great Vows, three Protections [*gupti*], five Attentive Actions [*samiti*]); (3) "your" — the path that belongs to "you, Lord Mahāvīra" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Terapanth is a Śvetāmbara sect in its scriptural inheritance but does not acknowledge the authority of the many otherwise influential Śvetāmbara teachers of the medieval period. "The only true authority was vested in the scriptures, the expression of eternal truth, and Mahāvīra and his disciples" (Dundas, ch. 9). Raghunāthji is acknowledged as Bhikṣu's teacher "only in the most provisional sense."

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

The Terapanth shares the Sthānakvāsī truncated canon of **32 authoritative texts** (the same list ultimately traced to [[lonka|Lonka]]'s rejection of 13–14 texts accepted by the image-worshipping Śvetāmbara mainstream). Image worship found even in the 32 accepted texts is handled by re-reading *ceiya* ("holy place / temple") as "monk" or another meaning satisfying aniconic commitments. See [[sthanakvasi]] for the full discussion of this canonical truncation.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**Non-violence as self-discipline.** Bhikṣu insisted it is "not compassion and giving and the merit which arose from them which lay at the heart of the religious path but continual self-discipline, based on a correct understanding of the nature of life-forms, which alone could stop karma being bound to the soul" (Dundas, ch. 9). Any act of violence, irrespective of intention or necessity, is bad.

**Laukika / lokottara distinction.** The worldly (*laukika*) realm — including virtually all merit-directed religious practice common among Jains — has "no connection of any sort with the worldly [sic]" transcendent (*lokottara*) sphere (Dundas, ch. 9). Buying animals from butchers and freeing them is a "purely social action." Teaching the path of restraint is *lokottara* giving; charitable donations are merely social.

**Rejection of image worship** and permanent wearing of the **mouth-shield (*muhpattī*)** — the latter "narrow and elongated" compared to the square Sthānakvāsī version.

**Single *ācārya* principle.** The *maryādā* (Bhikṣu's fixed code of practice, issued 1772–1794) makes all monastic authority exclusive to the *ācārya*: discipline, succession, initiation, annual assignment of each ascetic's itinerary and rain-retreat location all rest with him alone. This single-*ācārya* structure has remained without serious challenge to the present day.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Scripturalist-restorationist**: the texts are treated as the direct, eternal expression of Mahāvīra's teaching, unmediated by the medieval ascetic lineages which Bhikṣu regarded as corrupt. The daily requirement for each ascetic to read and sign the condensed *maryādā* — formalised by the fourth *ācārya* Jaya — is itself an institutionalisation of this hermeneutic: the rule must be personally understood and affirmed, not merely inherited through lineage.

**Modern innovation: *Preksādhyāna*.** Associated particularly with the tenth *ācārya* Mahāprajña, *preksādhyāna* ("insight meditation") is described as designed to "engage the mind fully in the perception of subtle, internal and innate phenomena of consciousness" (Dundas, ch. 9). It draws on scattered scriptural statements about perceiving the self with the self, while also drawing "eclectically on a wide range of sources in other traditions" — including Buddhist insight meditation in style.

## Historical Development

**Founding period (1759–1803).** Initial social hostility — difficulty getting alms and lodging, the first *cāturmāsa* spent in a cave. Bhikṣu "exulted in these difficulties, however, viewing them as part of the inevitable trials of the ascetic's life" (Dundas, ch. 9). He was a stern teacher who demanded "exacting standards of behaviour and obedience" and was "unwilling to give initiation to those whom he felt to be mentally and physically inadequate."

**Consolidation under Acarya Jaya** (fourth *ācārya*, died 1881). Jaya compiled Bhikṣu's anecdotes, collected *maryādās*, introduced sacred calendar days (bestowal of the *maryādā*, death of Bhikṣu, accession of *ācārya*), set up compulsory manuscript-copying, introduced the office of *sādhvīpramukhā* (chief nun, subordinate to *ācārya*), and founded (1857) a hospice for elderly ascetics.

**Expansion under Acarya Tulsi** (ninth *ācārya*, 1936–94; 1914–97). Key developments:
- Largest number of initiations of any Terapanthi *ācārya*.
- **Anuvrat movement** (1949): "dedicated to raising the moral tone of Indian public and commercial life by taking Jainism beyond the Jains" through modified lay vows. Tulsi's founding statement: "If an atom (*anu*) has in it the monstrous power to destroy the world … we have its counterpart in *anuvrata* — a small or atomic vow — which alone has the power to ward off and counter the threat of an atom bomb" (Dundas, ch. 9). Dundas assesses the Anuvrat movement as utopian and of limited actual social impact.
- **Pan-Indian lay organisation**: Akhil Bhāratīya Terapanth Mahāsabhā.
- **First Jain university** at Lādnūm, Rajasthan.
- **Samaṇas and samaṇīs**: lower-order male and female ascetics granted special dispensation to travel abroad and accept specially prepared food, breaking the traditional prohibition on crossing into "non-Aryan" lands. This has given the Terapanth a diaspora presence the image-worshipping sects (whose initiated ascetics cannot travel) largely lack.

## Relationship to Other Sects

The Terapanth does not regard itself as lineally descended from the [[sthanakvasi|Sthānakvāsīs]], despite Bhikṣu's initiation history. The [[kanji-svami-panth|Kanji Svami Panth]] is a separate modern movement, originating in Sthānakvāsī milieu but moving towards Digambara doctrine, with which the Terapanth has partial overlap in its lay community.
