## Identity and Origins

The Kharatara Gaccha is an image-worshipping [[svetambara|Śvetāmbara]] ascetic lineage (gaccha) that emerged in western India in the early eleventh century as a reform movement against the [[temple-dwelling-monks|temple-dwelling (caityavāsin) monks]]. "According to the chroniclers of the Kharatara Gaccha, their sect arose as a result of a monk called Vardhamāna abandoning his teacher, a temple-dwelling monk, in disgust with what he saw as his disrespect for correct Jain practice and starting a lineage of teachers whose sense of sectarian identity initially derived from victory over temple-dwelling monks in public debate."

The name *kharatara* — "Particularly Sharp-witted" or "Fierce" — was reportedly given to the sect because of the alacrity in debate of Vardhamāna's pupil Jineśvara at Patan in 1024, when the temple-dwelling monk Sūra was defeated before the royal court. Dundas notes that accounts differ: Vardhamāna is "pictured either as not being present or as delegating authority to his pupil Jineśvara" in accounts of this founding event. An alternative name, **Vidhisaṅgha** ("Assembly based on Scriptural Injunction"), is associated with the teacher Jinavallabha.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Kharatara Gaccha identifies itself as a stricter continuation of the authentic Śvetāmbara teacher lineage traceable through Mahāvīra's disciple Sudharman. Its foundational claim is that the temple-dwelling monks had departed from the scriptural standard: "It was recourse to the literal words of sacred texts which, through furnishing binding behavioural injunctions, governed the entire Kharatara approach to the ascetic life."

The Kharatara Gaccha's great rival, the [[tapa-gaccha|Tapa Gaccha]], challenged this scripture-first approach. The Tapa polemicist Dharmasāgara argued that "there cannot be any validation of practice through sacred texts alone … the tīrtha cannot be based solely on scripture but must take its strength from a properly constituted teacher lineage and its authoritative interpretation, enshrined in commentaries, of the ancient texts."

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

The Kharatara Gaccha does not diverge from the mainstream Śvetāmbara scriptural canon; its distinctive emphasis is hermeneutical rather than canonical: the *Dasavaikālika* and other canonical texts are binding in their literal sense as the supreme standard of ascetic behaviour. The Kharatara discovery of the Dasavaikālika in a concealed temple library — and Jinavallabha's realisation that his and his master's life "was not in accord with the scriptures" — is the founding hagiographic moment of the lineage's scriptural orientation.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Scripture-literal**: the binding injunctions of the canonical Śvetāmbara texts govern ascetic behaviour. The Kharatara Gaccha "specifically linked" the heretical behaviour of the temple-dwelling monks "to ignorance, misuse or travesty of the scriptural canon." This contrasts with the Tapa Gaccha's emphasis on the authority of a properly constituted teacher lineage and its commentarial interpretation of texts.

## Key Figures

**Vardhamāna** (died 1031): shadowy founder; authority rejected by other gacchas; one chronicle portrays him as Uddyotana's main pupil, another as having received his mandate directly from the deity Dharaṇendra.

**Jineśvara Sūri**: Vardhamāna's pupil; defeated the temple-dwelling monk Sūra at the royal court of Patan in 1024, the sect's key legitimating event.

**Jinavallabha** (eleventh century): as a boy, bought from his mother by a temple-dwelling monk; discovered the Dasavaikālika in a concealed library; realised the discrepancy with his way of life; became successor to Abhayadeva Sūri. His *Saṅghapattaka* ("Formal Letter to the Assembly," SP) is "a pessimistic and gloomy poem … about the state of Jainism in the eleventh century … [expressing] his often sardonic views about the corrupt forces with which the then relatively small Kharatara Gaccha felt itself to be contending." The poem describes Muslim incursions as "mighty armies of barbarians … appeared like the plague" (SP 40).

**Jinadatta Sūri** (1075–1154): "the most celebrated of the sūris of the Kharatara Gaccha." Given by his mother to Śvetāmbara nuns; became an "energetic and aggressive advocate" of the Kharatara Gaccha; journeyed into Sindh "then a dangerous land under Muslim control, to gain converts"; performed miracles including "raising the dead." After his death, a monument at Ajmer became a Dadabāri ("Garden of Dada"), and he became one of the **four Dada Gurus** — "affectionately known as 'grandad' Gurus" — who are objects of a cult in western India today, "function[ing] rather like the saints in Christianity" with the ability to "intervene in human affairs when propitiated."

Other Dada Gurus: Jinacandra Sūri (1139–65, "Jewel-wearing" from a jewel in his forehead); Jinakuśala Sūri (1279–1331, gained many converts through triumphal processions); Jinacandra Sūri (1537–1612, instituted reforms and won concessions from Muslim authorities).

## Relations with Muslims

Jinavallabha's *Saṅghapattaka* treats the Muslim incursions as a sign of the Kali Yuga. Jinadatta Sūri's disciple escaped Muslim slavery (KhGBG p. 18). Jinacandra Sūri (1537–1612) "won concessions for the Jain community from the Muslim authorities" and, on another occasion, "persuaded Akbar to protect Jain temples from Muslim assault" at Lahore in 1591.

## Rivalry with the Tapa Gaccha

Controversy between the Kharatara and Tapa Gacchas was intense, centring on disputes over the legitimacy of their respective teacher lineages and control of pilgrimage sites including [[mount-satrunjaya|Mount Śatruñjaya]]. Dharmasāgara's *Prabodha Parikṣā* (PP) attacked the Kharatara teachers as fictitious or self-serving and denied that any legitimate lineage could descend through Jinavallabha and Jinadatta. The conflict extended to the "supernatural level": a Kharatara chronicle records how Jinacandra Sūri "gained control of Manibhadra, the tutelary deity of the Tapa Gaccha" (KhGPS p. 35).

By the contemporary period, Kharatara numbers had dwindled to "some 19 monks and 205 nuns, nearly all localised in Rajasthan," against the Tapa Gaccha's 1,278 monks and 4,290 nuns.
