## Identity and Origins

The Tapa Gaccha is "the image-worshipping Śvetāmbara sect which is by far the most dominant today both in terms of numbers of ascetics, which continue to show a marked increase, and public and scholarly significance." It was founded in **1228** by **Jagaccandra Sūri**, who "abandoned out of disgust for its lax behaviour the 'Fig Tree' (Vaṭa) Gaccha, so called because it was supposedly under a fig tree, or banyan, that Uddyotana initiated his pupils." A king, on seeing the intensity of Jagaccandra's austerities, gave him the epithet *Tapa* ("Asceticism"), which subsequently named the gaccha. The Tapa Gaccha "locates him as the forty-fourth teacher in an unbroken Jain lineage traceable back to Mahāvīra's disciple, Sudharman."

Jagaccandra himself is "an even more elusive personage than the Kharatara teacher Vardhamāna." It is his successor **Devendra** who gave the Tapa Gaccha "an intellectual basis and identity" through works on ritual, lay behaviour, and karma theory.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Tapa Gaccha presents itself not as a new sect but as the continuation of the true and unbroken Śvetāmbara teacher lineage. Its leading polemicist Dharmasāgara "point[ed] out that Jagaccandra seceded from the Fig Tree Gaccha at the express command of his teacher, and the fact that the name Tapa Gaccha does not occur before him does not … signify any new doctrine or change of behaviour but a confirmation of the ancient ascetic values upon which Jainism was based and a continuation of the true teacher lineage."

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

The Tapa Gaccha adheres to the mainstream Śvetāmbara scriptural canon without addition or subtraction. Its hermeneutical distinctiveness vis-à-vis rivals lies in its insistence that scripture alone is insufficient: proper practice must rest on a legitimately constituted teacher lineage and its authoritative commentarial tradition. This is the core of Dharmasāgara's attack on the [[kharatara-gaccha|Kharatara Gaccha]].

## Hermeneutical Method

**Lineage-commentary primacy**: while the canonical texts are binding, the Tapa Gaccha position (as articulated by Dharmasāgara) is that "there cannot be any validation of practice through sacred texts alone … the tīrtha cannot be based solely on scripture but must take its strength from a properly constituted teacher lineage and its authoritative interpretation, enshrined in commentaries, of the ancient texts." This stands against the Kharatara emphasis on scripture's literal word as the sole standard.

## Key Figures

**Jagaccandra Sūri** (fl. 1228): elusive founding figure; credited with intense austerities that prompted his royal epithet.

**Devendra Sūri**: successor to Jagaccandra; produced works on ritual, lay behaviour, and karma theory that gave the Tapa Gaccha its intellectual foundations.

**Anandavimala Sūri** (early sixteenth century): "initiated important reforms of practice."

**Hiravijaya Sūri** (1527–96): head of the Tapa Gaccha; summoned in 1587 by the Mughal emperor **Akbar** to his court at Agra. "According to the Jain accounts, Akbar interrogated the Śvetāmbara teacher about the characteristics of true religion and, on being informed that it was compassion for all forms of life that was its most important constituent, he was moved to issue decrees ordering the freeing of caged birds and the banning of the slaughter of animals on the Śvetāmbara festival of Paryuṣan." Hiravijaya remained three years at Agra.

**Śāntīcandra**: pupil left behind by Hiravijaya at the Mughal court; further "prevailed upon Akbar to issue edicts banning the killing of animals as well as lifting the tax upon non-Muslims."

**Vijayasena Sūri**: another of Hiravijaya's pupils; "had to defend Jainism from a charge of atheism made to Akbar in 1592."

**Dharmasāgara** (sixteenth century): the Tapa Gaccha's "unusually dyspeptic controversialist." His main work, the **PP** (*Prabodha Parikṣā*, "Sun in the Eyes of Owlish Heretics" / "Examination of the Doctrine"), attacked the Kharatara sūris Jinavallabha and Jinadatta as fictitious and denied any legitimate lineage descending through them. He also attacked the Digambaras and the followers of Loṅkā (who rejected image worship). So aggressive was his tone that "some senior members of the Tapa Gaccha perceived Dharmasāgara's polemical tone as being so aggressive that attempts were made to suppress his writings and associate him with heresy." Controversy over Dharmasāgara "continued within the Tapa Gaccha throughout the seventeenth century."

## Relations with Muslims

Hiravijaya Sūri's relationship with Akbar was the Tapa Gaccha's most visible engagement with Muslim political power. The [[kharatara-gaccha|Kharatara Gaccha]] also had access to Akbar's court (Jinacandra Sūri, 1591), making the Moghul court a competitive arena for rival gacchas. The Tapa Gaccha's experiences with Jahangir were more mixed: he appointed a Tapa monk to teach his son and in 1616 issued an edict granting Jains freedom of worship, but also showed erratic hostility at other times.

## Rivalry with the Kharatara Gaccha

The controversy with the [[kharatara-gaccha|Kharatara Gaccha]] was long, intense, and extended to competing claims over [[mount-satrunjaya|Mount Śatruñjaya]] and the status of Abhayadeva Sūri (each gaccha claimed him in its lineage). On the supernatural level: "the Tapa Gaccha sūri Ānandavimala invoked Manibhadra to defeat the god Bhairava who had been summoned up by the Kharataras." The contemporary disparity in numbers is stark: 1,278 Tapa monks and 4,290 nuns versus 19 Kharatara monks and 205 nuns, the latter "nearly all localised in Rajasthan."

## Current Standing

The Tapa Gaccha is today the dominant image-worshipping Śvetāmbara lineage, with ascetic numbers continuing to increase.
