# Kanji Svami Panth

The **Kanji Svami Panth** is described by Dundas as "the most successful twentieth-century Jain movement" (ch. 9). Founded when Kānjī Svāmī (1889–1980), a former Sthānakvāsī monk, publicly renounced his ascetic status in 1934 and proclaimed himself a Digambara layman at Songadh, Gujarat, the movement centres on [[kundakunda|Kundakunda]]'s writings — especially the *Samayasāra* ("Essence of the Doctrine") — as the definitive expression of pure eternal Jainism accessible to all.

## Identity and Origins

Kānjī Svāmī was born in 1889 into a Sthānakvāsī family in Kathiawar, Gujarat. Orphaned early, he took Sthānakvāsī monastic initiation in 1913 and became a learned and famous monk known as the "Koh-i-noor of Kathiawar." Despite his reputation, "he was never able to engage fully with the Śvetāmbara scriptures which he was to claim lacked a sense of soul" (Dundas, ch. 9).

The decisive event was his discovery around **1921** of Kundakunda's *Samayasāra*. He then read other Digambara works including those of Todarmal and [[srimad-rajacandra|Śrīmad Rājacandra]], concluding that Digambara Jainism was the true path. He continued nominally as a Sthānakvāsī monk while preaching Kundakunda's ideas, creating increasing tension with his community.

In **1934**, during the celebrations of Mahāvīr Jayantī at **Songadh**, he threw away his *muhpattī* (the aniconic mouth-shield) and proclaimed himself a Digambara layman, "claiming that he had discovered the true, eternal Jainism which was not dependent on transmission through pupillary descent" (Dundas, ch. 9). The local Sthānakvāsī community was enraged; his life was threatened. He took refuge in a dilapidated house called "The Star of India" on the edge of Songadh — now regarded as the founding place of the Panth and visited annually by members on Kānjī Svāmī's birthday.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Panth occupies a peculiar structural position. It draws on **Digambara doctrine** (especially Kundakunda's metaphysics of the pure soul) but:
- Kānjī Svāmī never took Digambara ascetic initiation.
- The movement is a **lay movement**: "initiated ascetics are accorded no special status" and the taking of the five Great Vows is regarded as "little more than another means of attempting to gain merit" (Dundas, ch. 9).
- Kānjī did not see the movement in sectarian terms but as restoration of the "one true path."

Mainstream Digambara monks such as Acarya Śāntisāgara spoke respectfully of Kānjī while maintaining distance from his movement. The Panth has "always been a lay movement" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

The Panth does not reject Jain scripture wholesale but effectively centres its entire religious life on **Kundakunda's five main treatises**, the words of which are engraved on the walls of the central temple at Songadh and embossed with gold leaf. Kānjī did not claim originality but "merely to be reiterating the words of Mahāvīra and Kundakunda" (Dundas, ch. 9). His discourses took the form of "running commentaries on Kundakunda's writings," continuing as the characteristic form of religious instruction at Songadh to this day.

**Image worship** is practised: Kānjī "did not reject worship of images of the fordmakers, although he claimed that he tried to distance himself from it as he grew more spiritually advanced" (Dundas, ch. 9). He consecrated many temples and images on his pilgrimages. *Pūjā* at Songadh involves *darśana* and simple anointing of the image with water.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**Radical niścaya/vyavahāra priority.** Kānjī "outdid even Kundakunda in his insistence on the primacy of the higher level of truth (*niścaya naya*) over the lower one of ordinary life (*vyavahāra naya*)" (Dundas, ch. 9). The Three Jewels of right faith, knowledge, and practice "could only function properly on the basis of a prior experience of the soul, and the various rituals and merit-making practices of Jainism were regarded as subordinate and essentially irrelevant through being linked to the passions" (ch. 9). Only the pure soul (*śuddha ātman*) can effect deliverance.

**Myth of origin — the Simandhara connection.** In 1937, at the consecration of the Digambara Svādhyāya Mandir at Songadh, Kānjī announced he had in a previous existence lived in the region of **Mahāvideha** and been present when Kundakunda visited to hear the current fordmaker **Simandhara** preach. This claim was elaborated and confirmed by **Campābahena Mātājī** (1918–93), who became spiritual head of the Panth after Kānjī's death and "the only woman ever to lead a significant Jain sect" (Dundas, ch. 9). Campābahena stated she too had been present with Kānjī at Simandhara's assembly, and that both had heard the fordmaker expound the true doctrine to Kundakunda for eight days, after which Kundakunda composed the *Samayasāra*. A temple mural at Songadh depicts this lineage of transmission: "knowledge of the true doctrine cascading like streams of water from Simandhara to Kundakunda and downward to Kānjī Svāmī" (Dundas, ch. 9).

After Kānjī's death in 1980, Campābahena stated he would be reborn as a fordmaker called Sūryakīrti in the continent of Dhātakīkhaṇḍa.

## Hermeneutical Method

Commentary on Kundakunda's writings is the primary form of religious instruction. The hermeneutical priority of *niścaya naya* means that all conventional religious practice — dietary rules, ritual obligations, ascetic lineage — is re-evaluated as secondary and ultimately subordinate to direct experiential knowledge of the pure soul. "He ignored mundane topics, such as dietary prescriptions, in his preaching on the basis that all Jains would inevitably know about such matters" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Historical Development and Community

The **Digambara Svādhyāya Mandir** ("Study Temple") was founded at Songadh in 1937 and grew into a complex of massive marble buildings now visible from the road by Śvetāmbara pilgrims journeying to Śatruñjaya. The movement is administered by a board of trustees with a president elected every five years. It is missionary, training preachers and pandits and running a network of schools.

The Panth draws from a mix of Digambara and Sthānakvāsī backgrounds; many in Gujarat are converts from the Hālarī Osvāl caste (a relatively low Jain caste). Lower-order ascetics called *brahmacarinī* — almost all women in the early 1990s — live celibately at Songadh with comparative freedom to travel and own property.

Dundas assesses the Panth's future prospects as relatively strong, especially outside India, given its "emphasis on direct contact with an ancient scriptural tradition, skilful promulgation of a myth of origin, scaling down of ritual requirements, rejection of the ascetic lineage system, and advocacy of a direct, 'do-it-yourself' form of Jainism based on texts which translation has made accessible to all" — features well suited to the diaspora Jain community (Dundas, ch. 9).
