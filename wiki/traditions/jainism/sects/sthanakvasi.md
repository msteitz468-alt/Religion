# Sthanakvasi

The **Sthānakvāsīs** ("those who live in lodging-houses, *sthānak*") are a Śvetāmbara aniconic reform sect that emerged in the seventeenth century CE out of dissatisfaction with the [[lonka|Lonka Gaccha]], itself derived from the fifteenth-century critic of image worship [[lonka|Lonka (Lonka Sah)]]. The Sthānakvāsīs are the more numerous of the two principal Śvetāmbara aniconic subsects, the other being the [[terapanth|Terapanth]].

## Identity and Origins

The name refers to the practice, seen as authentically scriptural, of lodging in ruined and deserted buildings (*dhīmdhiyā*, "searchers for suitable lodgings") rather than in *upāśrayas* built adjacent to temples for image-worshipping monks. The Sthānakvāsīs trace their immediate origins to two merchants who broke away from the Lonka Gaccha around the third decade of the seventeenth century:

- **Lavajī** (c. 1609–59), an uncle of the Surat magnate Virjī Vora. There is "some dubiety about whether Lavajī took formal ascetic initiation," but his criticisms of the Lonka Gaccha were directed at its "general laxity" and he sought to revert to the model of monastic behaviour found in the *Daśavaikālika*, which of course did not include image worship (Dundas, ch. 9).
- **Dharmasimha** (1599–1671), with connections to Jamnagar.
- **Jīvarāj**, another Lonka Gaccha dissident, formed a loosely affiliated lineage at roughly the same time.

Although Lonka is *not* regarded as the Sthānakvāsī founder, he is "held to be a great precursor of the Sthānakvāsī interpretation of Jainism" (Dundas, ch. 9).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Sthānakvāsīs are Śvetāmbaras in scriptural and doctrinal inheritance but diverge from the image-worshipping Śvetāmbara mainstream (the various *gaccha* lineages) on:

- **Image worship**: rejected as a feature of the corrupt world age without genuine scriptural warrant.
- **Temple construction**: regarded as entailing destruction of life-forms.
- **Ritual**: much standard Śvetāmbara ritual rejected as accretion without textual basis.

Their relationship to the [[terapanth|Terapanth]] is genealogically close but contested: Acarya Bhiksu, the Terapanth founder, took initiation from the Sthānakvāsī *ācārya* Raghunāthji before breaking away in 1759.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Following the tradition attributed to [[lonka|Lonka]], the Sthānakvāsīs accept **32 texts** as having genuine authority, rejecting approximately 13–14 texts accepted in the image-worshipping Śvetāmbara canon. The rejected texts belong mostly to the class of *prākīrnaka* (mixed) texts that are "demonstrably late" (Dundas, ch. 9), though the precise rationale for the selection of 32 is historically murky. Dundas notes that "about a hundred years after Lonka's death, there was no unanimity among his followers about the number of scriptures" and enumerations of 27, 29, and 30 texts were also found among them (ch. 9).

Where image worship appears within the accepted 32 texts, the Sthānakvāsī interpretive strategy is to reread the Ardhamāgadhī word *ceiya* (conventionally: "temple") as "monk" or another sense satisfying sectarian criteria. There is "evidence of recent Sthānakvāsīs changing the accepted readings of texts to harmonise them with their views" (*PP* vol. 2 pp. 32–3, 44, 199 and 210; Dundas, ch. 9).

A related weakness: Dundas notes the "weakness in understanding of Prakrit and Sanskrit amongst the Sthānakvāsīs and Terapanthīs, with the exception of a few scholars, until recent times" (ch. 9) — a point pressed by Jñānsundar in his critique of Lonka's legacy.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

- **Rejection of image worship** and temple-related ritual.
- **Permanent mouth-shield (*muhpattī*)**: the square cloth worn permanently over the mouth by ascetics, introduced as a requirement by Lavajī, minimises destruction of air-bodies and insects. This became a defining mark distinguishing Sthānakvāsī and Terapanthi ascetics from image-worshipping Śvetāmbaras (who use the *muhpattī* only on certain occasions).
- **Mental worship (*bhāvapūjā*)**: devotional activity focuses on mental worship and, for the laity, on individual ascetics rather than images.
- **Lay access to scripture**: "the Sthānakvāsī laity is allowed full access to the scriptures, nuns do not always have to travel in the company of monks and no caste restrictions operate with regard to recruits to the ascetic community" (Dundas, ch. 9) — comparatively liberal features.

## Hermeneutical Method

Sthānakvāsī hermeneutics are fundamentally **scripturalist** (*peshat*-analogue): authority is located in the 32 accepted Śvetāmbara texts as the direct expression of Mahāvīra's eternal teaching. This generates a characteristic tension — the reinterpretation of *ceiya* and other terms to harmonise the 32 texts with the rejection of image worship shows that scriptural literalism is supplemented by a tradition of sectarian reinterpretation when the plain reading conflicts with the movement's founding commitment.

## Historical Development and Subdivisions

The Sthānakvāsīs split early into branches (*tol*), with Dharmadāsa supposedly founding as many as 22. Regional and preceptorial connections generated many subsequent subsects. During the nineteenth century, the sect experienced a crisis of apostasy, with monks defecting to image-worshipping Śvetāmbara ranks — most significantly Buterayājī and his pupil **Ātmārāmjī** (1837–96), who after study concluded the scriptures justified image worship and became the Tapa Gaccha *ācārya* Vijayānanda Sūri, an important reinvigorator of that tradition and a scholarly resource for early western Jainologists.

**Twentieth-century regeneration.** In 1952 the **Śramaṇ Saṅgh** was formed as the largest single Sthānakvāsī group, headed by one *ācārya* chosen by the ascetics with lay involvement. Not all lineages merged within it, and disputes have continued (including over whether microphones injure air-organisms). As of 1999, there were 533 monks and 2,690 nuns in the Sthānakvāsī community and 26 independent ascetic groups total, most located in Gujarat (Dundas, ch. 9).

One notable development: the Amarmuni Sampradāya promoted a nun, **Candanājī**, to the rank of *ācārya* — "an unprecedented occurrence in Jain ascetic history" (Dundas, ch. 9).

Notable figures include Acarya Susilkumar (1926–94), who in 1975 travelled to the United States (breaking the traditional prohibition on crossing the sea) and founded the Siddhacalam centre in New Jersey. The current leader of the Śramaṇ Saṅgh, Acarya Śiv Muni (born 1942), also travels regularly outside India.

## Relationship to the Kanji Svami Panth

The [[kanji-svami-panth|Kanji Svami Panth]] originated when its founder, born into a Sthānakvāsī family and initiated as a Sthānakvāsī monk, underwent a spiritual crisis, read Kundakunda's writings, and publicly renounced the Sthānakvāsī path in 1934.
