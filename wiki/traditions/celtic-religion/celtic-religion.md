# Celtic religion

**Chronology:** see the [[celtic-religion-timeline|Timeline of Celtic Religion]] and the [[comparative-timeline|Comparative Timeline of World Religions]].

**Celtic religion** is the pre-Christian polytheism of the Celtic-speaking peoples of Iron Age and early
medieval Europe — the **Gauls** of the Continent, the **Britons** of Britain, and above all the **Gaels** of
Ireland, whose myths survive in the richest written form. Unlike [[norse-religion|Norse]], Greek, or
[[egyptian-religion|Egyptian]] religion, the Celts left **almost no scripture of their own**: the druids
transmitted sacred learning **orally** and prohibited its writing. What we have instead is a **double
filtration** — Greco-Roman ethnographers (Caesar, Posidonius, Lucan) describing Gaulish cult from the
outside, and **Christian monks of medieval Ireland and Wales** recording the old gods centuries after
conversion, recasting them as ancient kings and heroes rather than deities (**euhemerization**). The result
is a mythology preserved chiefly as **legend and saga** rather than liturgy.

## The shape of the evidence

Three strata must be kept distinct:

1. **Continental / Gaulish** (archaeological + classical): votive inscriptions, the Gundestrup cauldron,
   sanctuaries, and named gods (Lugus, Taranis, Cernunnos, Epona, Belenus) reported by Roman writers and
   often mapped onto Roman gods (*interpretatio romana*).
2. **Insular Irish** (the [[gods-and-fighting-men|Mythological and Fenian Cycles]]): the **[[the-tuatha-de-danann|Tuatha Dé Danann]]**,
   the "people of the goddess Danu," remembered as a divine race who ruled Ireland before the human Gaels and
   withdrew into the **[[the-otherworld|Otherworld]]** of the *síde* (fairy mounds).
3. **Insular Welsh** (the **[[mabinogion|Mabinogion]]**): the families of **Dôn** and of **Llŷr**, and the
   lords of **[[annwn|Annwn]]**, the Welsh Otherworld — Pwyll, Rhiannon, Bran, Branwen, Math, Gwydion.

## Doctrine, cult, and the Otherworld

Celtic religion had **no fixed pantheon-list and no creed**; gods were often **local and tribal**, and the
same deity appears under many names. Recurring features the sources agree on:

- the **[[the-otherworld|Otherworld]]** — a parallel realm of the gods and dead (Irish *Tír na nÓg*, "Land of
  the Young"; Welsh **[[annwn|Annwn]]**), reachable across water, through mounds, or at the turning of the
  year, and **not** primarily a place of punishment but of feasting, youth, and abundance;
- **belief in an afterlife so strong** that Roman writers marvel the Celts would lend money to be repaid in
  the next world — and connect this to the **[[druidism|druids']]** teaching of the soul's survival (often
  read, cautiously, as a form of transmigration);
- **sacred kingship** bound to the land's fertility and to the goddess of sovereignty;
- **triplism** — gods and goddesses appearing in **threes** (the three Machas, the **[[morrigan|Morrígan]]**
  as a trio, the triple Brigid);
- cult at **springs, rivers, groves (*nemeton*), and severed-head shrines**, with festivals at the four
  quarter-days: **Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh**.

## Hermeneutics — reading gods through Christian scribes

Because the texts are Christian-era, the wiki's standing caution mirrors the [[snorri-sturluson|Snorri]]
problem in Norse: the **[[the-tuatha-de-danann|Tuatha Dé Danann]]** are presented in the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*
("Book of Invasions") not as gods but as one of several **invading races** of Ireland, defeated by the Gaels
and driven underground — divinity rationalized into pseudo-history. The interpreter's task is to read the
**god back out of the king**: Nuada of the Silver Hand, Lugh of the Long Arm, the Dagda, and the Morrígan
behave, beneath the saga surface, exactly like sovereignty-, light-, fertility-, and war-deities. Welsh
material is filtered the same way through the **bardic** schools.

## Map of sub-traditions

- **[[tuatha-de-danann-cult|Tuatha Dé Danann cult (Irish/Gaelic)]]** — the Irish divine race and the
  Mythological Cycle: Nuada, Lugh, the Dagda, the Morrígan, Brigid, Manannán.
- **[[mabinogion-tradition|The Mabinogion tradition (Welsh/Brythonic)]]** — the families of Dôn and Llŷr and
  the lords of Annwn: Pwyll, Rhiannon, Bran, Branwen, Math, Gwydion, Arawn.
- **[[druidism|Druidism]]** — the priestly-learned class (druids, bards, *vates*/filid) reported by classical
  authors and remembered in Irish law: the religion's "clergy," teaching, sacrifice, and lore.
- **[[celtic-reconstructionism|Celtic Reconstructionism / modern Druidry]]** — the modern revival.
