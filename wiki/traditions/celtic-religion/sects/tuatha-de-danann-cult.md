# Tuatha Dé Danann cult (Irish / Gaelic Celtic religion)

The **Irish / Gaelic** stream of [[celtic-religion|Celtic religion]] centers on the **[[the-tuatha-de-danann|Tuatha Dé Danann]]** —
"the people of the goddess **Danu**" — the **divine race** of Irish myth. In the medieval pseudo-history
*Lebor Gabála Érenn* they are remembered as one of the successive peoples to settle Ireland, arriving "in
dark clouds," skilled in magic and craft, who **defeat the Fir Bolg and the Fomorians** and rule until the
human **Milesians (Gaels)** conquer them — whereupon they retreat into the **[[the-otherworld|Otherworld]]**,
the *síde* (fairy mounds), becoming the *aes síde*, the fairy-folk of later Irish lore. This is
**[[celtic-religion|euhemerization]]**: gods recorded as ancient kings.

## Canon and scripture

The wiki's primary witness is **[[gods-and-fighting-men|Gods and Fighting Men]]** (Lady Gregory's retelling
of the **Mythological** and **Fenian** Cycles), drawing on the *Lebor Gabála*, *Cath Maige Tuired* ("The
[Second] Battle of Mag Tuired"), and the Fenian tales of Fionn mac Cumhaill. Its great set-piece is the
**Second Battle of Mag Tuired**, where **[[lugh|Lugh]]** leads the Tuatha Dé against the **Fomorian**
oppressors and slays his own grandfather, the one-eyed **Balor**.

## Doctrinal distinctives

- the **divine race** withdrawn into the mounds — gods who became fairies;
- **sovereignty**: kingship valid only by union with the land-goddess; a blemished king (Nuada's lost hand)
  cannot reign;
- **triple deities** — the [[morrigan|Morrígan]] as a war-trio, the three Brigids of poetry, smithcraft, and
  healing;
- the great craft-gods: **[[dian-cecht|Dian Cécht]]** (healing), Goibniu (smithing), the **[[the-dagda|Dagda]]**
  with his cauldron of plenty and life-and-death club.

## Hermeneutics

**Filid** (learned poets) and **druids** transmitted the lore orally; Christian monks then recorded and
rationalized it. Reading the cult means reading the **god back out of the euhemerized king** — recovering
Lugh the light/skill-god from "Lugh the war-leader," the Dagda the father-god from "the Dagda the gluttonous
chieftain."
