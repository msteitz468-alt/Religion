# Neo-Confucianism (Daoxue)

The movement calls itself **Daoxue** (道學, "Learning of the Way") — "Neo-Confucianism" is a Western
scholarly term. It is a profound revival and metaphysical deepening of Confucianism in the Song
(11th–13th c.) and Ming (14th–17th c.) dynasties, developed to answer the challenge of Buddhism and
[[daoism|Daoism]] on Confucian terms. Its synthesizer is **[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]]** (1130–1200).

## Identity and Origins

Daoxue emerged from a cluster of Song-dynasty thinkers who believed the authentic Confucian Way had
been lost since Mencius (4th c. BCE) and needed to be recovered. This claim was structured by the
concept of the **[[daotong|daotong]]** (道統, succession of the Way): a lineage of transmission
from the ancient sages through specific individuals to the present. Key figures in founding the
movement:

- **[[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]]** (1017–1073): author of the [[taijitu-shuo|Taijitu shuo]] and
  [[tongshu|Tongshu]]; retrospectively elevated by Zhu Xi as the post-Mencius bridge.
- **Cheng Hao** (1032–1085) and **Cheng Yi** (1033–1107): the Cheng brothers; introduced the
  systematic concept of *li* (principle); first generation to identify as recovering the ancient Way.
- **Zhang Zai** (1020–1077): "Western Inscription" (*Ximing*); developed the concept of *qi* and
  the unity of humanity with Heaven-and-earth.
- **[[zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]]** (1130–1200): synthesizer; wrote the canonical commentaries on the Four
  Books and on Zhou Dunyi's texts; constructed the daotong that organized the movement's history.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Claims to recover the authentic [[dao|Way]] of the ancient sages — lost, it claimed, after Mencius —
through a return to the classical texts read through *li/qi* metaphysics. This "return" is in fact a
powerful new metaphysical reading, responding to and incorporating Buddhist and Daoist concepts while
insisting on Confucian moral-political primacy.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Elevates the **Four Books** ([[analects|Analects]], [[mencius|Mencius]], *Great Learning* [*Daxue*],
*Doctrine of the Mean* [*Zhongyong*]) **over** the older Five Classics as the curriculum's core,
framed by Zhu Xi's commentaries. Also treats [[taijitu-shuo|Zhou Dunyi's *Taijitu shuo*]] and
[[tongshu|*Tongshu*]] as foundational texts for the movement's cosmological framework. Zhu Xi's
*Jinsilu* (Reflections on Things at Hand, 1175) opens with the *Taijitu shuo* as the first item.

Zhu Xi's Four Books commentaries became the basis of the civil service examinations from 1313 (Yuan
dynasty) to 1905 — shaping the education of the Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese literate
classes for ~600 years.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

A metaphysics of **li** (理, principle/pattern) and **qi** (氣, psychophysical stuff):
- Every thing has its *li*; the *li* of all things are unified in the **[[taiji|taiji]]** (Supreme
  Polarity = supreme principle); the *taiji* is the whole of *li*.
- *Li* has logical priority over *qi* (not temporal); they "never separate."
- Human nature (*xing*) = *li* as received by human beings; nature is originally good (Mencius).
- Self-cultivation = actualizing one's nature by eliminating the distorting effects of selfish desire.

**The central cultivation question** (Adler 2014): how to cultivate the still, unexpressed mind
(*weifa*, before emotions arise) and the active, expressed mind (*yifa*, after they arise) — and how
the two phases relate. Zhu Xi's resolution (1169): **interpenetration** — still within active,
active within still — joined by *jing* (reverent composure). This resolution was cosmologically
grounded in [[tongshu|Tongshu section 16]] and [[taijitu-shuo|*Taijitu shuo*]] section B.

The two great internal schools:
- **Cheng–Zhu** ("School of Principle," *lixue*): principle is discovered through things and study
  (*ge wu*, investigating things/affairs); the mind must be regulated through reverent composure
  and outward investigation.
- **Lu–Wang** ("School of Mind," *xinxue*) — Lu Jiuyuan (1139–1193) and Wang Yangming (1472–1529):
  principle is directly and innately present in the mind; "the unity of knowledge and action," realized
  by intuition. Lu criticized Zhu's *wuji* and *taiji* interpretation.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Li-investigation reading**: every text (canonical or not) is read to discover its internal *li*
(principle). Zhu Xi's commentaries present themselves as recovering the plain meaning (*peshat*-
equivalent) of the text, but the "plain meaning" is always mediated by the li/qi framework.

**Daotong reading**: texts within the succession (Four Books, Zhou Dunyi, the Chengs) are treated
as expressing a single, coherent Way; apparent discrepancies are interpretive problems, not evidence
of independent positions. This is the hermeneutical counterpart to the historical daotong claim.

**Reverent composure and investigation together**: cultivation requires both *jing* (attentiveness,
inward composure) and *ge wu* (studying texts and affairs outwardly). Neither alone is sufficient.

## The Daotong and Zhou Dunyi

A major theme from [[adler-reconstructing-confucian-dao|Adler 2014]]: the daotong lineage, and
especially Zhou Dunyi's place in it, was substantially **Zhu Xi's retrospective construction**,
nearly 100 years after Zhou died. The conventional story — that Zhou was always recognized as the
first post-Mencius sage — is historically incorrect. Zhu Xi built Zhou into the lineage between
1169 and 1196 for two interlocking reasons:
1. *Cosmological*: Zhou's *taiji* concept provided the cosmological ground for linking *qi* and *li*.
2. *Religious-practical*: Zhou's activity-stillness interpenetration (Tongshu 16) verified Zhu Xi's
   own spiritual breakthrough of 1169.

See [[daotong|daotong]] and [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] for the full account.

## Relationship to Buddhism and Daoism

Daoxue is deeply shaped by — and self-consciously reacting against — the Buddhist and Daoist
traditions that had dominated Chinese intellectual life for centuries:

- **Chan Buddhism**: Zhu Xi's cultivation methods (especially quiet-sitting and attention to the
  still mind) are structurally similar to Chan practice. He drew the distinction by insisting on
  the Confucian moral-relational content of *li*, against the Buddhist emptiness (*kong*).
- **Huayan Buddhism**: The *li-shi wu-ai* (non-obstruction of principle and phenomenon) structure
  parallels Zhu Xi's *wuji-taiji* relationship.
- **Daoism**: Zhou Dunyi's *Taijitu shuo* almost certainly derived from Daoist cosmological
  diagrams; *wuji* and *zhu jing* (emphasizing stillness) have Daoist resonances that Zhu Xi
  systematically re-read as Confucian.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

Declared heretical by the court in its early period (Cai Jing banned Cheng Yi's writings). Became
**state orthodoxy** after 1241 (emperor installed five Daoxue figures in Confucian temple) and
especially after 1313 (Yuan civil service exams). Internally split Cheng-Zhu vs. Lu-Wang; both
claim to be the authentic Daoxue.

## Historical Development and Influence

Founded by Zhou Dunyi and the Cheng brothers; synthesized by Zhu Xi; challenged by Wang Yangming;
institutionalized as state orthodoxy in Yuan, Ming, and Qing China; shaped Joseon Korea, Tokugawa
Japan, and Vietnam. The dominant intellectual framework of late imperial East Asia for over 600 years.
