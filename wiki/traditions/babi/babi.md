# Babi Religion

*Populated from [[smith-short-history-bahai-faith|Smith, A Short History of the Bahá'í Faith (1995)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Identity and Origins

The Babi Religion (Babism) emerged in Iran in 1844, rooted in the world of mid-nineteenth-century Iranian Twelver Shi'ism and particularly in the Shaykhi movement. Its founder was Sayyid 'Ali-Muhammad, known as [[the-bab|the Báb]] ("the Gate"), who declared his mission on the evening of 22 May 1844 in Shiraz. Smith describes it as having "emerged as a movement within Shi'i Islam" (line 271), with the Babis being "almost all Iranians who had formerly been Shi'i Muslims" (lines 272–273). By 1848, through the Báb's open claim to be the Imam Mahdi and the revelation of the Bayán, it had become a new religion distinct from Islam. By 1853, following armed conflict, persecution, and the Báb's execution in 1850, the movement had effectively collapsed as an independent force, though it was subsequently transformed into the [[bahai|Bahá'í Faith]] through the activities of [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh]].

## Relationship to Parent Tradition (Islam / Twelver Shi'ism)

Babism grew directly out of Twelver Shi'ism, particularly the Shaykhi sub-movement within it. The Báb's first disciples were all Shaykhis, and the initial missionary expansion concentrated within existing Shaykhi communities. Smith notes that the Babi movement was "effectively a sub-sect within Shaykhism" as it first developed (lines 607–608).

What Babism inherited from Islam and Shi'ism: the concept of a series of divinely appointed messengers; belief in holy law grounded in divine revelation; the messianic expectation of the Hidden Twelfth Imam (the Mahdi); the Shi'i veneration of suffering and martyrdom; and much Islamic and Shaykhi terminology and esoteric symbolism.

Where Babism departed: the Báb claimed to be not merely an intermediary but ultimately the Mahdi and an independent bearer of divine revelation whose new holy book — the Bayán — superseded the Quran. Islamic law (the shari'a) was declared abrogated. The resurrection (qiyáma) was declared already accomplished in spiritual terms. The concept of the Manifestation of God (mazhar-i iláhí), while drawing on Shaykhi gnostic exposition, was given new systematic form. Smith notes the sharp contrast: for Muslims, "Muhammad is the 'seal of the prophets' (understood to mean the last) and the Islamic holy law (the sharia) provides the standard for human behaviour until the resurrection," while for Babis "the resurrection has already come" (lines 292–296).

Smith cautions against treating Babism as simply a Muslim sect: "to describe the Baha'i Faith as a Muslim sect — as some writers are still inclined to do — is highly misleading, and as inaccurate as describing second-century Christianity as a Jewish sect" (lines 279–281).

## Canon and Scripture

The Báb was extraordinarily prolific. By around 1847 he himself estimated he had revealed some half-million verses (perhaps five million words), of which only 100,000 had been circulated; the Quran, by comparison, comprises 6,236 verses (lines 691–694).

**Major texts:**

- **Qayyúmu'l-Asmá'** — the most important of the Báb's early writings, begun on the night of his declaration. A commentary on the Quranic Sura of Joseph, it proclaimed the Báb's authority and was "composed in the manner of the Quran" and presented as "new verses from God" (lines 478–486). Widely copied by Babis and used as a proof-text.
- **The Bayán** (Persian and Arabic versions) — the Báb's book of holy law and doctrine, composed in Maku (late 1847 or early 1848), approximately 95,000 words. Smith calls it "a new holy book superseding the Qur'an" (line 855). It contains Babi law, doctrine, and explanations of Islamic eschatological terms. It also introduces the prophecy of "He whom God shall make manifest" (Man-yuzhiruhu'lláh).
- **Nine commentaries on the whole Qur'an**, composed during his imprisonment at Maku and Chihriq.
- Various **prayers, proclamatory letters, treatises on proper conduct, gnostic truth**, and a "discussion of the science of talismans."
- Numerous **tablets** — letters to the Shah, his chief minister, various clerics, and disciples.

Smith notes that the Báb's early writings took the form of Quranic commentaries, "but their main purpose was to proclaim the Báb's authority and the arrival of the day of resurrection rather than to provide commentary in any traditional sense" (lines 697–699).

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**The Báb's claims:** The nature of the Báb's early claims was initially ambiguous. He was at first widely understood to be claiming to be the intermediary (báb or "gate") between the Hidden Twelfth Imam and the Shi'i faithful. By 1848, however, he openly announced his claim to be the return of the Imam Mahdi, marking "the era of resurrection (qiyáma)" and representing "a challenge to the entire social order" (lines 780–782). His "second declaration" was made at a religious tribunal in Tabriz in July 1848.

**Abrogation of Islamic law:** The Bayán replaced the shari'a with a new holy law. The Báb's legal system was "entirely different from that of Islam, although similar terminology was often employed" (lines 866–867). Key Babi laws included:
- A new solar calendar of nineteen months of nineteen days, beginning at the spring equinox.
- Distinctive forms of ritual prayer, fasting, pilgrimage (to the Báb's house in Shiraz), and tithing.
- Marriage declared obligatory; divorce permitted only after a year of waiting.
- Prohibition of tobacco, opium, and alcohol; also the study of grammar, logic, scholastic philosophy, and unprofitable sciences.
- Study of talismans recommended.
- The dead to be buried in coffins of stone or crystal.
- Future Babi kings to expel non-Babis from their lands unless engaged in useful professions; non-Babi books to be destroyed.
- A stable monetary system; merchants permitted to charge interest (contra Islamic law).

**The Manifestation of God (mazhar-i iláhí):** Smith identifies this as "central to the theology of the Báb's system" (line 932). God is in essence unknowable; the Manifestations of God originate from the Primal Will or Logos, combining "the station of revelatory divinity with human servitude." There is a succession from Adam through Muhammad to the Báb and on to "He whom God shall make manifest" (lines 934–949).

**Eschatology:** Prophetic fulfilment was "a spiritual reality." The events of Shi'ite eschatology had all come to pass as spiritual rather than physical events. "There was no resurrection of the body" (line 976). Believers in the Manifestation had attained paradise; deniers were in hell-fire. The Báb's first disciples, the Letters of the Living, were identified with the holy ones of Shi'ism.

**"He whom God shall make manifest":** The Báb's doctrine of a coming messianic figure after him — the one for whom "a thousand perusals of the Bayán would not be equal to reading one of his verses" (lines 927–928). This personage would arise suddenly, and all were to leave a vacant place for him in every meeting (lines 923–931). The Baha'is would later identify this figure as Bahá'u'lláh.

## Hermeneutical Method

The Báb's hermeneutics combined several registers:

1. **Gnostic Quranic exposition** inherited from Shaykhi tradition: the Báb "shared the Shaykhi tradition of gnostic exposition of scripture" but developed it distinctively (lines 618–619). His writings abound in "esoteric allusions" and "complex esotericism."
2. **Revelation as proof:** The "actual act of revelation in which the Báb chanted new divine verses was intensely compelling for his adherents" (lines 712–715); the ability to reveal verses spontaneously was itself presented as the Báb's "proof."
3. **Quranic form adopted and superseded:** The Qayyúmu'l-Asmá' was composed in the manner of the Quran (lines 479–480). The Báb thus appropriated the Quranic form while simultaneously asserting a new revelation that superseded it.
4. **Esoteric symbolism:** Extensive use of numerology — "the number nineteen (equivalent to the words wáhid, unity, and wujúd, God's absolute being)" — and alchemical and colour symbolism (lines 979–982).
5. **Spiritual hermeneutics of eschatology:** Quranic and Islamic eschatological terms were reinterpreted as referring to spiritual rather than physical realities (lines 969–976).
6. **Progressive displacement of Islamic paradigm:** Smith notes a "clear contrast with his earlier writings, which were written within an Islamic paradigm, and in which heterodox ideas were likely to be concealed in esoteric language" (lines 919–921). By his later writings the Báb presented "a new religious framework distinct from that of Islam."

## Historical Development

**1844 — Declaration:** The Báb declared his mission on the evening of 22 May 1844 with the conversion of Mullá Husayn Bushrú'í. He dispatched disciples throughout Iran and a special messenger (Mullá 'Alí Bastámí) to the Shi'i holy cities in Iraq. The Báb journeyed to Mecca but elicited little response. He returned to Shiraz (July 1845) and was placed under house arrest.

**1845–1847 — Expansion under constraint:** Despite arrest, the movement continued to spread. Notable converts from outside Shaykhi circles included Sayyid Yahyá Dárábí ("Vahid") and representatives of Mullá Muhammad-'Alí "Hujjat" from Zanjan. A key figure was Fátimih Baraqhání (1814–52), known as Qurratu'l-'Ayn ("Solace of the Eyes") and Táhirih ("the Pure One"), the only woman among the Báb's leading disciples, who led Babi activity in Karbala with "audacious and often provocative" proclamation (lines 601–602) before being expelled to Iran in March 1847. The Báb was transferred to the remote prison of Maku (summer 1847) and then Chihriq (April 1848).

**1848 — Radicalization:** Two key events marked a turning point. The conference of Badasht (June–July 1848), held in a remote hamlet in northern Iran, saw Táhirih advocate abandonment of the Islamic shari'a and preparation for the messianic kingdom; she removed her veil and on one occasion appeared in men's clothing with a drawn sword. "A definite radicalization of the movement had occurred" (line 833). Then the Báb was brought before a religious tribunal in Tabriz (July 1848), where he openly announced his claim to Mahdihood. The assembled clerics issued a religious fatwa declaring him an apostate.

**1848–1851 — Armed conflicts:**
- **Shrine of Shaykh Tabarsí** (October 1848–May 1849): Mullá Husayn raised the "Black Standard" and marched from Khurásán with several hundred armed followers. After an attack, they fortified the shrine of Shaykh Tabarsí and endured a seven-month siege; finally worn down by starvation and cannon fire, they responded to a false truce and were massacred. Many leading Babis were killed, including Mullá Husayn and Quddús.
- **Nayriz** (May 1850): Approximately 1,500 Babis (nearly half the town's population) resisted government forces; ended within a month with the same pattern of truce and massacre.
- **Zanjan** (May 1850–January 1851): 3,000 Babis in a city of 20,000 conducted an eight-month urban resistance, including women among the combatants and the issuance of local coinage.

**1850 — Execution of the Báb:** Faced with continuing Babi resistance, Chief Minister Mirza Taqi Khan ordered the Báb's execution. On 8 or 9 July 1850, the Báb was shot before a large crowd in Tabriz. Remarkably, a first regiment of Armenian Christians apparently aimed to miss; a second (Muslim) regiment completed the task (lines 1192–1200).

**1851–1853 — Collapse:** After the Báb's execution the nominal leader was Mirzá Yahyá Núrí ("Subh-i Azal"), appointed by the Báb, but he was young, reclusive, and unable to maintain cohesion. In August 1852 a small group made a bungled attempt on the Shah's life; the ensuing mass arrests and executions, including that of Táhirih, effectively destroyed the organized Babi movement. Smith writes: "the Babi religion had to all intents and purposes collapsed, its roots torn up by its opponents. That it was to re-emerge, eventually transformed into the Baha'i Faith, was due almost entirely to the activities of Baha'u'llah" (lines 1241–1244).
