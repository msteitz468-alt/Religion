# Azali Bábísm

## Identity and Origins

Azali Bábísm is the sub-tradition of Bábísm that follows [[subh-i-azal|Subh-i-Azal]] (Mírzá Yahyá, 1831/32–1912) and rejects Bahá'u'lláh's claim to be He Whom God Shall Make Manifest — the messianic successor foretold in the Báb's writings (line 4474–4476).

The split crystallized during the 1860s when Bahá'u'lláh, in Edirne, made an open declaration of his mission. The great majority of Bábís became Bahá'ís; the minority that remained loyal to Azal became known as Azalis (lines 4477–4479).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Azalis regard themselves as the continuation of authentic Bábísm under the leadership authorized by the Báb's appointment of Azal c.1849. They hold the [[the-bayan|Bayán]] as their central scripture and regard Azal as its custodian.

Bahá'ís regard Azal's claims as illegitimate: his appointment by the Báb is interpreted by 'Abdu'l-Bahá as a stratagem to divert attention from Bahá'u'lláh, and not as a clear succession (line 4384–4386). From the Bahá'í perspective, the Azalis rejected the fulfillment of the Báb's own prophecy.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Azalis accept the Báb's writings (particularly the Bayán) as canonical but reject all of Bahá'u'lláh's writings as scripture. In this way the Azali canon is a strict subset of the Bábí canon, excluding the texts that Bahá'ís treat as the fulfillment and supersession of the Bábí revelation.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

The defining position is rejection of Bahá'u'lláh's claim. Azalis hold that Azal, not Bahá'u'lláh, was the lawful head of the Bábí community. The nature of Azal's role — whether as a simple custodian, a prophet, or a figure of independent authority — was not clearly systematized, and his reclusive character meant the sect had no strong doctrinal elaboration.

## Historical Development

Following Azal's death in Cyprus in 1912, he had appointed Hádí Dawlatábádí as his successor in lieu of one of his sons. By the 1970s there were perhaps only 500–5,000 Azalis remaining in Iran, with no formal leadership (lines 4486–4489).

Despite small numbers, Azalis included prominent Iranian political and literary figures (lines 4495–4504). Their cultural influence within Iranian intellectual circles was disproportionate to their numerical strength.
