# Sikhism

Top-level tradition page. **Sikhism** (Gurmukhi: *Sikhī*) is a monotheistic Indic tradition founded
in the Punjab by **[[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]]** (1469–1539) and developed through a lineage of **ten
human Gurus**, culminating in the investiture of scriptural and corporate authority in the
**[[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]** (the scripture) and the **[[khalsa|Khalsa]]** /
[[sikhs|Panth]] (the community). This is the parent node for the Sikh sects under
`traditions/sikhism/sects/`.

*(Tradition added 2026-06-27 — scaffolded from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod's *Sikhism*]], read in full.)*

## Self-Understanding and Definition

A *Sikh* is a "learner / disciple" (Skt. *śiṣya*); *[[gurmat|Gurmat]]* ("the teaching of the Guru")
is the tradition's own name for what English calls "Sikhism" (McLeod, agent 1 ~L438–440). McLeod's
governing thesis is that Sikhism's background was the **[[sant-tradition|Sant movement]]** of north
India and that [[guru-nanak|Nanak]] "was a representative of the Sant movement," expressing its
characteristic doctrines — *not* a syncretism of Hinduism and Islam. He further argues, more
contentiously, that Sikhism emerged as a *genuinely separate* religious system only during the **Singh
Sabha** period (late 19th–early 20th c.), when **Tat Khalsa** reformers stressed its distance from the
Hindu tradition (agent 1 ~L295–333). See [[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]].

## Historical Development

The ten Gurus: [[guru-nanak|Nanak]] (1469–1539) → [[guru-angad|Angad]] → [[guru-amar-das|Amar Das]]
(the *manji* system, the [[langar|langar]], the Goindval pothis) → [[guru-ram-das|Ram Das]] (founds
[[amritsar|Amritsar]], the *masand* system) → [[guru-arjan|Arjan]] (compiles the
[[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]], 1604; first **martyr** Guru, 1606) → [[guru-hargobind|Hargobind]]
(the two swords, **[[miri-piri|mīrī-pīrī]]**; the Akal Takhat) → [[guru-har-rai|Har Rai]] →
[[guru-har-krishan|Har Krishan]] → [[guru-tegh-bahadur|Tegh Bahadur]] (martyr) →
[[guru-gobind-singh|Gobind Singh]], who founds the **[[khalsa|Khalsa]]** on Baisakhi **1699** and, at
his death in 1708, closes the personal Guruship — vesting it jointly in the **Guru Granth** and the
**Guru Panth**. Thereafter: Banda Bahadur's rising, the *misls*, [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]]'s
kingdom, British annexation (1849), the Singh Sabha, the Gurdwara Reform Movement (→ the **SGPC**,
1925), Partition (1947), and the 1984 assault on the [[amritsar|Golden Temple]].

## Scripture and Canon

The central scripture is the **[[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]** (the Ādi Granth), venerated
as the living, perpetual Guru and "believed to embody the actual presence of the mystical Guru." The
**[[dasam-granth|Dasam Granth]]**, associated with Guru Gobind Singh, "occupies an uncertain position":
once equal to the Adi Granth in the 18th century, now disputed, though portions ([[jap-sahib|Jap
Sahib]], Ten Savayyas, Benatī Chaupaī) remain in daily liturgical use. The
[[janam-sakhis|janam-sākhīs]] and [[rahit-maryada|rahit-nāmās]] lie outside scripture.

## Hermeneutical Frameworks

*[[gurmat|Gurmat]]* reads the **bāṇī** of the Guru Granth Sahib as the living voice of
[[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]]; the heart of practice is **[[nam-simaran|nām simaraṇ]]** — remembrance of
the divine Name, especially through *kīrtan* (hymn-singing) and meditation — over against external
ritual, which Sikhism deprecates. The scripture is consulted as an oracle (the daily *[[hukam|hukam]]*,
a passage read at random). The fixed daily liturgy is the **[[nit-nem|Nit-nem]]** (see also
[[japji|Japji]], [[ardas|Ardas]]).

## The Ultimate Authority

After 1708 the Guru's authority is held to reside jointly in the **Granth** and the **Panth**. In
practice, McLeod observes, supreme authority is exercised by two bodies: the **Guru Granth Sahib**,
paramount on matters it "indisputably covers," and the **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee
(SGPC)** — created by the **Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925**, which publishes the **[[rahit-maryada|Sikh
Rahit Maryada]]** and appoints the *jathedār* of the **Akal Takhat** — on all the rest. Because the
scripture is silent on the [[khalsa|Khalsa]] and on Rahit matters, this authority gap is structurally
real and politically contested (agent 6 ~L1053–1198). McLeod names three "pillars" holding the Panth
together: **faith in the Guru Granth Sahib**, the **[[gurdwara|gurdwara]]**, and the **[[rahit-maryada|
Rahit]]**.

## Map of Sects

- **[[khalsa|Khalsa]]** — the order of initiated Sikhs founded 1699; the normative mainstream.
- **[[nihang|Nihangs (Akalis)]]** — militant Khalsa order; give full honour to the Dasam Granth.
- **[[udasi|Udasis]]** — early ascetic order claiming descent from Nanak's son Siri Chand;
  "Hinduizing," ejected from gurdwara control in the 1920s.
- **[[nirmala|Nirmalas]]** — celibate scholarly order; Vedantic learning; cordial with Sanatan Sikhs.
- **[[nirankari|Nirankaris]]** (Asali) — Baba Dayal's Nanak-panthi renewal movement; a continuing line
  of Gurus.
- **[[namdhari|Namdharis (Kukas)]]** — reformist "purified Khalsa"; a living Guru-line; treat the
  Dasam Granth as fully canonical.

## Political History of the Tradition (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 1)

[[khushwant-singh-history-of-the-sikhs|Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs*]] (Scope 2 of the Sikhism
corpus) supplies the tradition's political-historical spine, complementing McLeod's thematic survey. It
traces the institutional build-up under the early Gurus (the [[langar|langar]], the *manjī*/*masand*
network, the Gurmukhi script, the founding of [[amritsar|Amritsar]] and compilation of the
[[guru-granth-sahib|Granth]]), the militarization under [[guru-hargobind|Hargobind]] and the founding of the
[[khalsa|Khalsa]] under [[guru-gobind-singh|Gobind Singh]]; then the eighteenth-century era of
[[banda-bahadur|Banda Bahadur]], the persecution and the [[ghallughara|Ghallugharas]], and the
[[misls|misl]] confederacies governing through the **Sarbat Khalsa**; culminating in
[[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]]'s **Sarkar Khalsa** — a Sikh-led but deliberately multi-confessional Punjabi
state (1799–1839), the high-water mark of Sikh temporal power before the British annexation of 1849.
