# Namdhari Sikhism (Kuka Sikhs)

## Identity and Origins

The Namdharis, widely known by the popular name **Kukas**, are a Sikh sect whose defining conviction is that the personal line of Gurus has never ended. Their name derives from *nam* — the divine Name — reflecting the central role of *nam simaran* in their practice. They understand themselves not as a deviation from Khalsa Sikhism but as its truest continuation.

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

The Namdharis share with the orthodox Khalsa a deep loyalty to Khalsa traditions, the Five Ks, and the Sikh Rahit as they understand it. Their practice of the Anand marriage ceremony, their vegetarianism, their abstinence from alcohol, and their rigorous observance of the Rahit put them conspicuously within the Khalsa orbit. McLeod notes that "their loyalty to Khalsa traditions as they understand them is, however, altogether too obvious to be ignored and only the strictly orthodox would be prepared to place them outside the circle of Sikhs" (~L28–33).

The point of radical divergence is the doctrine of the **continuing Guru line**. The Namdharis hold that Guru Gobind Singh did not die at Nander in 1708 as orthodox Sikhism teaches. Instead, he "lived his later life in secret as Baba Ajapal Singh and personally passed the office of Guru on to Balak Singh before dying in 1812 at the age of 146" (~L22–24). This makes the Namdharis, in McLeod's words, "more overtly heretical than the Nirankaris" (~L19–20), since they reject the foundational Tat Khalsa doctrine that the personal Guru ended with the tenth Guru and authority passed to the Guru Granth Sahib and the Guru Panth.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

Unlike the Tat Khalsa majority, the Namdharis treat both the **[[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth / Guru Granth Sahib]]** and the **Dasam Granth** as fully binding canonical scripture. "Loyal Kukas obey these commandments, as indeed they obey all the words of scripture (both the Adi Granth and the Dasam Granth) that are amenable to belief or behaviour. These they obey literally" (~L15–18). This contrasts with the mainstream Khalsa position, which grants the Adi Granth singular scriptural authority while treating the Dasam Granth more cautiously.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

**Continuing Guru.** The central and distinguishing doctrine: the personal Guru line continues unbroken in the Namdhari leadership from Nanak through Gobind Singh (via Ajapal Singh) to Balak Singh and his successors. This is the "crucial difference between the Namdharis and the orthodox Khalsa" (~L25–26).

**Vegetarianism and teetotalism.** Ram Singh's Rahit-nama explicitly commands: "Never eat meat or drink alcoholic liquor" (~L12–13). This strictness influenced at least one later figure: McLeod notes that Bhai Randhir Singh "may possibly have derived this emphasis on vegetarianism from the example of the Namdharis" (~L466–467).

**Marriage reform.** Namdhari marriages are performed by the Anand marriage order using fire (havan) rather than the circumambulation of the Adi Granth alone. Mass wedding ceremonies — many couples married simultaneously — are a defining Namdhari practice. McLeod explains the dual purpose: "to give conspicuous publicity to the fact that marriages do not need to be expensive and also to guard against the provision of dowries" (~L1–4). The Namdhari Rahit-nama explicitly bans all dowries (~L2367–2368).

## Hermeneutical Method

The Namdhari hermeneutic is rigidly **literal and behavioural**: scripture is to be "obeyed literally" in all matters of belief and practice. There is no allegorical softening or selective application. This literalism extends to the Dasam Granth as well as the Adi Granth.

## Ram Singh's Rahit-nama

The Namdhari code of discipline (Rahit-nama) attributed to Ram Singh is "thoroughly practical," communicating "some very explicit instructions from Ram Singh to his followers" (~L5–6). McLeod quotes it directly:

> *Always wear the approved breeches (kachh). When taking off a kachh withdraw one leg and put it in the leg-hole of another pair before withdrawing the second leg. Never conceal an evil deed committed by another person. Do not sell or barter a daughter or a sister. Constantly repeat the Guru's name. Never eat meat or drink alcoholic liquor. Fear the Guru always.* (~L8–13)

## External Appearance

The Namdhari turban is distinctively white homespun cloth, tied horizontally across the forehead (~L928–929), contrasting with the inverted-V style of mainstream Khalsa turbans. Some punctilious non-Namdhari Sikhs also adopt the horizontal tie on the grounds that it follows older tradition (~L930–932).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

The Tat Khalsa ultimately accepted the Namdharis within the broad Panth despite their heterodox Guru doctrine. Principal Teja Singh concluded "that even if they were astray on one vital point they were at least potentially aligned with the Panth" (~L36–38). The historian Ganda Singh called the Ajapal Singh story "pure fiction of recent creation" but did not otherwise dispute the Namdhari claim to be Sikhs (~L40–43). "The battles over Nirankari and Namdhari membership were fought many decades ago and the issue of panthic membership has in their cases been conceded" (~L45–46).

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 2, Ch. 8)

Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2 frames the **Namdhari (Kuka)** movement as a
post-annexation revival with a proto-nationalist edge. He traces it from **Balak Singh** (1797–1862) of
Hazro to **Ram Singh** (1816–85), a Ramgarhia carpenter and former artilleryman of Bhaini, who
introduced the ecstatic worship from which the outsider nickname *Kuka* ("shrieker") derives, plus a
paramilitary organization and *hukumnamas* mandating non-cooperation with British institutions. Singh's
strongest claim is that **Ram Singh's 1860s *swadeshi* boycott** (of foreign goods, courts, schools, and
the post) "anticipated Gandhi by sixty years." He notes critically that Kuka violence was misdirected
onto Muslim butchers (cow protection) rather than the British. The **1872 Malerkotla affair** is recorded
in grim detail: 66 Kuka prisoners blown from cannon by the deputy commissioner Cowan (later dismissed)
and 16 more by Forsythe; Ram Singh was deported to Burma and died at Rangoon in 1885. ("Namdhari" is the
preferred self-designation; "Kuka" the outsider/worship nickname.)
