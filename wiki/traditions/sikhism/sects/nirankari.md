# Nirankaris (Asali / "true" Nirankaris)

A 19th-century **renewal movement** founded by **Baba Dayal (1783–1855)** in the Rawalpindi region,
who sought to reclaim Sikhs from the "military glories and economic opportunities" of [[ranjit-singh|
Ranjit Singh]]'s time and recall them to the practice of [[nam-simaran|nām simaraṇ]]. The name
*Nirankārī* derives from Baba Dayal's stress on the **formless** (*nirākār / niraṅkār*) quality of
[[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]] (McLeod ~L10762–10790). These are the **Asali ("true") Nirankaris**,
distinguished from the unrelated **Nakali ("spurious") Sant Nirankaris** with whom the Khalsa clashed
violently in the late 1970s–80s (the Sant Nirankari Mandal was permanently banished from the Panth by
an Akal Takhat *hukam-nāmā* after the Amritsar confrontation of 13 April 1978).

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Ch. 9.)*

## Identity and Origins

Confined to the Rawalpindi area, its followers were mainly Khatris and Aroras by caste. At Partition
(1947) they abandoned Rawalpindi and re-established at **Chandigarh** (McLeod ~L10768–10774). Baba
Dayal was himself a **Sahaj-dhari** Sikh, as were most followers; they were typically traders and
shopkeepers who pursued nām simaraṇ without renouncing their occupations.

## Doctrine and Hermeneutical Method

Strictly **Nanak-panthi**: liberation through the divine Name, interior worship, and the rejection of
external aids "as Guru Nanak had so clearly taught." Baba Dayal left a brief manual — confusingly
called a *hukam-nāmā* though modelled on a [[rahit-maryada|rahit-nāmā]] — whose essence is the
constant utterance *dhan dhan niraṅkār* ("Glory, glory be to Nirankar"). It upholds Nanak's teaching
"without mentioning the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (McLeod ~L10791–10801).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy

Their stress on nām simaraṇ and reverence for scripture do not make them heretics; their "most serious
fault in the eyes of the orthodox [[khalsa|Khalsa]]" is the acknowledgement of a **continuing line of
Gurus descending from Baba Dayal** (while not disputing the historic line from Nanak to Gobind Singh).
In the Singh Sabha controversies they aligned with the **Sanatan Sikhs**, yet aided the Tat Khalsa by
providing the model for the **Anand marriage ceremony** the reformers adopted; the reformer Jodh Singh
married his son to a daughter of the Nirankari Guru (McLeod ~L10836–10862).

## Khushwant Singh's Account (Vol. 2, Ch. 8)

Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2 corroborates the founder and dates above: **Dyal Das**
(d. 1855), a bullion merchant of Peshawar, preached the formless (*nirankar*) God, opposed idol worship
and Brahmanical ritual, and founded the Dayalsar temple near Rawalpindi, with succession through his
sons and grandsons. Singh judges the movement of "limited theological originality," significant chiefly
for **initiating Granth-based marriage rites** that resisted Hinduization — grouping it with the
[[namdhari|Namdhari]] and Radha Soami movements as revivals of "small impact on the Sikh masses" relative
to the later [[singh-sabha|Singh Sabha]]. (The violent 1978 clash with the unrelated **Sant Nirankaris**
that launched [[jarnail-singh-bhindranwale|Bhindranwale]] — see the disambiguation above — concerns the
Sant Nirankari Mission, not these Asali Nirankaris.)
