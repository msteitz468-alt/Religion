# Singh Sabha

The **Singh Sabha** was the late-nineteenth-century reform movement that, in Khushwant Singh's
account (*A History of the Sikhs* Vol. 2, Ch. 9), forged the modern definition of [[sikhs|Sikh]]
communal identity. It is recorded here as a sect/movement page because it set the hermeneutical and
doctrinal terms — distinct community, no hereditary gurus, scriptural worship purged of Hindu ritual —
that define mainstream Sikh orthodoxy to this day, and because it generated the **Tat Khalsa /
Sanatan Sikh** split. It is closely tied to the [[who-is-a-sikh|"who is a Sikh?"]] controversy.

## Identity and Origins

Khushwant Singh frames the Singh Sabha as a **reactive identity project** driven by an existential
fear of demographic absorption. After the British [[annexation-of-the-punjab|annexation]], he writes,
"time-servers returned to the Hindu fold," and genuine Sikh families "were now faced with the prospect
of being reabsorbed into Hinduism and ceasing to exist as a separate community." Three forces of
disintegration are named: **Christian missions** (the 1853 conversion of [[duleep-singh|Maharaja
Duleep Singh]] "a grievous shock to the Sikhs"), the **Arya Samaj**, and the "rationalism that came
with the introduction of scientific concepts."

The decisive provocation was the Arya Samaj. Swami Dayanand, in the Punjab from 1877, "denounced [Nanak]
as a *dambhī* (hypocrite)" and treated the Granth as secondary. Singh's causal formula: "The more the
Samajists claimed Sikhism to be a branch of Hinduism, the more the Sikhs insisted that they were a
distinct and separate community." The crystallizing text was **Kahan Singh Nabha's *Ham Hindu Nahin
Hain*** ("We are not Hindus").

## The Two Sabhas and the Tat Khalsa / Sanatan Split

- **Amritsar Singh Sabha** (founded c. 1873) — the conservative wing of "the rich, landed gentry and
  the orthodox," presided over by Thakar Singh Sandhawalia and including **Khem Singh Bedi**, a
  descendant of Nanak "wont to accept homage due to a guru." It sought revival of the Gurus' teaching,
  Punjabi religious literature, and — characteristically — "to cultivate loyalty to the crown."
- **Lahore Singh Sabha** (founded 1879) — "a group of educated and energetic men of the middle class"
  (Gurmukh Singh, Ditt Singh, Jawahar Singh Kapur), "radical and strongly opposed to the institution of
  'gurudom'."

Their 1883 merger failed. The **core clash** — which maps onto the later **Tat Khalsa** (reformist
"true Khalsa") versus **Sanatan Sikh** (traditionalist) division — was over caste: "the two groups
clashed on the right of untouchable Sikhs to worship in the gurdwaras; the conservatives sided with the
priests who allowed untouchables to enter only at specified hours without the right to make offerings."
The reformist Tat Khalsa agenda — rejecting hereditary gurus, removing Hindu idols from shrines (achieved
at the Golden Temple in 1905), and asserting a casteless scriptural Sikhism — became the dominant
modern orthodoxy.

## Institutions and Legacy

The two Sabhas reunited under the Arya Samaj threat to found **Khalsa College, Amritsar** (foundation
stone 1892), funded by a *dasvandh* appeal from the Golden Temple. The movement crystallized in 1902 in
the **Chief Khalsa Diwan**, pledged "to cultivate loyalty to the crown" and to secure Sikh
representation (its key leader the sugar magnate Sunder Singh Majithia). It produced a flowering of
Punjabi literature — Vir Singh's *Khalsa Samachar* and novels, Kahan Singh's *Mahankosh* encyclopaedia,
Gyan Singh's *Panth Prakash*, and M. A. Macauliffe's six-volume *The Sikh Religion* (1909). Sikh numbers
rose from 1.7 million (1881) to 3.1 million (1921), and the movement "retaliated by carrying
proselytizing activities into the Hindu camp."

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Successors

Singh notes the movement's loyalism made it a target of the next, more militant generation: "Although
the Singh Sabha movement petered out in the 1920s it left a legacy of a chronically defensive attitude
towards Hinduism." Its leaders "were loyalists who believed in doing no more than making representations
to officials or instituting suits" — a method that the [[akali-movement|Akali movement]] and the
Gurdwara Reform agitation explicitly rejected in favour of non-cooperation and passive resistance. The
Singh Sabha's redefinition of Sikh identity nonetheless remains the foundation of the modern
[[sikhism|mainstream tradition]].
