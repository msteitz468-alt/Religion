# Udasis

The earliest of the Sikh sects (McLeod: "unquestionably" the earliest), an **ascetic order**
claiming descent from **Siri Chand**, one of [[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]]'s two sons, and following the
path of renunciation (*udās*). Though they recognized the line of ten Gurus from Nanak to
[[guru-gobind-singh|Gobind Singh]], the Udasis attached far greater interest to the chain of
succession descending from Siri Chand down to the reigning **Mahant** ("Superior") of each branch
(McLeod ~L10660–10670).

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Ch. 9.)*

## Identity and Origins

An order of celibate ascetic yogis. Their austerities, celibacy, and refusal to keep the hair uncut
made them "very different from the [[khalsa|Khalsa]]," yet they maintained they were Sikhs (McLeod
~L10660–10664). Never uniform in organization or doctrine, by the end of Sikh rule (1849) they
numbered more than a dozen orders with over 250 *akhāṛās* (centres).

## Canon and Scripture

They revered the [[guru-granth-sahib|Adi Granth]] but imparted to its teachings "a distinctly Hindu
twist," writing commentaries on portions of scripture together with [[janam-sakhis|janam-sākhīs]] and
*gur-bilās* works (McLeod ~L10670–10674).

## Hermeneutical Method

Ascetic-Vedantic reading of the Adi Granth — the very "Hinduizing" tendency the
[[khalsa|Tat Khalsa]] later condemned.

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

Prior to the **Singh Sabha** period the Udasi way enjoyed considerable prestige and received large
land grants under the Sikh rulers; because they were not targeted by 18th-century persecution (as the
orthodox Khalsa were), many gurdwaras passed into their care. The eclipse of asceticism under the
**Tat Khalsa** reduced them to "the palest shadow" of their former wealth. During the **Gurdwara
Reform Movement** of the early 1920s the Udasi mahants were ejected from gurdwara control; the 1921
**Nankana Sahib massacre** of Akalis, caused by a mahant who declared himself an Udasi, "branded all
Udasis as the enemies of the true Khalsa" (McLeod ~L10686–10696). Many Sikhs today refuse to accept
them as co-religionists.
