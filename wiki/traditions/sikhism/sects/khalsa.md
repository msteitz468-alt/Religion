# The Khalsa

The order of initiated Sikhs founded by **[[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]]** on Baisakhi 1699,
when he instituted initiation by the double-edged sword (*khaṇḍe dī pāhul* / *amrit sanskār*), the
**[[five-ks|Five Ks]]**, and the Khalsa **[[rahit-maryada|Rahit]]** (code of conduct). The Khalsa is
the normative corporate form of the [[sikhs|Panth]] — *khālsā* ("pure / the Guru's own").

*(Scaffolded 2026-06-27 from [[mcleod-sikhism|McLeod]] Ch. 6.)*

## Identity and Origins

On **Baisakhi 1699** at Anandpur, [[guru-gobind-singh|Guru Gobind Singh]] summoned the Sikhs and
demanded heads; five who came forward became the **Pañj Piāre** ("Cherished Five"): Daya Singh, Dharam
Singh, Himmat Singh, Sahib Singh, Muhakam Singh. He initiated them by **khaṇḍe dī pāhul** — water
stirred in an iron bowl with a double-edged sword (*khaṇḍā*), sweetened with *paṭāshās* by Mata Jito —
then received initiation from the Five in turn. Men took the name **Singh** ("lion"), women **Kaur**
("princess") (agent 2 ~L692–776). McLeod notes the **date 1699 is "not definitively established" but
overwhelmingly accepted** by Sikhs today.

He gives **two reasons** for the founding: the traditional one — to make all Sikhs visibly
identifiable, unable to hide from danger, agents of [[akal-purakh|Akal Purakh]] against the Mughals;
and an administrative one — the Mughal term *khālsā* denoted lands under the emperor's *direct*
control, so the Guru's Khalsa = Sikhs under his direct supervision, the founding amounting to the
**abolition of the *masand* order** (agent 2 ~L793–851).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

"The Khalsa has never been identical with the wider Sikh [[sikhs|Panth]]." Brahman and Khatri Sikhs
largely declined initiation, leaving the Khalsa even more **Jat** in composition; **Sahaj-dhari**
Sikhs (who keep their hair cut and do not take the Rahit) stood apart. The modern taxonomy: **Amrit-
dhari** (formally initiated, ~15% of the Panth), **Kes-dhari** (keep the Rahit's principal precepts,
esp. uncut hair, but uninitiated, >70%), **Sahaj-dhari** (neither), and **Patit** (apostate). See
[[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]].

## Canon and Scripture

The Khalsa's scripture is the [[guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]]; its conduct is governed by the
[[rahit-maryada|Sikh Rahit Maryada]] (authoritative, not scripture). Portions of the
[[dasam-granth|Dasam Granth]] ([[jap-sahib|Jap Sahib]], Ten Savayyas, Benatī Chaupaī) are recited at
initiation and in the [[nit-nem|Nit-nem]].

## Doctrinal Distinctives — the Five Ks and the Rahit

The **[[five-ks|Five Ks]]** (*pañj kakār*: kes, kaṅghā, kaṛā, kachh, kirpān) and the Rahit's four
cardinal prohibitions (*kurahit*: cutting hair, smoking, eating *kuttha*/halal meat, extramarital sex)
define Khalsa life. Initiation is **[[amrit-sanskar|amrit sanskār]]**; infractions are adjudicated by
the Pañj Piāre with penance (*tanakhāh*) of service. The governing ethic is the **fight for justice**
(*dharam yudh*) — the [[miri-piri|mīrī-pīrī]] principle institutionalized. The historical *origin* of
the Five Ks is itself contested: McLeod dates their formal codification to the late-19th-century Tat
Khalsa, against the traditional view that Gobind Singh promulgated all five in 1699 (see
[[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]]).

## Hermeneutical Method

[[gurmat|Gurmat]] — the bāṇī read as the Guru's living voice; the discipline of [[nam-simaran|nām
simaraṇ]]; the Granth as oracle ([[hukam|hukam]]).

## Relationship to Orthodoxy and Other Sects

The **Tat Khalsa** of the Singh Sabha made the Khalsa normative — asserting that Sikhs are *not*
Hindus, that Khalsa membership is the objective of every Sikh, and that the Khalsa is the **Guru
Panth** — against the **Sanatan Sikhs**. Sibling sects diverge from this mainstream: the
[[nihang|Nihangs]] claim to be the truest Khalsa; the [[namdhari|Namdharis]] preach a "purified
Khalsa" under a living Guru-line; the [[nirankari|Nirankaris]], [[udasi|Udasis]], and [[nirmala|
Nirmalas]] sit further from Khalsa norms.

## Historical Development — the Misl Era (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 1)

Khushwant Singh's *History of the Sikhs* supplies the eighteenth-century political history of the Khalsa as a
corporate body. After [[banda-bahadur|Banda Bahadur]]'s execution (1716) left the Panth without a personal
leader, the Khalsa governed itself through the **Sarbat Khalsa** — the biennial general assembly at
[[amritsar|Amritsar]] whose resolutions carried the authority of a *gurmatā* ("decree of the Guru"). Its
fighting arm, the **Dal Khalsa** (1748), was organized into the twelve **[[misls|misls]]** under
[[jassa-singh-ahluwalia|Jassa Singh Ahluwalia]], who funded itself through the *rākhī* protection system and
weathered the Mughal persecutions and the Afghan invasions of [[ahmad-shah-abdali|Ahmad Shah Abdali]],
including the two [[ghallughara|Ghallugharas]]. This is the period in which "the rise of militant Sikhism
became the rise of Jat power in the Punjab," ending with the misls' absorption into [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit
Singh]]'s **Sarkar Khalsa**. See [[misls|the Misls]] for the institutions and the roster.
