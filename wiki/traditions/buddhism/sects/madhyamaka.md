# Madhyamaka

**Madhyamaka** ("the Middle Way [school]") is the philosophical school within [[mahayana|Mahayana]]
[[buddhism|Buddhism]] founded by **[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]]** (c. 150 CE) and built on his
**[[mulamadhyamakakarika|Mūlamadhyamakakārikā]]**. It is not a separate monastic community but a
*system* — a doctrine, a method, and a canon-emphasis — cutting across Mahayana and
[[vajrayana|Vajrayana]]; it is filed here as a philosophical school within the Buddhist parent.

## Identity and Origins

Madhyamaka systematizes the [[mahayana|Mahayana]] *Prajñāpāramitā* sūtras' bare proclamation that
"all things are empty." Where the early [[abhidharma|Abhidharma]] schools held that the person is a
conceptual fiction but the underlying **dharmas** are ultimately real (each with its own intrinsic
nature, [[svabhava|svabhāva]]), Madhyamaka extends emptiness to the dharmas themselves: **nothing
whatsoever has *svabhāva*.** The name marks the claim that this is the true "middle path" between
the extremes of **being** (eternalism — that there are ultimate reals) and **nonbeing**
(annihilationism / nihilism — that nothing exists at all).

## Relationship to the Parent Tradition

Madhyamaka understands itself not as innovation but as the correct reading of the Buddha's own
middle way and of dependent origination. It is, with **Yogācāra**, one of the two great Indian
Mahayana philosophical schools (the two were later harmonized in various syntheses); in Tibetan
scholasticism Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka is ranked the *definitive* highest view.

## Canon and Scripture (Canon Divergence)

No new scripture is required: Madhyamaka shares the Mahayana sūtra canon and elevates the
**Prajñāpāramitā** corpus (e.g. the [[heart-sutra|Heart Sutra]]) as expressing the *definitive*
([[neyartha-nitartha|nītārtha]]) meaning — emptiness — over teachings read as merely interpretable.
Its distinctive *added* texts are the **śāstras** of Nāgārjuna and his successors (the
[[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] above all), authoritative treatises rather than buddhavacana.

## Doctrinal Distinctives

Emptiness of *all* phenomena ([[sunyata|śūnyatā]]); the denial of [[svabhava|svabhāva]]; the
**[[two-truths|two truths]]** (conventional / ultimate) as the indispensable interpretive key; the
**emptiness of emptiness** (no reified Absolute); and — at least for the Prāsaṅgika branch — the
refusal to assert any positive thesis of one's own.

## Hermeneutical Method (mandatory)

The signature method is **prasaṅga** — purely *reductio ad absurdum*: exposing the unwanted
consequences of an opponent's position rather than advancing counter-theses. This is read through
the [[two-truths|two truths]] (ultimate claims are always cashed out conventionally) and the
[[neyartha-nitartha|neyārtha/nītārtha]] ranking (emptiness = the definitive sense).

## Subdivisions and Relationship to Other Schools

The four classical commentaries on the [[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] seeded a methodological split:

- **Svātantrika** (after **Bhāviveka**) — holds that the Mādhyamika may use independent
  (*svatantra*) positive syllogisms.
- **Prāsaṅgika** (after **Candrakīrti**, *Prasannapadā*) — holds that only *prasaṅga* (consequence)
  arguments are legitimate; the Mādhyamika asserts nothing. This becomes the Tibetan norm.

Madhyamaka debated **[[yogacara|Yogācāra]]** ("mind-only") over whether consciousness is ultimately
real: Yogācāra arose partly *because* its founders judged the Mādhyamika emptiness to court
**nihilism** (if literally everything is a conceptual construct, there is nothing left to construct
*out of*), and answered with a real, non-dual mental substratum (the "three natures"). Madhyamaka
returns the charge, reading Yogācāra's "Mind" as a covert [[svabhava|svabhāva]]. Madhyamaka was
likewise charged by [[abhidharma|Ābhidharmika]] and non-Buddhist (Nyāya) opponents with nihilism — a
charge Nāgārjuna answers in MMK 24 and the *Vigrahavyāvartanī*. Per the [Contradiction Protocol]
these mutual charges are recorded, not adjudicated.

## Historical Development and Influence

From Nāgārjuna and Āryadeva through Bhāviveka and Candrakīrti in India — and the 8th-c. poet
**[[shantideva|Śāntideva]]**, whose [[bodhicaryavatara|Bodhicaryāvatāra]] (ch. 9) became the standard
Tibetan textbook of the Prāsaṅgika view; the **Sanlun** ("Three Treatise") school in China; and the
dominant scholastic philosophy of Tibet. Modern philosophy reads Madhyamaka comparatively (Pyrrhonian
skepticism, Wittgenstein, deconstruction).
