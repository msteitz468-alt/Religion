# The Roman Imperial Cult

> **Sect page.** The worship of the emperors — and the flashpoint of early-Christian martyrdom. *(Stub.)*

## Identity and Origins

The cult of the **deified emperors** (the *divi*) and of the living emperor's **genius/numen** (divine
spirit). Beginning with the **deification of Julius Caesar** (42 BCE) and Augustus's careful cultivation of
it, the imperial cult bound the diverse empire together through **loyalty expressed as worship** —
provincial temples, priesthoods, sacrifices, and festivals to Rome and the emperor.

## Apotheosis

A deceased emperor judged worthy was **declared a god** (*divus*) by the Senate (*apotheosis*), his soul
seen ascending at the funeral pyre — an institution rooted in the older [[greek-hero-cult|hero cult]] and
the deification of [[heracles|Heracles]]. Living emperors were generally honored through their **genius**
rather than worshipped as gods directly (in the West), more openly divinized in the East.

## Significance — the Martyr Test

The imperial cult is the religious context of **Christian martyrdom**: refusal to offer a pinch of incense
to the emperor's image was treated as **treason**, and the demand "sacrifice to the emperor" is the test in
the martyr accounts (e.g. [[polycarp|Polycarp of Smyrna]]; cf. the apologist [[justin-martyr|Justin]] and
[[hurtado-destroyer-of-the-gods|Hurtado]]). Christianity's exclusive monotheism made the cult impossible to
honor — the structural collision that produced the persecutions. Recorded without adjudication.
