# Rabbinic Judaism

**Rabbinic Judaism** is the form of Judaism that emerged from the Pharisaic tradition after the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE) and became the dominant — effectively the sole surviving — form of normative Judaism from the 2nd century CE onward. Its defining features are: the authority of the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] alongside the Written Torah; the centrality of the rabbi (scholar-sage) as religious authority; the [[mishna|Mishna]] and [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] as foundational texts; and the replacement of Temple sacrifice by prayer, Torah study, and ethical practice.

## Origins and Historical Context

Before 70 CE, multiple Jewish sects competed for authority:
- **Pharisees**: Accepted the Oral Torah; developed the synagogue and prayer tradition; emphasized laypeople's access to Torah study
- **Sadducees**: Priestly aristocracy; accepted only the Written Torah; centered on Temple sacrifice
- **Essenes**: Withdrew from mainstream Jewish society; their writings survive in the Dead Sea Scrolls
- **Zealots and sicarii**: Political-military resistance movements

The destruction of the Temple by Rome in 70 CE eliminated the Sadducean priestly establishment and the Temple cult, leaving the Pharisaic tradition as the surviving organizational and intellectual center. The rabbis who gathered at Yavneh (Jamnia) under [[rabbi-judah-ha-nasi|Yohanan ben Zakkai]] restructured Jewish life without the Temple.

## Foundational Institutions

**The Sanhedrin** and its successor institutions in Palestine and Babylonia transmitted authority through the chain of teacher-student relationships (*shalshelet ha-kabbalah*).

**The yeshiva** (academy) replaced the Temple as the center of Jewish communal life. Two major Babylonian yeshivot — **Sura** (founded by Rav, c. 220 CE) and **Pumbeditha** (founded by Shmuel, c. 220 CE) — dominated the Geonic period (c. 600–1040 CE). Their heads, the **Geonim**, served as supreme halakhic authorities for world Jewry.

**The synagogue** became the primary institution of Jewish communal prayer and study, substituting liturgical service (including *Shacharit*, *Mincha*, and *Maariv* — corresponding to the Temple's daily sacrifices) for the defunct sacrificial cult.

## Hermeneutical Principles

Rabbinic Judaism operates through the following hermeneutical principles:

- **Oral Torah as co-authoritative**: The Oral Torah is not subordinate to the Written Torah but its necessary complement — the Written Torah is unintelligible without it
- **Dispute as legitimate**: Multiple valid positions can coexist ("these and these are the words of the living God"); the minority opinion is preserved
- **Inductive reasoning**: Legal argument proceeds from specific cases, not general principles
- **The [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot]]**: Formal rules for deriving law from Scripture
- **Fences around the Torah** (*seyag la-Torah*): Rabbinic legislation (*gezerot* and *takanot*) creates additional layers of protection around biblical commands

## Relationship to Christianity

Rabbinic Judaism and early Christianity developed in parallel from similar Second Temple Jewish roots. Both traditions claim the Hebrew Bible as sacred; both trace authority through chains of tradition (apostolic succession vs. rabbinic *shalshelet*); both replaced Temple sacrifice with alternative cult (eucharist/prayer). The parting of the ways between the two movements was a complex, multi-century process, not a clean break at 70 CE. [[darrell-bock|Bock's]] Acts commentary reflects on this from the Christian side: Acts narrates precisely the moment when the Jesus-movement began separating institutionally from the synagogue.
