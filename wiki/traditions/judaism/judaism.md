# Judaism

Top-level tradition page. **Judaism** is the religion and covenantal way of life of the
Jewish people, grounded in the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) and developed through a long chain
of interpretive tradition. This page is the parent node for the Jewish sub-traditions and
sects filed under [[judaism]] in `traditions/judaism/sects/`.

**Chronology:** see the [[judaism-timeline|Timeline of Judaism]] (patriarchs → modern
denominations), the detailed [[talmudic-period|Talmudic Period]] timeline, and the
[[comparative-timeline|Comparative Timeline of World Religions]].

## Shared / Mainstream Canon

The core scriptural canon is the **Tanakh** — Torah (Pentateuch), Nevi'im (Prophets), and
Ketuvim (Writings), the 24 books fixed by the late Second Temple / early rabbinic period.
The boundaries of the Writings were the last to settle; see [[esther-canonicity|the Esther
canonicity dispute]]. Canonical authority is keyed to community: most of the Tanakh is
`canonical_for: [judaism]` broadly, while the **Oral Torah** corpus ([[mishna|Mishna]],
[[talmud-bavli|Talmud]]) is canonical specifically for [[rabbinic-judaism|Rabbinic
Judaism]] and **rejected** by [[karaism|Karaism]] — a canon split now tracked structurally
via each text's `canon_scope`.

## Native Hermeneutical Frameworks

Judaism reads Scripture through the fourfold [[pardes|PaRDeS]] system — **Peshat** (plain),
**Derash** (midrashic), **Remez** (allegorical), and **Sod** (mystical) — together with the
formal [[thirteen-middot|Thirteen Middot]] for deriving law. Different sects privilege
different levels: Rabbinic Judaism centers peshat and derash; [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] claims
the sod level as the inner life of revelation.

Alongside these, a **philosophical-rationalist** register matured in the medieval Judeo-Arabic
world, exemplified by [[maimonides|Maimonides]]' [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the
Perplexed*]]: Scripture's anthropomorphic language is systematically allegorized (the *Remez*/
[[allegorical-reading|allegorical]] level put to philosophical use under the rule "the Law speaks
in the language of man"), Aristotelian demonstration is admitted as a neutral instrument, and the
deepest matters (*Maaseh Bereshit*, *[[maaseh-merkabah|Maaseh Merkabah]]*) are taught only
esoterically. Maimonides notes that such philosophical learning, "once cultivated by our
forefathers," had been "neglected" under persecution (Guide I:71). This stream provoked the
medieval **Maimonidean controversy** within Rabbinic Judaism.

## Map of Major Sects / Sub-traditions

- [[rabbinic-judaism|Rabbinic Judaism]] — the normative stream from the Pharisaic tradition
  after 70 CE; Oral Torah as co-authoritative; the Talmud as foundational.
- [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] — the mystical movement (12th c.–present); Ein Sof and the ten
  sefirot; the [[zohar|Zohar]] as canonical mystical scripture; theurgical reading of the
  commandments.
- [[karaism|Karaism]] — the scripturalist sect (8th c.–present) that rejects the Oral
  Torah and the rabbinic canon, reading the Tanakh by reason and direct exegesis.
- [[sadducees|Sadducees]] — the priestly-aristocratic Second Temple party (2nd c. BCE–70 CE);
  accepted the Written Torah and rejected the Oral Torah; forerunner of the Karaite position.
- [[essenism|Essenism (the Essenes)]] — the separatist Second Temple sect commonly identified with Qumran and
  the Dead Sea Scrolls; solar calendar, dualism, and an expansive canon (Jubilees, 1 Enoch).

The [[pharisees|Pharisees]] remain a **group** page (a party, not treated here as a distinct
sub-tradition); the Sadducees and Essenes now have sect pages because each carried a distinct
canon and halacha.

**Related parallel tradition:** [[samaritanism|Samaritanism]] is filed as its own top-level
tradition, not a sect of Judaism — the two share the Torah but diverge on canon (Samaritan
Pentateuch only) and sanctuary ([[mount-gerizim|Gerizim]] vs. Jerusalem). See
[[gerizim-vs-jerusalem|Gerizim vs. Jerusalem]].
