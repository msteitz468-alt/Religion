This timeline covers the major periods and figures in the development of the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]], from the Second Temple period through the end of the Geonic era.

## The Zugoth (Pairs), c. 150–10 BCE

Five generations of paired leading scholars transmitted and debated the Oral Torah in the Second Temple period:

| Pair | Dates (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Yose ben Joezer and Yose ben Yohanan | c. 150 BCE |
| Joshua ben Perahya and Nittai of Arbela | c. 100 BCE |
| Judah ben Tabbai and Simeon ben Shetah | c. 80 BCE |
| Shemaya and Avtalyon | c. 50 BCE |
| **Hillel and Shammai** | c. 30 BCE–10 CE |

**Hillel and Shammai** are the culminating pair, whose debates between their respective academies — **Beth Hillel** and **Beth Shammai** — permeate the Mishna. A heavenly voice (*bat kol*) declared that Halacha follows Beth Hillel (except in specific cases where Beth Shammai is stricter — Talmud Eruvin 13b).

## The Tannaim, c. 10–200 CE

The **Tannaim** ("teachers") are the sages whose opinions are quoted in the Mishna. The period spans five generations:

| Generation | Major Figures |
|---|---|
| 1st | Rabban Gamliel I, Yohanan ben Zakkai |
| 2nd | Rabbi Eliezer, Rabbi Joshua, Rabbi Akiva, Rabbi Ishmael |
| 3rd | Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Judah (bar Ilai), Rabbi Simeon bar Yohai |
| 4th | Rabbi Simeon ben Gamliel II |
| 5th | **Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi** (c. 135–217 CE) — compiled the Mishna |

After the Temple's destruction (70 CE), the rabbis of **Yavneh** (Jamnia) under Yohanan ben Zakkai restructured Judaism without the Temple. Rabbi Akiva (c. 50–135 CE) systematized the Oral Torah and is credited with the theological framework underlying the Mishna; his student Rabbi Meir was the major transmitter of Mishnaic tradition.

## The Amoraim, c. 200–500 CE

The **Amoraim** ("expounders") are the sages of the Gemara — they comment on, dispute, and develop the Mishna. Two parallel traditions developed:

**Babylonian Amoraim** (Bavli):
- **Rav** and **Shmuel** (1st generation, c. 220): Founded the yeshivot of Sura and Nehardea/Pumbeditha
- **Rav Judah** (2nd): Student of Rav; established Pumbeditha yeshiva
- **Rabbah and Rav Joseph** (3rd): Major Babylonian disputants
- **Abbaye and Rava** (4th, c. 280–350): Most famous pair; "Abbaye and Rava disputes" are among the Talmud's most celebrated

**Palestinian Amoraim** (Yerushalmi):
- Rabbi Yohanan (1st/2nd): Dominant figure of Palestinian Gemara
- Rabbi Abbahu, Rabbi Ami, Rabbi Assi (3rd–4th)

## The Saboraim, c. 500–600 CE

The **Saboraim** ("reasoners") are the anonymous editors who sealed and closed the Babylonian Talmud, adding explanatory comments and organizing the final text. Their activity ended the Amoraic period and established the Talmud's canonical form.

## The Geonim, c. 600–1040 CE

The **Geonim** ("excellencies") were the heads of the Babylonian yeshivot — Sura and Pumbeditha — who served as supreme halakhic authorities for world Jewry through their responsa (*Teshuvot*) literature.

Key figures:
- **Yehudai Gaon** (c. 760): First Gaon to write a halakhic compendium (*Halachot Pesukot*)
- **Saadia Gaon** (882–942): Philosopher, grammarian, translator; *Sefer ha-Emunot ve-ha-De'ot* (Book of Beliefs and Opinions)
- **Sherira Gaon** (906–1006): Wrote the *Iggeret Sherira Gaon*, the foundational historical epistle on Oral Torah transmission
- **[[hai-gaon|Hai Gaon]]** (939–1039): Last and greatest Gaon; unique synthetic legal method; died at 99

## End of the Geonic Period and Dispersion

With **Hai Gaon's death** (1039), the Babylonian center ceased to function as the world's supreme Jewish authority. Centers of learning had already developed in:
- **North Africa** (Kairawan): Chananel ben Hoshiel, Nissim ben Jacob
- **Spain** (Córdoba): Hasdai ibn Shaprut's patronage; Menachem ben Saruk, Dunash ibn Labrat
- **France and Germany** (Rhineland): Rabbenu Gershom ("Light of the Diaspora," c. 960–1028); then [[rashi|Rashi]] (1040–1105)

The codification chain that follows — Alfasi → [[maimonides|Maimonides]] → Jacob ben Asher → [[joseph-caro|Joseph Caro]] — represents the second great creative synthesis of the post-Geonic period.
