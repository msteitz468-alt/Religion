# Baghdad

*Populated from [[smith-concise-encyclopedia-bahai-faith|Smith, Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000)]]. All claims are from lines 5535–5640 unless noted.*

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Baghdad was the capital both of the Abbasid empire and caliphate and of modern Iraq. Effectively autonomous in the 18th century, it was restored to direct Ottoman control in 1831 (lines 5535–5540).

## Bahá'í Significance

As Ottoman provincial capital, Baghdad was: (1) the site of the trial of Mullá 'Alí Bastámí (1845); (2) the place of [[tahirih|Táhirih's]] house arrest (1847); and (3) [[bahaullah|Bahá'u'lláh's]] place of exile from 8 April 1853 until 3 May 1863, apart from a month spent in the nearby Shi'i shrine city of Kázimayn after his initial arrival, and his two-year retreat in Kurdistan (10 April 1854–19 March 1856) (lines 5541–5550).

From 1856 Bahá'u'lláh increasingly became the predominant figure in the Babi movement, many Babis from Iran visiting his house in Baghdad. He also gained the respect of many local people and the provincial governor, and wrote a number of his most influential writings there (lines 5550–5558). His initial declaration of mission was made in the nearby Garden of [[ridvan|Ridván]] immediately prior to his departure for Istanbul (lines 5558–5561).

Bahá'u'lláh called Baghdad the "City of God" and designated it a center of pilgrimage (*GPB* 110). Shoghi Effendi designated it the "third holiest city" in the Bahá'í world and wished to have the 1963 Bahá'í world congress there (lines 5562–5568).

### The House of Bahá'u'lláh in Baghdad

The house — referred to as "The Most Great House" (*Bayt-i-A'zam*) of God — is located in the Karkh district in the west of the city: a large but simple mud brick structure of two to three storeys surrounding a central courtyard. At first rented by Mírzá Músá whilst Bahá'u'lláh was in Kurdistan, Bahá'u'lláh lived there from his return (19 March 1856) until his departure for the Ridván garden (22 April 1863), at some point purchasing it (lines 5572–5585). Here he both wrote most of his early writings and received the increasing throng of visitors who recognized his role as the premier Babi leader (lines 5585–5588). He later named it a place of Bahá'í pilgrimage — the "Throne of His Glory" and the "Lamp of Salvation between earth and heaven" — with specific rites of pilgrimage (lines 5589–5593).

**Prophetic statement and subsequent history:** Bahá'u'lláh prophesied that the house would later be "so abased by the infidels as to cause tears to flow" but that God would eventually exalt it (lines 5594–5600). After Bahá'u'lláh's departure the house fell into disrepair. At the time of the British Mandate over Iraq (1920) 'Abdu'l-Bahá authorized restoration work, but this attracted hostile Shi'i attention; after the death of the old custodian they sued for possession (January 1921). A lengthy legal struggle took the case through Iraqi courts and then — through the work of Mountfort Mills — to the Permanent Mandates Commission and Council of the League of Nations. The Iraqi authorities first took possession (February 1922) and subsequently handed it over to the Shi'is (November 1925), who have remained in possession since; it is now used to commemorate the sufferings and martyrdom of the Imám Husayn. The League authorities generated enormous international publicity and sympathy for the Bahá'ís (lines 5601–5637).
