# Laṅkā

*Shell page — to be populated from William Buck Ramayana ingest.*

**Laṅkā** is the island capital of the Rākṣasa king [[ravana|Rāvaṇa]] in the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]] — a legendary city of great opulence, built by the divine architect Viśvakarmā for the god of wealth Kubera, then seized by Rāvaṇa. It is where [[sita|Sītā]] is held captive after her abduction. The war of the Rāmāyaṇa — Rāma's campaign to rescue Sītā — culminates in the siege and conquest of Laṅkā. The monkey army builds a causeway across the ocean to reach it. After Rāvaṇa's death, Rāma installs Rāvaṇa's righteous brother Vibhīṣaṇa as king.

**Identification:** The Rāmāyaṇa describes Laṅkā as an island south of India, and tradition identifies it with modern Sri Lanka (the tradition referred to in van Nooten's introduction: "the remains of [Rāma's causeway] are still visible — or so tradition goes"). The chain of limestone shoals between India and Sri Lanka (Adam's Bridge / Rāma's Bridge / *Rāma Setu*) is associated with this tradition.

## Dandaka as Frontier, Lanka as Center (Lines 5731–8500)

The sage Agastya describes Dandaka Forest as "the frontier of the Rakshasa kingdom on Earth" and Lanka as its heart: "We are far from Lanka, but the edge of Dandaka is the frontier of the Rakshasa kingdom on Earth. For the most part Dandaka is a huge wasteland. It knows no master but Ravana" (5877–5881). Fourteen thousand Rakshasa veterans garrison Dandaka from a stone-walled army station, commanded by general Khara (5893–5895). After Rāma kills Khara's entire force, the ground along the flight-paths between Laṅkā and Dandaka is described as "ruined" by Rakshasa overflights — empty houses sheltering jackals, no deer, only wild dogs (7330–7335). Lanka is approached from the north along this corridor.

### The Ocean Crossing

Lanka is separated from the mainland by a vast ocean. Rāvaṇa flies north "across the vast ocean and then across our continent" to visit Maricha (6588–6591); after the abduction he flees back south "very fast, as though driven through the sky before an evil wind" (7015–7016). Surpanakha travels from Dandaka to Lanka by flying through solid stone "right under the southern ocean" (6502–6504) — a Rakshasa power of underground travel. Later Hanumān tells Rāma: "Lanka is hard to reach and far away" (8383), while describing having once seen the Earth from above as "a wheel, and her cities were like small golden coins and her rivers were crossing like threads" (8388–8392).

### Ravana's Court and Palace

Rāvaṇa holds court on a throne in his palace in Lanka's city (6505–6508). He has Rakshasa physicians on call who can restore lost ears by snapping their fingers (6515–6516). He descends from his throne to sit beside Surpanakha (6518–6519). This is a functioning royal court. The palace has inner rooms to which Rāvaṇa retires alone after dismissing court (6583), stables where he keeps the golden chariot pulled by asses with fiends' faces (6587–6589), and rooms with unbreakable mirrors (6517).

### The Asoka Grove: Sita's Captivity

After the abduction, Rāvaṇa brings Sītā to "the back of his palace in the city of Lanka" (7058). When she refuses to enter his quarters, he orders her imprisoned in the **grove of tall Asoka trees behind his room**: "Take her and imprison her in the grove of tall Asoka trees back behind my room and guard her day and night" (7079–7081). The Rakshasi guards are instructed to use "hard words and soft, threats and gifts and temptations" to break Sītā's will (7085–7087). The grove is called **"scarlet"** when Indra visits it: "Indra entered the scarlet Asoka grove and touched Sita lightly so she awoke" (7101).

Lord Brahma commands Indra to protect Sītā: "Sita must not die or end her life in Lanka" (7092). Indra descends with the Goddess Sleep to Lanka at night (7095–7099): "Sleep spread out her arms over Lanka. For the good of the gods. For the downfall of the Night Demons." The guards fall asleep; Indra enters and sustains Sītā for a year with heavenly food (7095–7109). Laṅkā's inhabitants are framed here explicitly as "Night Demons" — beings whose power is nocturnal and whose undoing comes during their sleep.

### The Natural World's Response to the Abduction

As Rāvaṇa carries Sītā south over hills, rivers, and lakes: hills "recited charms aloud with the sounds of their falling waters . . . in anger they yawned, opening their valleys to the sky. Lakes looked up and whirled their waters . . . The waterfalls wept" (7021–7026). Rāvaṇa's shadow causes "lions and tigers to run after it, and claw at the shadow to kill it" (7027–7028). From above, Sītā sees "two monkeys standing on a hill by a lake watching them, shading their eyes, one gold monkey and one white one" (7032–7034) — Sugrīva and Hanumān, on Rishyamuka Hill — and drops her ornaments toward them.

### Nalakubara's Curse and Lanka's Internal Constraint

The critical theological fact about Lanka during Sītā's captivity: Rāvaṇa cannot force her. Indra tells Sītā: "Don't fear Ravana. He cannot force you, for he is under a strong curse, and he'll die if he does" (7110–7111). The curse, laid by Nalakubara, son of the Treasure King Vaishravana: "Ravana, when you next attack a woman who won't have you, your ten heads will burst!" (7206–7207). Lanka is therefore a prison with a limit built into its jailer — Rāvaṇa's power is absolute over most things but not over this.

### The Southern Search Party

When Sugrīva dispatches the four search parties, the southern party under Hanumān, Angada, and Jambavan is sent specifically toward Lanka (8366–8382): "we saw Ravana fly South. Lanka is South." After half a month's travel south from Kishkindhya, through a dead forest and into a mysterious underground cavern, the other three search parties return empty-handed. Sugrīva's final assessment: "Now it is only Hanuman the Son of the Wind who will succeed in finding Sita . . . but the time is past and where is he?" (8496–8500).

## The Monkey Causeway (Rāma's Bridge)

The construction of the causeway across the ocean is referenced retrospectively in Kumbhakarna's rebuke to Rāvaṇa: "they've built a bridge while you sat around saying it couldn't be done!" (ll. 11680–11681). The bridge-builder Nala is mentioned as one of the commanders killed in Indrajit's first invisible assault: "An iron hook dashed in the chest of Nala the bridge-builder; he fell coughing and choking, and died strangled and frightened" (ll. 11975–11982). Suka the minister, carrying Rāvaṇa's final letter, waits all night at "the place where Rāma's bridge touches our shore" (ll. 12829–12831). The chain of shoals between India and Sri Lanka — Rāma Setu / Adam's Bridge — is the traditional identification of this causeway.

## The Siege and the Battle

Laṅkā is besieged by Rāma's army of monkeys and bears encamped on the slopes of Mount Trikuta. The dead and wounded from the battlefield are carried up the hillside of Trikuta into the forests (l. 11519). Rāvaṇa spirits the demon dead away "into the sea to conceal his losses" — the sea-tides run red with demon blood by the end of the war (ll. 11524–11526, ll. 12729–12731).

The city has multiple gates: Rāvaṇa's armies march out through the western gate (ll. 12616, 12630); Rāvaṇa himself in his final sortie exits through the fifth gate — "the Gate of Illusion" — appearing suddenly out of the air on the western battlefield (ll. 13095–13100). Demons line the streets during Kumbhakarna's awakening: "Rakshasas lined the streets to the royal palace and called up to Kumbhakarna — Don't step on these fragile temples. Houses are there, here stand soldiers" (ll. 11591–11593).

The city's house-gods follow Rāvaṇa's armies out and do not return: "all unnoticed in the havoc and ruin of war the many house-gods of Lanka followed Ravana's armies out of their city, now homeless, looking afraid and sad to leave, and not knowing where they would live now" (ll. 12662–12665).

## Rāvaṇa's Final Night and the City at Dawn

On the night before his last battle, Rāvaṇa holds council in "the long indoor altar of Earth where the grass had long grown green from the lights of the glorious lamps, and from the brilliance of the weapons hung round on the walls, and where two holy fires still burned at each end for the blessing and good fortune of the Night Demons" (ll. 12744–12748). The smell of "fine sandalwood and incense . . . mingled with patchouli perfume and the flower scent of colorful war-garlands" (ll. 12757–12759). After the council, Rāvaṇa dances on a watchtower above the palace, calling down the North Wind which blows out every fire in the universe (ll. 12858–12918). "Stunning thunder and noise poured out of Lanka" (l. 12883); "the great gold walls rocked and trembled" (l. 12897).

After Rāvaṇa's death at dawn, the Rākṣasas "turned away in fear from the walls and hid in Lanka" (ll. 13406–13407). Suka the minister, walking back through the city at sunrise, finds it transformed: "she was golden in the morning when he stepped on the ground. The sunshine, coppery and yellow and red and white, touched her like melted metals from a mountain. It was still, and silent" (ll. 13423–13425). Suka's final tribute: "the like of this citadel and city of Beautiful Lanka as she was during your long reign was never seen before, nor will she ever be seen anywhere again" (ll. 12833–12836).

## Theological Significance

In Buck's retelling Laṅkā is consistently called "Beautiful Lanka of the Waves" — a city of genuine magnificence that is also the site of cosmic wrongdoing. The contrast between physical splendor and moral corruption structures the narrative. The departure of the house-gods signals divine abandonment before the military defeat, framing Laṅkā's fall as theologically determined before it is militarily accomplished. Rāma's bridge and the siege are the material instruments of cosmic *dharma*-restoration (as Indrajit himself acknowledges: "His army and his weapons are but the material instruments of Time" — l. 12271).
