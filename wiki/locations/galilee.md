# Galilee

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Galilee is the northern region in which, according to [[matthew|the Gospel of Matthew]], Jesus'
public ministry was almost entirely set until his final journey south. In the reading of
[[rt-france|R. T. France]] (*The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT, 2007), the recognition that Galilee
was "a distinct province with a history, political status, and culture which set it decisively
apart from the southern province of Judea" is one of the significant recent gains of Matthean
scholarship — a distinctness France dates back to the death of Solomon.

France attacks as "a gross distortion of the historical and cultural reality" the common
assumption that "the Jews" of the period were an undifferentiated community united only in
resentment of Rome. He qualifies the divide in one direction only: all Jews, north and south,
felt a natural allegiance to [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] "as the focus of the worship of the God of
Israel."

France sets out the differentiation along several axes:

- **Racial.** The former Northern Kingdom had a more mixed population since the eighth-century
  Assyrian conquest. More conservative Jewish areas "(like [[nazareth|Nazareth]] and
  [[capernaum|Capernaum]]) stood in close proximity to largely pagan cities" — chiefly the
  Hellenistic centres Tiberias and Sepphoris.
- **Geographical.** "Galilee was separated from Judea by the non-Jewish territory of Samaria,"
  and from Perea in the southeast by the Hellenistic settlements of the Decapolis.
- **Political.** Galilee had been administered separately from Judea for almost all its history
  since the tenth century BC, apart from the Maccabean reunification. In Jesus' time it lay
  "under a (supposedly) native Herodian prince" — [[herod-antipas|Herod Antipas]] — "while Judea
  and Samaria had since A.D. 6 been under the direct rule of a Roman prefect."
- **Economic.** Galilee had better agricultural and fishing resources than mountainous Judea,
  "making the wealth of some Galileans the envy of their southern neighbors."
- **Cultural.** "Culturally Judeans despised their northern neighbors as country cousins,"
  their supposed lack of Jewish sophistication compounded by greater openness to Hellenistic
  influence.
- **Linguistic.** Galileans spoke a distinctive Aramaic "whose slovenly consonants (they dropped
  their aitches!) were the butt of Judean humor."
- **Religious.** "The Judean opinion was that Galileans were lax in their observance of proper
  ritual," exacerbated by distance from the temple and from the Jerusalem-centred theological
  leadership.

France's conclusion is that an impeccably Jewish Galilean standing in first-century Jerusalem
"was as much a foreigner as an Irishman in London or a Texan in New York," his accent marking
him out at once.

On the region's earlier history France reports that by the Maccabean period Galilee had "become
so paganized that its remaining Jewish population was evacuated to Judea" (1 Macc 5:14-23);
Hasmonean incorporation restored a substantial Jewish population, especially in Lower Galilee,
but the population as a whole remained mixed. "By NT times southern Jews were suspicious of
Galilee's mixed population."

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

France treats Matt 4:12–16:20 as the "Galilee" division of the gospel, occupying nearly half the
book and reaching its climax near Caesarea Philippi; only from 16:21 does Jesus set off for
Judea. He contrasts the two halves formally: "the Galilean section of the gospel has no clearly
defined framework of time and movement" — an undated anthology — while 16:21–28:15 is cast as a
single north-to-south journey culminating in one week in Jerusalem.

Key Matthean loci in France's reading:

- **2:22-23** — the move to Galilee, which France reads as directed "not simply by prudence but
  also by divine guidance," ensuring that the Bethlehem-born Davidic Messiah begins his career
  as "Jesus of (Galilean) Nazareth."
- **4:12-16** — the relocation to [[capernaum|Capernaum]] and the Isaiah [[formula-quotations|formula
  quotation]] identifying "Galilee of the nations" as the place where the true light shines.
- **4:23-25** — the summary of Galilean enthusiasm, which France argues Matthew places at the
  outset so that readers "keep in mind this overall impression of general enthusiasm" against
  the later opposition of chs. 11–16.
- **11:20-24** — the woes on the Galilean towns (see below).
- **13:54-58** — the rejection at [[nazareth|Nazareth]].
- **21:10-11** — the entry into Jerusalem, where France notes only Matthew records the city's
  reaction alongside the accompanying crowds, so that "the two rival 'teams' of Galileans and
  Judeans are seen as starkly opposed."
- **26:69** — [[peter-apostle|Peter]], "as distinctive a northerner as his master," unmasked in
  the high priest's courtyard as a companion of "Jesus the Galilean."
- **28:16-20** — the return to Galilee, which France identifies as the one major Matthean
  departure from Mark's geographical outline, relaunching the messianic mission where it began.

France reads "the whole of Galilee" (4:23) as generalizing, observing that "we have no record of
Jesus ever visiting the two Hellenistic cities of Sepphoris and Tiberias"; his visits were
"primarily to the more traditionally Jewish parts of the province." He also notes that in
first-century Galilee "synagogue" may denote village assemblies rather than buildings erected
for worship as such.

**The woes on the Galilean towns (11:20-24).** France registers as surprising "the stark
accusation that the communities as a whole have failed to repent," given Matthew's earlier
accounts of widespread Galilean enthusiasm. He canvasses two explanations — a distinction
between superficial enthusiasm and life-changing repentance, or the woes as broad
generalizations about townspeople (especially leaders) to which the crowds and disciples are
exceptions — and prefers the second, concluding that "we should probably read Matthew's earlier
positive summaries of the response to Jesus as representing less than the majority of ordinary
Galileans." He frames the woes as the end of the honeymoon: "Even in Galilee, including Jesus'
'own' town of Capernaum, the honeymoon period is apparently over." France notes that Matthew's
*polis* covers "anything from the insignificant village of Nazareth (2:23) to the capital city of
Jerusalem"; the towns named here are middling provincial centres north of the lake, not rivals to
Sepphoris and Tiberias. At 13:54-58 France places Nazareth "alongside Chorazin, Bethsaida, and
Capernaum (11:20-24) in the inglorious roll of communities which failed to repent."

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

The France commentary as digested supplies little archaeological material on Galilee as a
region, and this page does not supplement it. What it does report:

- France states that "archeology has shown both [Chorazin and Bethsaida] to have been
  substantial communities comparable in size and importance to Capernaum"; Chorazin was less
  than an hour's walk from Capernaum, and Bethsaida lay outside Galilee in Philip's territory.
- France notes a first-century boat recovered from the northwest shore of the lake in 1986 (Yigal
  Allon Center, Ginosar), measuring 8.20 m by 2.35 m with a depth of 1.25 m, from which he
  infers that a boat might be overcrowded with more than thirteen people.
- On Nazareth's population France cites J. F. Strange; see [[nazareth|Nazareth]].

No survey of Galilean village archaeology, synagogue remains, or regional survey data appears in
the ingested material.

## Modern Identification and Geography

The commentary is concerned with first-century regional geography, not modern identification,
and supplies no discussion of the modern political geography of the region. France's own
geographical observations are relational: Galilee lay north of Samaria, west of Perea and the
Decapolis, and centred on a freshwater lake. On the lake's name he notes that Matthew, Mark and
John call it a "sea" (reflecting the OT *yām-kinneret*) while Luke and Josephus "more correctly"
call it a lake; France himself translates "lake" throughout his commentary except at Matt 4:15,
judging it "less misleading to modern readers."

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

This is the heart of France's treatment, and he makes it hermeneutically mandatory: "To read
Matthew in blissful ignorance of first-century Palestinian sociopolitics is to miss his point."

France reports the long-recognised view that Mark's geographical framework has "an ideological,
not merely a historical, basis": in Mark the Galilean ministry is a success story while Jerusalem
appears only as a source of opposition (3:22; 7:1), so that "the southern capital rejects and
kills the northern prophet," with hope resting on a reunion in Galilee (Mark 14:28; 16:7). France
argues that Matthew not only endorses this divide but "has also considerably enhanced it."

The core polarity France draws from the Isaiah 9 formula quotation at 4:15-16 is light and
darkness: the quotation serves "to designate Galilee as the place of light, as opposed to the
darkness which we shall eventually find to be settled over Judea." Galilee is both launch-point
and relaunch-point — the place "from which eventually, after the conflict and rejection in Judea,
the mission will be relaunched to reach all nations" (28:16-20). France reads the last two
pericopae of the gospel as a poignant juxtaposition of "the desperate cover-up maneuvers of the
defeated priests in Jerusalem" with the triumphant launch of the messianic mission in Galilee.

France further notes that the Galilean setting stands "despite the standard view expressed by the
priests and scribes in 2:5-6 and endorsed by the Jewish opinion cited in John 7:41-42, 52" — that
is, Matthew's geography is a deliberate counter to Judean messianic expectation. Communal
prejudice, on France's account, stood against a Galilean's claim to be heard even as a prophet,
let alone under a title "which, as everyone knew, belonged to Judea."

On "Galilee of the nations" France explains the phrase by the region's "greater openness to
surrounding Gentile populations" and by Isaiah's awareness of Assyrian deportations and foreign
resettlement (2 Kgs 15:29; 17:24-34). He reads it as foreshadowing the "all nations" mission of
28:19, even though Jesus' ministry meanwhile is largely restricted to Jews — "a principle which
will be explicitly stated in 10:5-6; 15:24, but breached in practice" at 15:21-39. On the
darkness/light imagery he holds that Isaiah's original reference was to Assyrian devastation but
that Matthew "takes it in a more eschatological sense," grounding the new hope in the preaching
of the [[kingdom-of-heaven|kingdom of heaven]]; Matthew's aorist tenses, against the LXX's
imperative and future, "make it clear that for him the prophecy has now found its
[[fulfillment|fulfillment]] in Jesus." See [[formula-quotations|formula quotations]] for the
text-form issues.

France also reads the withdrawal to Galilee at 4:12 as in part political: "This 'withdrawal' …
was in part a matter of political wisdom," since as [[john-the-baptist|John's]] successor Jesus
could not be safe in Antipas's Perean area, and "an itinerant preacher touring the Galilean
villages was a less obvious target for political concern" than John's centripetal campaign by
the Jordan.

## Key Events, Figures, and Controversies

- **The Galilee/Jerusalem polarity as a structural key.** France's position is that the
  sociopolitical divide is not background colour but the interpretive frame of the whole gospel.
  Recorded here as his position; the wiki does not adjudicate it against readings that treat
  Matthew's geography as primarily traditional or chronological.
- **How positive was the Galilean response?** France's own two canvassed options at 11:20-24 are
  recorded above, with his preference for the "broad generalization" reading and his consequent
  downgrading of the earlier summaries.
- **"Way of the sea" and "beyond the Jordan."** France leaves both unresolved; see
  [[capernaum|Capernaum]] and [[formula-quotations|formula quotations]].
- **Trans-Jordanian provenance for the gospel.** France reports and rejects the argument that
  Matthew's gospel originated in Trans-Jordan on the basis of the phrase "beyond the Jordan,"
  calling it "a lot to load onto a phrase included in a quotation from Isaiah."

Key figures associated with Galilee in this material: [[jesus|Jesus]],
[[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist]], [[peter-apostle|Peter]], [[herod-antipas|Herod Antipas]].

## Galilee in Mark: France's NIGTC Reading (2002)

From the full re-ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of Mark* (NIGTC, 2002) — see
[[france-mark-nigtc]] — where the Galilee/Jerusalem polarity is a structural key to
[[mark|Mark]] as it is to Matthew. All claims below are France's unless otherwise attributed.

- **Symbolic geography, deliberately deployed but held lightly.** France reports the
  Lohmeyer–Lightfoot view that Galilee and Jerusalem carry symbolic weight in Mark, supported
  by recent scholarship (he cites Vermes and Freyne) on the historical contrast and mutual
  suspicion between the regions, including Galilee's separate political history and "northern"
  Aramaic (cf. Mk 14:70): "At the time of Jesus a Galilean in Jerusalem, however strong his
  Jewish credentials, would have felt and been perceived as to some degree a foreigner." His
  own characterisation: "the overall characterisation of Galilee as the place of proclamation
  and response and of Jerusalem as the place of conflict and rejection seems very deliberate" —
  yet he judges it a dramatic vehicle, "not a matter of great theological weight."
- **A drama in three acts.** France structures Mark geographically: Act One in and around
  Galilee (to ~8:21), Act Two on the road to Jerusalem (~8:22–10:52), Act Three in the capital
  (11:1 on). Jerusalem intrudes on the Galilean phase only as the source of hostile scribes
  (3:22; 7:1).
- **Launch, not escape.** Since Galilee remained Antipas's territory, France reads the move
  there after John's arrest (1:14) not as a tactical withdrawal but as "the appropriate way for
  Jesus, the Galilean, to begin his own independent and public ministry." The kingdom
  announcement issues (France quoting Myers) in the gathering of "socially insignificant people
  in an unnoticed corner of provincial Galilee."
- **Jewish lakeshore, not Hellenistic cities.** As in his Matthew commentary, France notes
  [[capernaum|Capernaum]], not [[nazareth|Nazareth]], was Jesus' base, and that Mark records no
  visit to Sepphoris or Tiberias; the response came from the traditionally Jewish lakeshore
  population. He ends Act One with Jesus making "a conscious decision to terminate both his
  dialogue with the religious leadership and his public ministry in Galilee" (8:11-13).
- **Two crowds, not one fickle crowd.** France insists the Hosanna crowd of the entry (11:1-10)
  were Galilean pilgrims approaching the city, not Jerusalemites: "They are not the same crowd.
  The Galilean pilgrims shouted 'Hosanna' as they approached the city; the Jerusalem crowd
  shouted, 'Crucify him'" (cf. 15:11-14) — against the preacher's trope of one fickle crowd.
- **Galilean identity under pressure.** In the denial scene the accusers press Peter's Galilean
  identity (14:70), which France links to Judaean contempt for Galileans; and only at the cross
  does Mark reveal that Jesus' entourage had all along included a substantial group of women
  followers from Galilee (15:40-41), described in "the language of discipleship."
- **Galilee as the place of restoration.** France reads 14:28 and 16:7 as making Galilee
  structurally "the place of resurrection and restoration" over against Jerusalem as the place
  of rejection and failure: "Jerusalem is for Jesus the place of rejection and death… but
  Galilee is the place of resurrection and restoration." He argues the intended (in his view
  lost) ending of Mark would have included the promised Galilean meeting — see
  [[mark-ending|The Ending of Mark]].
- **Not a cipher for Gentile mission.** Against C. F. Evans and Telford, France denies that
  "Galilee" in 16:7 encodes the Gentile mission: "The very deliberate contrast between
  Jerusalem and Galilee throughout this gospel is not a contrast between Jew and Gentile," and
  "'Galilee of the Gentiles' is not a Marcan phrase, nor does it express a Marcan perspective."
- **Reception in the other Synoptics.** France holds that Matthew intensified Mark's
  Galilee/Jerusalem symbolism while Luke (and Acts) instead depicts Jerusalem as the church's
  true home: "the symbolism with which Mark has invested it was congenial to Matthew but not to
  Luke."
