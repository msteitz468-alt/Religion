# Uz

The homeland of [[job|Job]], named in the book's first clause and nowhere located. The evidence
[[john-hartley|Hartley]] surveys points in two directions and he declines to settle it.

## Geographical and Historical Overview

The [[book-of-job|book of Job]] opens: *"In the land of Uz there lived a man named Job"* (1:1,
Hartley's rendering). Nothing further in the book fixes the place.

Hartley's summary of the evidence is short and deliberately undecided:

> *"The evidence points in two different directions: a southern location in the vicinity of Edom and
> a northern site northeast of Palestine in the Hauran. **The evidence slightly favors the latter
> location.**"* ([p. 65–66], on 1:1)

That is the whole of his verdict — a slight lean toward the Hauran, not an identification. This wiki
records it as a preference, not a result.

The wider setting is easier to characterize than the site. Hartley says of the book generally that
*"the setting of this book is to the east of Canaan"* ([p. 429]) and that the author *"set his work
in the patriarchal period,"* a placement he detects in *"the use of archaic names for God and in
judging a character by adherence to a patriarchal standard of ethics"* ([p. 17]). Uz, on this
reading, is an antiquarian and non-Israelite setting chosen by the author, not a place the author
belonged to.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

Uz is named once, at 1:1. Its inhabitant is Job, introduced — pointedly — without ancestry. Hartley:
*"it is noteworthy that Job is introduced without genealogy and without reference to his tribe or
clan… The author thereby masterfully composes a literary piece in which Job is representative of all
who suffer"* ([p. 66–67]). The namelessness of Job's lineage and the vagueness of his homeland work
together.

The book's other place-names cluster around Uz without locating it:

| Name | Bearer | Hartley's placement |
|---|---|---|
| Teman | [[eliphaz-the-temanite]] | *"'the south land,' a principal site in the northern region of Edom (Ezek. 25:13)"* — and famed for wisdom (Jer. 49:7; Obad. 8) |
| Shuah | [[bildad-the-shuhite]] | Akkadian *Sūḫu*, *"located on the Middle Euphrates River, below the mouth of the Khabur River"*; but *"Pope thinks that the biblical genealogies place Shuah farther to the south, since it is connected with Dedan and Sheba"* |
| Naamah | [[zophar-the-naamathite]] | *"A possible identification for Zophar's home is the site 'Ain Ṣopar on the road between Beirut and Damascus"* |
| Buz | [[elihu]] (his tribe) | not located; Hartley says only that Elihu's names collectively *"identif[y] him with the kin of Abraham"* |

The friends' homelands thus pull north (Sūḫu, 'Ain Ṣopar), south (Teman, and Pope's Shuah), and
nowhere in particular — the same scatter as the evidence for Uz itself.

Two further names belong to the book's trade geography rather than to Uz: at 6:19 *"Tema was a center
of trade routes in northwestern Arabia, and Sheba was a center in southwestern Arabia."* Tema and the
Hijaz appear elsewhere in Hartley's survey only in a reported hypothesis — Guillaume's proposal that
the author was *"a Jew who lived at Tema (the area of present-day Hijaz) sometime between 552 and 542
B.C."* and wrote in Arabic — which Hartley reports without adopting.

### The raiders

The two human agents of Job's ruin come from outside Uz. Hartley reads the four disasters of 1:13–19
as a compass: *"the Sabeans from the south, lightning from a storm out of the west, the Chaldeans
from the north, and the treacherous sirocco blowing off the desert to the east. The number four also
symbolizes full measure, totality"* ([p. 77]).

Their portrayal carries dating weight. Among the evidence Hartley reports from N. Sarna for an
ancient epic substratum behind the prose frame is that the Sabeans and Chaldeans are *"presented as
wandering tribes"* (1:15, 17) — alongside wealth reckoned in animals and servants, the family head
sacrificing, and the *qəśîṭâ* as currency. Sarna's conclusion, as Hartley reports it, is that *"the
prose account comes directly from an ancient Epic of Job."* Hartley himself holds that the prose
frame *"has a very ancient substratum that possibly goes back to the prepatriarchal era."* Nomadic
Chaldeans, on this line of argument, belong to a period well before the Neo-Babylonian empire.

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

Effectively none, and this should be stated plainly. The ingested source cites no excavation, no
inscription, and no toponymic attestation bearing on Uz itself. The only extra-biblical evidence in
play is indirect: Akkadian documents attesting a district *Sūḫu* on the Middle Euphrates, adduced for
Bildad's Shuah rather than for Uz.

Hartley reports and rejects one ancient attempt at identification: the Septuagint at 42:18 equates
Job with Jobab the Edomite king of Gen. 36:33 (= 1 Chr. 1:44). Hartley's verdict: *"there appears to
be no connection between this Edomite and Job."* He likewise takes Job to be *"the real name of a
prepatriarchal hero"* rather than an etymological invention, rejecting derivations from *ʾôyēḇ*
("enemy") and Arabic *ʾwb* ("return, repent") — the latter because *"such a meaning stands in stark
contrast to Job's stern conviction that he need not repent."*

Hartley also reports an untestable proposal that the author was an Edomite descended from Esau,
noting that *"the lack of any significant literary documents from that region prevents the testing of
this hypothesis."*

## Modern Identification and Geography

No modern identification is offered in the ingested source. The two candidate regions Hartley names
are:

- **Edom / southern Transjordan** — modern southern Jordan and the Negev fringe. Supported by the
  Edomite associations of Teman and by the book's Edomite wisdom colouring.
- **The Hauran, northeast of Palestine** — the volcanic plateau of southern Syria, east of the Sea of
  Galilee. Hartley judges the evidence to favour this *slightly*.

He does not weigh the two arguments in the material read for this wiki, and gives no criterion by
which the Hauran wins. The honest statement is that Uz is unlocated, and that a modern reader should
treat any map placement as conjecture.

One further consideration Hartley raises bears on what kind of place Uz must have been rather than
where. Against the objection that Job's dual profile — great livestock holdings, yet a city gate and
a settled community (ch. 29) — fits no single social setting, Hartley reports Rowton's dimorphic
model: *"Job was chief of a tribe, living according to a dimorphic social and political structure, a
tribal system that integrated a nomadic and a sedentary way of life… a chief might even have his own
village with some four hundred houses along with a fortified residence for himself."*

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

The load-bearing fact about Uz is that it is *not* Israel. The book's hero is a non-Israelite, in a
non-covenantal setting, judged by a patriarchal rather than a Mosaic standard, addressing God under
archaic names. Hartley reads the book's whole religious frame this way: the *tōrâ* Eliphaz invokes at
22:22 *"means the precepts passed down through the wise patriarchs to whom God had given insight into
his ways"* — not the Sinai law; and Eliphaz's eventual charge is that Job *"has violated the standards
of patriarchal religion by abusing the weak and unfortunate."* Even Job's sacrifice in the epilogue is
offered, in Hartley's words, *"as a patriarch and not as an ordained priest."*

The theological consequence is that the book's argument about undeserved suffering runs entirely
outside the covenant machinery — no election, no Sinai, no temple, no priesthood. Combined with
Hartley's reading of Job's missing genealogy (*"Job is representative of all who suffer"*), the
placelessness of Uz functions as a universalizing device. The setting is deliberately marginal so
that the question can be general.

That said, Hartley does not himself state a symbolic theory of Uz. The above is the theological
significance of the non-Israelite setting as he describes it, applied to the location; he does not
argue that Uz is a fictional or allegorical place. See [[theodicy|theodicy]] and
[[wisdom-literature|wisdom literature]].

## Key Events, Figures, and Controversies

**Events set in or around Uz:** Job's prosperity and household sacrifices (1:1–5); the four disasters
(1:13–19); Job's affliction and the ash-heap (2:7–8); the arrival of the three friends (2:11–13) and
the whole dialogue; the Yahweh speeches from the tempest; the epilogue's sacrifice, intercession, and
restoration (42:7–17).

**Figures associated:** [[job|Job]] · [[eliphaz-the-temanite|Eliphaz the Temanite]] ·
[[bildad-the-shuhite|Bildad the Shuhite]] · [[zophar-the-naamathite|Zophar the Naamathite]] ·
[[elihu|Elihu]] · Job's wife · Job's kinsmen, whom Hartley glosses at 42:11 as *yōḏəʿîm*, *"close
friends, perhaps members of the town council (cf. 19:19)"* — a datum implying a settled community
with a governing body.

**Open questions in the ingested material:**

1. **Where Uz was.** Hartley reports two candidate regions and leans slightly north, without
   argument recorded in this pass. Unresolved.
2. **Whether the setting is Edomite in origin.** Reported as an untestable hypothesis about the
   author, not a conclusion about the place.
3. **Whether Job = Jobab of Edom.** The Septuagint says yes at 42:18; Hartley says no.
