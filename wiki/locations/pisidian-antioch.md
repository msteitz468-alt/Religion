# Pisidian Antioch

> **Mismatch note:** Pisidian Antioch is a **distinct city** from [[antioch|Syrian Antioch
> on the Orontes]], the Pauline mission base. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] flags the two as
> separate throughout; they share only the dynastic name.

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Pisidian Antioch was a **Roman colony** in the highlands of **Phrygia Galatica**, on the
border of Pisidia, within the Roman province of **Galatia** in Asia Minor.
[[darrell-bock|Bock]] ([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) locates the city's chief cult in
the god **Men (Men Askaenos)** and records a substantial resident Jewish population with
attached [[god-fearers|God-fearers]] and proselytes — the audience for Paul's synagogue
sermon. In [[acts|Acts]] the city is the site of Paul's **programmatic synagogue address**
on the first missionary journey (13:14–52), which Bock treats as "a model of synagogue
preaching" for the whole book.

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

Paul and Barnabas reach Pisidian Antioch and preach in the synagogue on the Sabbath
(Acts 13:14–52). Bock's exposition of the sermon — one of the
[[speeches-in-acts|major speeches in Acts]]:

- **Structure.** Bock outlines the address in three or four movements: setting (13:13–15),
  survey of Israel's history to John (13:16–25), the declaration about Jesus (13:26–37),
  and the application (13:38–41), with the aftermath running through v. 52.
- **The Davidic-promise / resurrection argument (13:22–37).** Bock highlights the wordplay
  on "raised up" (ēgeiren) — used of God raising David *as king* and then of God raising
  Jesus *from the dead* — and the chain of three OT citations (Ps. 2:7; Isa. 55:3;
  Ps. 16:10). He notes the **gezerah shewa** hermeneutic linking Isa. 55:3 and Ps. 16:10
  via shared "holy" terms, calling it a "rabbinic technique." The clinching move: David
  "saw corruption," so Ps. 16 "ultimately cannot be about David… It must be about someone
  else" — Jesus. On Ps. 2:7 Bock judges "a reference to the resurrection is slightly more
  likely" and insists it means Jesus is "made evident as Son," not adopted as Son.
- **The justification statement (13:38–39).** Bock treats this as the sermon's application:
  deliverance is stated as **forgiveness of sins** and as **[[justification|justification]]**
  — "Jesus can provide what Moses and the law could not, namely, justification." Against
  the view (Vielhauer) that Luke's justification is un-Pauline, Bock argues it "may be
  nothing more than a Lukan telescoping of Paul's view": the legal declaration of
  forgiveness "leads to a release from sin."
- **The turn "to the Gentiles" (13:44–48).** After Jewish leaders react "with jealousy,"
  Paul declares (citing Isa. 49:6) that he is now cast "in the role of the servant of
  Israel… a light for the Gentiles." Bock notes the strong predestinarian note of 13:48
  ("as many as were ordained to eternal life believed"), which he calls "as strong a
  passage on God's sovereignty as anywhere in Luke-Acts." He stresses the turn "is not
  absolute" — Paul still begins with the synagogue in each city.

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock connects the nearby figure **Sergius Paulus** (the Cyprus proconsul of 13:7) to
inscriptions naming a *Lucius Sergius Paullus* near Pisidian Antioch, and reports the
scholarly suggestion (Witherington) that Sergius Paulus, having family ties there, may
have recommended the city to Paul — while cautioning that the inscriptional
identification is uncertain.

## Modern Identification

Bock places the ruins near the modern Turkish town of **Yalvaç**. The site preserves
remains of the Roman colony, including its Augustan-era civic architecture.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

For Bock the Pisidian Antioch sermon is the paradigm of Paul's preaching **to Jews and
God-fearers**, paralleling Jesus' synagogue address at Nazareth (Luke 4:16–30, though in
reverse order). It states the Davidic-fulfillment and resurrection kerygma at length and
crystallizes the book's pivot "to the Gentiles" upon Jewish rejection — a pattern Bock
sees repeated across the mission.
