# Ashkelon

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Ashkelon was a major Mediterranean **seaport** and the largest of the five
**[[philistines|Philistine]] pentapolis** cities ([[ashdod|Ashdod]], Ashkelon,
[[ekron|Ekron]], [[gaza|Gaza]], Gath). It had been a [[canaanites|Canaanite]] port for
a millennium before the Philistines took it (Stager, Coogan Ch. 3).

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants (by period)

- **Canaanite**: a Bronze Age port; the **Merneptah Stela/reliefs** (c. 1209 BCE) name
  Ashkelon as a **Canaanite** city — proof that the Philistines had **not** yet arrived,
  a key datum in dating their migration (see [[sea-peoples]]).
- **Philistine (after c. 1175 BCE)**: the Philistines destroyed and rebuilt it as their
  **main seaport** (~50–60 ha), exporting grain, wine, and oil; later a royal **winery**
  occupied its center. Weaving installations (Aegean-style spool weights) attest the
  immigrants' culture.
- **The Bible**: one of the five Philistine lords' cities (Josh. 13; 1 Sam. 6 — the
  golden tumors); Samson kills thirty men there (Judg. 14); "tell it not in Gath…
  proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon" (2 Sam. 1.20, David's lament); prophetic
  oracles (Amos 1; Jer. 47; Zeph. 2).
- **Assyrian/Babylonian**: a vassal city (Sennacherib deported its royal family in 701);
  **destroyed by Babylon in 604 BCE**, with Ashkelonite deportees later attested in
  Babylon (see [[assyrians]], [[babylonians]]).

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

Excavations exposed massive Bronze Age **ramparts** and an arched mud-brick **Canaanite
gate**, Philistine pottery and architecture, the Iron II winery, and a long later
sequence. Ashkelon's stratigraphy is central to dating the Philistine arrival and
acculturation (Mycenaean IIIC → bichrome ware).

## Modern Identification and Geography

**Tel Ashkelon**, within Ashkelon National Park on the coast of southern Israel, beside
the modern city of Ashkelon.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

As a Philistine stronghold, Ashkelon belongs to the corporate "other" of the Iron Age
narratives and to the prophetic oracles against Philistia. Its Merneptah-era evidence is
crucial for the **archaeological history of the Sea Peoples' migration**.
