Punjab is the historical homeland of the [[sikhism|Sikh]] Panth and the geographical matrix of Sikh society, culture, and diaspora migration. The name derives from *panj ab* — "five rivers" — referring to the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Satluj rivers that define the region's agricultural character. The present discussion draws primarily on W. H. McLeod's *Sikhism* (1997) for the social-historical dimensions relevant to Sikh studies.

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Punjab's agricultural fertility — sustained by canal irrigation and the five river systems — made it the economic foundation of the Sikh Panth. The dominant agricultural caste, the Jats, hold large areas of rural land and constitute "a majority of Sikhs" (McLeod ~L365). Land ownership is the primary criterion of *izzat* (honor/self-respect) in Jat society: "Land is very definitely identified with power and with the maintenance of izzat (honour)" (~L372).

The region was annexed by the British in 1849 following the Anglo-Sikh Wars. This annexation marks the beginning of organized Sikh overseas migration, as Sikh men were recruited into the Indian Army in the years after the annexation and particularly after the 1857–8 uprising.

Punjab was partitioned in 1947 between India and Pakistan; the discussion of diaspora migration in McLeod concerns primarily the Indian Punjab and its sub-regions.

## Sub-regions and Emigration Geography

McLeod identifies **Doaba** — the plains region lying between the Beas and Satluj rivers — as "the heartland for all overseas migration from the Punjab" (~L604–617). Specifically: "An analysis of all the Punjabis who travelled to New Zealand prior to 1921 shows that an overwhelming majority came from the three tahsils (sub-districts) that occupy the eastern corner of Doaba" (~L614–616), with "the principal supplements coming from areas immediately adjacent to it, notably from Ludhiana District across the Satluj river" (~L617–618).

This geographic concentration applies to both the early wave of emigration (1870s–1920s) and the post-Second World War wave.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

The Guru Granth Sahib was composed and compiled within the Punjab — Guru Nanak's compositions emerge from the Punjab in the early sixteenth century, and the scripture was finalized at [[amritsar|Amritsar]] under Guru Arjan (d. 1606). The Punjab is thus the setting for the entire narrative of the ten Gurus.

Key population groups in McLeod's account of Punjabi Sikh society:
- **Jats**: rural agricultural majority; practice equal inheritance among sons but historically exclude daughters from land inheritance; land-based izzat drives much of the emigration pattern
- **Khatris and Aroras**: urban-based trading castes; "certainly make a deep impression on the life of the Panth, but the number who are Sikhs is vastly fewer than those who are Jats" (~L355–358)
- **Ramgarhias**: artisan Sikh caste, prominent in East Africa and subsequently in UK
- **Bhatras**: small caste from Sialkot and Gurdaspur districts, traditional fortune-tellers and hawkers; became prominent in British Sikh communities from the 1920s–1940s
- **Bedis**: a Jat *got* (clan) historically prominent enough that it had difficulty finding marriage partners for daughters, historically connected to female infanticide practices now "obliterated" (~L248–250)

## Land Inheritance and Gender

The Jat practice of land inheritance in the Punjab has direct consequences for gender inequality within the Panth. Land passes to sons in equal shares; daughters are provided for via dowry (cash and goods) but not landed property. In 1956 central government legislation granted daughters equal inheritance rights with sons, but "Jat society could not possibly accept it because it flew directly in the face of inviolable custom. An amendment to the legislation was subsequently enacted, which, in effect, reversed the concept of equal inheritance" (~L338–346). The result: "A majority of Sikhs are Jats; the Jats own large areas of rural land in the Punjab; and the Jats are exceedingly reluctant to let rural landownership pass through inheritance into the hands of their daughters" (~L365–370).

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

Punjab is not merely the homeland of the Panth but the site of its sacred geography: [[amritsar|Amritsar]] (site of the Harmandir Sahib / Golden Temple and the Akal Takhat) and Nankana Sahib (birthplace of Guru Nanak, now in Pakistan) are both mentioned in the Ardas prayer as places of special devotion: "Grant to the Khalsa continuing access to Nankana Sahib and to other gurdwaras from which it has been separated" (~L3112–3115). This reference reflects the 1947 partition.

## Key Events and Controversies

- **1849 British annexation**: begins Sikh military recruitment and overseas migration
- **1857–8 uprising**: Sikhs gain British favor as "martial race"; soldiers posted to Singapore and Hong Kong, opening Asian migration routes
- **Gurdwara Reform Movement (early 1920s)**: resulted in the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925, creating the SGPC as the institutional authority for historic Punjab gurdwaras
- **1947 partition**: divided Punjab between India and Pakistan; displaced populations; cut Sikhs off from Nankana Sahib and other sacred sites in Pakistani Punjab
- **Idi Amin 1972**: expulsion of Indians from Uganda brought many East African Ramgarhia Sikhs to UK
