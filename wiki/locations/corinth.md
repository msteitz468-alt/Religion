# Corinth

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Corinth was **refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BCE** (a century after Rome destroyed the
old Greek city) and became the administrative center of the province of **Achaia**, host
of the Isthmian Games and a byword for prosperity and vice. [[darrell-bock|Bock]]
([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) sums up its reputation as "the Las Vegas of its time,"
noting its temple to Aphrodite and its commercial energy astride the isthmus. In
[[acts|Acts]] Corinth is the site of Paul's **eighteen-month ministry** (18:1–18), the
longest settled stay narrated to that point.

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

- **Aquila and Priscilla (18:1–3).** Paul lodges with the Jewish couple **Aquila and
  Priscilla**, expelled from Rome by **Claudius's decree (AD 49)** over disturbances "at
  the instigation of Chrestus" (Suetonius) — which Bock reads as "either a misspelled
  reference to Christ," suggesting "the two were Christians while in Rome." They share
  Paul's trade as **tentmakers** (Bock: the work "likely included working with leather").
- **The synagogue and the break (18:4–8).** Paul "tries to persuade" (a conative imperfect)
  Jews and Greeks; met with opposition and reviling, he shakes out his garments — "Your
  blood be upon your heads! I am innocent. From now on I will go to the Gentiles." Bock
  calls this "a break in fellowship" but "not a complete abandonment of the Jews." Converts
  include **Titius Justus** (a God-fearer next to the synagogue — Bock: "the last mention
  of a God-fearer in Acts") and **Crispus** (the synagogue ruler, with his whole
  household) — "an exemplary Gentile and an exemplary Jew."
- **The vision (18:9–11).** The Lord tells Paul, "Do not be afraid… for I am with you… for
  I have many people in this city," and Paul stays **eighteen months**.
- **Gallio's ruling (18:12–17) — the legal precedent.** The Jews bring Paul before the
  proconsul **Gallio** (brother of Seneca the Younger), charging that Paul persuades people
  "to worship God contrary to the law." Bock reads the real accusation as the claim that
  the new faith "is not a *collegium licitum*, or a legitimately recognized religion" —
  that it "is not their [tolerated Jewish] faith." **Gallio refuses to hear the case**:
  "since it is a dispute of questions about words and names and your own law, see to it
  yourselves; I refuse to be a judge of these things." Bock: "Luke is making clear that
  Christians are not a legal threat to Rome" — the matter is an internal Jewish and
  Scriptural dispute, outside the court. When the crowd then beats the synagogue ruler
  **Sosthenes**, Gallio "showed no concern" — Bock notes the ambivalence "is not unlike the
  way Pilate handled Jesus."

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock treats the **Gallio episode as one of the firmest chronological anchors in Acts**:
Gallio's proconsulship of Achaia falls about **AD 51–52** (Claudius's twelfth regnal
year), dating Paul's Corinthian ministry. He cross-references **Suetonius** on the
Claudian expulsion (with **Orosius** dating the edict to AD 49) and notes the debate over
identifying Sosthenes and Crispus with figures in 1 Corinthians.

## Modern Identification

The ancient city is the excavated site of **Archaia Korinthos** in the northeastern
Peloponnese, near the Isthmus; the modern town of Korinthos lies nearby. Bock does not
adjudicate specific monuments (the Gallio inscription found at Delphi, not Corinth,
underlies the dating he reports).

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

For Bock, Corinth yields two enduring points. First, **Gallio's ruling is a Roman legal
precedent** that "Christianity is not a threat to the state" — Rome "will not rule for
Christianity or against it," and later persecution came only when allegiance to God "was
seen as a competitor to allegiance to Caesar." Second, the Corinthian converts (a
God-fearer and a synagogue ruler alike) exemplify the gospel's reach across the
Jew–Gentile boundary that structures Acts.

## Ciampa & Rosner (PNTC 1 Corinthians) — social and cultic profile

Attributed to [[roy-ciampa-brian-rosner|Ciampa and Rosner]] ([[ciampa-rosner-1-corinthians-pntc]]):

- **Roman colony.** Greek Corinth destroyed 146 BCE; refounded by Julius Caesar 44 BCE as *Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis*; capital of Achaia; isthmus trade (*diolkos*); Acrocorinth (c. 1,886 ft). Prosperity and status-consciousness frame the letter's social values thesis.
- **Church founding.** Paul spent about eighteen months (Acts 18:1–18); most members former Gentiles who had turned from idols (1 Cor 12:2). Letter from Ephesus, spring AD 54 or 55.
- **Matching vices.** C&R list features of Corinthian society said to match church problems: glorification of wisdom, food sacrificed to idols, denial of bodily resurrection, light assessment of sexual immorality, drunkenness, greed, vexatious litigation.
- **Cultic meat channels.** Temple precinct meals, *macellum* market meat, private dinner parties — the social matrix of [[food-offered-to-idols|chs. 8–10]].
- **Worship space.** Reconstructions of Lord's Supper abuse often invoke triclinium/atrium stratification in house churches (reported carefully, not over-claimed).
