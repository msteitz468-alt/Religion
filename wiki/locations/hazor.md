# Hazor

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Hazor, in the Huleh Valley of Upper Galilee, was the **largest city in Canaan** — about
**72 hectares (180 acres)**, bigger than Ebla, with perhaps **20,000 inhabitants** — and
"the head of all those kingdoms" (Josh. 11.10). Its size and importance in the Middle
and Late Bronze Ages are confirmed by both archaeology and its appearance in the **Mari
letters**, **Egyptian Execration Texts**, and **Amarna letters** (Pitard, Coogan Ch. 1).

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants (by period)

- **Canaanite (MB–LB)**: a dominant city-state of the [[canaanites|Canaanite]] world.
- **Joshua 11.10–13**: among all the conquered northern cities, **only Hazor is burned
  to the ground** by Joshua. Notably, of the ~31 cities Joshua claims, Coogan's volume
  finds **only Hazor and Bethel** archaeologically fit the conquest model — "the only
  agents who claim responsibility for destroying this Canaanite city are the Israelites.
  There is no reason to deny them their claim" (Stager, Coogan Ch. 3). See
  [[herem-canaanite-conquest]], [[israelites]].
- **Judges 4**: "Jabin king of Hazor" and his commander Sisera (the [[judges|Song of
  Deborah]] battle).
- **Israelite Iron Age**: rebuilt; a **Solomonic** six-chambered gate (1 Kings 9.15);
  Iron II city until **Tiglath-pileser III** destroyed it in 732 BCE (see [[assyrians]]).

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

Excavated by **Yigael Yadin** and later teams: a huge tell with an upper acropolis and a
vast lower city, Bronze Age palaces and temples, a Late Bronze destruction layer, and
Iron Age Israelite fortifications. Hazor's Late Bronze burning is debated as to whether
it is exactly synchronous with Bethel's, but it remains the strongest single
archaeological support for any Israelite conquest event.

## Modern Identification and Geography

**Tel Hazor** (Tell el-Qedah), northern Israel, ~14 km north of the Sea of Galilee; a
UNESCO World Heritage Site (with Megiddo and Beer-sheba) as a biblical tel.

## Theological and Narrative Significance

Hazor is the **archaeological linchpin** of the conquest debate: the one major site
where the burning fits the Joshua narrative, even as most others (Jericho, Ai) do not.
It thus sits at the center of the tension between the Deuteronomic conquest account and
the ruralization model of Israel's emergence.

### Woudstra (NICOT Joshua) — brief

Attributed to [[marten-woudstra|Woudstra]] ([[woudstra-joshua-nicot]]; digest D): Hazor is
“the head of all these kingdoms” (11:10); Jabin heads a last comprehensive northern
coalition. Woudstra rejects collapsing Josh 11 with Deborah/Barak (Judg 4) — two distinct
events; Jabin may be a hereditary title; the city may have been rebuilt. Burning is the
**exception** among mound-cities Israel did not burn (11:13); *ḥerem* on inhabitants makes
Hazor an example among degrees of the ban (stricter than other northern cities; no rebuild
curse as on Jericho). 13th-c. burn layers can support a late conquest date but cannot
prove Israelite agency alone (Introduction V). See [[herem|Ḥerem]].

## Key Events and Controversies

Joshua's burning of Hazor; the Deborah/Sisera war; the Solomonic gate; the Assyrian
destruction (732). Its evidence is repeatedly cited in the **conquest-historicity
controversy** ([[herem-canaanite-conquest]]). Related: [[megiddo]], [[jericho]],
[[shechem]], [[canaanites]], [[israelites]], [[assyrians]], [[joshua]], [[judges]],
[[marten-woudstra]], [[woudstra-joshua-nicot]], [[herem]].
