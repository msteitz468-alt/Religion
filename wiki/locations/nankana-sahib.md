# Nankana Sahib

The birthplace of [[guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]] — historically the village of **Talwandi**, renamed Nankana
Sahib — and one of the most sacred Sikh pilgrimage sites. Since 1947 it has lain in Pakistan, on the far
side of the [[partition-of-punjab-1947|Partition]] boundary. Khushwant Singh's *A History of the Sikhs*
Vol. 2 features it chiefly as the scene of the **1921 Nankana Sahib massacre**, a turning point in the
Gurdwara Reform movement.

## Religious Significance

As the birthplace of the founder of [[sikhism|Sikhism]], Nankana Sahib is among "the most richly endowed
Sikh shrines." Its loss to Pakistan in 1947 made access a standing Sikh grievance: in the
[[anandpur-sahib-resolution|Anandpur Sahib Resolution]], Kapur Singh demanded "**Vatican Status**" for
the town.

## The Nankana Sahib Massacre (20 February 1921)

By the early twentieth century the shrine was controlled by an **Udasi mahant, Narain Das**, who — in
Singh's account — "lived in the gurdwara with a mistress and was known to have invited prostitutes to
dance in the sacred premises." When an Akali *jatha* came to take over the shrine, "Narain Das's thugs
attacked the jathā with swords, hatchets, and firearms. The dead and dying Akalis were then dragged to a
pile of logs which had been collected earlier, and burnt. By the time the police and local Sikhs came on
the scene, 130 men had been consumed by the flames." Gandhi called it "a second edition of Dyerism, more
barbarous and more fiendish than the barbarism at Jallianwala Bagh." Three men were sentenced to death
and Narain Das to transportation. The massacre galvanized the [[akali-movement|Gurdwara Reform
movement]], drawing 50,000 Sikhs to Nankana that autumn, and helped drive the agitation that produced the
**Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925**.
