## Geographical and Historical Overview

Mathura owed its position as a cultural and trading centre from "about the fourth or third century BCE" to its situation "on the junction of the caravan routes to the west, east and south of India" and was "undoubtedly a cosmopolitan city exposed to a variety of influences" (Dundas, ch. 5). It served as a pivot for the Jain community's westward expansion out of the original Ganges-basin heartland, and as a hub where Jains cohabited with Buddhists and Hindus at an early stage in the religion's history.

Jain presence at Mathura eventually diminished. During the Gupta period (fourth–fifth centuries CE) substantial sections of the Jain community "had begun to drift away from the city and migrate further to the west," possibly in response to a Hindu revivalism reflected in the declining production of Jain sculptures. The Jain community relocated progressively to the "prosperous kingdom of the Maitrakas of Valabhi" (in what is now Gujarat), a shift confirmed by the fact that "the penultimate Svetambara scriptural recitation was held at both Mathura and Valabhi … while the last was held at Valabhi alone."

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

The key Jain locus for Mathura is the funerary monument (stupa) excavated at **Kankali Tila**. Dundas records a legendary tradition preserved by the "medieval Svetambara pilgrim Jinaprabha Siri" (VTK 9): two monks converted a goddess who became a Jain laywoman; she constructed a golden replica of the Meru stupa; after the fordmaker Parsva warned that the corrupt world age was at hand, the goddess had the gold stupa bricked up and a stone monument built outside. A reference to the stupa "created by the gods" dates to **157 CE**, indicating it was already regarded as of great antiquity by that point. A tenth-century Digambara account by Somadeva gives a different legend, describing its foundation "after considerable rivalry with the Buddhists."

The inscriptions from Kankali Tila record the fission of the Jain ascetic community "into troops (gaṇa) and branches (śākhā) descended from specific teachers," broadly consistent with the 'Succession of the Elders' (Sthavīrāvalī) appended to the Kalpasūtra. More significantly for social history, donative inscriptions show that "by the turn of the common era Jainism was patronised at Mathura by people such as traders, artisans, jewellers and indeed courtesans: in other words, the skilled and moneyed male and female middle classes."

As late as 980 CE, "a massive image of Parsva … was dedicated" at Mathura (Dundas, ch. 5).

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

"All our knowledge of the archaeological and inscriptional evidence for the Jain connection with this ancient centre polarises around the funerary monument (stupa) excavated at Kankali Tila." The co-habitation of Jains with Buddhists and Hindus at Mathura is "corroborated by archaeological evidence," and the evidence also suggests "that at an early period there was no great difference between them with regard to religious architecture."

Around the beginning of the common era, Dundas notes that "the Jains, no doubt in order to accommodate themselves to the Hindu population, started remodelling the story of Krsna, of whose cult Mathura was and still is an important centre, in order to link him with the twenty-second fordmaker Nemi." (not in Dundas's account as a primary textual claim, but reported as Dundas's inference from the evidence).

A **Mathura Sangha** — a deviant Digambara sub-group — is said by Devasena's early-tenth-century critique to have been "reputedly founded by Ramasena in the second century CE." Devasena accused it of having "advocated the abandonment of the whisk completely." The Mathura Sangha "disappeared some time in the late medieval period."

## Modern Identification and Geography

Mathura is a well-known city in Uttar Pradesh, India, on the Yamuna River. Kankali Tila, the site of the Jain stupa, is an archaeological mound within or near the modern city. Mathura remains a major Hindu pilgrimage centre for the Krishna cult (not in Dundas's account as detailed identification, but geographically self-evident from the text's context).

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

For the Jains, Mathura was understood as a holy place through the legend of the Kankali Tila stupa's dedication to the seventh fordmaker Supārśva (per Jinaprabha's account), and later the fordmaker Pārśva's presence there. It was the site of the penultimate Svetambara scriptural recitation and thus a node in Jain canonical and community memory. The migration away from Mathura to Valabhi is historically significant: it marks the consolidation of the Svetambara scriptural canon's final recension in Gujarat rather than the original eastern heartland.

## Key Events and Figures

- **Jinaprabha Siri**: medieval Svetambara pilgrim who preserved the fullest version of Mathura's foundation legend (VTK 9).
- **Somadeva** (tenth-century Digambara): gave an alternative legend emphasising Buddhist rivalry.
- **Kharavela**: king of Kalinga (inscription from Hathigumpha, c. first century BCE) who retrieved a Jain fordmaker image taken from Kalinga, providing early evidence of Jain image worship (not a Mathura event, but cited in this context).
- **Ramasena**: reputed founder of the Mathura Sangha, second century CE (Devasena's account).
- **Gupta dynasty** (fourth–fifth centuries CE): their Hindu revivalism is associated with the Jains' westward migration from Mathura.
