# Damascus

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Damascus is an ancient inland city of Syria, east of the Anti-Lebanon range on the
Barada River. In the first century CE it lay within the Roman orbit but under strong
Nabataean influence; [[darrell-bock|Bock]] ([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) notes
that the Nabataean king **Aretas IV** (or his ethnarch) had authority bearing on the
city at the time of Paul's stay, an anchor Bock uses for chronology (see below). For the
[[acts|Acts]] narrative Damascus matters chiefly as **the first city outside the land of
Israel noted as having Christians** — on Bock's reading its believing community was
probably founded by "fleeing Jewish Christian Hellenists" scattered by the persecution
of Acts 8 (Bock citing Hengel and Schwemer, at Acts 9:1–2).

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

Damascus is the setting of **Saul's conversion and first preaching** (Acts 9:1–25, retold
in Paul's own defenses at 22:5–16 and 26:12–20). Bock's exposition:

- **The road to Damascus (9:1–9).** Saul travels with letters from the high priest
  concerning "the right of extradition" of members of **[[the-way|the Way]]** — Bock
  defends the plausibility of the extradition scenario against the skepticism of
  Conzelmann and Haenchen. Near the city a light "flashes around" him (a verb, Bock
  notes, that occurs only at 9:3; 22:6) and the risen Jesus asks, "Why are you
  persecuting me?" Bock reads this as the seed of the "body of Christ" idea — "to
  persecute the Way is to persecute Jesus" — though he grants it "does not dawn on Paul
  immediately." Blinded, Saul is led into Damascus and fasts three days.
- **Straight Street and Ananias (9:10–19a).** The Lord directs the Damascus disciple
  **[[ananias-and-sapphira|Ananias]]** (a *different* Ananias — "a Jewish believer,"
  22:12) to "the street called Straight" to lay hands on Saul. Bock stresses that here
  **a non-apostle mediates both sight and the Spirit** — one of only three
  laying-on-of-hands-with-Spirit scenes in Acts (8:17; 9:17; 19:6), which he calls a
  "Pauline 'Pentecost'" (Larkin) and "the first time that Luke will indicate the Spirit's
  coming outside the land of Israel." Scales "as it were" fall from Saul's eyes and he is
  baptized.
- **Preaching in the synagogues (9:19b–22).** Saul proclaims Jesus as "the Son of God"
  (9:20) — Bock notes this is "the only occurrence of this full phrase in Acts" — and
  "confounds" the Damascus Jews by "proving that this Jesus is the Christ." His reversal
  amazes hearers who knew him as the persecutor; Bock's modern analogy: "as if a member
  of the Anti-Defamation League were now preaching Jesus."
- **The basket escape (9:23–25).** A Jewish plot leads Saul's disciples to lower him "in
  a basket through an opening in the wall." Bock harmonizes this with **2 Cor. 11:32–33**
  (where the danger is King **Aretas's** ethnarch) and **Gal. 1:17** (the sojourn in
  Arabia): Luke "is not tracing Paul's entire career but the points where it affects the
  church," and the two named threats (hostile Jews and the Nabataean authority) "are not
  incompatible" (Bock following Fitzmyer).

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock treats the Nabataean-ethnarch detail of 2 Cor. 11:32 as a **chronological anchor**:
since **Aretas IV died in AD 39**, "an AD 36–39 time frame fits for 9:1–26." He does not
develop separate archaeological data for the city itself in this passage.

## Modern Identification

The site is continuously identified with modern **Damascus (Dimashq)**, the capital of
Syria. The traditional "Street called Straight" (the Via Recta) is still shown in the
old city, though Bock does not adjudicate the modern localizations.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

On Bock's reading Damascus is the geographical hinge of Luke's Pauline narrative: the
place where the church's chief persecutor becomes its chosen instrument, and the first
named extension of the gospel beyond Israel's borders. Bock frames the whole conversion
in the mold of an **OT prophetic calling account** (1 Sam. 3; Isa. 6; Jer. 1) and insists
"the main character is God." He also treats the episode apologetically: that Saul could
"even consider a conversion" presupposes that disciples "must have been preaching the
resurrection of Jesus with accompanying facts," a seed planted "by people such as
[[stephen|Stephen]]."
