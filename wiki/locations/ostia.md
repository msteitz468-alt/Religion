# Ostia

> **Location page.** The ancient **port of Rome**, and the wiki's primary archaeological window
> onto [[mithraism|Roman Mithraism]] and the competition of religions in the imperial period —
> documented through the Ostia team study [[laeuchli-groh-mithraism-in-ostia|*Mithraism in
> Ostia* (1967)]].

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Ostia was the harbor town at the mouth of the Tiber that supplied the city of [[rome|Rome]]. Its
prosperity rested on dock and warehouse (*horrea*) labor. A **second-century population
explosion** pushed single-family houses (*domus*) to give way to apartment blocks (*insulae*,
*caseggiati*); the *domus* regained ground only in the late 3rd–4th centuries, by which point no
new Mithraic sanctuaries were being built. The imperial *horrea* were "abandoned in favor of
**Portus** about the middle of the third century," after which the town's wealth and population
declined. Around the Forum stood the three monuments of imperial power — the Capitol, the temple
to Roma and Augustus, and the Pantheon — while the new religious dynamism, in Laeuchli's reading,
had moved off the main street (*Decumanus Maximus*) and "into the intimacy of houses."

## Religious Pluralism

Ostia was densely packed with shrines: a Serapeum, a Bona Dea temenos, a Temple of Hercules
(*Invictus*), the [[magna-mater-cybele|Magna Mater]]–Attis–Bellona temenos, household *lararia*,
and a Round Temple, alongside the [[mithraism|mithraea]], a synagogue, and (by the 3rd–4th c.)
Christian material. Laeuchli reads the city as "a graphic archaeological demonstration" of the
**"religious bankruptcy of ancient Rome"** and a rapid "Eastern infiltration": within roughly a
century (mid-2nd to mid-3rd) the seaport "was converted to Oriental cults, especially to Mithra."

## The Fourteen Mithraea

Fourteen well-preserved mithraea were studied (in chronological order): the **Animals, Seven
Doors, Planta Pedis, House of Diana, Painted Walls, Seven Spheres, Imperial Palace, Lucretius
Menander, Baths of Mithra, Fructosus, Felicissimus, Sabazeo, Porta Romana, and Serpents**.
(The Sacello of the Three Naves is explicitly **not** counted as a mithraeum.)

- **Typical form** (Groh): a rectangular hall entered from the rear — never opening directly on a
  main street; a central aisle (often mosaic-paved, sometimes with floor basins); two reclining
  *podia* along the sides; and a worship center at the front of altar + *bema* + a niche holding
  the **tauroctonos**. "It is almost certain that every Mithraeum had the tauroctonos figure as
  its central object."
- **No developmental sequence**: across ~150 years of building (Animals ca. 160 CE → Serpents ca.
  250–300 CE) Groh found "no pattern which could reduce or systematize the diversity," attributing
  variation instead to adapted pre-existing buildings, economic inequality between congregations
  (the rude Sabazeo vs. the marble-lined Imperial Palace), and individual taste. **No building in
  Ostia was purpose-built as a mithraeum.**
- **The Felicissimus** preserves the famous aisle mosaic depicting the **seven grades** (see
  [[mithraism|the grade table]]); its street door was walled up.

## Topography and the Social Environment

The cult **originated in the south-central sector** near the Magna Mater temenos and spread in two
stages (northward into populated quarters in the late 2nd/early 3rd c., then south and east in the
3rd c.). Its most striking feature was an **even distribution across the city — including
non-residential horrea districts — for which "no other Ostian cult, Roman or Eastern, offers any
real parallel"** ("it looks as if a Mithraic board of evangelism had planned the distribution").

Inscriptional names (≈21 across the fourteen) point to a membership **primarily of slaves,
freedmen, and their descendants** — dock and warehouse workers — with a few patrons of means but
**no attested city magistrate or official**. Early (2nd-c.) mithraea sat on private property; later
(3rd-c.) ones moved into imperial *horrea*, public baths, and a collegiate temple — a shift
Schreiber reads as integration into mainstream city life that, by compromising the cult's
arcaneness, "may have contributed to the weakening of Mithraism's entire appeal." Laeuchli's
tentative census: perhaps ~1,000 Mithraists, ~2% of Ostia's population — comparable to Harnack's
estimate of ~3% Christians in 3rd-century Rome.

## Christianity at Ostia

The mithraeum is read by Laeuchli as a close structural parallel to the early Christian
**house church** — both a "transmutation of the temple religion for the sake of a new type of
communal experience." At Rome, the Santa Prisca mithraeum "stood beside a Christian sanctuary
until it was destroyed by the Christians." The full comparison is filed at
[[christianity-mithraism-parallels|Christianity–Mithraism]].

## Modern Identification

Ostia Antica is today an extensive excavated archaeological park about 25 km southwest of central
Rome, near the modern Tiber mouth in Lazio — the source of the *Scavi di Ostia* reports (Becatti)
on which the team study draws.
