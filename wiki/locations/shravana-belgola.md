# Shravana Belgola

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Shravana Belgola (Sravana Belgola) — "the White Lake of the Ascetics" in Kannada — is "a rural town of about ten thousand people situated in the southern state of Karnataka, roughly equidistant between the cities of Mysore and Bangalore" (ch. 7). It is the pre-eminent Digambara Jain pilgrimage site and "unquestionably" one of the two most famous Jain holy places in India (the other being [[mount-satrunjaya]] for the Svetambaras).

The town's defining topographical feature is "a small lake on each side of which lies a hill, known colloquially as the Big Hill and the Little Hill, although their classical names are Indragiri and Candragiri respectively" (ch. 7). The fame of the site rests above all on the colossal image of [[bahubali]] on Indragiri and on the ancient memorial landscape of Candragiri.

## The Image of Bahubali (Gommatesvara)

The site's principal feature is "a fifty-seven-feet-high image of [[bahubali|Bahubali]], according to the Digambaras the first person of this world age to achieve liberation, which stands on the summit of the Big Hill" (Indragiri; ch. 7). The colossal monument "was erected in **981** by Camundaraya, a general of one of the kings of the Ganga dynasty" (ch. 7).

The image "was unquestionably intended to allude to the accommodation which a Jain warrior had to make between the claims of his religion and his necessary engagement in acts of war, for Bahubali, the half-son of Rsabha, after defeating his half-brother Bharata in a duel, drew back from killing him and instead renounced the world and performed austerities in the forest" (ch. 7). The image portrays "the naked and motionless warrior ascetic fighting the solitary, spiritual battle, with arms hanging down by his sides in the *kayotsarga* position and with jungle creepers twining round them, while anthills cover the lower part of his legs and snakes slither at his feet" (ch. 7).

## The *Mastakabhiseka* (Head-Anointing Ceremony)

"The summit of the Big Hill has been since at least 1398 subject to astrological circumstances, but generally every ten to fifteen years the scene of one of the most spectacular of all Indian religious ceremonies, the *mastakabhiseka* ('head-anointment'), in which the head of Bahubali is anointed with a variety of substances, such as milk and liquefied saffron, poured from 1,008 pots by prominent members of the lay community who have bid for the privilege and who stand on a balustrade erected behind the image" (ch. 7). In recent years this event has drawn hundreds of thousands of pilgrims. "Novelties such as bombarding the image with flowers from a helicopter have been introduced" (ch. 7).

Historically the ceremony was not always regularly performed, depending on available funds. In **1887** a bhattaraka of Kolhapur performed it at the expense of 30,000 rupees. "Today, however, it is the State Government of Karnataka, with the assistance of the Digambara community, which is responsible for the management of the ceremony" (ch. 7).

Recorded recent ceremonies: 1612, 1659, 1677 (despite political disruption), and 1887. The bidding for the right to anoint the head is itself a major act of Jain lay religious patronage — see [[jain-puja]].

## Candragiri: The Little Hill

"It is the Little Hill, with its concentration of temples and memorials to ascetics and lay people greater than that found in any other Digambara holy spot, which remains the true goal of the committed pilgrim to Sravana Belgola" (ch. 7). The Little Hill was the original site of sanctity, long used as "a hallowed spot for the controlled religious death by fasting (*sallekhana*)" by generations of Digambara ascetics (ch. 7).

From about the seventh to the tenth centuries, the Little Hill accumulated *nisidhi* memorials — "stone reliefs, pillars, images and temples, which testify to the religious death of ascetics and, after the tenth century, lay people also" (ch. 7). Nameless ascetics scraped marks on the hill's summit surface to record the periods of their fasting. In the tenth century, hero stones (commemorating those who died violently in battle) were set up near the memorials to fasting ascetics, reflecting the site's attraction for the warrior class.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

**600 CE**: The earliest reference to Sravana Belgola is "an inscription of 600 CE on the Little Hill commemorating the supposed journey from the north of the great teacher **Bhadrabahu** and his disciples to escape famine" — an event traditionally linked to the origins of the Digambara sect (ch. 7). Dundas notes this claim is possibly anachronistic enhancement of the site's prestige rather than reliable history, but "the feature of this first inscription which has most bearing on the early history of Sravana Belgola is its commemoration of a monk, supposedly one of Bhadrabahu's followers, who fasted to death on the Little Hill" (ch. 7).

A "fissure in the rock of the Little Hill" was subsequently identified as "Bhadrabahu's Cave," likely as a further legitimating move (ch. 7).

**7th–10th centuries**: Period of the *nisidhi* memorials to ascetics on the Little Hill.

**10th century**: Camundaraya erects the colossal Bahubali image (981 CE) on the Big Hill. The site emerges as "the most important ritual centre of south Indian Jainism," with the founding of a monastery (*matha*) whose successive clerical heads (*bhattaraka*) "claimed descent from Camundaraya's guru, Nemicandra" (ch. 7). The tutelary goddess **Kusmandini** is promoted; according to legend she "appeared in the guise of an old woman to complete with the contents of a tiny pot the first ritual lustration of the image of Bahubali which Camundaraya had been unable to carry out fully" (ch. 7).

**12th century onward**: Large-scale lay endowments, including villages; continuous presence of monks of the **Mula Sangha**, the leading Digambara ascetic lineage. An inscription of **1118** describes a gathering of prominent monks apparently reconsecrating the Bahubali image on the Big Hill.

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

The concentration of inscriptional evidence at Sravana Belgola is among the richest for any Jain holy place. The *nisidhi* memorials (stone reliefs, pillars, images, temples) document the religious deaths of generations of ascetics and (after the tenth century) lay people. Hero stones commemorate warriors who died in battle. The inscriptional record tracks the shifting fortunes of the bhattaraka institution and monastic management of the site from the tenth through the nineteenth centuries.

## Modern Identification and Geography

Sravana Belgola is located in **Hassan district, Karnataka state**, southern India, approximately midway between Mysore (about 83 km) and Bangalore (about 144 km). It is a small rural town accessible by road. The two hills — Indragiri (Big Hill) and Candragiri (Little Hill) — and the lake between them remain the core of the sacred landscape. The Bahubali image atop Indragiri is visible from a considerable distance. (Approximate coordinates: 12.86°N 76.25°E.) The site draws pilgrims continuously and enormous crowds during the *mastakabhiseka*.

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

The site embodies the central Digambara virtue of *sallekhana* (the controlled religious death by fasting) — Candragiri is "a hallowed spot" where ascetics died the ideal death — alongside the renunciatory heroism depicted in the Bahubali colossus, which represents the ultimate Jain tension: military capacity transcended by spiritual renunciation.

Jain holy places more generally are "very often linked to the attainment of enlightenment or liberation by a fordmaker or some other ancient ascetic" rather than to relics (as in Buddhism), since "the fordmakers, having attained deliverance, are no longer physically accessible to humans" (ch. 7). Sravana Belgola is primarily the site of liberation and ascetic achievement, not of relic veneration.

The *mastakabhiseka* is also a major occasion for lay bidding — the competition to purchase the privilege of anointing the image — making Sravana Belgola a nexus of the Digambara values of lay piety, merit, and communal honour (ch. 7).

## Key Events, Figures, and Controversies

- **600 CE inscription**: earliest reference, commemorating Bhadrabahu connection and an ascetic's death by fasting.
- **7th–10th centuries**: accumulation of *nisidhi* memorials on the Little Hill.
- **981 CE**: Camundaraya erects the 57-foot Bahubali colossus on the Big Hill.
- **1118**: reconsecration of the Bahubali image; real fame of the Big Hill begins.
- **13th century onward**: gradual erosion of bhattaraka authority; evidence of financial irregularities.
- **17th century**: site attacked by Telugu chieftains; bhattaraka forced to flee; head-anointing ceremonies recorded 1612, 1659, 1677.
- **18th century**: zealous Muslim rulers placed interdict on the head-anointing ceremony.
- **1887**: bhattaraka of Kolhapur performs ceremony at cost of 30,000 rupees.
- **Today**: Karnataka state government manages the ceremony with Digambara community assistance.
