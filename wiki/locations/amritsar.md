# Amritsar

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Amritsar is the holiest city in Sikhism, situated in the Punjab of northwestern India near the modern Pakistan border. McLeod notes that "even Amritsar, the holiest of holies for the Sikhs, contains only a minority of Sikhs" — the Sikh population is concentrated in surrounding rural areas (McLeod ~L17–18).

The city arose as a new centre founded by the Sikh Gurus in the latter part of the 16th century. It has "ever since remained the chief centre of the Sikhs" (McLeod ~L1766).

## Founding

**Decision to excavate a sacred pool**: McLeod attributes the initial decision to Guru [[guru-amar-das|Amar Das]] (the third Guru, r. c. 1552–1574), who is "credited with the decision to excavate the sacred pool that marked the founding of Amritsar" (McLeod ~L1658–1659).

**Actual founding / excavation of the site**: The founding of the new centre is closely associated with **Guru [[guru-ram-das|Ram Das]]** (the fourth Guru, r. 1574–1581). "Guru Ram Das is very clearly associated with the founding of the new centre of Chak Guru or Ramdaspur, later to be known as Amritsar. The hostility of the sons of Guru Amar Das is said to have been responsible for the move from Goindval. Ram Das established himself in the new location and began excavating the sacred pool" (McLeod ~L1754–1761).

**Completion**: The pool excavation and the original gurdwara at the centre of the pool were completed by the fifth Guru, **Guru Arjan** (r. 1581–1606). "First, he it was who completed the excavation of the pool known as Amritsar and built the original gurdwara located in its centre. The eighteenth-century gurdwara that now occupies the site is known as Harimandir Sahib, later to be called by the British the Golden Temple" (McLeod ~L1972–1978). Guru Arjan also set up a camp on the outskirts of Amritsar where the Adi Granth was compiled: "Guru Arjan apparently planned the enterprise carefully. A camp was set up on the outskirts of Amritsar and there the Guru had a pool excavated. Meanwhile he dictated the contents of the scripture to his amanuensis, the celebrated Bhai Gurdas" (McLeod ~L2036–2039).

## Primary Textual Appearances and Key Events

- **Guru Ram Das**: Founded Chak Guru / Ramdaspur; began pool excavation (c. 1574–1581)
- **Guru Arjan**: Completed the pool; built Harimandir Sahib (original structure); compiled the Adi Granth at Amritsar (c. 1603–1604); arrested by Mughal authorities in Lahore and died in Mughal custody (1606)
- **Guru Hargobind**: Built **Akal Takhat** ("throne of the Timeless One") facing Harimandir Sahib — the seat of temporal authority in the Panth, as distinct from Harimandir's spiritual authority (McLeod ~L2211–2221); later abandoned Amritsar for the Shivalik hills under Mughal pressure
- **Mughal skirmishes**: Following Shahjahan's accession (1628), a hawk of the emperor's was captured by Guru Hargobind's followers near Amritsar, leading to fighting. Another skirmish followed near Sri Hargobindpur (McLeod ~L2427–2436)

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

Amritsar is the principal pilgrimage site and sacred centre of Sikhism. The Harimandir Sahib (Golden Temple) enshrines the Guru Granth Sahib and represents the spiritual heart of the tradition. The Akal Takhat nearby embodies temporal/community authority. Together the two sites enact the Sikh concept of **miri-piri** (spiritual and temporal authority existing in creative tension), though McLeod notes this terminology may be a later interpretive overlay (McLeod ~L2244–2308).

The city's name "Amritsar" means "Pool of the Nectar of Immortality" (*amrit* = nectar of immortality; *sar* = pool), a name that encapsulates its sacred significance as a place where the divine grace embodied in [[nam-simaran|nam simaran]] becomes tangible.

## Key Controversies

- The relative roles of Guru Amar Das vs. Guru Ram Das in initiating the Amritsar project are slightly ambiguous in McLeod's account: he credits the *decision* to Amar Das and the *founding and excavation* to Ram Das. This is consistent but may reflect different layers of tradition.
- The **historicity of the miri-piri doctrine** associated with the Akal Takhat is contested: orthodox Sikh tradition treats it as a deliberate theological innovation by Guru Hargobind; McLeod argues the terminology is likely a later development. See [[miri-piri|Mīrī-Pīrī]] and [[who-is-a-sikh|Who is a Sikh?]].

## The Eighteenth Century: Desecration and Rebuilding (Khushwant Singh, Vol. 1)

Khushwant Singh's *History of the Sikhs* makes Amritsar the barometer of Sikh fortunes in the persecution
era. Under [[guru-hargobind|Hargobind]] the **Akāl Taḵẖt** and the Lohgarh fort were built facing the
Harimandir. As the religious-political centre of the [[misls|Sarbat Khalsa]], the Harimandir was repeatedly
destroyed by [[ahmad-shah-abdali|Ahmad Shah Abdali]] — blown up and its pool fouled with cow carcasses in
1757, 1762 (the [[ghallughara|Vaḍā Ghallūghārā]]), and 1764, the last defended to the death by thirty Sikhs —
and each time rebuilt by the Sikhs, the martyr [[deep-singh|Baba Deep Singh]] dying in the attempt of 1757.
When [[ranjit-singh|Ranjit Singh]] took the city (c. 1803), Singh notes that "anyone who aspired to be the
leader of the Khalsa and Maharajah of the Punjab had to take Amritsar to make good his title"; the Maharajah
seized the great gun Zam Zama and "made a grant for the temple to be rebuilt in marble and covered with gold
leaf" — the gilding that gave the **Golden Temple** its name — later adding a bejewelled canopy sent by the
Nizam of Hyderabad.
