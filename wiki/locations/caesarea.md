# Caesarea

> **Mismatch note:** This is **Caesarea Maritima**, Herod's coastal port and the Roman
> capital of Judea. It is **not** Caesarea Philippi (Banias), the northern site of Peter's
> confession in the Gospels. [[darrell-bock|Bock]] treats the two as entirely distinct.

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Caesarea Maritima was built by **Herod the Great** on the site of the older anchorage
"Strato's Tower," with a great artificial harbor, and named for **Caesar** (Augustus).
[[darrell-bock|Bock]] ([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) describes it as the **Roman
provincial capital, the seat of the procurators of Judea**, and notes it was
"mostly Gentile-inhabited" (citing Josephus, *Ant.* 15.9.6; *J.W.* 3.9.1), furnished with
an amphitheater, a hippodrome, and a temple to Caesar. Bock also records that the
**Pilate inscription** — the epigraphic attestation of the prefect Pontius Pilate — was
found there. In the [[acts|Acts]] narrative Caesarea recurs more than almost any other
named city, functioning as the coastal gateway between Judea and the wider empire.

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

- **Philip's home base (8:40; 21:8).** After the Ethiopian eunuch episode
  [[philip-the-evangelist|Philip the evangelist]] preaches along the coast and settles at
  Caesarea, where Paul later lodges with him and his "four unmarried daughters, who
  prophesied" (21:8–9). Bock notes Philip is still there "twenty years later."
- **Cornelius's city (10:1–48).** Caesarea is the setting for what Bock calls "one of the
  most important units in Acts" and "the book's turning point": the conversion of the
  centurion **[[cornelius|Cornelius]]** of the "Italian Cohort," a
  [[god-fearers|God-fearer]], and the first Gentile household to receive the gospel and
  the Spirit (the "Pentecost of the Gentile world," Bock citing Bruce). Bock stresses the
  event is "coordinated by God," not decided by the Twelve.
- **Herod Agrippa I's death (12:19–23).** After the Passover escape of Peter, Herod
  **[[herod-agrippa-i|Agrippa I]]** goes down to Caesarea, delivers a public oration, is
  hailed "The voice of a god, and not a man," and — because he "accepts the praise… does
  not give God the glory" — is struck down and "eaten by worms." Bock cross-references
  Josephus (*Ant.* 19.8.2) and dates the death to **March AD 44**, "one of the key dates"
  for Acts chronology.
- **Paul's two-year imprisonment and the governors' hearings (23:23–26:32).** Under armed
  escort Paul is transferred from Jerusalem to Caesarea, "the seat of the Felix/Festus/
  Agrippa hearings" (Bock). He is held in **Herod's praetorium**. Bock's exposition of the
  trials:
  - **Before [[felix|Felix]] (24:1–27).** The advocate Tertullus charges Paul with being
    "a seditious member of a dangerous sect"; Paul answers the "sect" (*hairesis*) charge
    by calling the movement "the Way." Bock notes Felix, married to **Drusilla** (daughter
    of Agrippa I), hoped for a bribe and left Paul imprisoned "to do the Jews a favor" —
    "not a flattering portrait of a ruler."
  - **Before [[festus|Festus]] (25:1–12).** When Festus proposes moving the case to
    Jerusalem — where leaders plan an ambush — Paul **appeals to Caesar** (the
    *provocatio* of a Roman citizen). Bock: Roman law "unwittingly" protects Paul.
  - **Before Agrippa II and Bernice (25:13–26:32).** Bock calls this "the fullest
    discussion in Acts of Paul's situation," held "with great pomp"; it contains Paul's
    "last major discourse," the third telling of his conversion, and the third of a
    sequence of innocence declarations ("nothing to deserve death or imprisonment"). Only
    the prior appeal keeps Paul from release.

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock cites the **Pilate inscription** discovered at Caesarea as extra-biblical
attestation of the Roman prefecture, and repeatedly cross-references **Josephus** on the
city's Gentile character and on Herod Agrippa I's death there.

## Modern Identification

The site is the excavated **Caesarea (Qisarya)** national park on the Israeli
Mediterranean coast, with remains of the Herodian harbor, theater, and later Roman and
Byzantine construction. It must be distinguished from **Caesarea Philippi** in the north.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

For Bock, Caesarea is where the Lukan themes of **Gentile inclusion** and **Roman
protection of an innocent gospel** converge: the Cornelius breakthrough opens the mission
to the nations, and the governors' hearings repeatedly declare Christianity "a threat
neither to Rome nor to any state," an in-house dispute "about a certain Jesus, who was
dead but whom Paul asserted to be alive." From Caesarea Paul sails toward Rome, "the goal"
of the narrative.
