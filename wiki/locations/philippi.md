# Philippi

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Philippi was a **Roman colony** in Macedonia — "the highest status a provincial city could
have," which [[darrell-bock|Bock]] ([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) glosses as "a little
Rome," noting that roughly 80% of its inscriptions are in Latin (Witherington). Its Jewish
presence was minimal (only women are mentioned at the riverside "place of prayer"). In
[[acts|Acts]] Philippi is the **first European city evangelized** and the site of three
paradigmatic conversions (16:11–40); Bock observes that here "Paul encounters his first
major legal trouble with the Romans."

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

Bock divides the Philippi narrative into four subunits: the journey and Lydia (16:11–15),
the possessed slave-girl (16:16–24), the jailer (16:25–34), and the magistrates
(16:35–40).

- **[[lydia|Lydia]] — the first convert (16:13–15).** At a riverside "place of prayer"
  (Bock inclines, with Schnabel, to read it as a small synagogue) Paul meets **Lydia**, a
  "dealer in purple cloth" (*porphyropōlis*, a woman of status from Thyatira) and a
  **God-fearer**. Bock: "The Lord opened her heart to accept" — "God creates the initiative
  to faith from within." She and her household are baptized, and her home "becomes a center
  for the new faith."
- **The slave-girl and the exorcism (16:16–18).** A slave-girl with a "spirit of
  divination" (a *python* spirit, tied to Delphic soothsaying) earns her owners profit by
  fortune-telling; she proclaims Paul and Silas "servants of the Most High God" — a title
  Bock notes is "ambiguous in a polytheistic environment" and "potentially misleading."
  Paul, "annoyed," exorcises her "in the name of Jesus Christ." Bock (with Williams) notes
  the unit crosses "all gender, ethnic, and social barriers" — a religious woman, a
  possessed slave-girl, and a secular soldier.
- **Roman legal trouble and the jailer (16:19–34).** The owners, having lost their profit,
  charge Paul and [[silas|Silas]] with "disturbing our city" and "advocating customs… not
  lawful for Romans" — an ethnic/Roman-pride charge Bock calls the "first formal indictment
  against Paul in Acts." The two are beaten with rods and jailed in the inner prison, feet
  in stocks. An earthquake opens the doors; the **jailer**, about to kill himself, is
  stopped and asks, "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" Bock reads the answer — "Believe in
  the Lord Jesus… and you will be saved, you and your household" — as "the core of the
  kerygma." The jailer, a "pure Gentile," and his household are baptized at once.
- **The Roman-citizen protest (16:35–40).** When the magistrates order a quiet release,
  Paul reveals that he and Silas are **Roman citizens** who were beaten "publicly… without
  a trial." Bock explains the delayed disclosure as strategic — Paul "made a strategic
  decision not to mention it during the judgment because it would confuse the understanding
  of his loyalties" — and its purpose as protecting the Philippian believers and making the
  faith's innocence "publicly" clear. He notes that under Roman law (the Valerian, Porcian,
  and Julian statutes) it "is against Roman law to cane a Roman citizen," and that a false
  citizenship claim carried execution, so the detail "is unlikely to be fabricated."

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock reports that the **bēma (judgment seat)** has been excavated at Philippi and that the
magistrates styled themselves *duoviri/praetores*. He defends the historicity of the
earthquake and exorcism narrative against Haenchen's skepticism, citing Rapske, Omerzu,
and Sherwin-White on the accuracy of the Roman-citizen and appeal background.

## Modern Identification

The ruins lie near **Krinides**, in eastern Macedonia (northern Greece); the ancient port
of Neapolis is modern **Kavala**. Bock does not develop specific monument identifications
beyond the excavated bēma.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

Bock reads Philippi as a showcase of the gospel's breadth: the two framing conversions —
"a pious woman (Lydia)" and "a 'pure Gentile' jailer," plus the liberated slave-girl —
gather (in Stott's words that Bock endorses) "few people more diverse ethnically, socially,
psychologically, and culturally," under "a gospel intended for all." The city also opens
the recurring Acts 16–18 pattern in which "Christians are brought before officials, are
accused, and are told to stop preaching… but the mission continues."

## Hansen (PNTC Philippians) — colonial setting for the letter (2026-07-18)

Attributed to [[g-walter-hansen|G. Walter Hansen]] ([[hansen-philippians-pntc]]). Complements Bock’s Acts narrative with the letter’s social-theological frame.

- Named for **Philip II** (fortified 356 B.C.); Hellenizing legacy via Alexander/koine.
- After the **42 B.C.** battle, a **Roman colony** for veterans; under Augustus renamed **Colonia Iulia Augusta Philippensis**; *ius Italicum*, tax exemption, Latin as official language; modeled on Rome.
- **Imperial cult** the most prominent cultic focus; withdrawal viewed as subversive.
- Acts 16 founding (Hansen’s date ~A.D. 49): Via Egnatia from Neapolis; no synagogue quorum of ten men; riverside prayer meeting; [[lydia|Lydia]]; slave-girl; magistrates; shared suffering with Paul (Phil 1:30).
- First church membership largely **Gentile**; little Jewish Scripture quotation in the letter; women leaders ([[lydia]], [[euodia-and-syntyche|Euodia and Syntyche]]) culturally plausible.
- Payoff for reading Philippians: palace guard / Caesar’s household (1:13; 4:22); heavenly [[citizenship|*politeuma*]] (1:27; 3:20); *kyrios*/Savior vs imperial titles; opponents and cultural pressure (3:18–19).
