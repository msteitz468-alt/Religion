# Capernaum

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Capernaum is the lakeside town in [[galilee|Galilee]] to which, according to
[[matthew|Matthew's Gospel]] (4:13), Jesus moved from [[nazareth|Nazareth]] and which becomes
the base of the Galilean ministry. [[rt-france|R. T. France]] (*The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT,
2007) describes it as a local administrative centre — evidenced by the centurion at 8:5 and the
customs post at 9:9 — whose first-century "population… was perhaps as high as ten thousand,"
substantially bigger than Nazareth, and traditionally Jewish, unlike Hellenistic Tiberias.

The town lay in the old tribal territory of Naphtali while Nazareth lay in Zebulun; France notes
that these "tribal areas had little actual relevance by NT times" and that "Matthew combines the
two tribes in order to echo Isaiah's prophecy."

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

- **Matt 4:13-16** — the relocation, and the Isaiah [[formula-quotations|formula quotation]].
  France reads *kataleipō* + *katoikeō* as indicating "a decisive move away to a new home."
- **Matt 8:5** — the centurion. France notes that "there was no Roman legion stationed in
  Palestine at this time," but that [[herod-antipas|Herod Antipas]] had a small force of
  auxiliary troops drawn from the non-Jewish population of surrounding areas such as Phoenicia
  and Syria, so both centurion and servant are non-Jewish.
- **Matt 9:9** — the customs post.
- **Matt 11:23-24** — the woe on Capernaum.
- **Matt 12:9** — "their synagogue," probably Capernaum's.
- **Matt 13:1** — France identifies the setting of the parable discourse as "probably intended
  to be the shore at Capernaum, where Jesus' home now was," a placement he hedges as probable.
- **Matt 13:54-58** — by contrast, at Nazareth "their synagogue" is, France says, no longer
  "his" precisely because of the move to Capernaum: "'Their synagogue' need have no pejorative
  connotation… It simply denotes the synagogue of the people whom he is teaching."

France reads Matthew's "their synagogue" at 12:9 differently, holding that "Matthew's use of the
possessive probably begins to hint at the growing rift between Jesus and the synagogue
establishment," adding wryly that the [[pharisees|Pharisaic]] group "may not have been reluctant
to have it described as 'theirs'!"

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

The France commentary as digested supplies no direct archaeological detail on Capernaum itself —
no discussion of the excavated synagogue, the insula, or the house tradition — and this page does
not supplement it. The only adjacent archaeological reports France makes are that "archeology has
shown both [Chorazin and Bethsaida] to have been substantial communities comparable in size and
importance to Capernaum," Chorazin being less than an hour's walk away and Bethsaida lying
outside Galilee in Philip's territory; and the first-century boat recovered at Ginosar in 1986.
The population estimate of "perhaps as high as ten thousand" is reported by France without a
stated evidential basis in the digested material.

## Modern Identification and Geography

The commentary does not treat the modern identification of the site. France's geography is
relational and first-century: a busy lakeside fishing and administrative town on the northern
shore, near Chorazin, in a traditionally Jewish part of the province rather than near Sepphoris
and Tiberias. On the body of water he notes that Matthew, Mark and John call the freshwater lake
a "sea" (reflecting the OT *yām-kinneret*) while Luke and Josephus "more correctly" say "Lake";
France translates "lake" throughout except at 4:15, judging it "less misleading to modern
readers."

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

**The dawning light (4:13-16).** The move to Capernaum is what prompts Matthew's insertion of one
of his longest formula quotations, from Isa 8:23–9:1, which — in France's reading — "identifies
'Galilee of the nations' as the place where the true light is to shine." France judges the
quotation abbreviated and "in a form closer to the Hebrew than to the LXX, but not fully
corresponding to the sense of either"; by dropping the verbs Matthew produces a string of
geographical terms that fill out the identity of "the people who sat in darkness," and "his
abbreviation throws the focus on the geographical terms which are appropriate to Jesus' new home
in Capernaum" rather than Nazareth. France also observes that the abbreviation shifts the syntax:
in Isaiah three terms stand in apposition contrasted with Zebulun and Naphtali, whereas "Matthew's
abbreviation has put all four terms in apposition." He flags as remarkable that the great
messianic prophecy of Isa 9:6 which immediately follows the quoted verses "is remarkably never
directly referred to in the NT" — see [[isaiah|Isaiah]].

**Privilege and judgment (11:23-24).** France flags the irony directly: "It is sobering to note,
however, that even Capernaum, favored with so much of Jesus' presence, will be denounced as
unresponsive." In the woe itself he identifies "an unmistakable echo of Isa 14:13-15," the taunt
against the king of Babylon, invoked "not because of any specific equivalence, but as a
proverbial example of pride going before a fall" — and he concedes that the comparison "seems
remote," since Capernaum was no imperial power, was strongly Jewish, and "we have no information
to enable us to identify any such desire on its part to 'be exalted to heaven.'" On the
comparisons with Sodom generally, France insists they are "designed to emphasize the guilt of the
Jews who failed to respond to Jesus rather than to pronounce on the destiny of the Gentile
peoples."

The two poles together give Capernaum its structural place in France's Galilee reading: the town
that receives the most light in ch. 4 is the town most sharply denounced in ch. 11.

## Key Events, Figures, and Controversies

- **"Way of the sea" (4:15).** France judges that Matthew understood the phrase of the Lake of
  Galilee, but that "it is more likely that Isaiah was referring to the major route from Damascus
  to the Mediterranean" (later the Via Maris) — i.e. Matthew reapplies the phrase. Recorded as
  France's position.
- **"Beyond the Jordan" (4:15) — unresolved.** The phrase normally means the east side (as at
  4:25), but here Matthew clearly means the west. France raises but does not answer the question:
  "Did he then understand Isaiah to be speaking not from his own Palestinian standpoint but from
  that of the Assyrian invader?" He separately reports and rejects the argument that the phrase
  supports a Trans-Jordanian provenance for the gospel, calling it "a lot to load onto a phrase
  included in a quotation from Isaiah."
- **Why leave Nazareth?** France's two reasons — a more public platform, and "escaping the
  suspicion attaching to a local boy who becomes a celebrity" — are recorded on
  [[nazareth|Nazareth]].
- **The woes and the earlier enthusiasm summaries.** See [[galilee|Galilee]] for France's two
  canvassed explanations and his preference.

## Capernaum in Mark: France's NIGTC Reading (2002)

From the full re-ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of Mark* (NIGTC, 2002) —
see [[france-mark-nigtc]]:

- **The "day in Capernaum" (Mk 1:21-39).** France reads this as a single 24-hour day
  constructed as a graphic overview of the ministry, combining teaching, exorcism, healing,
  and proclamation: "This 'day in Capernaum' combines within it all the main features of
  Jesus' Galilean ministry."
- **Base of operations.** France notes that in Mark too Capernaum, not
  [[nazareth|Nazareth]], was Jesus' base, and that Mark records no visit to the Hellenistic
  cities Sepphoris and Tiberias; the response to Jesus came from the traditionally Jewish
  lakeshore population of [[galilee|Galilee]].
