# Ayodhyā

**Ayodhyā** ("the invincible" or "the unconquerable") is the capital city of the solar dynasty (*sūrya-vaṃśa*) in the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]], ruled by King [[dasaratha|Daśaratha]] and subsequently by [[rama|Rāma]]. It is the epic's primary setting for dharmic kingship, royal sacrifice, festive celebration, and the pivotal crisis of the two boons. In Hindu tradition it is one of the seven sacred cities (*sapta-mokṣadāyinī* — the seven places that grant liberation).

## Geographical and Historical Overview

### Physical Description in Buck's Retelling

Kuśa and Lava open their Rāmāyaṇa performance with a description of Ayodhyā addressed directly to Rāma (lines 1231–1265):

> "On the banks of the Sarayu river is Fair Ayodhya, the royal capital of Kosala. She is a fabled city, famed among men, **twelve leagues long and ten wide**, with Sala trees, filled with grain and gold. Heaven is fair, Ayodhya is fairer; Heaven is cool in summer, but the Kosala hills are better." (lines 1231–1235)

The city was built on rising ground: "the tall white Ayodhya palace built atop a rising hill" (lines 1237–1238). The Sarayu River ran alongside and was "filled with boats" (line 1251). The land was prosperous and orderly: "the Kosalas were well-fed and healthy," every cow's horns were covered with silver rings and gold bands, and "every man could keep what he had in peace and gain what more he wanted" (lines 1249–1254).

### Urban Character

The city described in the song is wealthy, cosmopolitan, and splendid:

- "Three- and seven-storied mansions lined the wide straight streets" (lines 1257–1259)
- "Long-tusked elephants walked the streets wearing bells on their necks" (lines 1260–1261)
- "There were rows of full shops with open doors, pale white palaces, and lordly trees; rattling chariots drove by and there was music; foreign caravans came bringing merchants and rich tribute from lesser kings" (lines 1261–1265)
- "Flowers grew all over" (line 1260)
- "The Kosalas had no enemies and Ayodhya was unconquerable" (lines 1259–1260)

The warriors of Ayodhyā were formidable but untested by any foe: "her warriors were impatient and deadly to foes. Each man of them could alone defeat ten thousand chariots, but no one came against them" (lines 1268–1271). Daśaratha's ministers were "loyal and wise, able to find hidden motives and fight with words, cautious, never binding themselves to a lie, acting by their word, and sending out spies to report what took place in all the world" (lines 1272–1275).

### The Sarayu River

The **Sarayu** River (modern Ghaghara) is consistently the city's defining geographical feature. Vasiṣṭha chose the Sarayu's bank as the site for the sacrificial fires that produced Rāma's birth (line 1289). When Viśvāmitra led Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa out of the city, "just outside the white walls of Fair Ayodhya they stopped by the Sarayu River" (lines 1657–1658) for Viśvāmitra to give Rāma his first mantras. At the end of these lines, Nārāyaṇa (Viṣṇu) speaks of being called "to Ayodhya by the sleepy Sarayu" (line 1461).

## Primary Textual Appearances

### As the Site of the Horse Sacrifice

The events leading to Rāma's birth unfold in Ayodhyā: Daśaratha's desire for sons, Vasiṣṭha's arrangement of the sacrifice, and the appearance of the divine messenger from the fire (lines 1280–1496). The sacrifice by the Sarayu is the mechanism through which Viṣṇu descends to earth.

### As the Hub of Righteous Kingship

During the twelve years of Rāma and Sītā's marriage, Rāma gradually took over the kingdom's work, and the description of his style of rule is embedded in descriptions of the city's wellbeing:

> "He collected the King's taxes so that over half the people didn't really mind paying him. He was a remarkable prince and every Kosala loved him except for five or six fools." (lines 3031–3034)

Sumantra the charioteer described to the council how Rāma moved through the city: "I have seen Rama riding by the fields and as a father enquiring after his well-loved sons, he will ask the teachers — Do your students obey you?" (lines 3110–3112). He knew all their names. "He asks of our shops, and wishes well to our sons, to our fires, to our wives and animals" (lines 3124–3125).

### The Eve of the Coronation

On the night before Rāma's planned coronation, Ayodhyā erupted in festival. The entire city celebrated: "the Ayodhya marketplace was impassable. Music and cheerful noise poured out into all the streets from all the houses. The lame broke their crutches and danced; sick people got up out of bed; misers gave away gold; the King's kitchen served out free food, butter and roasts and steaming rice" (lines 3224–3229). Crystal lamp-trees were set up at roads and crossings. The palace glowed. The description is almost utopian in its register — which makes the crisis that follows (Manthara's manipulation, Kaikeyī's boons) all the more devastating.

### As the Place Rāma Must Leave

The departure from Ayodhyā — announced at the end of these lines — is the pivot of the entire epic. Rāma accepts exile: "I will go today out from Ayodhya for fourteen years. I will return to say farewell before I leave" (lines 3431–3432). Kaikeyī's demand is precisely that Rāma exchange Ayodhyā — whose people he knows by name, whose soil he has walked — for the forest.

## Modern Identification and Geography

The modern city of Ayodhyā in **Uttar Pradesh, India**, situated on the Sarayu (Ghaghara) River approximately 135 km east of Lucknow, has been identified as the city of the epic since at least the medieval period. In Buddhist sources the same city was known as **Sāketa** and was a significant urban center in the Kosala kingdom. Coordinates: approximately 26.795°N 82.195°E.

The Ram Janmabhoomi site — traditionally identified as Rāma's birthplace — became the center of a major political and religious controversy in modern India. See the relevant controversies page and the discussion in [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]] (Modern Indian Politics section).

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

### Seat of Solar Dharma

Ayodhyā is not merely a city but the seat of the solar dynasty's cosmic function. The gods from the air "saw Kosala to be clear as a mirror, with no least touch of evil to make any black shadow over the land" (lines 1240–1246). The city's prosperity, its cow-horns covered in gold, its full shops, its invincible warriors — all of these are signs of a world in cosmic right order, a dharmic kingdom. The description belongs to the same conceptual cluster as the first yuga's abundance: plenty flowing from righteousness without effort.

### Rāma-Rāj (Rāma's Rule)

The concept of **Rāma-rāj** — Rāma's reign as the ideal of just and prosperous governance — is grounded in the physical city of Ayodhyā. The twelve years of Rāma and Sītā's married life in the city, and the description of how Rāma governed it, provide the textual basis for the later political tradition of invoking Rāma's rule as a model of righteous kingship. Rāma "didn't give good advice and tell others what he thought best and show them their mistakes. He knew when to save and when to spend. . . . Half a benefit was more to him than a hundred injuries" (lines 3017–3023).

### "Heaven Is Fairer"

The specific claim in the opening song — "Heaven is fair, Ayodhya is fairer" (line 1234) — inverts the standard religious hierarchy. Ayodhyā under a righteous solar king is not merely a symbol of heaven; it surpasses it, because it is the place where the avatar walks embodied among his people.

## The Triumphant Return and Coronation (Lines 13566–16313)

### The Return

Rāma returns to Ayodhyā by the flying chariot Pushpaka after fourteen years. The city had been kept
in mourning during Rāma's absence: Bharata sat at the village of Nandigrama in barkcloth at the foot
of the Ayodhyā throne — which held only Rāma's wooden sandals, painted with flowers (l. 14368–14372).
No one in Kosala "wore good clothes or any color" during the exile except the women, "because Sita did
not leave her robes and ornaments behind to go into the forest" (l. 14374–14376). The Revels Master —
kept in abeyance for fourteen years — reopens the wine vaults the morning of Rāma's return (l. 14453–14460).

The city's revival is depicted in near-rapturous lyrical prose: "gay silk pennons and streamers were
unfurled. Musicians restrung their silent lutes; drivers put the wheels back on the Kosala chariots.
They dressed and painted the royal white elephant, and Fair Ayodhya threw open her gates and waited
for her Lord Rama" (l. 14463–14466). The celebration included men, monkeys, and bears jammed into
wineshops "shoulder to shoulder, trading drinks and stories and lots of loose talk" (l. 14610–14611).

### The Coronation

The coronation takes place in the royal park with all assembled — animals, demons, men — gathered on
the grass. Vasishtha pours waters from all four seas and the rivers of the world over Rāma's head
(l. 14680–14697): "made him King and Lord of Earth; the Solar King of Fair Ayodhya, the Kosala Lord,
and Sita was his one Queen with him" (l. 14697–14698).

Sita is given a smooth pearl necklace by Rāma, which she immediately gives to Hanumān: "This gift is
for your courage and strength, your valor and bravery and skill, and for your faithful service" (l. 14708–14710).

### Ayodhya Under Rāma's Reign: The Idealized Description

Buck inserts a lyrical meditation on Ayodhyā under Rāma's rule (l. 14467–14524) that functions as
the fullest description of the city's character as an ideal:

> "Behold! Fair Ayodhya! the city of my dreams… No war was there; the war-songs were made into dances,
> and the marches were for parades for the children. In Ayodhya you could walk anywhere, anytime, free
> and unafraid. You could love openly, and couples embraced in daylight in the gardens." (l. 14467–14472)

> "Fair Ayodhya, my old home; the city I love, my own free city; you are very dear to me. You are the
> home of friendship and alive, gleaming with lamps at night. You are where the great roads cross, and
> people traveling often stay here and go no farther. Fair Ayodhya, my love to you where the food is
> good and no man poor." (l. 14515–14521)

This idealization is central to the concept of → [[rama-raj|Rāma-rāj]]: Ayodhyā is the earthly
instantiation of the ideal dharmic kingdom. See also the description of the reign: old never buried
young; rain was fertile; bad things did not happen; men grew kind and fearless (l. 14726–14735).

### Ayodhya as the Site of Sita's Banishment

The same city is also the site of Rāma's most contested decision. Within the ideal reign, Rāma learns
that the people gossip about Sīta's captivity in Lanka (l. 14812–14847) and banishes her while she is
pregnant. Ayodhyā — the city of freedom and openness — is also the source of the political pressure
that destroys Rāma's family. The tragedy is explicitly civic: "A king must be blameless. People tread
in their king's footsteps, so I must avoid even the report of any wrong" (l. 14818–14820).

### After Rāma's Departure

After Rāma enters the Sarayu River and returns to Viṣṇu's heaven, Ayodhyā empties: "Fair Ayodhya was
empty of men for many long years, and much of her wealth and beauty was lost forever to the trees and
vines that buried her. Kusa and Lava had sons, and their sons had children, and later there were Kosala
kings again in Ayodhya. But today she is a city much like any other" (l. 16289–16293). The narrator
adds: "I think the Fair Ayodhya that Rama knew must now be sought for elsewhere than on this Earth.
Those were other days. But though I must dream or die to do it — I've been to Fair Ayodhya and I'll
go back there again" (l. 16059–16062).
