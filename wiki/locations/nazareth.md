# Nazareth

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Nazareth is the [[galilee|Galilean]] village in which, on the account of
[[matthew|Matthew's Gospel]], Jesus was brought up (2:23) and which he later left for
[[capernaum|Capernaum]] (4:13). [[rt-france|R. T. France]] (*The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT,
2007) characterizes it as "the rather remote hill village in which he had been brought up" and,
more fully, as "an obscure Jewish village in the Galilean hills, rapidly being overshadowed by
the growing Hellenistic city of Sepphoris" — four miles away, which [[herod-antipas|Antipas]]
rebuilt as Galilee's capital. France judges it probably smaller than Bethlehem, without
historical connections, and likely founded late in the OT period.

France stresses a fact that becomes load-bearing for his reading of Matt 2:23: Nazareth "is
never mentioned in the OT or in any non-Christian Jewish writing" before a third- or
fourth-century inscription listing the priestly courses.

## Primary Textual Appearances and Inhabitants

- **Matt 2:19-23** — the settlement in Nazareth after the return from Egypt, sealed by the
  "he shall be called a Nazorean" formula (see below). France reads the move to Galilee as
  directed "not simply by prudence but also by divine guidance," ensuring the Bethlehem-born
  Davidic Messiah begins his career as "Jesus of (Galilean) Nazareth."
- **Matt 4:13** — the departure for Capernaum. France reads the verb combination *kataleipō* +
  *katoikeō* as indicating "a decisive move away to a new home," not a visit. He gives two
  reasons for leaving: a more public platform in a busy lakeside fishing town, "as well as
  escaping the suspicion attaching to a local boy who becomes a celebrity."
- **Matt 13:54-58** — the rejection at Nazareth, Matthew's only recorded return.

On the rejection episode France notes that it contains "the last mention of Jesus teaching in a
synagogue; from this point on Jesus will be seen increasingly outside the structures of
traditional Judaism." He reads the villagers' reaction as "the predictable scepticism of a small
village community" toward "the 'local boy made good,'" and specifies that "the problem is not so
much doubt over Jesus' ability to carry out any specific healing as scepticism as to his whole
image as a miracle-working 'man of God.'" The Nazarenes' "stumbling," he holds, is not the
drastic spiritual disaster of 5:29-30 or 13:21 but is "perhaps closer to
[[john-the-baptist|John the Baptist's]] 'stumbling' over Jesus' surprising style of being
Messiah (11:6)." France notes that Matthew "diplomatically avoids saying, as does Mark, that
Jesus could not do many miracles there" while preserving the effect of unbelief; and he draws a
lexical distinction, *apistia* ("unbelief") being used only of those who reject Jesus, whereas
[[discipleship|disciples]] are rebuked with the less absolute *oligopistia* ("little faith").

Matthew records the proverb at 13:57: "A prophet is not without honor except in his own homeland
and in his own household." France observes that the saying "has a proverbial sound," with several
parallels in ancient literature, particularly with reference to Greek philosophers, plus John
4:44 and Gospel of Thomas 31 (the latter expanded with a doctor saying he calls "hardly
self-evident").

On the inhabitants: France notes that in the village Jesus is known as the son of the *tektōn*
— "a general term for a 'constructor,'… probably… general building work including masonry as
well as woodwork." He suggests that Mark's calling Jesus himself "the carpenter" reflects
[[joseph|Joseph's]] death, "and Jesus, as the eldest son, had taken over the business until he
moved away." He reads the four brothers' names as "taken from the patriarchs of Genesis,
indicating the conservatively Jewish background of Jesus' own family," and infers that the
sisters, "having presumably married local men, have never moved away," while
[[mary|Mary]] and the brothers had gone down to Capernaum. France also notes the social
implausibility the village perceives: "the village carpenter is hardly the person to pose as a
distinguished teacher if he has not received any formal training" (cf. John 7:15).

## Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Data

France cites J. F. Strange (*ABD* 4:1050) for the archaeological estimate that Nazareth's
population was "a maximum of about 480 at the beginning of the 1st century A.D."

Beyond this figure and the priestly-courses inscription noted above, the France commentary as
digested supplies no archaeological detail on Nazareth — no excavation history, no discussion of
domestic architecture or of the venerated sites — and this page does not supplement it from
elsewhere.

## Modern Identification and Geography

The commentary is concerned with the first-century village and supplies no discussion of the
modern city or of the identification of the ancient site. France's only geographical placements
are relational: a hill village in Lower Galilee, four miles from Sepphoris, lying in the old
tribal territory of Zebulun (a tribal designation which, he notes, "had little actual relevance
by NT times" and which Matthew invokes only to echo Isaiah).

## Theological and Symbolic Significance

Nazareth's theological weight in France's reading is almost entirely a function of its
insignificance. Two moves depend on it.

**The "Nazorean" crux (2:23).** France stresses two formal peculiarities of this
[[formula-quotations|formula quotation]]: the plural "the prophets" and *hoti* in place of
*legontos*, which together suggest Matthew is citing "not a quotation of a specific passage but
rather a theme of prophecy" (cf. 26:56). Since the words occur nowhere in the OT or in any known
contemporary literature, "the search for a specific OT source for 'He shall be called a
Nazorean' is therefore likely to be futile." France canvasses and rejects the two standard
proposals:

- *nēṣer* ("branch," Isa 11:1), and the alternative *nṣr* ("watch, preserve") — rejected because
  they explain only *Nazōraios* and not "he shall be called," and because "such a wordplay is
  totally invisible in Greek, the language in which Matthew is writing."
- *naziraios* / the nazirite of Judg 13:5 — rejected on four grounds: the inexact echo, the
  unexplained change to "he will be called," Samson's amoral lifestyle as an unattractive
  messianic type, and the backfire at 11:18-19 where Jesus is contrasted with John's nazirite
  lifestyle. "Jesus was no nazirite, and it does not seem that anyone 'called' him that."

France's own "most promising approach" takes its cue from Nazareth's very nonexistence in
Scripture: to be "called a Nazorean" was to invite ridicule, "the name is in itself a term of
dismissal if not of actual abuse," the verb pointing to derogatory name-calling as at John 1:46
and Matt 26:71. He then locates the prophetic *theme* in "a less prominent but nonetheless
significant expectation of a Messiah who would be unrecognized" — Zech 9–14 (9:9-10; 11:4-14;
12:10; 13:7), the righteous-sufferer psalms (22, 69), and above all Isa 52:13–53:12, especially
53:1-3, plus Isa 49:7; John 7:27 shows the motif alive in the first century. His conclusion is
that the words represent the expectation of "a Messiah who came from the wrong place, who did not
conform to the expectations of Jewish tradition," the embarrassment of Nazareth thereby turned to
apologetic advantage.

France ends with an explicit admission of irresolution: "No solution to the exegetical problem
posed by 2:23 is straightforward," and if Matthew's readers shared a clearer understanding of
*Nazōraios*, "it is not now available to us." That non-resolution is recorded here as his
position and is not adjudicated.

**Nazareth in the Galilee/Judea polarity.** France reads Matthew 2 as linking the Messiah's
Judean birth to his Galilean domicile, and takes the "he should be called a Nazarene" motif as
reflecting "the dismissive tone of a superior Judean observer" — Nazareth thus functioning as
the sharpest point of the sociopolitical contrast set out on [[galilee|Galilee]].

## Key Events, Figures, and Controversies

- **The 2:23 formula quotation.** Positions canvassed by France (*nēṣer*, nazirite, his own
  "term of abuse" reading) are recorded above; he rejects the first two and leaves the question
  formally open.
- **The rejection at 13:54-58.** France places Nazareth "alongside Chorazin, Bethsaida, and
  Capernaum (11:20-24) in the inglorious roll of communities which failed to repent." On the
  synoptic placement he holds that Luke "has moved it out of its natural place in the narrative
  … in order to use it as a 'frontispiece' for his account of the Galilean ministry," while
  Matthew and Mark retain its natural position — a [[synoptic-problem|synoptic]] judgment.
- **The imperfect "was teaching."** France offers two readings without choosing: an extended
  period of teaching, or a teaching begun and then interrupted by hostility, "as Luke's account
  does."
- **The brothers and sisters of Jesus.** France treats them as historical figures known to
  Matthew's readership — "certainly James (who was leader of the Jerusalem church until his
  martyrdom in A.D. 62) and probably the other brothers … were well-known members of the
  Christian community." Within this range he nowhere raises the question of Mary's perpetual
  virginity, referring the reader back to 1:18-25 for "the theological truth about Jesus' birth"
  while noting that "to the people of Nazareth he is simply Joseph's son." Recorded here as a
  negative finding of this ingest, not as a verdict on the question.

## Nazareth in Mark: France's NIGTC Reading (2002)

From the full re-ingest of [[rt-france|R. T. France]], *The Gospel of Mark* (NIGTC, 2002) —
see [[france-mark-nigtc]] — on the Marcan rejection scene (Mk 6:1-6):

- **A hill village against a lakeside ministry.** France holds that Mark's lakeside
  geographical coherence makes "the following visit to Nazareth, up in the hills, stand out
  still more starkly as a contrasting and disappointing experience." In Mark too Jesus' base
  was [[capernaum|Capernaum]], not Nazareth.
- **A christologically bolder rejection.** France finds Mark's "he could do no miracle there"
  (6:5) bolder than Matthew's softened parallel, suggesting "not even the ἐξουσία of Jesus is
  unlimited" in the face of unbelief (*apistia*); Jesus' surprise (6:6) underlines Mark's
  "human" portrait. (Cf. the Matthean diplomacy noted above.)
- **The family at 6:3.** France identifies the brother Jakōbos of 6:3 with James the Just,
  leader of the [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] church, killed A.D. 62 (Josephus, *Ant.* 20.200), and
  notes the family as a whole eventually joined the church (Acts 1:14; 1 Cor 9:5).
