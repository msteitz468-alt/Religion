# Athens

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Athens was the **intellectual capital** of the Greek world, a Roman-era free city in the
province of Achaia, still defined by its philosophical schools.
[[darrell-bock|Bock]] ([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) calls the Areopagus episode
"the most complete example of how Paul addresses a purely Gentile audience," and Fitzmyer
(whom Bock quotes) "the most important episode of Paul's second journey." In
[[acts|Acts]] the city hosts Paul's speech before the **Areopagus** (Acts 17:16–34), his
paradigmatic engagement with pagan philosophy.

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

- **The idol-full city (17:16–21).** Waiting for Silas and Timothy, Paul's "spirit was
  provoked" (a verb Bock notes is used of God's anger at idolatry) by the city's idols. He
  disputes in the synagogue and the marketplace with **Epicureans** (followers of Epicurus,
  "like agnostic secularists," indifferent to the gods) and **Stoics** (followers of Zeno,
  pantheists teaching "the unity of humanity and kinship with the divine"). Some call him a
  *spermologos* ("babbler," lit. a seed-picking bird) and "a preacher of foreign
  divinities" — Bock notes the parallel to the charge against Socrates. Bock reads the scene
  as **a discussion, not an arrest**: Paul stands "in the middle of the Areopagus" (the
  council with power over cults, education, and morality), and Luke gently satirizes the
  Athenians who "spend their time on the latest philosophical fad."
- **The Areopagus speech (17:22–31).** Bock's exposition of the
  [[speeches-in-acts|speech]]:
  - **The "unknown god" (v. 23).** The altar lets Paul "open the door" to the one Creator.
    Bock is emphatic that "Paul is not equating the god worshiped here… and the God he will
    preach," but uses the altar as a "segue" — a monument to polytheism "turned in a
    monotheistic direction" (Barrett).
  - **God as Creator and Sustainer (24–25).** God does not dwell in "handmade" shrines and
    "needs nothing"; Bock (with Polhill) insists "every statement Paul made was rooted in
    Old Testament thought," "not the immanent God of philosophy."
  - **The nations from one (26–27).** God "made from one" every nation and fixed their
    "allotted epochs and boundaries," so that people "should seek God" — the optatives
    ("grope," "find") marking mere possibility; Bock stresses "God will not be discovered
    through nature alone." Against the Stoic kinship idea, Paul says "there is a kinship at
    creation but that this is not enough"; one "must come to grips with God's revelation."
  - **The Aratus quotation, "we are his offspring" (v. 28).** Introduced as "some of your
    poets," the line (from **Aratus**, *Phaenomena*) is **contextualized**: "we are God's
    creation, we do not create him by making images," reframing the Greek "spark of the
    divine" as being "made as God's children by the Creator."
  - **Repentance and judgment (30–31).** God "overlooked" the "times of ignorance" but
    "now… commands all men everywhere to repent" — Bock: "To charge the Athenians with
    ignorance is a bold move… given their intellectual history." God "has fixed a day" to
    judge the world "through a man" (Jesus) whom he raised, the **resurrection** furnished
    "as proof to all."
- **The reaction (17:32–34).** At the mention of resurrection some **mock** (Bock notes the
  Greek conviction, "once he has died, there is no resurrection," Aeschylus); others say
  "we will hear you again" — which Bock (with Croy) reads as genuine openness, not a polite
  dismissal. Converts include **Dionysius the Areopagite** (a council member) and "a woman
  named Damaris"; Bock argues the rare, unattested name Damaris and the "meager success…
  speaks for the scene's authenticity."

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock treats the **altar to an unknown god** as historically credible against Haenchen's
skepticism (with Witherington and Schnabel), noting that singular altars of this class
"likely existed." He locates the speech at the **Areopagus / Mars Hill**, the seat of the
Athenian council.

## Modern Identification

Athens is continuously identified with the modern Greek capital; the **Areopagus (Mars
Hill)** is the rock outcrop below the Acropolis. Bock does not develop separate
topographical argument.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

Bock frames the speech as **natural (or "creation") theology that requires revelation to
complete it**, and connects it to the earlier pagan-preaching model at Lystra (14:15–17;
see [[natural-theology|natural theology]]). Against Vielhauer's claim that the Areopagus
Paul is un-Pauline (too unlike Romans 1), Bock replies that the difference is one of
audience and purpose — "the attempt to make a bridge to the culture" versus addressing
Christians "about the fallenness of the culture" — and that "the Paul of Rom. 1… is still
able to love and connect with that society."
