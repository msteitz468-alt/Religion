# Ephesus

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Ephesus was the leading city and effective capital of the Roman province of **Asia**, a
major trade center on the west coast of Asia Minor with a large, legally privileged
Jewish community (Bock, citing Trebilco). [[darrell-bock|Bock]]
([[bock-acts-becnt|*Acts*, BECNT]]) identifies it above all as the home of the shrine of
**Artemis** — "one of the seven wonders," a temple roughly four times the size of the
Parthenon and "the largest building in the Greek world," served by eunuchs and cultic
slaves. In [[acts|Acts]] Ephesus is **Paul's missionary center for the third journey**
(Bock), the setting of the whole of Acts 19 and of the Miletus farewell to the Ephesian
elders (20:17–38). Bock notes the density of Luke's Ephesian detail: Sherwin-White judged
that "Acts does not show such detailed knowledge of any other city as of Ephesus."

## Primary Textual Appearances (in Acts, per Bock)

- **Paul's brief first visit (18:19–21).** Paul argues in the synagogue, declines to stay,
  and leaves Priscilla and Aquila behind, promising to return "if God wills." Bock notes
  Ephesus "will be Paul's missionary center for his third journey."
- **[[apollos|Apollos]] instructed (18:24–28).** The eloquent Alexandrian Jew, "fervent in
  spirit" but knowing "only the baptism of John," is taken aside by Priscilla and Aquila
  and taught "the way of God more accurately." Bock reads Apollos as already a believer
  whose "preaching is not inaccurate, merely incomplete."
- **The disciples of John (19:1–7).** Paul meets about twelve "disciples" who had "never
  even heard that there is a Holy Spirit" in the sense that "the Spirit of God has come."
  This is the **only case in Acts of a second baptism** (Christian baptism following
  John's); Paul lays hands, the Spirit comes, and they speak in tongues and prophesy —
  "the final mention of tongues in Acts." Bock draws the key lesson: "There is no pattern
  in how the Spirit comes in Acts."
- **The hall of Tyrannus (19:8–10).** After three months in the synagogue Paul withdraws
  to the lecture hall of Tyrannus, teaching daily for two years so that "all the residents
  of Asia heard the word."
- **Sons of Sceva and the magic-book burning (19:11–20).** "Extraordinary miracles" through
  Paul (handkerchiefs and aprons) frame Bock's **miracle-versus-magic** distinction
  (Witherington): magic manipulates the gods by formula; miracle is God's sovereign act.
  The seven **sons of Sceva**, itinerant Jewish exorcists, are overpowered by the evil
  spirit ("Jesus I know, and Paul I am acquainted with; but who are you?"). Fear spreads,
  and converts burn magic scrolls worth **50,000 pieces of silver** — Bock notes the
  destruction is voluntary and marks maturing faith, not a precondition of conversion.
- **The Artemis riot (19:23–41).** Bock calls this the "second and last direct
  Paul-versus-Greco-Roman-religion confrontation." The silversmith **Demetrius**, whose
  trade in silver Artemis-shrines is threatened by the preaching that "gods made with
  hands are not gods," incites a riot; the crowd chants "Great is Artemis of the
  Ephesians!" and drags Paul's companions Gaius and Aristarchus into the **theater**
  (eventual capacity ~24,000). The **Asiarchs**, friendly to Paul, keep him from entering;
  the **town clerk** finally calms the crowd, warning of the charge of rioting that could
  cost Ephesus its free-city status. Bock notes the clerk's appeal to the sacred stone
  "that fell from the sky" as a self-authenticating proof of Artemis — "an argument that
  might parallel how Christians argued for the resurrection," turning on "whether the 'if'
  premise is correct."
- **The Miletus farewell to the Ephesian elders (20:17–38).** Summoning the elders to
  Miletus, Paul delivers his **only speech exclusively to Christians** (Bock). Key points:
  the elders (*presbyteroi*) are made **overseers** (*episkopoi*) by the Spirit to
  **shepherd** the flock — Bock stresses this is **not** the single presiding bishop that
  "emerged in the second century with Ignatius and Irenaeus"; elder, overseer, and
  shepherd name one office. The charge to guard the flock against "fierce wolves" from
  within and without, and the agraphon "It is more blessed to give than to receive," round
  out what Bock calls a speech that is "deliberative and at the same time expresses a
  farewell." He also treats 20:28 ("the church of God, which he obtained with the blood of
  his own") as one of "only two direct references to Jesus's sacrificial work in Luke-Acts."

## Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Data

Bock reports that silver shrine-replicas of Artemis "are not found archaeologically" but
that molds and coins are, and he notes the excavated theater and the epigraphically
attested civic offices (Asiarchs, the town clerk/*grammateus*). He observes the "we"-source
character and precise nautical/geographic detail of the surrounding travel narrative.

## Modern Identification

The ruins lie near **Selçuk** in western Turkey; the harbor has long since silted, leaving
the site inland. Bock does not adjudicate the modern site's specific monuments.

## Theological / Symbolic Significance

For Bock, Ephesus stages the gospel's confrontation with **magic and idolatry** and its
victory expressed as the growth of "the word of the Lord" in "power" (19:20), while the
Miletus farewell gives the fullest Lukan picture of **Paul the pastor** and of the
church's **elder/overseer** order. The Artemis episode also exhibits the recurring Lukan
apologetic that the disturbances of the mission arise from its opponents, not from Paul.
