# Ashkhabad

*Populated from [[smith-concise-encyclopedia-bahai-faith|Smith, Concise Encyclopedia of the Bahá'í Faith (2000)]]. All claims are from that source unless marked otherwise.*

## Geographical and Historical Overview

Ashkhabad (Persian: *'Ishqábád*) is the capital of Turkmenistan and was the capital of the former imperial Russian governorate of Turkestan. Its close proximity to the Iranian border made it a destination for large numbers of Iranian immigrants from the 1880s onwards, including many Bahá'ís seeking both economic opportunity and refuge from persecution in Iran (lines 3895–3906).

## Bahá'í Community

The Bahá'í immigrants to Ashkhabad — many of them merchants and builders — soon numbered about four hundred. Growing anti-Bahá'í sentiment among Iranian Shí'í settlers culminated in the brutal public murder of a prominent Bahá'í, Hájí Muhammad-Ridá Isfahání, on 8 September 1889, as a preliminary to a projected general attack on the Bahá'ís. To the assailants' surprise, however, the Russian authorities intervened, arrested the murderers and their supporters, and sentenced several to death or internal exile; the severer sentences were commuted only at the Bahá'ís' own request (lines 3906–3922).

The trial served to mark off the Bahá'ís as a recognized and protected religious community — a situation unique at the time (line 3923–3925). Ashkhabad thereafter attracted more Bahá'í immigrants. The community developed rapidly into a model Bahá'í settlement, establishing:
- A 'Spiritual Board of Counsel' (1895–6), possibly the first Bahá'í [[bahai-administrative-order|Assembly]] in the world
- A travellers' hospice, dispensary, and clinic
- Schools for boys (1894) and girls (1897)
- A cemetery, kindergartens (1917–18), and a reading room and library
- The **first Bahá'í House of Worship** ([[mashriqu-l-adhkar|Mashriqu'l-Adhkár]]) (1902–7), whose foundation stone was laid by the Russian governor-general General Subotich in 1904 (lines 3926–3952)

In accordance with Russian law there was no attempt to gain ethnic Russian converts.

## Soviet Period and Dissolution

Following the 1917 Russian Revolutions, the Bahá'ís initially enjoyed open conditions but opposition from Communist Party organizations mounted. In 1928 the temple was confiscated. Schools were closed, and some Bahá'ís were arrested. The community — by then consisting of about 1,400 families — was effectively destroyed in 1938: many men were arrested and exiled; women, children, and the remaining men were deported to Iran (lines 3952–3970).

## Revival

When conditions in the Soviet Union were finally relaxed, the Ashkhabad Bahá'í assembly was the first to reform, reconstituting in 1989 (line 3970).
