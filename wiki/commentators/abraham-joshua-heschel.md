# Abraham Joshua Heschel

**Abraham Joshua Heschel** (1907–1972) was a Polish-born American rabbi, theologian, and
philosopher of religion, one of the most influential figures in twentieth-century
[[judaism|Jewish]] thought. Descended from distinguished Hasidic dynasties, educated in
Berlin, and after emigration a professor at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York,
he is best known for a phenomenological and poetic philosophy of Judaism centered on
*wonder*, *radical amazement*, the *ineffable*, and the *divine pathos*.

His major works include *The Sabbath* (1951), *Man Is Not Alone* (1951), *The Prophets*
(based on his Berlin dissertation), *Heavenly Torah*, and **[[god-in-search-of-man|God in
Search of Man: A Philosophy of Judaism]]** (1955), the source ingested here.

## Hermeneutical Method

Heschel develops what he calls **depth-theology** — an inquiry not into the creeds and
propositions of religion but into the pre-conceptual *acts* and *moments of insight* out
of which faith arises. His reading of the Bible is governed by the conviction that
Scripture is **God's anthropology** (an account of God's concern for man) rather than
man's theology, and that the prophetic word is an *understatement* of an overwhelming
reality. Within the Jewish frameworks this combines [[pardes|peshat]] attentiveness with a
strongly [[aggadah|agadic]] / [[pardes|derashic]] sensibility.

More specifically, his epistemology is **situational** rather than conceptual ("the
philosopher … is a witness, not an accountant"), beginning in "amazement, awe, involvement"
rather than Cartesian doubt; it privileges a **preconceptual** level of encounter ("concepts
are second thoughts") and reads the Bible strictly in its own categories, faulting both
Spinoza (Bible as naïve myth) and [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] (Bible as Greek philosophy
in disguise). He extends [[maimonides|Maimonides']] via negativa into a rule of **adverbial
theology**: "we have no nouns by which to describe His essence; we have only adverbs."

## Characteristic Positions

From [[god-in-search-of-man|*God in Search of Man*]], Part I:

- **[[radical-amazement|Radical amazement]]** as the root of religious awareness — wonder
  that "does not come to an end when knowledge is acquired."
- A graded ascent from wonder through **[[awe]]** (*yir'ah*, not fear — "awe precedes
  faith") and the **[[the-ineffable|sense of the ineffable]]** to the **[[kavod|glory]]**,
  God's perceptible presence in the world.
- The **[[divine-pathos|"God in search of man"]]** thesis: religion is response to a God
  who is "concern, not only power"; faith is "an act of the whole person … not the same as
  belief," and *teshuvah* is "an answer to Him."
- An **anti-foundationalist** stance toward proofs of God: "We praise before we prove";
  certainty of God is an "ontological presupposition," not a syllogistic conclusion. He
  rejects the argument from design, God-as-symbol theology, and a purely apophatic "God is
  the great unknown."
- Critique of religious **rationalism** and of an over-creedal Judaism that elevates ideas
  above events (a pointed qualification of Maimonides' Thirteen Principles).

From Part II ("Revelation"):

- A constructive theory of **[[revelation|revelation]]** as *event* not *process* and "an
  ecstasy of God" — the divine answer to the human quest (see [[divine-pathos]]). Judaism is
  thereby **[[religion-of-time|a religion of time]]**, grounded in unique historical events.
- The **paradox of Sinai** ("to reveal He must conceal") and the rule that revelation is
  told by *understatement* and *via negationis*; "the Bible itself is a midrash" and "God
  spoke" is *more* than literal, not less.
- **Co-revelation**: "the Bible is more than the word of God: it is the word of God and
  man"; authority rests on prophecy, not chronology; "Judaism is based upon a minimum of
  revelation and a maximum of interpretation."
- A self-conscious **two-front** position on Scripture — against both fundamentalist verbal
  dictation and liberal/critical reduction to myth.

From Part III ("Response"):

- The **[[mitzvah|mitzvah]]** as the human response: "a leap of action rather than a leap of
  thought"; the **partnership of God and man**; the redemptive power of the deed ("the deed
  is wiser than the heart") balanced by **[[kavanah]]** (inner devotion).
- The structural **polarity of [[halakhah|halacha and agada]]**, rejecting both **religious
  behaviorism / pan-halachism** (Spinoza, Mendelssohn; *Torah ≠ nomos*) and antinomian
  spiritualism.
- An account of evil as real and "disguised in the good," sustained by a holy spark — against
  evil-as-privation (see [[nature-of-evil]]); Job read as **anthropodicy**.
- **[[freedom|Freedom]]** as event grounded in creation; "God is in need of man"; the spirit
  of Judaism as *theotropism* and the Sabbath; **the People [[judaism|Israel]] as a spiritual
  order**, chosen relationally.
