# Severus of Antioch

Severus (c. 465–538), Patriarch of Antioch (512–518) and the dominant theologian of the [[oriental-orthodoxy|miaphysite]] movement, is treated by Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], Ch. 4) as the pivotal — if "transitional" — figure in sixth-century miaphysite philosophy. His texts "were soon accorded similar authority as those by Cyril or the Cappadocians." Against the older thesis of Joseph Lebon (that miaphysites simply identified *physis* with *hypostasis* in a "radical philosophical particularism"), Zachhuber argues Severus's thought is "both more complex and more traditional," altering the [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|classical theory]] under doctrinal pressure rather than breaking with it.

## Philosophical Method

Zachhuber characterizes Severus as "a theological conservative for whom orthodoxy meant to think with the fathers." He was "not prone to conceptual innovation," seeking solutions "by considering in depth the existing body of Patristic witness." He "started from a full reception of the classical theory," modelling his own presentation on Cappadocian texts (Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus) often without explicit attribution.

## Ousia and Hypostasis

- **Hypostasis.** In *Homily 125* Severus defines hypostasis using the Cappadocian Peter/Paul property-bundle, echoing Basil's *Against Eunomius* II.4 and *Epistle 38*: "hypostasis does not deny genus or existence... but it sets apart and limits in particular icons the one who is." He thus considered the individual "as limited and marked out from a generic class by means of a unique set of properties" — neither detached from the classical theory nor merely a concrete individual.
- **Ousia as concrete universal.** Severus "emphasizes the extensional dimension of the universal more strongly than previous Patristic authors," invariably referring to ousia as "a totality, a class": "humanity, the whole ousia, comprehends many hypostases, that of Peter, Paul, John... The whole ousia... is not, therefore, contained in each single hypostasis" (*C. imp. Gramm.* II.17). The parallel for the Trinity: the Son "is one hypostasis out of those that are in the divine ousia, but he is not the whole ousia."
- **Being and subsisting.** Ousia and hypostasis both fundamentally signify "being": "While ousia makes known that a thing is, hypostasis [makes known] that it subsists... ousia signifies what is common, but hypostasis what is particular" (*Hom.* 125). Zachhuber reads this not as a regression to Athanasian conflation but as consistent with the *concrete dimension* of the Cappadocian theory.
- **Reticence toward the abstract dimension.** Severus "studiously avoided" the *logos tēs ousias* (account of being) as the mark of substantial identity — a "striking" omission given his knowledge of *Ep.* 38, indicating reticence toward the theory's abstract side, the very phraseology his opponents had adopted.

## Against John the Grammarian (the WT/WHI argument)

Severus's "overriding complaint" against [[john-the-grammarian-of-caesarea|John the Grammarian]] was that John's universal-nature Christology neglected that Cyril's *mia physis* expressed "the unique hypostatic unity of the person of the Incarnate." His standard objection (the **WT/WHI argument** — "whole Trinity / whole humanity incarnated"): if Christ's two natures are universal substances, "the whole Trinity would have been incarnated into the whole of humanity." Zachhuber notes Severus thereby "comes dangerously close to suggesting that the ousia is quantitatively divided between its individuals," and writes with "a feeling not only of exasperation but in a way also of desperation" in the absence of a conceptual solution.

## The Conundrum Bequeathed to Philoponus

Severus's thought "evidently tended towards the introduction of particular natures or substances" without ever naming them — the logic of his Christology pushed toward particular substances, but such a move "would threaten to undermine the conceptual foundation on which rested the account of the Trinity." [[john-philoponus|John Philoponus]] would later make this implication explicit (and reap the tritheist controversy). A rival reading by Dirk Krausmüller holds that Severus tends not to particularism but to a "hyper-realism" in which individuals are "little more than accidental modifications of a universal substance"; [[damian-of-alexandria|Damian of Alexandria]] later read him this way. Zachhuber presents this as a live scholarly debate.

## Major Works

- ***Contra impium Grammaticum*** (*Against the Impious Grammarian*) — his refutation of John the Grammarian's *Apology of Chalcedon*, preserved in Syriac (Lebon).
- ***Homiliae cathedrales*** (esp. *Homily 125*) and an extensive correspondence.
