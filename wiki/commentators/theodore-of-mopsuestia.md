# Theodore of Mopsuestia

Bishop of Mopsuestia (from 392), pupil of [[diodore-of-tarsus|Diodore of Tarsus]], fellow-student of [[john-chrysostom|Chrysostom]], and the most rigorous representative of the [[theoria|Antiochene exegetical school]]. Called "the Interpreter" (*ho exēgētēs*) in the East Syrian tradition, he was later condemned (with his Christology) at the Second Council of Constantinople (553) in the "Three Chapters" controversy, even though his exegesis remained foundational for the Church of the East. This page records his treatment in [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], chs. III (exegesis/inspiration); his Christology will be developed from later scopes.

## Hermeneutical Method

Theodore carried Antiochene literalism to its furthest point, drastically restricting the directly Messianic reading of the Old Testament:

- He **rejected as directly Messianic** a long list of traditionally accepted texts (Hos. 11:1–2; Mic. 4:1–3; 5:1–2; Hag. 2:9; Zech. 11:12–14; 12:10; Mal. 1:11; 4:5–6).
- He reduced the **directly prophetic Psalms** of the Incarnation and the Church to just four: **Pss. 2, 8, 45, 110**.
- For Psalms applied to Christ by the apostolic writers or by Christ himself (e.g. Pss. 21:2; 69:22), he argued the Psalmist was merely "in an analogous spiritual predicament," not actually predicting.
- He conceded that certain Psalms (16, 55, 89) and prophecies (Joel 2:28–29; Amos 9:11; Zech. 9:9; Mal. 3:1) could be read as Messianic *as types*.
- On the **Song of Songs**, he insisted the literal sense be given "full weight" as an epithalamium for Solomon's marriage to the Egyptian princess (without, Kelly notes, necessarily excluding a spiritual reading).

## Theory of Inspiration

Theodore is, with [[augustine|Augustine]], one of Kelly's two "isolated pioneers" who actually analysed the *manner* of [[inspiration-of-scripture|inspiration]]. He held the Holy Spirit's action "varied from writer to writer," distinguishing the special inspiration of the prophets — an ecstasy that "withdrew the prophet's attention from his immediate surroundings and focused it on the visions…vouchsafed him by the Spirit," affecting first sight and then hearing — from the lesser "grace of prudence" given to Solomon. Yet he still affirmed all the authors of both Testaments "wrote under the influence of one and the same Spirit."
