# Plotinus

Founder of Neoplatonism (c. 204/5–270 CE) and teacher of [[porphyry|Porphyry]], who edited his
*Enneads*. In [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Books VIII–IX**, [[augustine|Augustine]] names him among
the greatest Platonists and treats him with marked respect — "Plotinus… enjoys the reputation of having
understood Plato better than any other of his disciples."

## Quoted by Augustine

- On providence and beauty: the divine care visible even in the splendor of flowers and leaves
  (*Ennead* III).
- On embodiment as mercy: "The Father in compassion made their bonds mortal" — which Augustine turns into
  an argument that the *mortal* body of man is a divine mercy, whereas the demons' *immortal* bodies seal
  an eternal misery.
- On the soul's true path: "Our way is, to become like God" — endorsed by Augustine as pointing to
  spiritual likeness, not physical ascent, as the soul's return, and used to rebuke the Platonists for
  then retreating to demonic mediation.

Augustine ranks Plotinus, with [[plato|Plato]], [[porphyry|Porphyry]], and [[apuleius|Apuleius]], among
the Platonists who glimpsed the immaterial God yet missed the one Mediator.

## Sharpe: Plotinus as the One Non-Christian "True Mystic"

A. B. Sharpe devotes Chapter VIII of *[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True Nature and Value]]*
(1910) entirely to Plotinus, who poses a problem for his thesis that genuine (supernatural)
[[mysticism]] is the prerogative of Christianity. Plotinus "stands out as the sole instance in
which all the conditions of true mysticism (with the necessary exception of faith) seem to have
been fulfilled by one who was neither a Catholic nor a Christian."

Sharpe's summary of the system: it "insists strongly on the transcendence of God, the supreme
unity and absolute Good, which is above all being and all thought"; beneath the One are
intelligence (*nous*, with which the Platonic ideas are identified) and soul (*psychē*), which
impresses form on indeterminate matter — so "the body is in the soul, rather than the soul in
the body." The mystical union (sixth *Ennead*) takes place "in the substance of the soul" and is
"rather contact than mere knowledge" — ecstasy, the projection of the soul out of itself toward
the One, by the affinity of "the self-centred monad" (*to tēs psychēs oion kentron*) for unity.
Porphyry reports Plotinus achieved this union four times in their six years together.

Sharpe stresses the **close parallels with [[pseudo-dionysius|Dionysius]]**: like Dionysius,
Plotinus does not clearly bring out grace as a necessary condition, yet insists on the
distinction between the initiated and the uninitiated (*mē memyēmenoi*; cf. *Mystical Theology*
i), and enlarges on the abstraction from all multiplicity required before union. Plotinus's
imagery — the master entering the mansion so the ornaments are forgotten; passing the vestibule
of statues to see the divinity in the sanctuary; the soul that "alone will enjoy Him alone"
(*hina dexētai monē monon*) — Sharpe finds "practically identical" with the Mansions of St
Teresa, the prayer of quiet, St John of the Cross's dark night, and the spiritual marriage; the
"ground" (*kentron*) of the soul is as central for him as for the German mystics.

**Two (really three) views of his relation to Christian mysticism**, which Sharpe weighs:
1. **Anti-Christian imitation** — later Neoplatonism as "a desperate effort on the part of
   Paganism to fight the growing power of the Church on its own ground" (citing Bigg), of a piece
   with the quasi-Trinitarian triad, the Mithraic revival, and Philostratus's *Life of Apollonius
   of Tyana*.
2. **Natural-order mysticism by auto-suggestion** — "a strong emotional realisation of
   intellectual principles obtained by remarkable philosophical acumen," with no supernatural
   illumination. Sharpe allows this is possible but notes "the direct evidence for it is of the
   scantiest possible description," and that Plotinus lacks "the warmth of personal relationship —
   the *familiaris amicitia Jesu*" of Christian mysticism.
3. **Genuine grace outside the regular channels** (Sharpe's preferred alternative) — accepting
   Plotinus's experience as a real manifestation of divine grace, making him "an involuntary
   witness to the truth of the Christian view of mysticism," perhaps granted for a conversion he
   "failed to correspond with" (as the Magi might have failed to follow the star).

Sharpe notes Plotinus "seems… to have disregarded Christianity altogether" — neither a convert
like Victorinus nor an opponent like his disciple [[porphyry|Porphyry]] — an *anima naturaliter
Christiana* who nonetheless rejected the faith. His verdict: the Plotinian system "on its
mystical side, is practically identical with that of Dionysius and of all Christian mystics,
though it has nothing whatever of all that gives Christianity its power to attract or influence
or console." He closes with Augustine's judgment (*City of God* x.3; *Confessions* vii.9) that
the Platonists glimpsed God but lacked the Incarnation — the same point Augustine makes above.
