# Robert W. Yarbrough

## Biographical and Institutional Context

Robert W. Yarbrough is a New Testament scholar whose career has spanned Trinity Evangelical Divinity School and Covenant Theological Seminary (the denominational seminary of the Presbyterian Church in America). His PhD background and extensive engagement with German-language scholarship are unusual in American evangelical NT studies; this shapes several of his most distinctive argumentative moves. D. A. Carson selected Yarbrough to write the Timothy-Titus volume for the Pillar New Testament Commentary series (Eerdmans, 2018) — a commission that took many years to fulfill. Yarbrough's prefatory note indicates he experienced the PE as a formative pastoral text across his career, serving with churches in Sudan, Illinois, and St. Louis.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Primary Framework**: Grammatico-historical and conservative evangelical. Yarbrough reads the text literally and historically, vigorously defending Pauline authorship against historical-critical claims of pseudepigraphy. His approach is distinctly theocentric and ethically oriented, viewing the epistles as divinely inspired, coherent apostolic instructions.

### Lexicographic Depth

Yarbrough describes his approach as "from below" (word meanings in contemporaneous usage) + "from above" (discourse patterns and theological flow). He argues against two bad practices: etymology-based meaning (James Barr's critique) and vague theological-concept generalization. Instead, he ransacks Josephus, Philo, Epictetus, the Apostolic Fathers, and the LXX for actual synchronic usage of PE vocabulary, presenting results in extended footnotes and tables. This produces unusual density of primary-source parallels at the lexical level.

### Theocentric Frame

Against the dominant framing that the PE are primarily about church organization, Yarbrough argues they are *primarily about God*. He demonstrates this quantitatively: words for divinity (*theos*, *kyrios*, *Iēsous*, *Christos*) constitute roughly 4% of total word count in each letter — the highest-density topical cluster. He structures the introduction around the Apostles' Creed and treats God the Father, God the Son, and the Spirit separately before covering historical and introductory matters.

### Pastoral Orientation via Oden

Yarbrough organizes his pastoral-hermeneutical framework around Thomas Oden's eight rubrics for classical pastoral ministry: self-care of the pastor; preaching; the worshiping community; sacramental action; the Lord's Supper; teaching; institutional accountability; lay support. He brings each of the three PE into dialogue with these rubrics, arguing that the letters, while not a homiletics guide, are a "motivational refresher in the imperative and substance of sound and sustainable pastoral proclamation."

### Global Christianity Hermeneutic

Yarbrough's most distinctive non-exegetical move is invoking global Christianity as a corrective to the Schleiermacher-derived pseudonymity consensus. By 2050, an estimated 53% of the world's Protestants will be in Africa, 17% in Asia, and only 10% in Europe, 8% in North America. The African, Asian, and global South church reads the PE as Pauline. Yarbrough argues that the dominance of German Enlightenment hermeneutics in defining the "scholarly consensus" is itself a culturally contingent judgment that global Christianity increasingly challenges — not from ignorance but from different hermeneutical priorities and different relationships to persecution and suffering.

## Characteristic Positions

### 1. Pauline Authorship of All Three PE

Yarbrough defends Pauline authorship comprehensively, against what he acknowledges is the majority position in Western scholarship. His most distinctive contribution is bringing Adolf Schlatter (1852–1938) — a German-language scholar largely unknown to English readers — into the debate. Schlatter catalogued over 70 literal points of contact between the PE and the undisputed Paulines (mostly Romans and 1–2 Corinthians). His argument from the *sociology of knowledge*: the pseudonymity hypothesis became a *dogma* in German academia (Johnson's term), driven partly by the cultural agendas of the Enlightenment project, not by purely exegetical logic.

### 2. The Ethics Argument against Pseudepigraphy

Yarbrough raises a pointed ethical-theological objection to the pseudonymity reading. A pseudonymous author who writes "I am telling the truth, I am not lying" (1 Tim 2:7) and who melodramatically invokes God's presence in doxology while depicting his imminent execution (2 Tim 4:17–18) is not merely using an accepted literary convention but is engaged in deception that borders on blasphemy. This argument is distinct in kind from stylometric and vocabulary arguments and has largely been sidestepped in the pseudonymity literature.

### 3. Theocentric Reading of the PE

The PE are not primarily a church order document (contra Dibelius, Conzelmann, and the "bourgeois Christianity" school) but a Trinitarian witness to God's saving work in Christ. Klinker–De Klerck's study confirms there is no evidence of "bourgeois Christianity" (*christliche Bürgerlichkeit*) in the PE's soteriology, ethics, or missions outlook.

### 4. Complementarian on 1 Tim 2:11–15

The prohibition on women teaching and exercising authority (2:12) is grounded in creation order (Gen 2: Adam formed first) and the fall narrative (Gen 3: Eve first deceived) — not in the specific circumstances of Ephesus. The universal scope is signaled by "everywhere" in 2:8. *Authenteō* = neutral/positive exercise of authority (Wolters, Burk), not a pejorative term ("usurp authority," "murder"). Köstenberger's grammatical construction: both "teach" and "exercise authority" are positive activities whose exercise is contextually prohibited — the prohibition applies to congregational oversight, not to women's teaching in all settings. Women are to be *active, attentive learners* (hēsychia = peaceful receptivity, not enforced silence) in congregational worship.

### 5. Christ as *Theos* in Titus 2:13

"Our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13) is one of the NT's clearest affirmations of Christ's full divinity. Granville Sharp's canon, confirmed by Wallace's exhaustive study, establishes that the single article with two nouns joined by *kai* refers to the same person. Wallace: "as secure as any verse in the canon when it comes to identifying Christ as *theos*."

### 6. *Theopneustos* as Origin, Not Process

"God-breathed" (2 Tim 3:16) denotes the *origin* of Scripture (from God), not the *manner* of inspiration. Wallace's grammatical argument: *pasa graphē theopneustos* = "all Scripture is God-breathed," not "every Scripture that happens to be inspired" — the construction implies no hint that some passages are uninspired.

### 7. Sound Doctrine as Behavioral-Relational, Not Merely Propositional

The *hygiainōn didaskalia* (sound doctrine) cluster in the PE is not abstract dogma but instruction oriented toward the ordering of everyday life. Rengstorf (Schlatter's student): wherever *didaskalia* appears, "abstract dogma is not in view but rather instruction for the ordering of everyday life (ethics)." Sound doctrine is not an achievement to be defended but a medicinal antidote to what weakens, sickens, and destroys — social and relational healing, not merely propositional accuracy.

## Relationship to Other Commentators

- **L. T. Johnson** (Sacra Pagina): shared Pauline authorship defense; Johnson's shift *from* pseudonymity to authenticity is a key reference point for Yarbrough
- **Philip Towner** (NICNT 2006): acknowledged as extensive conversation partner; Yarbrough follows Towner closely on *eusebeia* and salvation-historical framework
- **I. Howard Marshall** with Towner (ICC): represents sophisticated pseudonymity position Yarbrough respectfully engages
- **Adolf Schlatter** (1852–1938): unusual choice; Yarbrough invests heavily in him precisely because his pre-consensus arguments have not been adequately engaged in English scholarship
- **Thomas Schreiner** and **Andreas Köstenberger** (*Women in the Church*, 3rd ed. 2016): primary sources for the complementarian reading of 1 Tim 2:11-15
- **Dibelius and Conzelmann** (Hermeneia, 1972): primary foils; represent the "bourgeois Christianity" pseudonymity reading
