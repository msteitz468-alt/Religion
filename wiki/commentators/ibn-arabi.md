# Ibn Arabi

**Muhyi al-Din Ibn al-Arabi** (560/1165–638/1240), honored as **al-Shaykh al-Akbar**, "the Greatest
Master," is the supreme metaphysician of [[sufism|Sufism]] and the fountainhead of the **Akbarian**
tradition of Islamic mysticism. Born in **Murcia** in al-Andalus and formed in the rich Andalusian
Sufi milieu, he travelled east on pilgrimage, experienced his decisive "openings" at Mecca, and
settled finally in **Damascus**, where he died and is buried. He is the towering figure of esoteric
([[allegorical-reading|*ta'wil* / *batin*]]) Qur'anic reading and the author of the most influential —
and most contested — system in Islamic thought.

## Major Works

- **[[fusus-al-hikam|Fusus al-Hikam]]** ("The Bezels of Wisdom") — his late, concentrated masterwork:
  twenty-seven chapters, each unfolding a facet of divine wisdom through a prophet, which he claims to
  have **received from the Prophet Muhammad in a vision**. The seedbed of the whole later tradition.
- **Al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya** ("The Meccan Openings/Revelations") — his vast, encyclopedic summa of
  metaphysics, cosmology, and the science of sainthood, drawing on visionary "openings" (*futuhat*)
  received at Mecca.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ibn Arabi reads the [[quran|Qur'an]], the cosmos, and the self as a single fabric of **signs**
(*ayat*) to be unveiled by **disclosure** (*kashf*) rather than discursive reason:

- **Esoteric *ta'wil* / the *batin***: every verse, letter, and prophetic figure conceals an inner
  reality; the Qur'an is an inexhaustible ocean of meanings, each legitimate for the reader whose
  spiritual station can receive it. This is the mystical pole of the [[tafsir|tafsir]] tradition and
  the structural counterpart of the [[kabbalah|Kabbalist]] *sod*.
- **Knowledge as witnessing, not proof**: the gnostic ('*arif*) knows God by direct unveiling, which
  is why Ibn Arabi can **hold together** what the dialecticians keep apart — most famously God's
  transcendence and immanence (see [[tanzih-tashbih|tanzih / tashbih]]).
- **The prophets as loci of the Names**: in the *Fusus*, sacred history is read as a typology of the
  divine Names, each prophet the perfect mirror of a particular wisdom.

## Characteristic Positions

- **[[wahdat-al-wujud|Wahdat al-wujud]] ("the unity of being")** — the doctrine (the *term* is his
  school's, systematized by al-Qunawi and al-Qashani) that there is but one Being, the Real
  (*al-Haqq*), of which all things are self-disclosures (*tajalliyat*); the "fixed essences"
  (*a'yan thabita*) are the eternal archetypes of creatures in the divine knowledge.
- **[[al-insan-al-kamil|The Perfect Man]] (*al-insan al-kamil*)** — the human being as the
  all-comprehensive image of God, the *barzakh* (isthmus) between Real and cosmos, the reason and
  preserving principle of creation; realized supremely in the **Muhammadan Reality**
  (*al-haqiqa al-Muhammadiyya*).
- **The God created in belief** — each worshipper knows the Lord shaped to his own receptivity; the
  perfect knower recognizes the Real in every form of belief while absolutizing none.

## Known Controversies

Ibn Arabi is the most polarizing figure in Sunni intellectual history. *Wahdat al-wujud* was
condemned as monism or pantheism by jurists and theologians — the Hanbali **Ibn Taymiyya** foremost —
while his defenders read it as a non-ontological "oneness of witnessing." Particular chapters of the
*Fusus* (the suggestion of Pharaoh's salvation; the treatment of the idols in Noah's bezel) drew
charges of heresy. He remains simultaneously **al-Shaykh al-Akbar** to his admirers and a byword for
deviation to his critics — a contradiction the wiki records **without adjudication** (Contradiction
Protocol).

## Influence on Later Tradition

Through his student and son-in-law **Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi** at Konya, Ibn Arabi's thought became the
metaphysical grammar of later Sufism across the Persianate, Ottoman, and Indian worlds — generating a
centuries-long commentary tradition on the *Fusus*, shaping Persian poetry, and provoking the
corrective of Ahmad Sirhindi's *wahdat al-shuhud*. No single thinker more deeply shaped how Islamic
mysticism articulates the relation between God and the world.
