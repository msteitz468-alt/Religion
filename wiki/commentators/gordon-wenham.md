Gordon J. Wenham is a British evangelical Old Testament scholar, widely regarded as one of the most important contributors to evangelical Pentateuchal commentary. His *Leviticus* commentary in the NICOT series (published by Routledge and Kegan Paul, c. 1979) is distinctive for its integration of social anthropology (Mary Douglas) into evangelical exegesis.

## Hermeneutical Method

Wenham describes his approach as following the **"classic Protestant approach" of Calvin**: reading the text for its plain historical meaning while drawing out its enduring theological significance. He deliberately de-emphasizes source-critical debates in order to focus on the text's meaning as a whole.

Key methodological features:
- **Social-anthropological grounding**: Wenham is unusual among evangelical commentators in adopting and extending Mary Douglas's structural analysis of purity. He reads the food laws, bodily purity codes, and priestly requirements as a coherent symbolic system — not isolated rules.
- **Literary analysis**: He reads Leviticus as a structured whole with theological architecture, not a collection of disparate priestly regulations.
- **ANE comparative work**: He engages Ugaritic sacrifice texts, Hittite ritual parallels, and Mesopotamian law codes to establish Leviticus' cultural context.
- **NT canonical reading**: He explicitly addresses how Leviticus functions in the NT, especially Hebrews, without subordinating the OT text to its later application.

## Characteristic Positions

**On authorship**: Wenham presents three positions without dogmatic commitment: traditional Mosaic; standard critical (postexilic P, Wellhausen); mediating preexilic view (Kaufmann). He finds the postexilic P date "difficult to maintain" — Ezekiel's quotations of Levitical material, and P's linguistic distinctiveness from demonstrably late Hebrew, suggest an earlier date. He does not argue for Mosaic authorship as a theological necessity.

**On holiness**: Following Mary Douglas, Wenham defines *qāḏôš* (holiness) as **wholeness or completeness** — conformity to categories, integrity of form. Holy things are perfect specimens of their type; unclean things violate categorical boundaries. This framework unifies the food laws (clean animals exhibit complete locomotion types), the priestly requirements (no physical blemish in the officiating priest, Lev 21:17-23), and the sacrificial animal requirements (unblemished offering).

**On the food laws**: Wenham strongly endorses Douglas's structural analysis over hygienic or anti-pagan explanations. Clean/unclean distinctions mirror Israel's election: as YHWH separated Israel from the nations, Israel separates clean from unclean animals. The system teaches categorical holiness through daily dietary practice.

**On sacrifice**: Different offerings embody different relational models — the burnt offering (whole consumption) = personal ransom/substitution; the sin offering = sanctuary purification from pollution; the reparation offering = debt payment to the offended party. Sacrificial efficacy depends on God's grace, not priestly performance.

**On Azazel (Lev 16)**: Wenham concludes the scapegoat ceremony's meaning is clear (complete removal of Israel's sin from the community) even if the etymological meaning of *Azazel* remains uncertain.

## Key Scholars Engaged

Wenham interacts most substantially with: Mary Douglas (*Purity and Danger* — strongly adopted), Yehezkel Kaufmann (preexilic P dating — sympathetically engaged), Julius Wellhausen (postexilic P — critically engaged), Jacob Milgrom (Levitical vocabulary — frequently cited), Rolf Rendtorff (Holiness Code redaction — engaged), Rashi (Azazel — noted), David J. Davies (sacrifice as relationship restoration).
