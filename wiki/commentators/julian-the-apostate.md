# Julian the Apostate (as Commentator and Polemicist)

See also: [[julian-the-apostate|Julian the Apostate (figure page)]]

## Overview

Julian, Roman emperor 361–363 CE, is unique among the ancient pagan critics of Christianity treated by Wilken in that he wrote as a sitting emperor, a former Christian, and a trained philosopher. His polemical work *Against the Galilaeans* (*Contra Galilaeos*) — written during the winter of 362–63 CE in Antioch — revived and deepened "the tradition of pagan criticism of Christianity reaching back to the second century" (rel. line ~72). Like the books of [[celsus]] and [[porphyry]], Julian's work was destroyed, but substantial fragments survive embedded in [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril of Alexandria]]'s fifth-century refutation, *Contra Julianum* (composed c. 440 CE), from which Wilken draws throughout this chapter.

Cyril stressed the particular danger Julian posed: "before he became emperor he was numbered among the believers; he was worthy of holy Baptism, and he was trained in the Holy Scriptures" (rel. line ~471–472). Julian thus "knew Christianity from the inside and was able to meet Christian apologists on their own terms" (rel. line ~473). Cyril conceded that Julian's books "had disturbed many and done much harm" and that even strong believers "were troubled because they thought he knew the Holy Scriptures" (rel. lines ~477–480).

## The Emperor's Piety: Hellenic Religiosity

Julian's intellectual formation combined classical Greek rhetoric and Neoplatonic philosophy. After early Christian education, he was drawn toward the experiential, theurgic strand of Neoplatonism transmitted through Iamblichus (via Aedesius and Maximus of Ephesus). Iamblichus had "transformed the philosophical religion of the Platonists into an experiential religion nurtured by religious rites and theurgy" (rel. line ~118–119). Theurgy held that "union is attained 'by the efficacy of the unspeakable acts performed in the appropriate manner … by the potency of the unutterable symbols which are comprehended only by the gods … without intellectual effort'" (Iamblichus, *Myst.* 2.11; rel. lines ~123–128).

Julian was initiated into the cult of Cybele and later into the cult of Mithras (rel. lines ~154–157). He dated his conversion away from Christianity to age twenty, when Maximus of Ephesus led him to "cast off his error" and embrace "our old gods instead of this recent intruder and his baneful rites" (Libanius, *Or.* 13.12; rel. lines ~196–203).

Once sole emperor, Julian wrote to Maximus: "We worship the gods openly … We sacrifice oxen in public. We have offered to the gods many hecatombs as thank-offerings. The gods command me to purify everything as far as possible, and I obey them enthusiastically" (*Ep.* 8; rel. lines ~234–238). He was not a cultural conservative like Libanius, for whom religion was chiefly social; Julian exhibited "all the signs of an exuberant convert — acquiescence in the face of a reality larger than himself, obedience to the will of the gods, a fervor — even fanaticism — about his new faith" (rel. lines ~283–285). He even lamented that sacrifices were mostly public occasions without opportunity for meditation (*Ep.* 58; rel. lines ~287–288).

## Greek Education and Christian Values: The School Edict

Julian's edict on schoolteachers (summer 362 CE; *Codex Theodosianus* 13.3.5) was "the first salvo in his attack on the Christians" (rel. lines ~350–352). The law required teachers to be evaluated not only on competence but on "character" — defined as believing in the specific religious and moral values transmitted through Greek literature (rel. lines ~335–339). Julian explained explicitly:

> "I do not … call on them to change their beliefs. I give them rather the choice either not to teach what they do not believe, or if they do teach, to do so honestly … If they are real interpreters of the ancient classics, let them first imitate the ancients' piety towards the gods. If they think the classics wrong in this respect, then let them go and teach Matthew and Luke in the church." (*Ep.* 36; rel. lines ~358–370)

The edict struck Christians as claiming "the Greek language belonged to religion exclusively and not to the tongue" (Gregory Nazianzus, *Or.* 4.5; rel. lines ~378–382). Wilken notes that Julian "claimed the Greek intellectual and literary tradition for the exponents of the traditional religion, thereby hoping to cement the bond between religion and culture which the new Christian movement threatened to dissolve" (rel. lines ~429–432).

## *Against the Galilaeans*: Hermeneutical Method and Major Arguments

### The Work

The *Contra Galilaeos* ran to three books; surviving fragments (from Cyril) cover mainly the first book. Julian stated his intention to strip "that new-fangled Galilaean god" of "the divinity falsely ascribed to him" (*Ep.* 55; rel. lines ~452–453). Libanius reports it was written "during the long winter nights" (*Or.* 18.179; rel. lines ~454–456).

Julian's method combined:
- Close exegesis of the Jewish and Christian Scriptures, exploiting inside knowledge from his Christian upbringing
- Philosophical comparison with Plato (especially the *Timaeus* vs. Genesis)
- Use of Judaism as a witness against Christianity
- Argumentation from internal Christian inconsistencies (e.g., the Synoptics vs. John on Jesus' divinity)

### The Tribal God of Jews and Christians

Julian's sharpest theological criticism concerned the character of the biblical God. He argued that "the god of the Hebrews … was not the creator of the whole universe with lordship over all things, but is confined within limits and since his rule has bounds we must conceive of him as one among other gods" (*Gal.* 100c; rel. lines ~559–562).

The true God, for Julian, is known universally: "all of us, without being taught, have attained to a belief in some sort of divinity" (*Gal.* 52b; rel. lines ~580–583). This "God of all" cannot be the property of any one people or knowable through a particular historical revelation. Julian singled out the Jewish doctrine of election as "the most offensive idea": "Moses says that the creator of the universe chose the Hebrew nation, that to that nation alone did he pay heed" (*Gal.* 100a; rel. lines ~590–594). He charged that Christians had taken over this particularism — "Jesus the Nazarene, yes and Paul also … assert that he is the God of Israel alone and of Judaea, and that the Jews are his chosen people" (*Gal.* 100a; rel. lines ~595–598).

Julian added his own version of the objection developed by Celsus and Porphyry — why did God neglect all other peoples for so long? "To us no prophet, no oil of anointing, no teacher, no herald … If he is the God of all of us alike, and the creator of all, why did he neglect us?" (*Gal.* 106d; rel. lines ~609–612).

Julian ridiculed the Garden of Eden narrative: what kind of God creates humans without moral knowledge, so that the serpent must teach it? "The serpent was a benefactor rather than a destroyer of the human race" (*Gal.* 93d; rel. lines ~648–650). The biblical God, he charged, is also petty and jealous: "I am a jealous God" — "If a man is jealous and envious you think him blameworthy, whereas if God is called jealous do you think it a divine quality?" (*Gal.* 155c; rel. lines ~662–663). In Greek religion, by contrast, God is never shown "angry, or resentful, or wroth" (*Gal.* 160d; rel. lines ~664–666).

Julian compared the Genesis creation account unfavorably with Plato's *Timaeus*: Moses "has failed to give a complete account of the fashioner of the universe" (*Gal.* 99e; rel. lines ~704–706), saying nothing about intermediary spiritual beings or angels, and leaving unresolved whether the Spirit was "ungenerated" or "generated" — a live controversy within Christianity at the time (rel. lines ~681–690).

### An Apostasy from Judaism

Wilken identifies this as Julian's most distinctive contribution, deepening and sharpening what Celsus had begun:

> "The distinctive mark of the *Contra Galilaeos*, in contrast to earlier works on the Christians, is that Julian singles out apostasy from Judaism as the most vulnerable point of Christianity." (rel. lines ~740–741)

Julian's argument:

1. Christians claim to be inheritors of Moses and the Jewish prophets.
2. Yet Christians do not observe *any* of the laws of Moses — not circumcision, not sacrifice, not the dietary laws.
3. Meanwhile, Jews continue to observe the Law, which Moses said would endure forever and to which nothing may be added or removed (Deut. 4:2; 27:26; Exod. 12:14–15; cited at rel. lines ~1003–1009).
4. Therefore Christians are apostates — they have "forsaken their teaching even more than ours [the Greeks']" (*Gal.* 238a; rel. lines ~935–938).

Julian deployed Christian Scripture against Christians: "I have not come to abolish the Law and the prophets but to fulfill them" (Matt. 5:17) and "Whoever relaxes one of the least of these commandments … shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven" (Matt. 5:10) — implying it was the disciples, not Jesus himself, who led Christians to apostasize from Jewish traditions (rel. lines ~1030–1034).

On circumcision, Julian quoted Paul's spiritualizing interpretation and then rebutted it with Gen. 17:1–11, showing that Moses explicitly commanded physical circumcision as a covenant sign (rel. lines ~1013–1026).

On sacrifice, Julian's challenge was pointed: "Why is it that after deserting us [the Greeks] you do not love the Law of the Jews or abide by the sayings of Moses?" (*Gal.* 305d; rel. lines ~1037–1038). When the anticipated Christian response was raised — Jews too no longer sacrifice — Julian replied: "Neither do you observe any of the other customs observed by the Jews," and "the Jews do sacrifice in their own houses … everything that they eat is consecrated; and they pray before sacrificing, and give the right shoulder to the priests as the first fruits" (*Gal.* 305d; rel. lines ~1042–1045).

The net verdict: "Christianity, though it claims Judaism as its origin, has nothing to do with Judaism; it is a new and strange rite dreamed up recently by Jesus' followers with no claim to antiquity. The Greeks have more in common with the Jews, for they have 'temples, sanctuaries, altars, purifications, and precepts'" (*Gal.* 306b; rel. lines ~1062–1066).

Julian also argued that Christian prophetic exegesis was unsound. He scrutinized the key proof-texts Christians used to show that Moses or the prophets predicted Jesus:
- Deut. 18:15–18 (the prophet like Moses): the text says "a prophet like himself and born of men, not a god" (*Gal.* 253d; rel. line ~960).
- Gen. 49:10 (the scepter of Judah): refers to the Davidic monarchy, ended with Zedekiah; cannot apply to Jesus anyway since Jesus was said to be born "not of Joseph but of the Holy Spirit," and Matthew and Luke's genealogies disagree (*Gal.* 253e, 261e; rel. lines ~970–975).
- Isa. 7:14 (the virgin): Isaiah nowhere says "a god will be born of the virgin," so Christian claims about Mary as "mother of God" have no scriptural warrant (*Gal.* 262d; rel. lines ~988–992).

### Julian's Relative Respect for Judaism

Julian's attitude toward Judaism was deliberately ambivalent — used rhetorically rather than as a principled endorsement. He respected Jewish "fervid piety" and "zeal to preserve the customs of their ancestors" (*Ep.* 20; rel. lines ~292–293, ~547–549). He noted that Jews practice something akin to sacrifice in their homes through ritual preparation of food (kashruth), and that "Abraham used to sacrifice even as we Hellenes do, always and continually" (*Gal.* 356c; rel. lines ~897–898). He wished to "conscript the Jews on his side against the Christians" (rel. line ~1068), arguing that Greeks actually have more in common with Jews (temples, altars, purifications) than Christians do.

## The Temple Rebuilding Project

Julian's critique was not merely literary. His attack was "supported by a conspicuous historical gesture, and one that could only have been made by an emperor: the plan to rebuild the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem" (rel. lines ~743–746).

Christians had long argued that the Temple's destruction (70 CE) and the Bar Kochba aftermath (135 CE — Aelia Capitolina replacing Jerusalem) were fulfillments of prophecy (Dan. 9:27; Matt. 24:1–2), evidence that Judaism was superseded. Julian understood this Christian apologetic: "the Temple had become a symbol of the legitimacy of Judaism" (rel. lines ~828–829). If rebuilt, Jesus' prophecy that "no stone will be left standing on another" would be proven false (rel. lines ~922–924). "The single excuse" Christians gave for Jewish non-observance was that Jews could not sacrifice outside Jerusalem (*Gal.* 351d); if the Temple were restored, the excuse collapsed and Christian superiority claims with it (rel. lines ~1081–1086).

In winter 362–63 Julian appointed Alypius, a former provincial governor, to oversee the project, declaring "I will rebuild at my own expense the holy city of Jerusalem" (*Ep.* 51; rel. lines ~1087–1091). The construction was halted abruptly — Ammianus Marcellinus attributed it to balls of fire bursting from beneath the foundations (*Amm. Marc.* 23.1.2–3); Christian historians claimed fire from heaven. Julian was killed in battle against the Persians in June 363 CE; the project was never resumed (rel. lines ~1093–1102).

## Hermeneutical Framework

Julian's interpretive method was:
- **Historical-literal** when it served to expose biblical inconsistencies or the literal absurdity of biblical narratives
- **Philosophical-allegorical** in his own Neoplatonist reading of Greek tradition (Plato's *Timaeus* as genuine cosmology)
- **Polemical-comparative**: measuring Jewish and Christian Scriptures against Greek philosophical criteria (the *Timaeus* vs. Genesis; universalism vs. particularism)
- **Intra-textual**: exploiting the New Testament against Christian claims — he knew "already … the Christian answers to the questions he raised and thus could anticipate what the replies would be" (rel. lines ~981–982)

## Significance and Reception

Wilken identifies Julian's unique contribution as the combination of emperor's power with philosopher's argument with insider's knowledge: "how vulnerable Christianity really was did not become apparent until Christians were faced with a critic who knew the Christian religion from the inside" (rel. lines ~1141–1142). The Temple plan was "the final, and most brilliant, stroke in the ancient conflict between paganism and Christianity" (rel. lines ~1128–1129).

Cyril of Alexandria, writing eighty years later when the empire was officially Christian, still felt compelled to refute Julian's work; pagans were reportedly circulating it saying "none of our teachers is capable of rebutting or refuting his ideas" (PG 76.508c; rel. lines ~485–486). Gregory Nazianzus composed bitter invectives; John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch in 386 CE, still had to contend with Jews emboldened by the Temple episode (rel. lines ~1113–1117).
