# Joachim of Flora (c. 1135–1202)

**Joachim of Flora** (Gioacchino da Fiore) is the Calabrian abbot and exegete whose doctrine of the
**Three Ages** of salvation history — the Age of the Father (OT), the Age of the Son (the present
Gospel), and the imminent Age of the Holy Spirit — constitutes the most theologically consequential
innovation in medieval biblical hermeneutics and the sharpest divergence from the patristic-medieval
tradition of [[fourfold-sense|spiritual interpretation]]. He is the central polemical subject of
[[de-lubac-medieval-exegesis|de Lubac's *Medieval Exegesis* Vol. 3]], Chapter 6.

Dante places Joachim in Paradise beside Saint Bonaventure (*Paradiso* XII.140–141) — a tribute to
his genuine spiritual gifts, not a full canonization of his system.

## Biographical Context

Born c. 1135 in Celico, Calabria; visited Constantinople and the Holy Land as a young man; became a
Cistercian monk, then abbot of Corazzo; founded the independent Florensian order at San Giovanni in
Fiore (c. 1189), with its own constitutions approved by Pope Celestine III (1196). He composed his
major exegetical works in the final decades of his life and submitted them all to papal judgment
before his death. His works were posthumously condemned: the doctrine of the Three Ages and the
related trinitarian teaching were condemned at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), though Joachim's
personal orthodoxy was not impugned.

A crucial (unverified) biographical detail: Geoffrey of Auxerre reported that Joachim was of Jewish
origin and converted to Christianity relatively late. If true, this would explain several features of
his system — the "Judaizing" of Christianity's history by making the NT letter analogous in kind to
the OT letter; his *Adversus Judaeos*, which unusually argues entirely from the literal sense. De
Lubac notes: "Geoffrey... might have been sufficiently well informed; but he is perhaps not a very
reliable witness." The claim remains unconfirmed; de Lubac treats it with appropriate reserve.

The recapture of Jerusalem by Saladin on 28 October 1187 appears to have crystallized Joachim's
sense of prophetic urgency: "Tempus praefinitum est!" ("The end of time is at hand!").

## Hermeneutical System

### The Two Kinds of Understanding

Joachim distinguishes two modes of biblical understanding:
1. **Spiritual understanding**: the traditional fourfold sense (history / allegory / tropology /
   anagogy), plus its subdivisions.
2. **Typic understanding (*concordia*)**: NOT spiritual understanding but the perception of a
   *literal* parallelism between two literal sequences — OT *historical events* correspond one-to-one
   with Church-historical events. Joachim himself explicitly: "some think that concord is the same
   thing as the spiritual understanding, which properly is called allegory; it is first necessary for
   us to show what difference there is between concord and allegory."

The *concordia litterae* (concord of the letter) is therefore a second hermeneutical register,
operating below the level of spiritual sense, mapping OT history onto Church history event by event.
This is Joachim's decisive innovation and the source of all the problems de Lubac identifies.

### The Three Ages

| Age | Divine Person | Canonical Basis | Characteristic | Approximate Era |
|-----|--------------|-----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| First | Father | OT Israel | "Science" (*scientia*) | Adam to Christ |
| Second | Son | NT Gospel (letter) | "Knowledge of knowledge, letter from letter" | Christ to ~1260 |
| Third | Holy Spirit | Proceeds from both Testaments | Spiritual understanding | ~1260 onward |

The formula: *duo significantia, unum significatum* — the two Testaments are two signifying things
pointing to one signified reality (the Spirit).

The third age was initiated by **Benedict of Nursia** and was soon to arrive "in the fullness of
fruit" around 1201–1260. Its firstfruits: the Cistercian order. Its mature form: a new contemplative
monastic religion that would replace the institutional clerical Church.

### Structure of Eschatology: Two Antichrists

Joachim's eschatological scheme introduces a **duplication** of the last Antichrist:
1. **First Antichrist** (the seventh king / seventh head of the dragon): comes at the end of the
   sixth age; will be vanquished; the devil bound; the *Sabbath of rest* — the third age — begins.
2. **Third age (Age of the Spirit)**: peace, Jewish and Gentile conversion, contemplative monastic
   religion; duration indeterminate.
3. **Second (and final) Antichrist**: Satan released from prison; global assault; vanquished; Last
   Judgment; entry into eternity.

Historical list of the dragon's seven heads (from *Liber Figurarum*, Table XIV): Herod, Nero,
Constantius, Mohammed, Mesmot, Saladin = six precursors; the seventh = the first proper Antichrist
still to come; the eighth = "the last and biggest Antichrist."

This specific chronological identification of historical figures with prophecy-slots distinguishes
Joachim sharply from the mainstream tradition, which applied the Antichrist name as a *common noun*
to any persecutor or heretic of any age (citing Gregory: Cain was already a "member" of the
Antichrist), and which consistently refused to calculate the end times.

## De Lubac's Critique

### The Structural Aberration

De Lubac argues that Joachim's Three Ages system commits a **structural** error that his personal
orthodoxy could not prevent. By making the Gospel of Christ the "second age" (= a letter analogous
in kind to the OT letter), Joachim implicitly treats Christ's revelation as provisional and
incomplete, awaiting the Spirit's third-age clarification. This "by a fatal consequence... dissociates
Christ himself from his Spirit."

- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae*): "No status of the present life can be more perfect than
  the status of the new law."
- **Bonaventure**: "After the New Testament there will not be another one."
- **Étienne Gilson**: "to preach a gospel of the Holy Spirit was completely to reverse the economy
  of the ages of the world."

### Alleged "Antecedents" Are Not Antecedents

De Lubac refutes each alleged precursor of the Three Ages doctrine:
- **Origen's "Eternal Gospel"** (Apoc 14): for Origen = (a) the spiritual comprehension of one
  Gospel by spiritual vs. carnal believers, or (b) perfect knowledge in eternity after the Second
  Coming — neither of which = a third earthly age.
- **Hugh of Saint Victor's "three days"** (fear / truth / charity): interpenetrating spiritual
  *states* within the Christian life, not successive historical epochs.
- **Rupert of Deutz's trinitarian division** (Creation / Redemption / Sanctification): maps to the
  traditional Creed, not to three successive earthly ages; positively excludes Joachim's scheme.
- **Anselm of Havelberg's** progressive revelation: builds on Gregory Nazianzus but goes no further
  than the traditional passing from the present age to the future age — never a third earthly period.

The contrast with **Paul Alvarus of Cordoba** (854) is instructive: Paul saw Mohammed as an
Antichrist type, applied Daniel's prophecy to Islam, but pulled back to the Tyconian principle —
one prophecy holds good for multiple applications across all ages (*species* → *genus*). Joachim
treats the same material as specific chronological prediction.

## Personal Greatness and De Lubac's Qualified Appreciation

De Lubac refuses a simply negative verdict: "This man was great." Comparable in spiritual gifts to
Rupert of Deutz; deeply passionate for the unity and spiritual renewal of the Church; he "thirsted
for the Spirit." His error was not malice but the projection into historical chronology of symbols
that scriptural meditation had raised in his heart.

De Lubac's constructive conclusion: there are two equally destructive errors — (1) dreaming of a
Kingdom of the Spirit that outpaces Christ, and (2) imagining a Christ who perpetually restrains the
Spirit. "The sap of Christianity can still always bear unexpected fruits that astonish the Christians
themselves." Joachim's positive impulse points toward a genuine truth, even if his system distorted
it.

## Influence and Legacy

- The Spiritual Franciscans claimed Joachim's mantle, identifying Francis of Assisi with the
  "angel" of Apoc 14 who inaugurates the third age.
- **Dante** places Joachim in Paradise (*Paradiso* XII.140–141) alongside Bonaventure.
- Condemned at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): specifically the trinitarian teaching implied by
  the Three Ages (condemned on the complaint of Peter of Capua against Abbot Joachim's attack on
  Peter Lombard's doctrine of the Trinity).
- Modern reception: Ernest Benz, Norman Cohn, Marjorie Reeves, and Bernard McGinn have studied
  Joachim's eschatology and its influence on Western prophetic and revolutionary thought.
