# Augustine of Hippo

Aurelius Augustinus (354–430 CE), bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa, the most
influential of the Latin Fathers and the principal architect of Western Christian theology.
This page is anchored, so far, in his apologetic masterwork [[city-of-god|*De Civitate Dei*]]
(*The City of God*), begun **413 CE** in the year of the death of his friend
[[city-of-god|Marcellinus]] and completed in **426**, issued in detached installments over
that span (the maturest years of his life, ages 59–72).

## Biographical Context

Born in Thagaste in Numidia; trained as a rhetorician; passed through Manichaeism
([[manichaeism|Manichaeism]]) and Neoplatonism before his conversion (386) and baptism by
Ambrose of Milan. Bishop of Hippo from 395/6. He had, by his own account in *City of God*,
"only sufficient Greek to refer to Greek authors on points of importance" — his learning was
overwhelmingly in the **Latin** tradition (Virgil, Cicero, Varro, Sallust, Apuleius), a fact
that shapes which authorities he engages.

## Hermeneutical Method

Augustine reads Scripture on multiple levels, anticipating the medieval **Quadriga**
([[fourfold-sense]]): literal/historical alongside [[allegorical-reading|allegorical]],
moral (tropological), and anagogical senses. In *City of God* his characteristic moves are
(1) **figural/typological** reading of Old Testament history as prefiguring the Church and the
heavenly city ([[typology]]), and (2) a **philosophy of history** that reads all of time as the
intertwined pilgrimage of two communities. He treats pagan literature and philosophy critically
but respectfully, conceding what is true (especially in the Platonists) and exposing what is
incoherent.

His **theory of interpretation** is set out programmatically in [[on-christian-doctrine|*De
Doctrina Christiana*]], which structures all interpretation into two halves: the *modus
inveniendi* (discovering meaning, Books I–III) and the *modus proferendi* (expressing it, Book
IV) — Christianizing the classical rhetorical division of *inventio* and *elocutio*. The
governing principles there are: (1) **caritas as the rule of interpretation** — a reading that
builds up love of God and neighbor "does not yet understand [Scripture] as he ought" if it
fails to, and even a mistaken reading that builds charity errs only as a traveler who reaches
the destination by leaving the road (Bk. I, chs. 35–36); (2) the master rule that **anything
which, taken literally, cannot be referred to purity of life or soundness of doctrine must be
taken [[figurative-interpretation|figuratively]]** (Bk. III, ch. 10); and (3) the
[[spoils-of-egypt|"spoils of Egypt"]] licence to conscript true pagan learning for Christian
use. The work rests sign-theory on Christology via the analogy of the [[use-and-enjoyment|Word
made flesh and the spoken word]] (Bk. I, ch. 13).

## Major Works (as engaged in this wiki)

- **[[city-of-god|*The City of God*]]** — apology occasioned by the sack of Rome (410),
  defending Christianity against the charge that abandoning the old gods caused Rome's fall, and
  developing the doctrine of the two cities. (Whole work / Books I–XXII ingested.)
- **[[on-christian-doctrine|*On Christian Doctrine* (*De Doctrina Christiana*)]]** — the
  four-book charter of Western Christian hermeneutics (Books I–III, 397 CE; Book IV, 426/427).
  The foundational source for medieval [[allegorical-reading|allegory]], the
  [[fourfold-sense|Quadriga]], and the preaching manuals. (Whole work / Books I–IV ingested.)

## Characteristic Positions

- **History has two cities** ([[two-cities]]): the *civitas Dei* and the *civitas terrena*,
  distinguished by two loves — love of God to the contempt of self, and love of self to the
  contempt of God.
- **Earthly prosperity is not the reward of piety**: the true God grants and withholds temporal
  empire by inscrutable [[divine-providence|providence]], not in exchange for worship; good and
  ill befall good and bad alike in this life.
- **Critique of Roman civil religion and of Neoplatonism**: the Platonists came nearest the truth
  about God yet failed to find the one [[two-cities|mediator]], Christ, and conceded worship to
  demons.
- **Evil is privation, not nature** ([[evil-as-privation]]): no nature is contrary to God but
  non-being; the evil will has no efficient cause, only a "deficient" one — a defection from the
  highest good. This grounds his answer to the Manichees and to the problem of evil.
- **Creation and time**: time was created *with* the world, not within a prior time; God's eternity is
  changeless; the cyclic recurrence of the philosophers is rejected. The world is young (under 6,000
  years).
- **The fall, original sin, and grace** ([[original-sin]]): pride is the root of the fall; death is its
  penal consequence; disordered concupiscence is transmitted to all "in that one man." The will, freed
  by grace, is the seat of the two loves.
- **Inspired Septuagint**: the seventy translators ([[septuagint|Septuagint]]) were moved by the same
  Spirit as the prophets, so their divergences from the Hebrew are themselves prophecy — a position
  against Jerome's preference for the *hebraica veritas*. In *De Doctrina* II.8 he likewise gives the
  pre-eminence to the Septuagint among Greek versions and to the *Itala* among Latin ones.
- **A forty-four-book Old Testament including the deuterocanonicals**: in *De Doctrina* II.8 Augustine
  enumerates an OT canon of forty-four books that unambiguously includes Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees,
  Wisdom, and Ecclesiasticus (he flags uncertainty over the authorship of the last two but keeps them as
  "authoritative"). The criterion of canonicity is the consensus of the catholic churches, weighting the
  apostolic sees. This is the canon ratified at the councils of his region (Hippo/Carthage) and a key
  patristic warrant for the later Catholic deuterocanon. See [[on-christian-doctrine]].
- **Qualified reception of a schismatic's method**: he commends the seven exegetical rules of
  [[tichonius|Tichonius]] the [[donatism|Donatist]] (*De Doctrina* III), while correcting Tichonius's
  proto-Pelagian construal of faith and rejecting his Donatist ecclesiology — adopting the
  [[tichonius-seven-rules|method]] across a sectarian boundary.
- **Typological reading of sacred history**: the patriarchs and their acts (Abel, Isaac, Jacob, the
  ark, Melchizedek, the Passover) prefigure Christ and the Church; OT prophecy carries a **threefold
  sense** (of the earthly Jerusalem, of the heavenly, or of both).
- **Peace as the tranquillity of order** ([[peace]]): the [[supreme-good|supreme good]] is eternal life
  and peace; a "people" is defined by the objects of its love; the pilgrim city *uses* earthly peace. No
  philosophy can find happiness in this life (Book XIX).
- **Eschatology — the ends of the two cities** (Books XX–XXII): the **[[last-judgment|last judgment]]**
  proved from both Testaments; the **two resurrections** and the **amillennial** reading of Revelation 20
  ([[millennialism]]) against the chiliasts; the bodily **[[resurrection|resurrection]]** and the
  **[[beatific-vision|beatific vision]]** ("we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise").
- **Eternal punishment and a nascent purgatory** ([[eternal-punishment]], [[purgatory]]): hell is
  endless and bodily; against [[origen|Origen]]'s [[universal-salvation-controversy|universalism]] and
  the softening *misericordes*; a limited pre-judgment purgatorial cleansing for the regenerate.
- **Post-apostolic miracles continue** ([[relic-veneration]]): against incipient cessationism, Augustine
  catalogues healings he personally attests at the relics of [[stephen-protomartyr|Stephen]], read as
  witnesses to the resurrection — "our martyrs are not our gods."

## Trinitarian Theology and Ayres's Defence (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) reads Augustine as a
**fully [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene]]** theologian and rebuts "the modern attack on
Augustine" (Plantinga, Lossky, et al.) — the charge that he over-stressed divine unity, founded
the Trinity in the unitary essence (the [[filioque|filioque]] as symptom), and let an alien
Platonism block a personal Trinitarianism. Ayres's counter-case:

- **The two-stage thesis is discredited**: Augustine met the Platonic books *during* his return
  to Christianity, after [[ambrose|Ambrose]]; Cipriani shows his early Neoplatonic borrowings run
  through Ambrose and [[marius-victorinus|Marius Victorinus]] — "little… he could not have found
  through close reading of Ambrose."
- **Inseparable operation from the start**: *Ep.* 11 (389) — "the Son does not do anything which
  the Father and the Holy Spirit do not also do" — locates his earliest theology "within the
  Latin pro-Nicene tradition." *Sermon* 52 makes inseparable operation a "fundamental doctrinal
  rule": "the distinction of persons, and the inseparableness of operation" (see
  [[opera-trinitatis]]).
- **The grammar of [[divine-simplicity|simplicity]] does not make the essence prior to the
  persons.** In *De Trinitate* 5–7, only two categories obtain — "according to substance" and
  "according to relation"; pressing into the Son's full reality (*essentia de essentia*; the Son
  as "wisdom itself," not wise by participation) *yields* the unity rather than positing a
  substance behind the persons: "we do not find the unity by focusing on something different from
  the persons." Augustine "consistently and specifically rules out the idea that the divine
  essence is prior to the divine persons… there is nothing but the three coeternal and
  consubstantial persons," with the Father as personal source.
- Ayres's irony: Augustine's Platonism plays "virtually the opposite" role to the one critics
  assign it — it "enhance[s] the explanatory power of a fully Nicene Trinitarianism."

Paired with [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]] (Ch. 14), Augustine is Ayres's evidence that
the [[de-regnon-paradigm|East/West (Greek/Latin) typology]] is "inadequate and misleading."

## Influence

The fountainhead of Western theology — on grace, sin, the Church, history, and the relation of
the heavenly and earthly orders — claimed by Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed traditions alike.
