# John Chrysostom

The greatest preacher and homiletical exegete of the Greek Church, pupil of [[diodore-of-tarsus|Diodore of Tarsus]] and fellow-student of [[theodore-of-mopsuestia|Theodore of Mopsuestia]], later Archbishop of Constantinople (398–404) before his deposition and death in exile. His enormous corpus of exegetical homilies made him the practical face of the [[theoria|Antiochene exegetical school]]. This page records his treatment in [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], chs. II–III.

## Hermeneutical Method

Chrysostom is the school's great practical exegete. He divided Scriptural statements into three classes: (a) those admitting a "theoretic" addition to the literal sense; (b) those to be understood solely literally; (c) those admitting only a non-literal (allegorical) meaning. While stating a clear preference for the literal sense, he was "not averse on occasion from citing the figurative as well." His classic definition of a type — **"a prophecy expressed in terms of things"** (*hē dia pragmatōn prophēteia*) — is the standard Antiochene formula. See [[theoria]].

He stressed the value of even the apparently trivial in Scripture: chronological figures and catalogues of names have "profound value," and he devoted two whole homilies to the personal salutations of Romans 16.

## Theory of Inspiration

On the [[inspiration-of-scripture|inspiration of Scripture]] Chrysostom shows both currents of the patristic debate. He could speak of John and Paul as "musical instruments played upon by the Holy Spirit" (the older possession imagery), yet equally emphasised the personal contribution of Moses, John, and Paul to the actual composition of their books.

## On Scripture and Tradition

Kelly cites Chrysostom for the view that the Bible is straightforward and sufficient, while also (with Gregory of Nazianzus, Epiphanius) contrasting written (*engraphōs*) and unwritten (*agraphōs*) tradition.
