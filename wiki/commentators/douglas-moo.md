# Douglas J. Moo

Douglas Moo is Kenneth T. Wessner Professor of New Testament at Wheaton College Graduate School (Wheaton, IL). He taught for over twenty years at Trinity Evangelical Divinity School (TEDS, Deerfield, IL) before moving to Wheaton. He received his PhD from the University of St. Andrews (Scotland). He served as chair of the Committee on Bible Translation for the updated New International Version (NIV 2011), a project that delayed the completion of his Galatians commentary by several years.

Moo is among the most prolific evangelical NT commentators of his generation, with major contributions in BECNT (Romans, 1996; Galatians, 2013), NICNT (Colossians/Philemon), and Pillar/TNTC series. He is one of the leading evangelical voices on Pauline justification and Paul's theology of the law, occupying a position close to Schreiner but with a more sustained and dialogical engagement with the New Perspective on Paul (NPP).

## Biographical Context

His dedication of the Galatians commentary is to his wife Jenny, described as *synergatis* ("fellow worker") in Greek — a Pauline term — for having read the entire manuscript and improved it. This reflects a characteristic scholarly seriousness that permeates the commentary.

His long tenure at TEDS (now Trinity Evangelical Divinity School, part of Trinity International University) placed him in an institution associated with D. A. Carson, Kevin Vanhoozer, and other leading evangelical scholars; Wheaton College is similarly a flagship evangelical liberal-arts institution. His location within this tradition shapes his commitments: grammatical-historical exegesis, theological synthesis, serious critical engagement, confessional evangelical conclusions.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Grammatico-Historical with Theological Synthesis.** Like other BECNT contributors, Moo primarily employs a grammatico-historical framework, attending closely to the Greek text, the LXX background, and the historical situation while explicitly integrating the exegetical results into systematic-theological conclusions. The BECNT format presents Greek texts and detailed additional notes, while the main commentary follows paragraph-level argument.

**Sustained NPP dialogue.** Unlike Schreiner (who presents the anti-NPP case more summarily) or Seifrid (whose engagement is primarily from a Lutheran justification angle), Moo is the most consistently dialogical evangelical commentator on the NPP. He takes Sanders, Dunn, Wright, Barclay, and Watson seriously on their own terms before arguing against them. He is willing to concede important NPP insights (the salvation-historical argument; the ethnic-inclusion dimension) while arguing the NPP does not go far enough in recognizing the anthropological/human inability dimension.

**Moderate Salvation-Historical framework.** Moo steers between Martyn's apocalyptic (which severs continuity with salvation history) and a "flat" reading that minimizes the epochal disruption of the Christ event. He insists both continuity and discontinuity are present in Paul's reading of the OT. 

**Against "faith of Christ" (subjective genitive).** Moo defends the traditional objective genitive reading of *pistis Christou* phrases against the growing scholarly movement toward the subjective ("faithfulness of Christ") reading championed by Hays, Longenecker, and D. Campbell.

**Evangelical-Critical (Selective Historical-Critical).** Moo uses historical-critical tools selectively but operates ultimately within a confessional framework that insists on the theological coherence and authority of the canonical text. He accepts the possibility of an amanuensis for Galatians but does not use this to undermine Pauline authorship.

## Characteristic Positions

**1. South Galatian destination (slight preference).** Moo slightly favors the South Galatian theory (churches of the first missionary journey in the Roman province). This allows for the early date hypothesis (c. AD 48, pre-Jerusalem Council) but he acknowledges the evidence is too finely balanced for confidence.

**2. Against the purely NPP logic.** Moo's most important theological contribution in the commentary: he argues that Paul's polemic against Torah observance in Galatians operates on at least three levels: (a) salvation-historical (Torah era ended with Christ); (b) ethnic/social (Torah observance excluded Gentiles); and (c) anthropological (human inability to do Torah). The NPP accounts for (a) and (b) but neglects (c). His key argument: Gal. 3:10 presupposes human inability in a three-stage syllogism:
  - Only those who do *everything* in the law escape the curse (Deut. 27:26)
  - No one can do everything in the law (assumed, but massively attested in the OT)
  - Therefore, all who rely on Torah observance are under the curse
  Westerholm's summation (quoted approvingly): "The fundamental question addressed by Galatians is not 'What is wrong with Judaism?' but 'What is wrong with humanity that Judaism cannot remedy?'"

**3. Justification is forensic and future-oriented.** Against Wright's membership-in-God's-people redefinition, Moo insists the forensic/judicial sense of *dikaioun* is primary throughout Galatians (and is grounded in the OT judicial *tsādaq* Hiphil). But he adds a dimension often missed: in Galatians, justification has a primarily *future* orientation. The letter is not mainly about "how you get saved initially" but about "ultimate justification" — the final vindication at the judgment. The agitators are arguing that only Torah observers can expect eschatological vindication; Paul argues that faith in Christ, not Torah, is what secures that final verdict in one's favor.

**4. Union with Christ is more fundamental than justification.** Both justification and sanctification/transformation are products of being "in Christ." This preserves the forensic distinction between justification and sanctification while grounding both in the more fundamental category of union. Calvin's "double grace" is the theological model: union with Christ simultaneously gives forensic justification AND real transformation.

**5. "The law of Christ" (6:2) ≠ OT law as fulfilled.** Moo argues that "the law of Christ" in 6:2 is not the Mosaic Torah interpreted and fulfilled in Christ (Longenecker, etc.) but the distinctive ethical authority of the new covenant — Christ's own teaching and the apostolic tradition. Believers are "not under the [Mosaic] law" but under a new covenantal authority. The OT law has no direct authority over believers but serves as an indirect source (mediated through NT teaching) for moral guidance.

**6. Objective genitive on *pistis Christou*.** "Faith in Jesus Christ" (2:16; 3:22), not "the faithfulness of Jesus Christ." Moo's argument is primarily contextual: Abraham's human faith in 3:6–9 dominates the central argument and must shape the reading of "faith" throughout 2:16–3:22. The subjective genitive reading has theological motivations (participatory Christology) but inadequate contextual grounding in Galatians. Parallel constructions in Paul always indicate human faith directed toward God or Christ, never Christ's own faith.

**7. Salvation history AND apocalyptic — not in opposition.** Against Martyn's "pure apocalyptic" reading, which denies any real salvation-historical continuity in Paul's argument, Moo insists that Christ is the *climax* of salvation history (Messiah = the expected one) and at the same time a world-rupturing apocalyptic event. Paul's appeal to Abraham, the covenant, and OT prophets requires the salvation-historical framework; Paul's language of "new creation" and "rescue from the present evil age" (1:4) requires the apocalyptic framework. The two are complementary, not contradictory.

## The Letter of James (PNTC, 2000)

Moo's *The Letter of James* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, Eerdmans, 2000) is a substantial
revision and near-doubling of his earlier Tyndale (TNTC) commentary (1985). It shows the same
grammatical-historical method applied to a very different kind of book, and demonstrates Moo's range
beyond the Pauline corpus into the General Epistles. D. A. Carson (series editor) praises its
"deceptive simplicity" and "gentle tone of thoughtful application." Characteristic positions:

**1. Traditional authorship, early date.** Moo defends [[james-the-just|James the Lord's brother]]
as the author and argues for a date in the *middle 40s* — possibly the earliest NT document,
predating the Apostolic Council. He answers the four standard critical objections and presses a
counter-objection: ancient pseudepigraphal letters were not accepted as authoritative when known to
be forged, so canonical reception presumes belief in genuine authorship. See
[[james-authorship|James: Authorship and Date]].

**2. Spiritual wholeness as the central concern.** Against Dibelius's "chaotic paraenesis" reading
and over-structured rhetorical schemes alike, Moo finds the letter's unifying concern in the call to
undivided commitment to God — *teleios* (wholeness) against *dipsychos* (double-mindedness) and
"friendship with the world" (4:4). The emotional climax (4:4–10, "you adulteresses") is its heart.
See [[double-mindedness|Double-Mindedness and Spiritual Wholeness]].

**3. The James/Paul justification resolution.** Moo's signature contribution. *dikaioō* means
different things in the two authors: for Paul, the initial forensic declaration at conversion; for
James, the ultimate *vindication at the final judgment*, which takes works into account. James's
"faith alone" denotes the *bogus*, word-only faith he attacks (cf. the demons, 2:19), not Pauline
faith. He rejects both the "demonstrate/show-to-be-righteous" escape (the popular evangelical move)
and the "two justifications" model, integrating James's apparent synergism into Pauline monergism by
treating works as Spirit-produced fruit. See [[james-paul-justification|James and Paul on
Justification]] and [[covenantal-nomism|Covenantal Nomism]].

**4. Exegetical decisions.** Divine jealousy (not human envy) in 4:5; anointing as a *consecration
symbol* (not medicinal, sacramental, or exorcistic) in 5:14; physical (not spiritual) illness and a
non-mechanical "prayer of faith" in 5:14–16 (with a sharp critique of "enough faith" healing
theology); the voluntary-oath (not legal-oath) reading of 5:12. See
[[anointing-healing-james-5|Anointing and Healing of the Sick]].

This volume shows Moo applying his Pauline expertise *ecumenically across the canon*: rather than
forcing James into a Pauline mold, he lets James's distinct voice stand while seeking underlying
theological coherence — "Paul strikes at legalism; James at quietism. Each message needs to be
heard."

## Relationship to Other Commentators in the Wiki

**Schreiner (BECNT Romans)**: Both in the same commentary series; both evangelical, Reformed-Baptist in orientation; both anti-NPP on justification. Schreiner is somewhat more summarily anti-NPP; Moo is more dialogical. They share the forensic core of justification and the imputation framework.

**Seifrid (PNTC 2 Corinthians)**: Both anti-NPP on justification; Seifrid from a more Lutheran *theologia crucis* angle, Moo from a more grammatical-historical and systematic-theological approach. Seifrid emphasizes the passive dimension of salvation ("salvation never a possession"); Moo emphasizes union with Christ as the foundational category.

**Ciampa-Rosner (PNTC 1 Corinthians)**: Less engaged with the NPP debate; more OT-primary and biblical-theological. The contrast in method is notable: Ciampa-Rosner locate the structure of 1 Cor. in OT moral templates; Moo in Galatians is engaged with the forensic logic of justification.
