# Porphyry of Tyre

Neoplatonist philosopher (c. 234–305 CE), student and editor of [[plotinus|Plotinus]], and the most
formidable pagan critic of Christianity in late antiquity. He is the principal philosophical adversary
of [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Book X**, where [[augustine|Augustine]] engages him at length — both
as the pagan who came nearest the truth and as the paradigm of the *pride* that could not accept the
incarnate Mediator. (Augustine's fullest critique continues into the later books, not yet ingested.)

## Works Engaged by Augustine

- **_De Regressu Animae_ ("On the Return of the Soul")** — Augustine's most-cited Porphyrian source. It
  endorses **theurgy** (with hesitation) as purifying the lower "spiritual" part of the soul but not the
  intellectual part; discusses the "Principles" (a Father and a paternal Intellect/Son, with something
  dimly like a third); rejects Plato's transmigration of human souls into animals; and famously confesses
  that **no universal way of the soul's deliverance** had yet been found "either from the truest
  philosophy, or… the Indians, or… the Chaldæans, or from any source whatever."
- **_Letter to Anebo_** (the Egyptian priest) — written "in the character of an inquirer," it raises
  searching objections to theurgic and Egyptian mysteries (why better spirits obey worse, why incorporeal
  gods are drawn by sacrificial fumes, why threats coerce the gods). Augustine reads it as Porphyry all
  but unmasking the demonic character of theurgy he elsewhere commends.

## Augustine's Critique

- **Theurgy is incoherent and demonic**: it cannot cleanse the intellectual soul, and its claimed
  cleansing of the spiritual part is defeated by the envy of the very spirits invoked — "O excellent
  theurgy!… in which the violence of an impure envy has more influence than the entreaty of purity and
  holiness."
- **The "Principles" dimly anticipate the Trinity** yet Porphyry, "under the dominion of these envious
  powers," refused to recognize that Christ is the Principle by whose incarnation the soul is purified.
- **The universal way Porphyry sought is the Christian religion** — "I am the way, the truth, and the
  life" — which he lived through but despised because it was being persecuted, and because the humility
  of the Incarnation and the Cross offended his philosophical pride.

Augustine's lament captures his ambivalence: "Would that you had known Him, and… committed yourself for
healing to Him rather than to your own frail and infirm human virtue, or to pernicious and curious arts!"

## Wilken: Porphyry as Critic of Christianity

*(Source: Robert Louis Wilken, [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|*The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*]], Ch. VI, "Porphyry: The Most Learned Critic of All.")*

In Wilken's assessment Porphyry was "the most learned and astute" of all ancient critics of Christianity — a foe of such stature that his writings "claimed the attention of several generations of Christian intellectuals" (Eusebius, Methodius, Apollinarius, Jerome, and [[augustine|Augustine]], still wrestling with him in the *City of God*). Constantine ordered his books burned; Theodosius II repeated the order in 448 CE. **A textual caution Wilken stresses:** almost none of his anti-Christian work survives intact — fragments are scattered, often without secure attribution. The title *Against the Christians* is first attested only c. 1000 CE; Harnack's 97 fragments include many from Macarius Magnes that Timothy Barnes showed cannot be confidently assigned to Porphyry. Wilken therefore leans also on the better-attested ***Philosophy from Oracles***.

**Formation as a critic.** Born at Tyre (c. 233), a Hellenized Phoenician whose native tongue was Syriac, Porphyry studied philology under Longinus at Athens and philosophy under [[plotinus|Plotinus]] at Rome (whose *Enneads* he edited). As a young man he heard [[origen|Origen]] lecture at Caesarea and was repelled — Origen "played the Greek, giving a Greek twist to foreign tales" (Eusebius, *H.E.* 6.19) — but, Wilken suggests, "may even have learned the art of biblical criticism from him." Henry Chadwick proposed Porphyry was asked by Diocletian to furnish a philosophical justification for persecution.

**The Daniel argument (his most consequential criticism).** Porphyry argued — through chronological and literary analysis — that the Book of [[daniel|Daniel]] was not sixth-century prophecy but a *vaticinium ex eventu* composed in Judaea under Antiochus Epiphanes (2nd c. BCE): "whatever he spoke of up till the time of Antiochus contained authentic history, whereas anything he may have conjectured beyond that point was false" (Jerome, *Comm. in Danielem*, prologue — our chief source). Wilken: on the central question Porphyry "had the stronger case." P. M. Casey: "Centuries before the advent of modern biblical criticism, Porphyry already knew that the book of Daniel was a Maccabean pseudepigraph." He refused the **allegorical escape** Origen used — accepting Scripture's literal/historical sense and then exposing its difficulties. This makes Porphyry a recognized ancient precursor of the historical-critical method; see [[biblical-criticism-antiquity|biblical and historical criticism in antiquity]] and (as its opposing hermeneutic) [[allegorical-reading]].

**New Testament criticism.** Porphyry catalogued discrepancies among the evangelists (divergent genealogies and infancy narratives; misattributed OT citations — Mark 1:2 to Isaiah; Matt 13:35 to Isaiah rather than Ps 77; "Jeremiah" for Zechariah at Matt 27:9); and seized on the **Peter–Paul quarrel** at Galatians 2 to argue "that the disciples were not united in their teaching and that from the beginning there was strife and division within the church." His thesis (preserved via Augustine's *De consensu Evangelistarum*): the disciples, not Jesus, "claimed more for their master than he really was," making him Son of God and object of worship.

**Philosophy from Oracles — Jesus the pious sage.** Wilken regards this as Porphyry's deepest and most feared attack. Within his hierarchical theology (one supreme incorporeal God; lesser gods; *daimones*; deified heroes at the bottom), Porphyry placed [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]] in Book 3 **among the heroes** — "a human being, a sage who had been elevated to divinity after his death." Unlike Celsus and Hierocles, **he did not call Jesus a magician** but praised him: an oracle of Hecate calls Jesus "a man of outstanding piety," while faulting Christians, who "are polluted and contaminated and entangled in error" because "they worship this soul" in ignorance. The four-part argument: (1) Jesus a good and pious sage; (2) the disciples corrupted his teaching by worshipping him; (3) one ought to worship the one high God; (4) the Jews are praised for doing so. Eusebius turned this against Hierocles to prove Jesus "not a sorcerer but pious and wise."

**The double apostasy and the universal way.** Porphyry charged Christians with **apostasizing from both** the ancestral Greco-Roman religion *and* the Jewish God — they "cut out for themselves a new way" (Eusebius, *Praep. Ev.* 1.2). The official rhetoric of Maximin Daia's anti-Christian rescript ("persist in that accursed folly"; cities "purged of all . . . *asebeia*") closely echoes him: "Porphyry became the theoretician for the ideas implicit in the actions of Roman officials beginning with Pliny." Against the **particularity** of Christian revelation (John 14:6, "no one comes to the Father but by me") he pressed the problem of the unevangelized: "What became of the innumerable souls" before Christ? "Why did he who is called the Savior hide himself for so many ages?" No philosophy, he concluded, "offers a universal way for the liberation of the soul." (See [[christianity-apostasy-from-judaism]].) He likewise raised sharp puzzles about the **resurrection of the body** (would it conform to Christ's or to Lazarus's? why the wounds, why eating?) — see [[bodily-resurrection]] — and, with Galen and Celsus, faulted Christian **"unreasoning faith"** and the doctrine that God can act contrary to nature ([[divine-providence]]).
