# Thomas R. Schreiner

American evangelical NT scholar at The Southern Baptist Theological Seminary (SBTS), Louisville, KY. One of the foremost Reformed Baptist Pauline scholars of the late 20th and early 21st centuries. His BECNT Romans (1998) is a one-volume alternative to the multi-volume commentaries of Cranfield (ICC) and Dunn (WBC), aimed at pastors and scholars alike. His stated goal: trace the flow of thought and demonstrate inductively that "the glory of God" is the central theme of Romans. Dedicated to John Piper, whose influence on Schreiner's theology is explicit and acknowledged throughout.

## Biographical Context

Student and colleague tradition: closely aligned with John Piper (Desiring God, Bethlehem Baptist Church, Minneapolis), Wayne Grudem (Reformed theology; systematic theology), and D. A. Carson (The Gospel Coalition). These relationships locate Schreiner firmly within the "New Calvinist" or "Young, Restless, Reformed" movement of evangelical Calvinism. Southern Baptist by denomination; Reformed/Calvinist in soteriology (the 2000 Baptist Faith & Message Convention included a significant Calvinist bloc; Schreiner represents the Calvinist wing). Major works beyond the wiki: *The Law and Its Fulfillment* (1993), *Paul, Apostle of God's Glory in Christ* (2001), *New Testament Theology* (2008), *The King in His Beauty: A Biblical Theology of the Old and New Testaments* (2013).

## Hermeneutical Method

**Grammatico-historical exegesis with explicit theological synthesis.** Schreiner is not primarily a literary or social-scientific commentator; his method is the paragraph-by-paragraph tracing of the flow of argument, with close Greek grammatical analysis and systematic theological awareness at every point. He explicitly wants to do both exegesis and theology: "I have also tried to wrestle with the meaning of Romans theologically, and this task is not always in vogue today."

**Reformed canonical framework.** All 13 Pauline letters are taken as authentic (including Pastorals), and Schreiner draws on parallels across the Pauline corpus. The historic formulations of Reformed doctrine (especially justification by faith alone, unconditional election, perseverance of the saints) are treated as "arising from a legitimate reading of Scripture" rather than as impositions upon it.

**Calvin's brevity and lucidity as model.** Schreiner explicitly cites Calvin as the model commentator: "the chief virtue of the interpreter" is "lucid brevity." The BECNT Romans is one volume in deliberate contrast to Cranfield's two (ICC) and Dunn's two (WBC). He reads Calvin's commentary on Romans regularly.

**Inductive argument for God's glory as central theme.** Rather than imposing a doctrinal scheme, Schreiner claims to demonstrate inductively that every section of Romans converges on the glorification of God's name. This is his most distinctive contribution at the macro-level.

## Characteristic Positions

**1. Righteousness of God: forensic + transformative.** Schreiner holds that *dikaiosynē theou* is neither purely forensic (Luther's "gift of righteousness from God") nor purely a divine saving power (Käsemann's apocalyptic reading). It is both: God's declaration of righteousness is a genuine forensic act, and it is inseparable from transformation. The two cannot be divorced because God's effective declaration creates a new ontological reality in the justified person. See [[righteousness-of-god|The Righteousness of God]] and [[justification|Justification]].

**2. Opposition to the New Perspective on Paul.** Schreiner is a leading evangelical critic of E. P. Sanders's covenantal nomism and James Dunn's "works of the law as ethnic boundary markers" thesis. He maintains that "works of the law" in Paul denotes human meritorious achievement broadly, not merely circumcision/food/Sabbath observance. His 1993 monograph (*The Law and Its Fulfillment*) is the fullest evangelical critique of NPP prior to the commentary. See [[new-perspective-on-paul|The New Perspective on Paul]].

**3. Unconditional election (Romans 9).** Schreiner reads Romans 9 as teaching unconditional individual election to salvation — God's elective purpose stands apart from any human works or foreseen faith (9:11–13). This is a Calvinist reading against both Arminian "election based on foreseen faith" and the New Perspective's "corporate/historical election" readings. His primary dialogue partner here is John Piper's *The Justification of God* (1993).

**4. Future mass conversion of ethnic Israel (Romans 11:26).** "All Israel will be saved" refers to the future large-scale salvation of ethnic Israel, probably near the second coming. The hardening of Israel is temporary; God's covenantal faithfulness to ethnic Israel requires a future fulfillment beyond the present-day remnant. This is distinct from the amillennialist "Israel = church" reading and the "elect remnant only" reading. See [[all-israel-saved|"All Israel Will Be Saved" (Rom 11:26)]].

**5. Romans 7:14–25 — the law's inability, not the regenerate/unregenerate question.** Contrary to a common shorthand that files Schreiner with the Reformed "regenerate believer" reading (Cranfield, Murray), his actual position (following M. Seifrid) is that "Paul does not intend to distinguish believers from unbelievers in this text." The passage is about *whether the law can transform* — it cannot. The "I" refers primarily to Paul himself, retrospectively and **paradigmatically** (recapitulating Adam and Israel). He rejects Kümmel's rhetorical-*egō* reading, the Bultmann/Käsemann "self-righteous striving" reading, and the exclusively-Adamic and exclusively-Israel referents alike. See [[romans-7-identity|Romans 7 — The Identity of the "I"]].

**6. Moral law still binding on believers.** Schreiner distinguishes between ceremonial/civil law (food, Sabbath, circumcision — no longer literally binding) and moral norms of the Mosaic law (still applicable to believers, now empowered by the Spirit). Romans 13:8–10 (love as fulfillment of the law) and 8:4 (the Spirit enabling the "righteous requirement of the law") are the key texts. This is a "third use of the law" position (law as rule of life for believers, Reformed) as opposed to Lutheran "law and gospel" antithesis.

**7. Glory of God as ultimate purpose.** The salvation of Jew and Gentile, justification, the mission to Spain, the reconciliation of weak and strong — all are ultimately "from him and through him and to him" (11:36). Schreiner's inductive argument is that Paul's final and deepest motive in Romans is always God's glory, not merely human benefit. He treats the two doxologies as an *inclusio* (1:1–7 ↔ 16:25–27, "soli deo gloria") and reads the "root sin" of both Gentiles (1:21) and Jews (2:24) as the failure to glorify God.

**8. *Hilastērion* (3:25) = propitiation (with expiation), not expiation alone.** Against C. H. Dodd's expiation-only reading, Schreiner follows Morris and Hill: the *hilaskesthai* word-group "cannot be separated from God's wrath." Strikingly, "God himself took the initiative to satisfy and appease his own wrath" — God both propitiates and is propitiated. The background is the mercy seat (Lev 16), with the martyrological sense (4 Macc) not excluded; both propitiation and expiation are present. See [[atonement|Atonement]].

**9. Romans 5:12 — "original death" and federal headship.** On *eph' hō*, Schreiner reads "upon the basis of which": on the basis of death entering through Adam, all *personally* sinned. Sin's universality is explained by the **imputation of Adam's sin through representative/federal headship** plus an inherited corrupt nature — he affirms original sin, but says "original death is more prominent than 'original sin' in this text." Adam is historical. See [[adam|Adam]].

**10. Romans 9:5 — Christ is called "God."** After weighing the doxology-to-the-Father alternative, Schreiner concludes Paul here departs from his usual practice and ascribes deity to Christ ("who is over all, God blessed forever"), heightening the pathos of Israel's unbelief.

**11. Romans 10:4 — Christ the "end" (not "goal") of the law, qualified experientially.** *Telos nomou* = "end," but "Christ is the end of *using the law to establish one's own righteousness* for everyone who believes" — an anthropological/experiential claim, not a global law-gospel abolition statement.

**12. Romans 13:1–7 — submission as a general norm, not an absolute.** Civil (not angelic) authorities are God-ordained; "bear the sword" endorses capital punishment (grounded on Gen 9:6). But the command is "a general exhortation that delineates what is usually the case," qualified by Acts 5:29 — the state may become the beast of Revelation 13, and believers must obey God rather than rulers who command against his will.
