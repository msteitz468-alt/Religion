# C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji)

**Chakravarti Rajagopalachari** (1878–1972), universally known as **Rajaji**, was an Indian statesman, independence leader, and prolific popularizer of Hindu classical literature. He was the last Governor-General of India (1948–1950) and Chief Minister of Madras, a close associate of Gandhi, and founder of the Swatantra Party. In the sphere of religion and culture he is best known for his prose retellings of the two great Hindu epics — the *[[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]]* (Tamil original c. 1943; English 1950) and the *Rāmāyaṇa* (Tamil 1954) — both published by Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan and widely read across generations of Indians and the diaspora.

## Biographical Context

Rajagopalachari came from a Tamil Brahmin family deeply formed by the Vaishnavite devotional tradition. He learned the epics at his mother's knee — a formative experience he explicitly references in his prefaces — and absorbed them as living moral and spiritual literature before he encountered them as texts. His political life was conducted in the shadow of the Mahābhārata's dharmic dilemmas; he often described the experience of writing the Tamil *Mahābhārata* during the crisis year of 1940 (when the Congress had resigned from provincial governments and World War II had begun) as a source of "great peace."

He explicitly regards his retellings as the best service he rendered to his people — "not the political work."

## Hermeneutical Method

Rajagopalachari's approach is **devotional-pedagogical** rather than philological or critical:

- **Stripping accretions**: He views the Sanskrit Mahābhārata as a noble epic core surrounded by centuries of interpolated material (historical, geographical, political, theological) added across "nearly thirty centuries." His retelling removes these to expose the dharmic narrative.
- **Moral and didactic reading**: The epics are valued primarily for their ethical teaching — the "code of life" and "philosophy of social and ethical relations" — rather than as mythological or purely literary artifacts.
- **The Gītā as center**: He consistently identifies the Bhagavad Gītā as the climax and heart of the Mahābhārata — "the noblest of scriptures" — and structures his retelling to build toward it.
- **Character emphasis**: His most memorable interpretive contributions are his character portraits, which tend toward moral sympathy without judgment: Karna as "vain but chivalrous," Duryodhana as having "perverse pride redeemed by great courage in adversity," Bhishma as "the perfect knight," Krishna as "most energetic of men, whose divinity scintillates through a cloud of very human characteristics."
- **Living cultural transmission**: He treats the epics not as ancient documents but as living culture — "these immortal stories as they learnt their mother tongue, at the mother's knee" — and his retelling is self-consciously an act of intergenerational transmission.

**Not a scholarly commentary**: Rajagopalachari makes no claim to philological, text-critical, or academic engagement with the Sanskrit tradition. His retellings are popular condensations, written with the sensibility of a devotee and the craft of a politician-writer rather than a pandit.

## Major Works (religious)

- *Mahabharata* (Tamil 1943; English 1950) — 106-chapter prose retelling; see [[rajagopalachari-mahabharata]]
- *Ramayana* (Tamil 1954; English published by Bhavan) — prose retelling of Vālmīki
- *Bhagavad Gita* — commentary and translation
- *Upanishads* — selections and commentary
- *Kural* — retelling of Tiruvaḷḷuvar's *Tirukkuṛaḷ*

## Influence

Rajagopalachari's Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa became, for millions of English-educated Indians and diaspora readers, **the** accessible entry-point to the epics — a function they continue to serve. This gives his interpretive choices (his sympathy for Karna, his moral emphasis, his Gītā-centered framing) significant cultural reach, even though they reflect one prominent 20th-century Hindu reading rather than the text's own polyphony. Scholars like Wendy Doniger (see [[doniger-the-hindus|*The Hindus*]]) have noted that this kind of popular condensation inevitably flattens the epic's profound moral ambiguity — the very quality captured in *dharmo hi sūkṣmaḥ* ("dharma is subtle") — but Rajagopalachari is not unaware of this: his character portraits preserve considerable moral complexity even within a devotional framework.
