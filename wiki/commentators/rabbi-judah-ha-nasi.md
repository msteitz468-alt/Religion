Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi (c. 135–217 CE), known simply as "Rabbi" (*ha-Rav*) in rabbinic literature, was the Patriarch (*Nasi*) of the Sanhedrin and the editor and compiler of the [[mishna|Mishna]] (c. 200 CE). His compilation transformed the [[oral-torah|Oral Torah]] from a living oral tradition into a written text — a watershed moment in Jewish intellectual history.

## Biographical Context

Born into the dynasty of Hillel, Judah ha-Nasi united extraordinary social status (political head of the Jewish community under Roman authority) with unmatched Torah scholarship. He maintained close relationships with Roman authorities, which gave him the stability to undertake the Mishna's compilation. His academy was located at Beit She'arim and later at Sepphoris in the Galilee.

## The Compilation of the Mishna

Judah ha-Nasi's decisive act was to take the scattered oral traditions, earlier written notes (*megillot setarim*), and the *Mishnah of Rabbi Akiva* and compile them into a single authoritative collection. His editorial decisions involved:

- **Selecting rulings**: He chose which opinions to include as the normative ruling and which to preserve as rejected minority positions
- **Organizing by subject**: The six-order, 63-tractate structure reflects his systematic organization
- **Linguistic standardization**: He edited the text in Mishnaic Hebrew, distinct from Biblical Hebrew and from the Aramaic vernacular

The decision to write down the Oral Torah was itself a legal departure from tradition, justified by the rabbinic principle that "it is time to act for YHWH; they have violated your Torah" (*Ps* 119:126 via *Berakhot* 54a) — the emergency of potential loss overrides the prohibition on writing.

## Hermeneutical Method

As an editor and compiler, Judah ha-Nasi's hermeneutic is one of **selection and presentation**. He preserves minority views alongside majority rulings, establishing a principle that rejected opinions remain part of the living tradition and may be legally relevant in future circumstances. The Mishna almost never cites Scriptural proof-texts for its rulings — this economy distinguishes it from midrashic literature and focuses it as a practical legal handbook.

## Influence

The Mishna became the fixed text upon which all subsequent rabbinic commentary operates. Both the Babylonian Gemara and the Palestinian Gemara are structured as running commentaries on the Mishna. Every subsequent codifier from Alfasi through Maimonides to Caro is in dialogue with the Mishna as the foundational halakhic text.
