# Zhu Xi (1130–1200)

**Zhu Xi** (朱熹, style name Hui'an 晦庵; also known as Huiweng in his later years) is the great
synthesizer of [[neo-confucianism|Daoxue/Neo-Confucianism]] and, after [[confucius|Confucius]] and
[[mencius|Mencius]], the most influential figure in the Confucian tradition — the man whose selection,
commentary, and canon-construction determined the operative form of Confucianism for roughly six
centuries. His achievements range across cosmological metaphysics, moral philosophy, textual
criticism, educational reform, and political thought.

[[adler-reconstructing-confucian-dao|Adler (2014)]] proposes that Zhu Xi is better compared to
**Benedict of Nursia** (primarily concerned with facilitating religious practice — building a
sustainable structure for sagehood-cultivation) than to Thomas Aquinas (the systematic synthesizer).
The comparison illuminates that Zhu Xi's deepest concern was not speculative philosophy but the
practical question of how to become a sage.

## Intellectual Biography (Adler 2014)

### Early Formation
- Born 1130 in Youxi, Fujian province.
- Father died young. Educated by private scholars.
- **Age ~18–22**: Studied Chan Buddhism under Daoqian, a disciple of Dahui Zonggao (the great
  proponent of koan/active-engagement Chan).
- Turned to Confucianism: 1153, began study under **Li Tong** (1093–1163, also known as Teacher
  Yanping), in the lineage Cheng Yi → Yang Shi → Luo Congyan → Li Tong.
- First read [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi's]] texts in 1152 (didn't understand); again in 1160 (began
  to grasp partial meaning under Li Tong's guidance).

### The Zhong/He Problem and Spiritual Crisis (1153–1169)

The central problem of Zhu Xi's intellectual development was the *weifa/yifa* (未發/已發,
"unexpressed/expressed") problem from *Zhongyong* 1: before the emotions are aroused, the mind is
in a state of centrality (*zhong*, 中); when aroused in proper proportion, it achieves harmony (*he*,
和). The question: how do you cultivate the still, pre-arousal phase of mind, and how does it relate
to the active, post-arousal phase?

**Stage 1 (1153–1163, under Li Tong):** Focus on the still, unexpressed phase; quiet-sitting;
*weifa* = human nature (*xing*), *yifa* = mind (*xin*). The sage's work is primarily in nourishing
the still mind.

**Stage 2 (1167–1168, under Zhang Shi's influence):** Zhang Shi (student of Hu Hong's Hunan school)
persuaded Zhu that the sage's work must be primarily in activity — that genuine cultivation happens
in response to affairs, not in stillness. Excessive focus on stillness risked Chan Buddhist quietism.

**Stage 3 — The Ji-chou Enlightenment (1169):** Resolution through **interpenetration**. Zhu Xi
found the synthesis: stillness within activity, activity within stillness — the two phases are not
separate moments to be cultivated successively, but aspects of a single, always-present mind,
joined by *jing* (reverent composure). The formula: Zhang Zai's dictum that "the mind connects the
nature and the feelings" (*xin tong xing qing*), read through the *ti-yong* (substance-function)
rubric: still-mind as substance (*ti*), expressed-mind as function (*yong*).

This enlightenment was then found cosmologically confirmed in [[tongshu|Tongshu section 16]]:
"That which has no activity in activity, and no stillness in stillness, is spirit" — the
interpenetration of activity and stillness at the level of principle. This is the core reason Zhu Xi
elevated [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] into the [[daotong|daotong]] succession.

### The Construction of Zhou Dunyi's Canonical Status (1169–1196)

Zhu Xi wrote 22 pieces promoting Zhou Dunyi between 1169 and his death in 1200. Key moments:
- **1166**: Published the Changsha edition of Zhou's collected works.
- **1169**: First postface (*Zhouzi Taiji Tongshu houxu*) — placed the *Taijitu shuo* first;
  connected Zhou to the *daotong* succession as the bridge between Mencius and the Chengs.
- **1179**: Revised postface (*Nankang edition*) — deepened the daotong claim; acknowledged but
  dismissed the Daoist diagram-origin evidence.
- **1187**: Completed commentary on the *Tongshu* (preface dated ding-wei/1187).

The traditional account before Zhu Xi credited **Cheng Hao** as the one who had independently
revived the Way since Mencius. Zhu Xi systematically replaced this with Zhou Dunyi as the common
teacher of both Cheng brothers.

## Hermeneutical Method

### The Yijing Commentary (*Zhouyi benyi*, 1188)

Zhu Xi's hermeneutical approach to the Yi is stated in his fundamental principle: **"The Yi was
originally created for divination"** (*Yi ben wei bushi er zuo*). This breaks from both **Wang Bi**
(who minimized the graphic/numerological elements to focus on the text as moral guidance) and **Cheng
Yi** (who read the Yi as a book of principle for the *junzi*). Against both, Zhu Xi:

1. **Insists on the images**: the yin-yang structure of the lines and trigrams must be analyzed
   first; the moral reading must be grounded in the graphic fact.
2. **Universalizes access**: Fuxi created the Yi for all people — farmers, merchants, artisans —
   not only the literate. Moral guidance is accessed *through* divination, not reading alone.
3. **Synthesizes xiangshu and yili**: incorporates Shao Yong's cosmological/numerological analysis
   (River Chart, Fuxi sequences, hexagram fluctuation diagrams) with Cheng Yi's moral-principle
   reading.

His commentary method: begin each hexagram entry with the yin-yang structural analysis (which trigrams,
which lines are "central and correct," which line is the "ruler"), then derive the prognostication for
the diviner, then explicate the Tuanzhuan and Xiangzhuan. The commentary is deliberately minimal —
the meaning is activated in the reader's personal engagement through divination, not delivered by
exegesis. Adler calls this "editorial conservatism + hermeneutic activism."

The **Fu hexagram** (24, Return: one yang line at the base of total yin) is the theological heart of
Zhu Xi's Yi commentary: "In Fu is seen the mind of Heaven and Earth." This is the cosmic moment of
incipient creativity — yang reborn at the point of extinction — which mirrors the human moral moment
of the original mind reasserting itself at the maximum point of selfish clouding.

### Li/Qi Metaphysics as Reading Framework
Every text Zhu Xi commented on was read through his *li* (理, principle/pattern) and *qi* (氣,
psychophysical stuff) framework:
- **Li** has logical priority over *qi*; li is the ordered, patterned character of things.
- Li and *qi* are never actually separate — "li is never separate from *qi*."
- The *taiji* = li in its cosmological totality (Zhu's equation with [[taiji|taiji]]).
- Human nature (*xing*) is li as received by individual humans; human nature is originally good
  (following Mencius), though the *physical endowment* (*qi zhi zhi xing*) of particular persons
  varies in clarity and purity.

### Self-Cultivation Method: *Jing* and *Ge Wu*
Two complementary pillars:
1. **Jing** (敬, reverent composure): the cultivation of the unexpressed mind; attentiveness,
   recollectedness, not allowing selfish desire to scatter the mind. Borrowed from Cheng Yi:
   "The effort to maintain reverent composure joins the states of activity and stillness at their
   point of intersection."
2. **Ge wu** (格物, investigating things/affairs): the intellectual dimension; extending knowledge
   by examining the *li* of things, texts, and affairs. From the *Daxue* (Great Learning).

Against the Lu-Wang school's emphasis on direct, intuitive grasp of principle in the mind (*cheng
yi*, making the will authentic), Zhu Xi argued that the *ge wu* — outward study — was essential.
Lu Jiuyuan (1139–1193), his great contemporary opponent, charged that *ge wu* was endless external
study that missed the immediate presence of *li* in the mind itself.

### Canonization by Commentary
Zhu Xi's *Collected Comments on the Four Books in Chapters and Sentences* (*Sishu jizhu*) is
simultaneously commentary and canon-construction: it defined the Four Books as the operative canon
and presented Zhu's reading as the recovery of the ancient sages' intention. This is a
Confucian parallel to the Vedanta *bhashya* tradition: the commentary is the interpretive lens
through which the text henceforth *is* the text.

## Major Works

1. **Commentary on the *Tongshu*** (*Tongshu zhu*, 1187) — full annotated translation with
   additional comments from the *Yulei*. See [[tongshu|Tongshu]].
2. **Commentary on the *Taijitu shuo*** — standard interpretation equating *taiji* with *li*.
   See [[taijitu-shuo|Taijitu shuo]].
3. **Four Books Commentaries** (*Sishu jizhu*): commentaries on the [[analects|Analects]],
   [[mencius|Mencius]], *Great Learning* (*Daxue*), and *Doctrine of the Mean* (*Zhongyong*).
   Became the basis of the civil service examinations from 1313 (Yuan) to 1905.
4. ***Jinsilu*** ("Reflections on Things at Hand," compiled 1175 with Lü Zuqian): anthology of
   the first generation of Daoxue thinkers; opens with Zhou Dunyi's *Taijitu shuo*.
5. ***Classified Conversations*** (*Yulei*, compiled posthumously by Li Jingde, 1270): records of
   Zhu Xi's teaching discussions; an invaluable supplement to the formal commentaries.
6. **Commentary on the *Zhouyi*** (*Zhouyi benyi* 周易本義, 1188) — the definitive Yijing
   commentary; first complete English translation by Adler (Columbia, 2020). See
   [[yijing|Yijing]] and [[adler-original-meaning-yijing|Adler 2020]].
7. *Introduction to the Study of the Yi* (*Yixue qimeng*, 1186) — shorter companion to the
   *benyi*; covers the divination method in detail and the xiangshu diagrams (Shao Yong-based).

## Key Concepts

- **Daotong** (道統): the "succession of the Way" from the ancient sages through Zhou Dunyi to
  the Chengs and (implicitly) to Zhu Xi. His construction of [[zhou-dunyi|Zhou Dunyi]] as the
  post-Mencius link was his most consequential historical claim. See [[daotong|daotong]].
- **Taiji** (太極): Supreme Polarity = li in its cosmological totality. See [[taiji|taiji]].
- **Cheng** (誠): being authentic / actualizing principle; the foundation of sagehood in the
  *Tongshu*; Zhu Xi connected this to his *li* metaphysics.
- **Ji** (幾): incipience; the subtle moment at the onset of activity when the moral order and
  selfish desire begin to differentiate; the critical moment for moral attention.
- **Daoxin/Renxin** (道心/人心): moral mind / human mind; from the "sixteen-word teaching"
  in the *Shujing*: "The human mind is precarious, the mind of the Way is barely perceptible."

## Characteristic Positions and Controversies

1. **Against Lu Jiuyuan**: Zhu Xi insisted on the necessity of outward study (*ge wu*) and rejected
   Lu's claim that the mind directly contains all principle without investigation. This is the
   "Goose Lake debate" of 1175 and subsequent correspondence.
2. **Against Chan Buddhism**: Zhu Xi carefully distinguished *jing* (reverent composure) from Chan
   "silent illumination" (*mo zhao*) even though the surface resemblance was obvious (noted by both
   sides). Cultivation must be grounded in the Confucian moral order, not in empty awareness.
3. **The taiji/wuji dispute**: Against Lu Jiuyuan's charge that *wuji* was Daoist, Zhu Xi argued
   it simply described the formlessness and omnipresence of *li*.

## Historical Influence

- Imperial canonization: 1227 (Lizong ennobled Zhu Xi), 1241 (installed in Confucian temple).
- Yuan dynasty (1313): Zhu Xi's Four Books commentaries become basis of civil service examinations.
- Late imperial China, Joseon Korea, Tokugawa Japan, Vietnam: the Cheng-Zhu school was the
  dominant official framework — arguably the most institutionally powerful body of commentary in
  world history.
- Opposed by Wang Yangming's "School of Mind" (*xinxue*) in the Ming; both traditions contested
  dominance through the Qing.
