# Zhu Xi

**Zhu Xi** (1130–1200) is the great synthesizer of [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] and, after
[[confucius|Confucius]] and [[mencius|Mencius]], the most influential figure in the Confucian tradition —
the man whose **selection and commentary created the operative Confucian canon**.

> **Note:** complete English translations of his commentaries are `[COPYRIGHT]`/partial (Daniel Gardner's
> selections; see *Needed Sources.md* → Outstanding). Written here from secondary knowledge.

## Achievement — Canonizing the Four Books

Zhu Xi grouped the [[analects|Analects]], the [[mencius|Mencius]], the *Great Learning*, and the
*Doctrine of the Mean* as the **Four Books** (*Sishu*), wrote authoritative commentaries on them, and
established them — over the older Five Classics — as the **core curriculum and the basis of the imperial
examinations** for roughly six centuries (until 1905). Few commentators in any tradition have so directly
determined what a canon *is*.

## Characteristic Positions

A metaphysics of **li** (理, principle) and **qi** (氣, material force): all things share in principle,
unified in the **Supreme Ultimate** (*taiji*); self-cultivation proceeds by the **"investigation of
things"** (*gewu*) to apprehend principle and recover one's originally good nature. His is the
**Cheng-Zhu "School of Principle"**, later challenged by Wang Yangming's "School of Mind."

## Hermeneutical Method

Canonization-by-commentary: Zhu Xi reads the Four Books through the li/qi framework and presents that
reading as the recovery of the sages' original meaning — a Confucian parallel to the Vedanta
[[brahma-sutras|bhashya]] tradition and the Daoist [[wang-bi-vs-heshang-gong|commentary]] divide, where
the *commentary* defines the school.

## Influence

Zhu Xi's synthesis was the orthodox framework of late imperial China, Joseon Korea, Tokugawa Japan, and
Vietnam — arguably the most institutionally powerful body of commentary in world history.
