John Goldingay is one of the most prolific and theologically distinctive British evangelical OT scholars of his generation. A lifelong Anglican, he spent much of his career at St John's College Nottingham before moving to Fuller Theological Seminary as Robert W. Jacks Distinguished Professor of Old Testament. His NICOT Jeremiah (2021) and Lamentations (2022) are the most recent volumes in the wiki. He is also known for his three-volume *Old Testament Theology*, three-volume commentary on Psalms (BCOTWP), and landmark WBC commentary on Daniel.

Goldingay occupies a distinct position in the wiki: he is more willing than Oswalt or Waltke to work with the compositional complexity of prophetic texts, more attentive to their literary and rhetorical texture than to source-critical reconstruction, and more inclined to let the text's tensions stand without resolution.

## Background

Goldingay grew up in the Anglican tradition and taught at St John's Nottingham (the college that trained many British evangelical pastors) before moving to Fuller Theological Seminary. His move to Fuller brought him into a different ecclesial and academic environment — American evangelical, ecumenical, global. His later work shows increasing engagement with feminist, postcolonial, and reader-response approaches, not as a capitulation to these but as ways of reading that the text itself invites.

His two-volume commentary on Jeremiah (2021) and Lamentations (2022) represent the new NICOT under series editor Bill T. Arnold (who joined as co-editor in 2020). Both are among the most intellectually ambitious commentaries in the series.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. The Gospels Analogy (Goldingay's Signature Contribution)

Goldingay's most memorable and productive methodological move: comparing the composition of the Jeremiah scroll to the composition of the NT Gospels.

The analogy works as follows:
- Both combine messages and stories, redacted over time with multiple contributors
- Both have multiple versions that are all "authentic" rather than one being simply superior
- The MT Jeremiah (longer, incorporating the story's aftermath) is "a bit like Luke-Acts"
- The LXX Jeremiah (shorter, its own coherent shape) is "a bit like Matthew"
- The 604 BCE scroll (Baruch's first production) is "a bit like Mark" — nearest the original
- The people who shaped the scroll are "curators and storytellers," not unlike the Gospel evangelists

Implications: the two-version phenomenon of Jeremiah (MT vs. LXX) is not a textual embarrassment but analogous to the Gospel tradition's enriching diversity. Neither version needs to be subordinated to the other.

### 2. "Curators and Storytellers" Instead of "Deuteronomists"

Goldingay refuses the term "Deuteronomists" — too loaded, too hypothetical, too dependent on a scholarly construct that may not correspond to any actual social group. Instead:
- **Curators**: Those who preserve, arrange, and make material available across time; they select and sequence without claiming to author
- **Storytellers**: Those who shape narrative to communicate the prophet's significance for their contemporaries

This reframing depoliticizes the source-critical question: it doesn't matter whether material came from "the historical Jeremiah" or from a curator working 50 years later. Both are "authentic" as vehicles of God's word.

### 3. Focus on the Received Text

Goldingay does not attempt to date individual passages to specific historical moments within Jeremiah's career, nor to distinguish "more factual" from "more fictional" in the narratives. "I focus on what the curators who generated the documents present to us in each chapter." This is neither naive conservatism nor pure synchronic reading — it is a principled acknowledgment that the history of composition is irrecoverable and that the received text is what the community of faith has identified as authoritative.

### 4. The "Both-And" Hermeneutic

The most theologically sophisticated feature of Goldingay's commentary: resistance to systematic, univocal readings. The scroll consistently speaks from multiple angles on major questions (see "Theology of a Both-And Character" in the Introduction). Goldingay: "Its combustible mix of politics and prophecy points to a Word of God that is dynamic, contextually responsive, and stubbornly resistant to commodification by any one group or ideology."

### 5. First Testament (Not "Old Testament")

Goldingay consistently uses the term "First Testament" rather than "Old Testament" — both because "old" implies superseded and because the Christian OT is in fact a set of documents that functioned as a "first" or prior testament to what became the NT. This terminological commitment reflects his concern to read the texts as Christian Scripture without subordinating them to a later canonical horizon.

## Key Theological Positions

**The "word became flesh" in Jeremiah**: "In Jeremiah, the message — the *word* (*haddābār*) — became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." Jeremiah's person and suffering are themselves part of the divine word. The Confessions embody this: Jeremiah's protests to God are not failures of faith but features of his prophetic calling.

**The "both-and" theology**: The scroll's refusal to speak univocally on theological questions is not incoherence but wisdom. A prophet "knows what time it is" — can discern which of the both-ands needs emphasizing in this moment. The scroll equips readers for contextual theological judgment, not just systematic application.

**Finalization in the Babylonian period**: Against the scholarly consensus (Persian period, ca. 530-400 BCE), Goldingay argues for finalization ca. 550-540s BCE. No Persian references, no Persian loanwords, no awareness of Cyrus's decree in the scroll.

**Lamentations: The problem of presence, not absence**: "The problem in Lamentations is not the absence of God. It is the presence of God." God is directly responsible; that is what makes both confession and protest possible. The book provides neither pure theodicy nor pure anti-theodicy but holds both in irreducible tension.

**The canonical response to Lamentations**: Isaiah 40-55 is Yahweh's response to Lamentations' unanswered prayers within the First Testament. The NT confirms: the Gospels' story and Revelation's promises are the ultimate response.
