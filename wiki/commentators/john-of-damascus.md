# John of Damascus

John of Damascus (c. 675–749) — Yanah ibn Mansur ibn Sarjun, who served the Muslim rulers of Damascus before moving to Jerusalem — is traditionally seen as "the final flowering of Patristic theology," its summary and consummation. Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §9.2) accepts his role as **synthesizer** but stresses he is also a beginning: "as much as the Arabic conquest was an end, it was also a beginning." His ***Dialectica*** (part of the *Fount of Knowledge*) "draws more heavily on Aristotle and the commentary tradition than any previous Chalcedonian writer," yet by a "via media" — he mocked miaphysites for following "'St. Aristotle' whom they consider the thirteenth apostle." His argumentative patterns are "much closer to... sixth- and early seventh-century thinkers than to the great Maximian synthesis."

## Starting with Hypostases

John makes the **hypostasis ontologically foundational**: "neither undetermined ousia, nor the substantial difference, nor the species, nor the accident subsist by themselves, but only hypostases or individuals. In them are seen substances... and accidents" (*Dial.* 43). Hypostases "do not inhere in each other" — each "separate and by itself." This is an **asymmetric ontology**: "hypostases are the carrier of being. Everything else inheres in them, but they never inhere" (the Trinity excepted — see below).

Following U. M. Lang, Zachhuber credits John as **"the originator" of the classical [[enhypostaton|*enhypostaton*]] doctrine** — that Christ's human nature is *enhypostaton* insofar as it exists "in" the hypostasis of the Logos. This **reinterprets** [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]]'s term (for whom it meant "existing in itself, not as an accident"), combining two earlier trajectories: the Leontian *enhypostaton* and the insubsistence Christology of [[john-the-grammarian-of-caesarea|John the Grammarian]] and [[leontius-of-jerusalem|Leontius of Jerusalem]].

## The Impossibility of a Composite Nature

John uniquely holds "there can be no composite natures" (beyond cross-breeds like the mule) — going beyond all earlier Chalcedonians, who allowed composites elsewhere. [[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus]] had permitted composite natures under strict conditions; Leontius of Jerusalem allowed God to merge natures. John's "no class of Christs" argument bars a single composite nature for Christ. Philosophically, composite natures were only needed under the NNWH-principle; John effectively **abandons NNWH in its classical form**, reformulating it as the demand that substances inhere in *at least one* hypostasis, not in *their own*. Once multiple *ousiai* can inhere in one hypostasis, no single nature is needed to unify it.

## Hypostasis, Property, and Historical Uniqueness

Compared with Leontius of Jerusalem, John shows "remarkably little interest in properties." The hypostasis is essentially a "container": "two kinds of being, hypostases and the things that exist in them." This risks reducing primary being "to a brute fact" (its bare *Vorhandenheit*). John's response is **historical/biological uniqueness**: hypostasis is "the original composition (σύμπηξις) of the existence of each thing as itself"; "it is therefore impossible for that which once came into being independently to receive another beginning of its existence." The "mode of origin (τρόπος τῆς ὑπάρξεως) introduces the difference between hypostases" — what Zachhuber calls "the beginnings of a notion of personal existence in Eastern Patristic thought." (Christologically: the virgin birth means no human hypostasis, since absent human sperm "the hypostasis... [is] the generative divine Logos" — a framework formally like [[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinarius]]'s but turned to a different end.)

## Universal Nature and the Threefold Universal

John polemicizes against miaphysite "particular substances" — citing [[john-philoponus|Philoponus]]'s *Arbiter* as evidence of tritheism, a citation "with long-lasting consequences for our understanding of sixth-century Patristic philosophy." The Incarnation "was made of common ousiai... it is not possible to find a particular and individual nature." His **threefold universal** (EF 55): nature in pure thought (not assumed — would be a deceitful Incarnation); nature in the species (not assumed — all humans would be incarnate); and **nature in the individual** — "the same as the one in the species" but with added accidents in one hypostasis. The Logos assumed the third. Yet elsewhere (EF 8) John says the common nature of created beings is "only seen in thought," so Zachhuber finds an unresolved tension over what John meant by "nature in the species."

## Divine Nature: Perichoresis and the Symmetric Exception

In the doctrine of God, John relies mainly on ps.-Dionysius and divine oneness; the Cappadocian ousia/hypostasis language migrates to the **Christological** section — evidence of the broad sixth-century migration of that account from Trinity to Christology. His key real/conceptual inversion (EF 8): in *created* being, hypostases are seen "in reality" and the common nature "only in thought"; in the *Trinity* the reverse — unity is "in reality," distinctions known "by conception." The Trinity is "the paradigm of his symmetric ontology": hypostases are *enousion* and the ousia *enhypostaton*, perfectly inhering through **perichoresis** (his "most celebrated doctrinal innovation"). Thus the Damascene's "radical ontology from below" had to be **shielded** from corroding the Trinity; little remains of Maximus's cosmic unity — instead, a "world of hypostases."

## End and Beginning

John "was not a mere compiler" but forged "his own distinctive voice," synthesizing the philosophy-of-the-hypostasis line (John the Grammarian → Leontius of Jerusalem) with the Leontian *enhypostaton* school. His departure from Cappadocian assumptions is radical: where their philosophy "was not a philosophy of individuality let alone personality," John "began with the hypostasis... the carrier of all other being." The "breathtakingly innovative idea" — that "a whole individual... can be conceived of without concrete realization because the latter is reduced to the notion of existence pure and simply" — is "deeply alien to the whole of ancient metaphysics, including Cappadocian metaphysics." Here Zachhuber locates a fairly clear-cut **essence/existence duality**, "a far-reaching philosophical innovation" pointing toward later (medieval and modern) developments. Through Burgundio of Pisa's Latin *De fide orthodoxa* and Peter Lombard's *Sentences*, the Damascene's ideas passed into Western scholasticism.
