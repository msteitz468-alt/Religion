# Arthur Green

Contemporary scholar of Jewish mysticism and constructive Jewish theology. In the corpus currently grounding this page, Green is the author of the long scholarly **Introduction** to [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol1|Daniel C. Matt’s *The Zohar: Pritzker Edition*, Volume I]] (Stanford University Press) — the essay to which Matt’s Translator’s Introduction defers for Kabbalah’s development, the Zohar’s historical and literary context, style, the complex question of authorship, and sefirotic symbolism.

---

## Biographical and Scholarly Context

*Only what the Pritzker Vol. I front matter itself supports is claimed here; fuller biography awaits other sources.*

Green identifies himself as a student of **Alexander Altmann** (1906–1987) at Brandeis University — “the first to bring Scholem’s approach to kabbalistic studies to North American shores,” whose students include both Green and [[daniel-matt|Daniel C. Matt]]. Green presents his introduction as a **digest** of what he considers the finest post-Scholem Zohar scholarship (three or four generations of “reapers in the field”), not as an attempt to break new ground on most topics; he accepts responsibility for misunderstandings while attributing the insights to that collective, including members of the Academic Committee for the Translation of the Zohar.

---

## Hermeneutical Method (as enacted in the Pritzker introduction)

Green’s introduction functions as **historical and symbolic initiation** for readers of the English Zohar:

- **Historical map** of Kabbalah in the century before the Zohar (Provence / *Bahir* / Isaac the Blind → Gerona / Naḥmanides → Castile / “Gnostic Circle” → Zohar as Castilian culmination) — see [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]].
- **Literary-theological framing**: the Zohar as “sacred fantasy” and “lush garden of sacred eros,” without (in Green’s explicit caveat) impugning the truth of its insights.
- **Authorship as contested positions** (traditional rediscovery claim; de León distribution and pseudepigraphy; Scholem single authorship; Liebes–Meroz multi-generation school) — recorded without collapsing into a single verdict; see [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]].
- **Sefirotic symbolic lexicon**: a summary portrait of the sefirot as they appear in emerging late-thirteenth-century Castilian Kabbalah (not a single-passage paraphrase), to equip readers for the Zohar’s symbolic language.
- **Reception history**: from early veneration as quasi-scripture through print-era canonicity, liturgical digests, Hasidic reverence, Enlightenment/Wissenschaft marginalization (Graetz), and modern scholarly renewal (Scholem, Tishby, Liebes, Wolfson, Idel, Matt).

## Characteristic Framing Theses (Pritzker Vol. I Introduction)

All claims below are **Green’s** (or Green’s attributed summaries of other scholars):

- **Sacred fantasy / sacred eros.** The Zohar is “the great medieval Jewish compendium of mysticism, myth, and esoteric teaching,” possibly “the highest expression of Jewish literary imagination in the Middle Ages,” and “a lush garden of sacred eros” — loves of master and disciple, companions, Israel and Shekhinah, and male–female within the Godhead. Calling it sacred fantasy, Green argues, does not impugn religious profundity; medieval Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theologies all elaborate unseen realms pictorially, and Judaism’s aniconic constraints concentrated that energy in word and exegesis.
- **Traditional appearance claim.** Distributors c. 1300 claimed an ancient text recently rediscovered, composed in Rabbi Shim’on son of Yoḥai’s second-century circle; majority reception came to rank it with Bible and Talmud as inspired truth; only in modern times, “largely for apologetic reasons,” was it deleted from what counted as “mainstream” Judaism.
- **Scripture’s true subject is God.** Green reports the *Bahir* and subsequent kabbalists as claiming that revelation’s inner subject is divine self-disclosure; the ten sefirot name the image-clusters of that inner life.
- **Secrets published against Maimonidean rationalism.** Green argues kabbalistic secrets were made public to combat a perfect, commandment-indifferent philosophical God with a cosmos-sustaining account of prayer and *mitsvot*.
- **Castilian culmination and de León.** Green places the Zohar’s emergence in late-thirteenth-century Castile, with [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] central to writing and circulation, likely self-understood as disciple of Scholem’s “Gnostic Circle”; composition after 1270 (main body likely 1280s) while still allowing multi-author school models (Liebes–Meroz).
- **Aramaic as muse.** Speculatively (Green’s own framing), writing in Aramaic rather than Hebrew may have released de León’s poetic freedom; Scholem’s linguistic analysis is pointed to, with further detail deferred to Matt’s Translator’s Introduction.

## Known Controversies and Influence

Green’s introduction is a principal **entry map** for English readers of the Pritzker Zohar and for this wiki’s attributed account of pre-Zoharic Kabbalah and authorship options. He does not, in this text, claim to settle authorship; he both affirms medieval composition on critical grounds and reports that “no simple and unequivocal statement” of authorship can be made even now. Influence on later wiki pages should remain attributed (“Green frames…,” “Green summarizes Scholem…”) rather than promoted to bare wiki voice.
