Peter C. Craigie (1938–1985) was a Canadian evangelical Old Testament scholar. He died in a car accident at age 47, leaving behind significant contributions to Deuteronomy studies, Psalms scholarship, and Ugaritic-Hebrew comparison. His *Deuteronomy* commentary in the NICOT series (1976) remains a standard evangelical reference on that book.

## Hermeneutical Method

Craigie's approach is **conservative evangelical with strong ancient Near Eastern grounding**. He explicitly rejects the Wellhausian documentary hypothesis as philosophically compromised (by Hegelian evolutionary religion theory) while engaging its literary observations seriously. His methodological innovation is to use **ancient Near Eastern covenant form analysis** — specifically the Hittite suzerain-vassal treaty structure — as both a literary and historical-critical tool that supports traditional dating.

Key methodological commitments:
- The Pentateuch's historical claims are to be taken seriously and tested against archaeological and comparative evidence
- The canonical text is the primary object of interpretation; source-critical atomization is resisted
- ANE parallels illuminate the text's historical context and literary genre without undermining its theological claims
- Theological reading and historical-critical engagement are not in competition

## Characteristic Positions

**On Deuteronomy's authorship and date**: Craigie argues Deuteronomy is "essentially Mosaic" in substance, though he allows that the text underwent transmission-era updating (orthographic modernization). He dates the book to the Mosaic period (c. 1400–1200 BCE) based primarily on the [[suzerain-vassal-treaty|Hittite suzerain-vassal treaty]] parallel — second-millennium treaties include historical prologues; first-millennium Assyrian treaties do not. See [[deuteronomy-authorship|Deuteronomy: Authorship and Date]].

**On the Josianic reform**: Craigie treats the "book of the law" discovered under Josiah (2 Kgs 22) as a genuine rediscovery of an ancient document, not a 7th-century composition fabricated to authorize Josiah's reform program.

**On covenant as love**: Deuteronomy's legal demands are embedded in a relationship of *ʾahavah* (love). Craigie emphasizes that the Shema's command to love God (6:5) is not a sentimental addition but the covenantal heart of all legal obligation. Law expresses relationship, not the condition for acquiring it.

**On the prophet like Moses (18:15–18)**: Craigie adopts a christological reading: as Moses is the prophet who founded the old covenant kingdom, Jesus is the eschatological prophet who inaugurates the new. He follows the NT's own application (Acts 3:22–23; 7:37) explicitly.

**On the suzerain-vassal treaty argument**: Craigie's use of this argument involves an important limitation he acknowledges: the formal parallels are suggestive, not conclusive. Moshe Weinfeld's counterargument — that seventh-century Esarhaddon vassal treaties show equally close parallels — is a genuine challenge. Craigie accepts the parallel's force as supporting evidence within a cumulative case, not as proof.

## Key Scholars Engaged

Craigie interacts most substantially with: M. G. Kline (treaty structure as dating argument), G. E. Mendenhall (covenant forms), Moshe Weinfeld (counterargument), E. W. Nicholson (northern provenance hypothesis), Martin Noth (Deuteronomistic History), Gerhard von Rad (theological interpretation), Kenneth Kitchen (ANE chronology), Dennis McCarthy and Klaus Baltzer (covenant genre).
