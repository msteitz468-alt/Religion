Peter C. Craigie (1938–1985) was a Canadian evangelical Old Testament scholar at the University of Calgary. He died in a car accident at age 47. His *Deuteronomy* commentary in the NICOT series (1976) remains a standard evangelical reference on that book; he also worked on Psalms and Ugaritic–Hebrew comparison.

## Hermeneutical Method

Craigie's approach is **conservative evangelical with strong ancient Near Eastern grounding**. He presupposes that Deuteronomy is part of the Word of God, not “simply the product of human imagination” (Author’s Preface; Appendix I). Under that constraint he uses comparative ANE form (especially Hittite suzerain-vassal treaties), Ugaritic philology (largely in footnotes), and engagement with critical scholarship without adopting Wellhausen evolutionary religion or Noth’s Deuteronomistic History as controlling frames.

Key methodological commitments (fidelity re-ingest 2026-07-18, [[craigie-deuteronomy-nicot]]):
- Canonical text as primary object; source-critical atomization resisted when unity is formally coherent
- ANE treaty and law parallels illuminate genre and historical possibility without automatically settling theology
- Treaty form **supports but does not prove** Mosaic dating (explicit caution)
- Theological reading and historical-critical engagement are not in competition once inspiration is not reduced to a scientific verdict

## Characteristic Positions (NICOT Deuteronomy)

**Authorship and date**: Deuteronomy is **essentially Mosaic** in substance; written down soon after the Moab renewal (possibly after Moses’ death, likely on a leather scroll). Orthographic/syntactic updating of living prose is allowed. Second-millennium treaty form (historical prologue; Kitchen’s four distinctions) favors early dating over Weinfeld’s seventh-century Assyrian-curse case. See [[deuteronomy-authorship|Deuteronomy: Authorship and Date]].

**Josianic reform**: The “book of the law” in 2 Kgs 22–23 is a genuine rediscovery (*terminus ad quem*), not a fabrication to authorize reform. Centralization is the *result* of reform, not Deuteronomy’s theoretical purpose; Deuteronomy never names Jerusalem/Temple and does name the Shechem region (Ebal/Gerizim).

**Covenant as love**: Law is embedded in *ʾahavah*—God’s initiating love and Israel’s commanded reciprocal love. The [[shema|Shema]] (6:4–5) is the principal commandment and structural center of the general stipulations.

**Prophet like Moses (18:15–18)**: Primary sense is ongoing prophetic succession within Israel; Craigie also follows Acts 3:22–23 christologically. At 34:10–12 he adds an explicit Christian-reader close: a prophet like Moses appears again in Jesus Christ, who is more than a prophet (Heb 3:1–6).

**Treaty argument limit**: Formal parallels are cumulative evidence, not proof. Weinfeld’s Esarhaddon/VTE affinities are a real challenge; Craigie rejects direct borrowing of VTE curse series in favor of a shared ANE curse repertoire (ch. 28 commentary).

**Ḥerem / war**: Framed as protection of exclusive covenant allegiance and judgment on abominable cult (including child sacrifice), not bare ethnic hatred; distant vs near cities distinguished in ch. 20.

**Exile curses**: Contingent covenant sanctions natural to land-gift theology; not inevitable prophecy. Optional exilic gloss only for isolated phrases (e.g. 29:27 “as it is this day”).
