# Rodney Stark

## Biographical Context

Rodney Stark (b. 1934) is an American sociologist trained in the social-scientific study of religion. He spent most of his career at the University of Washington (sociology and comparative religion) and later moved to Baylor University as the co-director of the Institute for Studies of Religion. He is the author of over 30 books spanning the sociology of religion, the history of Christianity, and the economics of religious markets.

Stark describes himself as someone who was a non-believer for most of his career, approaching Christian history strictly as a sociologist. (He later described himself as sympathetic to Christianity but still in an uncertain faith position.) His stated goal is to apply rigorous social science to questions historians have either avoided or answered only impressionistically.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Discipline**: Sociology of religion; rational choice theory applied to historical religion.

**Framework**: Stark does not operate within any of the standard religious-hermeneutical frameworks (neither the Christian Quadriga, nor PaRDeS, nor Islamic tafsir categories). His framework is explicitly social-scientific:
- **Rational choice theory**: agents are modeled as purposive actors seeking to maximize rewards and minimize costs, including in religious contexts. Applied to martyrdom, conversion, and organizational commitment.
- **Religious economies model**: religious groups operate like firms in a market; supply-side competition (degree of regulation, exclusivity) determines market outcomes.
- **Network theory**: conversion follows social ties, not persuasion. The Lofland-Stark model from 1965 fieldwork is the theoretical foundation.
- **Quantitative historical analysis**: Stark imposes demographic and statistical discipline on questions usually answered qualitatively (growth rates, correlations between synagogue presence and Christianization, sex ratio implications).
- **Comparative historical analysis**: parallels from modern religious movements (Mormons, Christian Scientists, Moonies) are used to constrain inferences about ancient Christianity.

**Tone and stance**: Explicitly non-confessional; also explicitly non-reductionist. Stark insists that sociological explanation of Christianity's growth does not debunk its truth claims. He resists both the apologetic impulse (claiming growth "proves" Christianity) and the dismissive impulse (claiming growth is "merely" social). His final chapter (*Rise of Christianity*, Ch. 10) argues that Christian doctrine — particularly the love ethic and mercy as virtue — was socially effective precisely because it was genuinely novel. Doctrines matter.

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Conversion follows networks, not sermons**: The primary mechanism of religious conversion is pre-existing social ties. Ideology explains little; who you know explains most. Applied to early Christianity, this means the church grew through friendship and kinship, not preaching campaigns.

2. **The Jewish mission was not a failure**: Contra the consensus, Stark argues Hellenized diaspora Jews converted in substantial numbers into the 4th–5th century. The Marcion affair is his key evidence: the church rejected Marcionism, implying Jewish-Christian dominance into the mid-2nd century.

3. **Paganism was already dying before Constantine**: Constantine did not kill paganism — paganism was in collapse from the 1st century CE onward. Bagnall's data on Egyptian temple construction and evidence from Asia Minor support this. Constantine was effect, not cause.

4. **Exclusivity creates quality**: Strict religious organizations, by demanding sacrifice and stigma, filter out free-riders and produce higher average member commitment — and therefore better collective goods. Christianity's exclusivity was an organizational advantage, not a liability.

5. **Doctrines matter sociologically**: Classical ethics did not value mercy or love for enemies. Christianity's insistence on these virtues as the highest goods was historically revolutionary and generated behaviors (nursing the sick, caring for strangers) that had demographic and social consequences.

## Known Controversies and Criticisms

**The "model"-driven approach**: Critics argue Stark imposes anachronistic social-science models on ancient material. The "rational choice" framework is contested even in modern religious sociology. Applied to ancient actors, it is even less obviously appropriate.

**Quality of historical evidence**: Stark sometimes works with thin or contested sources (e.g., using McNeill's epidemiological estimates directly as inputs to demographic calculations). Historians have questioned whether the data can bear the quantitative weight he places on it.

**The women argument**: His sex-ratio/fertility argument (Ch. 5 of *Rise*) has been criticized for relying on sex ratio data from earlier periods and places extrapolated to the 1st-century Roman Empire in ways that are speculative.

**The Jewish-mission claim**: Stark's argument that Jewish conversions continued in substantial numbers into the 4th century goes against the dominant evidence-based view that Jewish-Christian community was largely absorbed or marginalized by the late 2nd century.

**Religious economies model**: The application of firm-competition models to religious groups has been criticized by sociologists (notably Christian Smith) as reductionist and as poorly fitting the actual sociology of conversion (which is not primarily a choice among competing suppliers).

## Influence and Legacy

*The Rise of Christianity* became a widely read and widely assigned text in courses on early Christianity, religious sociology, and history of religion. It has been credited with:
- Reinvigorating quantitative approaches to early church history
- Giving sociological credibility to network-conversion models
- Providing a widely cited alternative to the "Constantine-made-Christianity" narrative
- Introducing the [[religious-economy|religious economy]] framework to a historical audience

His work is regularly cited — positively or critically — in textbooks and monographs on early Christianity and Roman religion. [[stark-rise-of-christianity|*The Rise of Christianity*]] is discussed alongside Meeks' *The First Urban Christians* and MacMullen's *Christianizing the Roman Empire* as the standard sociological reference points for the period.
