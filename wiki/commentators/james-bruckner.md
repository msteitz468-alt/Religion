James K. Bruckner is an OT scholar at North Park Theological Seminary, associated with the Evangelical Covenant Church (Swedish pietist heritage). His *Exodus* commentary in the Understanding the Bible Commentary Series (UBCS, Baker Books, 2008) represents the "believing criticism" approach characteristic of that series — a middle position between precritical apologetics and critical scholarship divorced from faith.

## Hermeneutical Method

Bruckner's approach is explicitly shaped by six interpretive perspectives he names in the Introduction:

1. **Missional freedom**: The exodus was freedom *for* service and worship, not freedom *from* bondage as an end in itself. "They are my people. Let them go — so that they may serve me." Half of Exodus concerns worship (chs. 20–40).
2. **Abrahamic continuity**: The exodus fulfills the promises to Abraham that his descendants would bring blessing to *all* the nations of the earth.
3. **Prevenient grace before law**: The exodus (grace) preceded the Sinai law. "The law was never the means of that salvation." This anticipates E. P. Sanders' *covenantal nomism* formulation from the opposite direction.
4. **Formation of an emerging people**: God did not accomplish everything at once. The wilderness was a formation process; even rebellion was incorporated into the people's education.
5. **Cooperative tabernacle**: God invited the people into a joint creative venture for the first time — the building of the tabernacle as a cooperative act of worship.
6. **Hope through memory**: Remembering the exodus is the mechanism of hope in the prophets and in Deuteronomy.

### Critical Method

Bruckner uses **literary-chiastic analysis** extensively, reading the book's theological architecture through its structural symmetries (the golden calf crisis as the center; the Passover section's interlocking A-B-A′ structure; the tabernacle instructions as seven speeches mirroring Genesis 1's seven days).

He engages **form criticism** and **source criticism** without the apologetic anxiety of the NICOT/NAC commentators. He accepts that the final form of Exodus "was likely redacted from diverse sources in the crisis of the sixth century, in view of deliverance from Babylon" — a softer version of the documentary hypothesis. He does not argue for Mosaic authorship as a theological necessity.

He draws heavily on **creational theology** following Terence Fretheim (Lutheran OT theologian): the exodus is fundamentally a restoration of God's creational purposes; the tabernacle is a microcosm of creation; the plagues are God's demonstrating lordship over the creation Pharaoh claimed to control.

He is the first commentator in the wiki to engage **liberation hermeneutics** seriously — both positively (Brueggemann's sociopolitical liberation reading, Gutierrez's Latin American option for the poor, King/Cone's African American reading) and critically (Warrior's Native American critique that the liberated become the dispossessors).

## Characteristic Positions

**On Exodus 34:6-7**: Bruckner calls this the theological center of the entire book — the fullest proclamation of God's name and character. He provides detailed treatments of each attribute:
- *Rakhum* (compassionate): from the same root as "womb" — softness of a mother for her child
- *Khannun* (gracious): freely giving without reciprocal demand
- *ʾErek ʾapayim* (slow to anger): literally "lengthened nostrils"
- *Rab khesed* (abounding in unrelenting love): "pursing love," "tenacious fidelity" — not generic affection
- *Rab ʾemet* (abounding in faithfulness): from *ʾemet*, "completely reliable and trustworthy"

His reading of the difficult "yet he will by no means clear the guilty" clause: God does not add punishment but "the ongoing impact of wickedness will remain as a negative effect upon the family and the community." Forgiveness restores relationship; it does not remit all consequences. This is a pastoral, non-retributive reading.

**On the tabernacle**: He resists Philo's purely allegorical reading (colors = elements, lampstand = planets) and the historical-critical dismissal (postexilic "pious fiction"). The tabernacle's extensive detail is itself the theological point: God wanted to dwell with the people in a specific, beautiful, materially particular way. The seven-speech structure mirrors Genesis 1; the tabernacle is a microcosm of creation. John 1:14's "tented among us" deliberately echoes the Exodus tabernacle texts.

**On the Passover blood**: Not apotropaic magic. The blood is "a sign for the people" — their visible acceptance of YHWH's lordship and offer of protection. God does the protecting; the blood marks those who have accepted God's offer. The Passover is a *zebakh* (fellowship meal for eating), not a *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (sin offering) — a common Christian misidentification.

**On hardening Pharaoh's heart**: God's hardening "intensifies" Pharaoh's already-forming resolve but does not create it from nothing. God's purpose is stated: "so that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth" (9:16). The result is that even the destruction of Pharaoh's army is a means of self-revelation.

**On Moses killing the Egyptian (Exod 2:12)**: Not premeditated murder. Moses looked around and "saw that there was no one to intervene" — the same expression used in Isaiah 59:15 ("he saw that there was no one to intervene"). Moses acted out of "zealousness for justice," possibly committing unintentional manslaughter. Stephen in Acts 7:25 confirms that Moses "thought God was using him to rescue them."

**On liberation theology's limits**: Following Warrior, Bruckner notes that many contemporary Christians are not the enslaved Hebrews in the analogy but the "silent Egyptian populace" — or even the Canaanites. The liberation paradigm is valid but limited: "honesty does not allow many of us the hermeneutical luxury of identifying ourselves as oppressed slaves."

## Key Scholars Engaged

Bruckner draws most extensively on: **Terence Fretheim** (*Exodus*, OBT — major influence throughout); **Walter Brueggemann** (*Exodus*, NIB — liberation themes); **John Durham** (*Exodus*, WBC — detailed exegesis); **Brevard Childs** (*Exodus*, OTL — canonical approach); **Benno Jacob** (*Exodus* — Jewish traditional reading); **Nahum Sarna** (*Exploring Exodus* — historical/ANE background); **Philo** (allegorical tradition, critically engaged); Robert Warrior (Native American liberation critique).
