# Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite

**Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite** is the modern scholarly name for the anonymous author
of the **Dionysian corpus** — a collection of theological writings that appeared in the late
fifth or early sixth century under the name "Dionysius the Areopagite," the Athenian convert
mentioned in Acts 17:34 as a follower of Paul. Modern scholarship universally rejects the
apostolic-era attribution; the works are now dated to c. 485–530 CE on linguistic, doctrinal,
and historical grounds. The author's identity remains unknown. Despite the pseudonymous
character of the corpus, its influence on Christian mystical theology, Eastern Orthodox
theology, and Scholasticism is immense.

[[john-parker-translator|John Parker]] (1897–1899) was among the last serious scholars to
defend the traditional attribution; his two-volume translation of the complete Works provided
this wiki's primary source for the corpus.

## Authorship and Identification Problem

### Traditional Attribution
The writings claim apostolic-era authorship. The texts address "Timothy" (Paul's companion),
"Gaius" (another New Testament figure), and describe eyewitness presence at the death of the
Virgin Mary alongside [[james-apostle|James the Just]] and [[peter-apostle|Peter]]. The author
calls himself a disciple of "Hierotheus" and claims Paul as his chief initiator. For centuries
these internal claims were taken at face value; the Council of Constantinople (553), the Second
Council of Nicaea (787), and generations of medieval theologians treated the works as
apostolic.

### Parker's Defense (1897–1899)
Parker's edition represents the last sustained apologetic for traditional authenticity. He
argued from external testimony (Dexter's Chronicle, Dionysius of Alexandria c. 250 CE,
Polycarp, Origen, Theodore) that the works were known in the first century and cited by those
who had known the apostles. Parker specifically drew a line connecting the author to the
Alexandrine School (Pantaenus, Ammonius Saccus, Plotinus) through parallel passages,
arguing that Pseudo-Dionysius was the *source* of Neoplatonic ideas, not their borrower.

### Modern Scholarly Consensus
The modern consensus, established in the 19th century and confirmed by further analysis, dates
the corpus to c. 485–530 CE. The primary grounds:
1. **Proclus dependence** — the corpus contains extensive borrowing from the Neoplatonist
   philosopher Proclus (412–485 CE), whose works postdate any first-century author.
2. **Absence from earlier sources** — neither Eusebius nor Jerome mention the works, despite
   comprehensive catalogues of Christian authors; the first certain citation is at the
   colloquy of 533 CE.
3. **Theological precision** — the Christological formulations reflect post-Chalcedonian
   debates (451 CE).
4. **Probable Syrian milieu** — linguistic and doctrinal features point to a Syrian Christian
   environment in the tradition of Syriac-speaking monasticism.

**Note on Parker's edition**: Parker reads the texts as authentic first-century works and
interprets all historical and theological content accordingly. The wiki records his positions
faithfully while noting the scholarly consensus that the texts are 5th–6th century. See
[[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|Pseudo-Dionysius Authorship Controversy]].

## Hermeneutical Method

Pseudo-Dionysius employs a distinctive two-tier theological method:

### Cataphatic / Affirmative Theology (*kataphasis*)
Affirms divine names and predicates drawn from Scripture: God as Good, Beautiful, Being, Life,
Wisdom, Power, Love. The "goodly progressions" (*proodoi*) of the Godhead outward to creation.
Each affirmative name reflects a genuine aspect of divine activity toward creatures.

### Apophatic / Negative Theology (*apophasis*)
Denies all predicates of God as ultimately inadequate — God is beyond being, beyond goodness,
beyond wisdom, beyond any name. The *Mystical Theology* is the locus classicus for this
movement: ascending through negation of all things, arriving at the "Divine Darkness" —
*supra-knowledge* (*agnosia*) that transcends both knowledge and ignorance. Connects to
[[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]] more broadly.

The two methods are not opposed: cataphatic theology ascends; apophatic theology transcends
even that ascent. Together they constitute the Dionysian theological movement from symbol to
concept to union.

### Symbolic / Mystical Theology
A third, lower level: symbolic theology interprets the sensible symbols used for God in
Scripture (fire, stone, wind, bodily forms). Dionysius cites a lost work (*Symbolic Theology*)
as treating this level; the surviving corpus addresses it only in passing.

### Hierarchical Mediation
A distinctive Dionysian structure: all divine illumination descends through and ascends through
**hierarchy** (*hierarchia*) — ordered ranks of intelligences that both receive and transmit
divine light. This applies to the celestial hierarchies (nine orders of angels) and the
ecclesiastical hierarchies (clergy, sacraments, laity). The principle: each rank elevates those
below by communicating what it has received, without loss to itself.

## Major Works

### [[on-divine-names|On the Divine Names]]
The longest and most systematic treatise. Addressed to Timothy. Interprets the biblical names
of God (Good, Being, Life, Wisdom, Power, Love, Peace, Being, Perfection, One, etc.) as
cataphatic "progressions" of the Godhead. Extensively treats divine providence, the problem of
evil (evil as privation, non-being), the beautiful, Eros, and the Good as source of all.

### [[mystical-theology|Mystical Theology]]
The shortest work; five brief chapters. Addresses Timothy with an exhortation to ascend beyond
all things — sensible, intellectual, affirmative, negative — to union with the divine darkness.
The locus classicus of Christian apophatic mysticism. The framework: Moses' ascent into the
cloud on Sinai as the type of mystical theology.

### [[celestial-hierarchy|On the Celestial Hierarchy]]
Addresses Timothy. Treats the nine orders of angels in three triads: Seraphim/Cherubim/Thrones;
Dominations/Powers/Authorities; Principalities/Archangels/Angels. Argues that hierarchical
mediation is necessary because direct divine illumination would overwhelm lower beings; angels
convey divine light proportionally. Interprets the symbolic, material representations of angels
in Scripture as pedagogical accommodations.

### [[ecclesiastical-hierarchy|On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy]]
Treats the sacraments and orders of the church in a hierarchical framework parallel to the
celestial hierarchy. Three triads of initiating rites (Baptism, Eucharist, Chrism); three
orders of clergy (Bishop/Priest/Deacon); three orders of laity (Monks/People/Catechumens).
Each rite is a vehicle of deification (*theosis*); the hierarchy mediates divine illumination
downward and aspiration upward.

### [[letters-of-dionysius|Letters I–XI]]
Ten letters to various addressees — Gaius (4), Dorotheus, Sopatros, Polycarp, Demophilus,
Titus, John the Theologian (on Patmos), and Apollophanes. Treat topics including the Divine
Darkness, divine love, the mystery of the Incarnation, and right hierarchical conduct.

## Influence and Reception

### Eastern Christianity
Profoundly formative. [[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus the Confessor]] (c. 580–662) wrote
extensive scholia on the corpus and integrated Dionysian theology into Byzantine Christianity.
[[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] cited the corpus as authoritative. The [[celestial-hierarchy|
Celestial Hierarchy]] shaped Byzantine angelology; the [[ecclesiastical-hierarchy|
Ecclesiastical Hierarchy]] shaped liturgical theology.

### Western Medieval Scholasticism
The corpus reached the Latin West via the translation of [[john-scotus-eriugena|John Scotus
Eriugena]] (9th c.). [[thomas-aquinas|Thomas Aquinas]] cited "Dionysius" over 1,700 times
in the *Summa Theologica*, treating him as an authority second only to Augustine. The
[[fourfold-sense|fourfold sense of Scripture]] and the Scholastic use of negative theology
both show Dionysian influence.

### Christian Mysticism
Foundational for the mystical tradition: Meister Eckhart, John of the Cross, the *Cloud of
Unknowing*, and the Rhineland mystics all draw on Dionysian apophatic method. The concept of
the divine darkness (*skotos*) and unknowing (*agnosia*) as the path to union became central
to Western and Eastern mystical traditions.

### Relation to Neoplatonism
The corpus stands at the intersection of Proclus's Neoplatonism and Christian theology. Key
Neoplatonic concepts — the One, procession (*proodos*) and return (*epistrophe*), the good as
the source of all being, hierarchical mediation — are transposed into a Trinitarian and
Christological frame. The extent of the debt to Proclus vs. the originality of the Dionysian
synthesis is itself a contested scholarly question.

## Liturgy of St. Dionysius

Parker's Vol. 1 (1897) includes a liturgy attributed to Dionysius under the title "Liturgy of
St. Dionysius, Bishop of the Athenians" (lines 585–599 of the source file). Parker's prefatory
note argues:

- The liturgy "gives the doctrine of Dionysius in a liturgical form" and could be restored in
  Greek from the Dionysian writings.
- It should be compared with the Coptic Liturgy of Dionysius, Bishop of Athens (disciple of
  Paul), and with the Liturgy of St. Basil as adapted for the Uniat Copts.
- Parker believes it was written for the **Therapeutae near Alexandria** (Philo's *contemplative
  life*), "who were Christians; who occupied themselves with the contemplation of the Divine
  Names, and the heavenly Hierarchy."
- Terminus post quem: death of James the Apostle and Martyr, AD 42. Terminus ante quem:
  AD 67, when Dionysius left Athens at Paul's request for Rome.
- A "note of primitive antiquity": the church is described as "from one end of the earth to
  the other" — absent the formula "one, holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Orthodox Church" found
  in the later Liturgy of St. Basil.
- As a result of this liturgy and the Dionysian writings, Dionysius was "frequently
  commemorated in the diptychs as one of the Doctors of the Church."

**Content summary**: The liturgy is a full anaphora (eucharistic prayer) with: Prayer before
the Pax; Sursum Corda; Preface praise of the angelic hierarchy (Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones,
Principalities, Archangels, Angels); Sanctus; Institution narrative (body "broken and given"
for remission of sins; blood of the new covenant); Memorial (*anamnesis*); Epiclesis (invocation
of the Holy Spirit upon the oblations); Intercessions for the Church, bishops, the departed,
kings, orphans and widows; Lord's Prayer; Communion prayers. Theologically: Trinitarian
throughout; Christ as "heavenly Hierarch" and "Lamb of God"; strong eschatological tone
(expectation of Christ's "second and glorious manifestation from heaven"). The hierarchical
orders of clergy — "Divine Hierarchs," "holy Priests," "Deacons," "choirs of virgins" — are
invoked within the intercessory prayer.

## Parker's Case for Authenticity

*Appended from "Objections to Genuineness," Vol. 1 (1897), lines 600–622.*

Parker constructs a systematic apologetic for the apostolic-era authorship of the Dionysian
corpus, engaging the principal critics:

**Against Dupin** (who argued from Eusebius and Jerome's silence): Parker counters that
Eusebius "acknowledges that innumerable works have not come to him" and that Jerome explicitly
"disclaims either to know or to give an accurate catalogue either of authors or works." Eusebius
does not list the writings of Hymenseus, Narcissus, Athenagoras, or Pantaenus, and gives only
partial lists of Clement, Origen, and Dionysius of Alexandria. Parker attributes Eusebius's
silence specifically to *odium theologicum*: "according to Eusebius, Jesus is διττός; according
to Dionysius, Jesus is ἁπλοῦς" — a theological divergence that explains deliberate omission.

**On Pearson, Daillé, Erasmus, and others**: Parker notes that the critics partially destroy
each other: "Pearson demolishes Daillé; Vossius pulverises Blundellum; Erasmus repudiates
Valla." Pearson's *Ignatii Vindiciae*, Chapter X, provides Parker's strongest positive
witness: "No one is so ignorant as not to know that these writings were recognised as genuine
by the best judges in the sixth, fifth, fourth, and third centuries." (Pearson later confessed
uncertainty and deferred to a more learned judge.) Erasmus's doubts rested solely on the
improbability that an apostolic-era convert could be so learned — not on textual evidence;
his prime work "ratio verae religionis" calls the Dionysian works not Stoic, Platonic, or
Aristotelian, but "celestial" philosophy.

**Gregory of Tours**: Parker argues that Gregory misread an ancient document about seven men
sent by Peter into Gaul under Claudius ("sub Cldio") as "consulibus Decio," and inserted
Dionysius into the list while omitting Valerius — thus mistakenly placing Dionysius's arrival
in Gaul c. AD 250. The Pères Bolandistes are criticized for following Gregory's error over the
explicit declaration of Pope John XIX (that Martial of Limoges was "an apostolic man") and
Gregory XIII.

**General objection on style and learning**: Parker responds that the Apostles were not
uneducated: "Jesus opened their understanding, that they should understand the testimony of
the Scriptures, respecting Himself; and further, that the Holy Spirit should recall to them
whatever He had said to them." The works contain "not a single allusion to persons or events
after the first century" (unless the Epistle of Ignatius, AD 108, is quoted). The Antwerp
edition counts approximately 500 Scripture references; Dionysius "quotes every book in the
Bible, except the two last particular Epistles of St. John, or John Presbyter." Parker's
conclusion: "Faith is more trustworthy than criticism."

### The Alexandrine School Argument

*Appended from "Dionysius the Areopagite and the Alexandrine School," Vol. 2 prefatory essay
(1899), lines 674–711.*

Parker's Vol. 2 opens with a sustained argument that Dionysius was the **source** of the
Alexandrine School's philosophical theology — not a borrower from Neoplatonism, but its
originator through the chain: **Dionysius → Pantaenus → Ammonius Saccus → Plotinus → Proclus**.

**Core thesis**: "Athens was [the Alexandrine School's] birthplace. Pantaenus and
Ammonius-Saccus were chief founders of the Alexandrine School. They were both Christian. They
both drew their teaching from the Word of God... and from the writings of Hierotheus, and
Dionysius the Areopagite—Bishops of Athens."

**Pantaenus link**: Pantaenus (Athens, b. AD 120; Alexandria, d. AD 213), Stoic then
Pythagorean then Christian, appointed chief instructor in the Didaskaleion by Demetrius of
Alexandria c. AD 186. Eusebius states Pantaenus "expounded the treasures of the Divine dogmas
preserved direct, as from father to son, from St. Paul and other Apostles." Photius says
Pantaenus "was pupil of those who had seen the Apostles, but... certainly had not listened to
any of them themselves." Parker infers: "pupil through their writings" — specifically the
writings of Hierotheus and Dionysius, "both ordained Bishop of Athens by St. Paul." Routh
preserves two fragments of Pantaenus; Parker identifies the second as "a distinct echo of
Dionysius." Dionysius (*Divine Names* c. VII): God "not as learning existing things from
existing things, but from Himself, and in Himself, as Cause... knows existing things."
Pantaenus: "He knows things being as His own volitions... by willing (θέλων), He made all
things being." Ammonius uses βούλημα; Dionysius and Pantaenus use θελήματα — the same
doctrine, the same vocabulary.

**Clement of Alexandria parallel**: Parker cites the ship/anchor image as evidence Clement
drew from Dionysius: Clement writes "those riding at anchor at sea, drag the anchor, but do
not drag it to themselves, but themselves to the anchor." Dionysius (*Divine Names* c. III.1):
"as if after we have embarked on ship, and are holding on to the cable, attached to some rock,
we do not draw the rock to us, but ourselves, and the ship, to the rock. Wherefore, before
everything, and especially theology, we must begin with prayer; not as though we ourselves
were drawing the power, which is everywhere, and nowhere present, but as, by our godly
reminiscences and invocations, conducting ourselves to, and making ourselves one with It."

**Origen parallel on resurrection**: Origen (letter to Gregory) and Papias (fragment V:
"they ascend through the Spirit to the Son, and through the Son to the Father") are connected
by Parker to the "three last chapters of the Book of Hierotheus," arguing Photius's reference
to Pantaenus as "pupil of the Presbyters who had seen the Apostles" designates Hierotheus and
Dionysius.

**Plotinus**: Born AD 205, died AD 270; studied under Ammonius Saccus for eleven years until
Ammonius's death in AD 242; began teaching in Rome AD 244. His teaching is transmitted through
Porphyry (Christianity's greatest literary enemy), whom Parker uses precisely because Porphyry
is not "prejudiced in favour of the truth," yet the *Enneades* contain no anti-Christian content.

Three Plotinus–Dionysius parallels:

1. **Trinity**: Plotinus, "We need not go beyond the three Hypostaseis." His triad (One, Mind,
   Soul) versus Dionysius's (Father, Son, Spirit); Parker traces even Plotinus's "One" to
   Dionysius's ἐναρχικὴ ὑποστάσεων formula: "the 'One' represents the Father." Plotinus: the
   first principle "pours Itself in a multitude of streams without being diminished by what it
   gives" — Dionysius: "the Godhead is undiminished by the gifts imparted."

2. **The Beautiful** (*Enneades* I.6-7): Plotinus's ascent to "the Beautiful surpassing earth
   and heaven" through love, transport, and the rendering of the soul analogous to its object.
   Dionysius (*Divine Names* c. IV): "all things aspire to the Beautiful and Good — and there
   is no existing thing which does not participate in the Beautiful and Good." Parker cites
   Augustine: "with the change of a few words, Plotinus became concordant with Christ's
   religion."

3. **Mystical ecstasy**: Porphyry records Plotinus attained vision of the Beautiful three
   times during his life. The Dionysian *Mystical Theology* provides the method: "by unchecked
   and absolute extasy, in all purity, from thyself, and all, thou wilt be carried on high to
   the superessential Ray of the Divine Darkness."

**Key differences between Plotinus and Dionysius** (noted by Parker): Plotinus treats creation
as an act of necessity; Dionysius, of love. Plotinus traces evil to matter ("an elongation
from God"); Dionysius, to "the fallacious choice of a free agent." Parker suggests Porphyry's
anti-Christian editing may be responsible for the Plotinian divergences.

**Proclus and the priority question**: Parker cites Suidas's assertion that Proclus (450–485)
"cribbed whole passages from Dionysius." Professor Stiglmayr fills seven pages with parallel
passages. Vachérot describes certain chapters of the *Divine Names* as "extracts from Proclus,
word for word" — but Parker inverts this: Dexter's Chronicle commemorates the *Divine Names*
at AD 98; if Proclus is 450–485, the direction of borrowing must run from Dionysius to Proclus,
not the reverse. "Rational men will not hazard the surmise that works known in the first century
were gleaned from writings composed four hundred years afterwards."

**External testimony chain**: Polycarp (quotes Dionysius as "a certain one"); Jerome ("quidam
Graecorum"); Dionysius of Alexandria (c. AD 250, to Pope Sixtus II: "no one can intelligently
dispute their paternity... Dionysius was disciple of St. Paul, and piously governed the Church
of Athens"); Tertullian; Origen (by name); Theodore (AD 420); Gregory ("an ancient and
venerable Father"); Second Council of Nicaea (quotes *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* c. I.4 as
those of "the great Dionysius"). Parker: "Bishop Pearson proves that the best judges in the
sixth, fifth, fourth and third centuries regarded the writings as written by Dionysius the
Areopagite. German scholars to-day admit that the external testimony is in favour of their
genuineness."

**Conclusion**: "the Alexandrine School was Biblical, Christian, and Philosophical, that its
Philosophy was a Divine Philosophy of the Faith, not a pagan philosophy against the Faith,
and that the main sources of its Divine Philosophy were the writings of Hierotheus and
Dionysius, Bishops of Athens." Signed: John Parker, Cannes, Epiphany 1899.

## Perl's Philosophical Reading (*Theophany*, 2007)

Eric D. Perl's *Theophany: The Neoplatonic Philosophy of Dionysius the Areopagite* (SUNY Press, 2007) represents the most sustained attempt to read Dionysius as a rigorous Neoplatonic philosopher in the tradition of Plotinus and Proclus, deliberately setting aside the theological reception documented elsewhere on this page. The methodological notes state the exclusion explicitly: Dionysius's background in earlier Christian thought is omitted "not because it is unreal or unimportant, but because it does not contribute to the specifically philosophical understanding of Dionysius" [4685–4686]. Trinitarian theology, Christology, and liturgy are all outside Perl's scope — not denied to Dionysius, but bracketed as irrelevant to the philosophical questions of metaphysics and gnoseology. The author's unknown historical identity is set aside as "philosophically irrelevant" [4723–4724].

Perl rejects two dominant scholarly patterns. The first treats Dionysius as a Christian thinker who merely borrowed Neoplatonic vocabulary (Brons; Vanneste) [4699–4701]. The second defends Dionysius as genuinely Christian precisely by arguing he differs significantly from Neoplatonism (von Ivanka; Golitzin) [4702–4704] — Golitzin himself recognizing the fundamental presupposition: "the assumption that the Platonic tradition and Christianity are mutually exclusive" [4710–4712]. Perl observes that this presupposition has driven much scholarship into a pattern of accusation (*plus platonizans quam christianizans*) and exculpation, producing what Balthasar called "bad humour and resentment" and Golitzin a "thinly concealed 'freight of venom'" [4732–4736] in the reception of the corpus. Perl's reading is a studied alternative: Dionysius is read as "Christianus simulque vere Platonicus" (Beierwaltes [4695]) without treating either attribute as a liability to the other.

The specifically Dionysian claims that Perl's reading illuminates in Chapters 1–3 challenge several elements of the theological reception. The apophatic method — which the mystical tradition treats as a path of spiritual transformation, and scholars like Rorem treat as a rhetorical acknowledgment of human limitation — is for Perl a philosophical necessity: "human concepts and minds are unable to capture God, not because they are human, but because they are concepts and minds" [4873–4875]. This reframes the *Mystical Theology*'s ascending negations not as devotional exercises but as conclusions forced by the philosophical argument that being is necessarily finite and hence God cannot be a being. The transcendence of even the opposition between affirmation and negation — God "above the privations" — is not a mystical surprise but a logical consequence.

Equally significant is Perl's demonstration that the features of Dionysius most often cited as distinctively Christian — God as ecstatic Love going out of himself, God as the immediate creator and sustainer of all things, the full dignity of all participants in being — are, properly understood, already present in non-Christian Neoplatonism. "The shift from Aristotle's 'unmoved' God to Dionysius' God who as Love 'is moved' is thus a restatement of the shift from God as form or being to God as beyond being. Hence the difference between a God who is the object of love but does not love the world and a God who is not only beloved but also productively loves all things, is fundamentally a difference not between pagan and Christian but between Aristotelian and Neoplatonic conceptions of God" [5232–5237]. A critical note adds that the "divine difference" constitutive of all beings as theophany "has nothing to do with the trinitarian differentiation of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" [5017–5019]: "Although trinitarian doctrine is fully present in Dionysius… it does not enter into his philosophical understanding of being as theophany" [5040–5041]. Perl's reading thus challenges any account of Dionysius — including the theological reception from Maximus through Aquinas — that treats the trinitarian and Christological frame as essential to the metaphysical argument rather than as its confessional context.

Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], endnotes to Introduction and Chapters 1–3, range lines 4678–5302.

## Sharpe's Reading (*Mysticism*, 1910)

A. B. Sharpe's *[[sharpe-mysticism|Mysticism: Its True Nature and Value]]* (1910) treats
Dionysius as the **fountainhead of the technical Christian doctrine of supernatural
[[mysticism]]** and supplies a second English translation of the *[[mystical-theology|Mystical
Theology]]* and of [[letters-of-dionysius|Letters I, II, and V]]. Where [[perl-theophany|Perl]]
reads Dionysius as a philosopher and brackets the theology, Sharpe reads him as the orthodox
*Christianiser* of Neoplatonism: "the author would seem to have intended to give the Christian
rendering of the philosophico-religious system evolved by Plotinus and later Neoplatonism… in
much the same way Aristotelianism was christianised by St Thomas." Crucially, Sharpe stresses
that Dionysius **rejects** the pantheism of his Neoplatonic sources — his doctrine of creation
makes God transcendent of, not identical with, the world.

On method, Sharpe credits Dionysius with first applying "mystical" in its technical sense and
with founding the via that ascends by **negation** to the "luminous darkness" (see
[[apophatic-theology]], [[negative-theology]]). He repeatedly pairs Dionysius with
[[plotinus|Plotinus]]: both insist on the distinction of initiates from the uninitiated and on
abstraction from all multiplicity, but neither clearly foregrounds *grace* as the condition of
union. On authorship he holds the "balance of probability is strongly against" the apostolic
attribution while refusing the charge of "forgery" — see the
[[pseudo-dionysius-authorship|authorship controversy]] page. For the *Divine Names* he notes the
corpus gives "the Christian rendering of the Neoplatonic 'one,' the Neoplatonic and Gnostic
doctrines of emanation, and the Gnostic 'Pleroma.'"

### Chapters 1–2: Transcendence and Theophany

The body text of Chapters 1 and 2 supplies the philosophical core of Perl's reading of Dionysius. Where standard theological reception — from Maximus the Confessor through Aquinas — emphasizes Dionysius's apophatic *method* as a spiritual path and his doctrine of divine names as a theology of revelation, Perl reads both as consequences of a single rigorous philosophical argument inherited from Plotinus and Proclus.

Chapter 1 argues that Dionysian negative theology is not a rhetorical acknowledgment of human limitation but the conclusion of the Neoplatonic argument that to be is to be intelligible. Because being is necessarily finite and determinate — a being is intelligible only by virtue of the limiting determination that distinguishes it from other beings — the infinite first principle cannot be a being. "Beyond being" (*hyperousios*) therefore does not denote a superior mode of being but the strict absence of being: God "is simply not anything, not 'there' at all. If our thought cannot attain to God, this is not because of our weakness but because there is no 'there' there, no being, no thing that is God" (lines 621–623). Dionysius states the argument's Neoplatonic premise explicitly: "if all knowledges are of beings and have their limit in beings, that which is beyond all being also transcends all knowledge" (DN I.4, 593A, lines 606–608). The apophatic requirement to transcend even negations — "that which is beyond the privations is beyond every affirmation and negation" (*MT* I.2, 1000B, lines 641–644) — is not mystical paradox but logical consequence. And the conclusion is the silence of the mind, "the cessation of every intellectual activity [*pases noeras energeias apopasin*]" (DN I.5, 593C, line 671): "the strictly philosophical consequence of the identification of being and intelligibility" (lines 683–685).

Chapter 2 shows how this radical transcendence does not isolate God from the world but is its opposite. Through the Neoplatonic doctrine of participation and formal causation, the transcendent form is at once wholly present in its instances as the nature by which they are what they are, and wholly unconditioned by them as one universal nature appearing in many modes. God is present to all things as the constitutive determination by which each is itself: "the life of living things and being of beings [*ton zonton zoe kai ton onton ousia*]" (DN I.3, 589C, lines 1242–1243). The governing formula is from the *Celestial Hierarchy*: "the being of all things is the divinity beyond being [*to gar einai panton estin he huper to einai theotes*]" (CH I.4, 177D, lines 1282–1283). God is "all things in all things and nothing in any [*en pasi panta esti kai en oudeni ouden*]" (DN VII.3, 872A, lines 1359–1360). This is neither monism (beings are not God, since to be a being is to be finite and hence not God) nor dualism (God is not another being alongside beings) but what Perl calls "theophanism" (line 1485): the doctrine that the entire intelligible content of reality is God's differentiated self-presence, and reality is the appearance of God. The understanding of being as theophany is, Perl argues, "a strict consequence, developed in the Neoplatonic tradition, of the original principle that to be is to be intelligible" (lines 1413–1414).

For the reception of Dionysius, this reading matters because it challenges any account — including the dominant Byzantine and Scholastic traditions — that treats the trinitarian and Christological frame as constitutive of the metaphysical argument, rather than as its confessional context. Chapter 2 explicitly notes that the "divine difference" constitutive of all beings as theophany "has nothing to do with the trinitarian differentiation of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" (Perl endnotes, lines 5017–5019): the production of being is common to all three Persons indifferently, and "although trinitarian doctrine is fully present in Dionysius… it does not enter into his philosophical understanding of being as theophany" (lines 5040–5041). The philosophical argument of *Divine Names* I stands on its Neoplatonic foundations independent of the Christological and trinitarian elaborations of the later chapters.

### Chapters 3–4: Goodness, Love, and Evil

*Source: Chapter 3 and 4 body text, range lines 1492–2752.*

Perl's reading of Chapter 3 (DN IV.1–17) reveals how Dionysius's account of God as Good, Beautiful, and Love constitutes a single unified statement of the theophanic structure. The Good is God's most fundamental name because goodness just is intelligibility and intelligibility just is being — meaning the Good is not one divine attribute but the universal formative principle through which every being is. Dionysius explicitly follows the Proclean cycle of remaining/procession/reversion: the Good is the source "from which all things originate and are...and to which all things are reverted...and which all things desire" (DN IV.4, 700AB) [lines 1739–1743]. Perl's most precise philosophical contribution in this chapter is the account of reversion as *constitutive of* beings, not subsequent to them: any being's active appropriation of its goodness is its participation in its own being made to be, and "God cannot make beings without their active cooperation, for without that activity they would not be anything" [lines 1809–1812]. God's ecstatic Love — "becomes out of himself in his providences toward all beings...without going out from himself" (DN IV.13, 712AB) [lines 1974–1981] — is the most complete expression of this: God is "pure exteriority, having no inner core of 'selfhood'" [lines 1991–1992], present in all things as their constitutive differentiation, not a being who then decides to love creatures but Love itself. Perl's conclusion challenges the standard "Christian transformation of Neoplatonism" narrative: Dionysius's terminological innovation (calling this divine productivity *erōs* and *ekstatikos*) may reflect Christian influence, but "the conception of God that it expresses is already present in the thought of Plotinus and Proclus" [lines 1969–1971].

Chapter 4 (DN IV.18–35) directly addresses the problem this raises: if all being is theophany, what is evil? Perl's body-text account shows that Dionysius extends the privation theory to every level of reality — including intellects and demons — more radically than either Plotinus or Proclus. Demons are "not evil by nature, but by the deficiency of angelic goods" (DN IV.23, 725B) [lines 2511–2512]: their angelic nature and its positive perfections remain; it is the failure to exercise those perfections that makes them evil, and that failure is a failure of *being*. The same applies to human souls: "Neither, then, in demons nor in us is evil as an evil being, but as failure and absence of the perfection of our proper goods" (DN IV.24, 728A) [lines 2524–2526]. Perl's reading foregrounds the most philosophically demanding aspect of Dionysius's doctrine: evil is without cause (*anaition*) precisely because causality is intelligibility and intelligibility is goodness — anything caused is to that extent good. The fall "cannot be understood" [cf. lines 2707–2709]; this unintelligibility is not a weakness in the theory but its most truthful moment. Perl argues that theodicies fail insofar as they succeed — "to the extent that they satisfactorily account for or make sense of evil, they tacitly or expressly deny that it is evil" [lines 2730–2731]. This analysis deepens the reception of Dionysius: the privation theory, transmitted through Thomas Aquinas's heavy use of *Divine Names* IV, carries within it a rigorous refusal to "explain away" evil that most later scholastic treatments either miss or soften.

### Chapters 5–7 and Conclusion: Hierarchy, Cognition, Symbolism

*Source: [[perl-theophany|Perl, *Theophany*, 2007]], body text, lines 2753–4669.*

Chapter 5 (*The Hierarchy of Being*) applies the theophany doctrine to Dionysius's hierarchical ontology of DN V.1–3. The divine names Good, Being, Life, and Wisdom describe God's constitutive presence at ranked degrees of universality: the Good reaches even matter as pure receptivity; Wisdom reaches only cognitive beings. But the levels are not separate providential zones — each more specific perfection is the more universal one in a more intense mode: "Life, in plants, therefore, is not superadded to Being, but is the more specific, intense mode of Being proper to them as compared to stones" [lines 2974–2976]. God is present "according to its rank (*kat' axian*)" to each being via illuminations "analogous to each of beings" (DN I.2, 588CD [lines 2998–3001]). Perl's central argument against the conventional reading: hierarchical order does not separate lower beings from God but is "itself the very ground of his immediate presence in all things" [lines 3085–3086], since God does not stand at the apex of the hierarchy but "all things in all things and nothing in any" (DN VII.3, 872A [lines 3117–3118]). The formula Perl coins for the result is "immediate mediation": "it is by the hierarchical mediation of beings that God is immediately constitutively present to all" [lines 3392–3393]. Each being's very identity consists in its hierarchical relations; "there is no such thing as an individual, a being conceived as a closed, self-contained unit" [lines 3435–3437]: "Each thing, indeed, is nothing but its relations to others, its place within the structure of immediate mediation. Hierarchy is thus, as its name signifies, the principle of sacredness, which is to say of being" [lines 3439–3441]. The chapter closes describing Dionysian hierarchy as a *thiasós*, a "communion of saints," whose "hierarchical structure of reality is the articulated manifestation of the divine love that constitutes all things" [lines 3455–3459].

Chapter 6 (*The Continuum of Cognition*) argues that sense, discursive reason, intellection, and mystical unknowing constitute a single continuum differentiated by degree of unity, not a series of faculties aimed at different objects. Sensation is dim intellection; intellection is concentrated sensation. Because all cognition grasps being and being is theophany, God is the object of every mode of knowing, including sensation. Perl highlights DN VII.3, 872A — Dionysius's affirmation that God is known through "touching and sense-perception and opinion and imagination" as much as through intellection [lines 3882–3886] — as the chapter's pivot: "'Of God there is sense-perception': this stunning but wholly consistent affirmation cannot be overemphasized. It overturns all conventional misrepresentations of Neoplatonism in general, and Dionysius' thought in particular, as a 'gnostic' repudiation of the senses and the sensible world" [lines 3896–3899]. The continuum culminates in the *agnosia*, continuous with the cognitive ascent: "the mystical unknowing of God as no thing is the inseparable completion of the knowledge of God, by every mode of cognition, as all things in all things" [lines 4048–4050]. For cognitive beings, the entire cognitive ascent and its ecstatic culmination *is* their mode of reversion to God and hence their mode of being: "for us as rational souls capable of ascending to intellect and beyond, the cognitive ascent is our reversion, and the culmination of the ascent in the ecstatic union above intellect is the fulfilment of our nature" [lines 4085–4089].

Chapter 7 (*Symbolism*) completes Perl's argument by showing that material symbols are not a concession to human weakness but the necessary mode of any genuine knowledge of God given that being is theophany. Perl collapses the distinction between intelligible names and sensible symbols: since God is equally beyond every being, "it is as false, and false in the same sense, to say that God is Word, Mind, or Being as to say that he is lion, stone, fire, or worm" [lines 4270–4272]; and since all things participate in Beauty, all are genuine manifestations of God — "all things, then, are in effect symbols of God... and this is simply a restatement of the doctrine that being is theophany" [lines 4308–4310]. The technical verb *proballein* — simultaneously "to present/expose" and "to screen/shield" [lines 4388–4390] — names the dual nature of every being as symbol: "the very being of all things... is a presentation which screens and a screen which presents God" [lines 4446–4448]. Every being reveals God by making him available to cognition and conceals him by interposing a finite form; "it conceals precisely in and as revealing, and reveals precisely in and as concealing" [lines 4315–4316]. There can therefore be "no non-symbolic knowledge of God" [lines 4206–4207]. To ascend to mystical union is not to discard symbols but to penetrate them: "The ascent from symbols is the penetration into them" [line 4517]. The chapter closes with the Incarnation as the fullest expression of this structure — "Being as symbol, as theophany, and hence as being, is perfectly realized in Christ, in God incarnate, the finite being which is God-made-manifest" [lines 4565–4567].

The Conclusion defends the Neoplatonic-Dionysian vision as a whole against the nihilism Perl sees as the consequence of rejecting the principle that to be is to be intelligible. The denial of this principle means that "whatever we call 'being' is not being but a projection, interpretation, illusion — in short, nothing" [lines 4598–4600]. Against this, Perl offers Dionysius's contemplative-liturgical alternative: "being is not an object for mastery and exploitation but a gift which is received only in being given and a symbol which is known only in being unknown" [lines 4657–4660]. The book ends with an argument for the contemporary relevance of the Dionysian vision, including dialogue with Heidegger's critique of onto-theology and what Perl calls "environmental philosophy" — noting that the understanding of all things as theophanies "which analogously think, live, and love" [line 4663] offers a resource against the reductionist objectification of nature.

## Rolt's Reading (1920)

C. E. Rolt's *[[rolt-dionysius|On the Divine Names and the Mystical Theology]]* (SPCK, 1920)
reads Dionysius through the lens of British Idealism. Where [[perl-theophany|Perl]] later reads
him as a strict Neoplatonist and [[sharpe-mysticism|Sharpe]] as the orthodox Thomistic
*Christianiser* of Neoplatonism, Rolt identifies the **Super-Essential Godhead** with the
**Supra-Personal Absolute** and embraces the formula (from F. H. Bradley) that **"God is but an
Appearance of the Absolute."** He reconstructs Dionysius's emanation language into a metaphysics
of **two streams** (Universal Being and the Particular stream of Non-entity), each creature
holding "two sides to its existence" — one in the Super-Essence, one in itself. Rolt also reads
*theosis* terminologically (the *thearchia* emanates *theotēs*, in which deified souls
participate) and argues from man's actual deification to God's *Supra*-Divinity.

Rolt is sympathetic but candid about the dangers: Dionysius under-emphasizes "the ugliness of
evil" and "the Cross of Christ," and "the study of him is for the few." His editor,
W. J. Sparrow-Simpson, brackets several critical notes warning that the Supra-Personal Deity
doctrine threatens the orthodox account of the Person of Christ and the Atonement. Rolt's
identification of the corpus's **Hierotheus with Stephen bar Sudaili** (a contemporary of
Proclus) and his dating "near the very end of the fifth century" align with the modern consensus
against Parker. See [[rolt-dionysius|Rolt's edition]] and the
[[dionysius-christian-or-neoplatonist|Christian-or-Neoplatonist controversy]].

## Reception History (Sparrow-Simpson, 1920)

The reception essay appended to Rolt's edition by **W. J. Sparrow-Simpson** adds several data
points to the influence record above:

- **Pope Martin I, Lateran Council (649)**: an early Western appeal to Dionysius — and an early
  dispute over his *meaning*. Martin complained that the Areopagite was being misquoted as
  ascribing to Christ "one divino-human activity" (*una operatio deivirilis*), whereas Dionysius
  had written "**a new divino-human activity**" (*kainē theandrikē energeia*) — a phrase central
  to the Monothelite controversy and the [[chalcedonian-definition|two-energies/two-wills]]
  debate.
- **[[john-scotus-eriugena|John Scotus Eriugena]]** (9th c.): not merely translator but the
  thinker who built Dionysian principles into the *De Divisione Naturae*; his exposition pushed
  the apophatic doctrine to the brink of pantheism (denying that the Absolute can love; reducing
  the Trinity to subjective "names").
- The **orthodoxy controversy** that opened once the apostolic identification fell: defenders
  (Bernard de Rubeis, the Paris and Louvain faculties, the Jesuit Lessius — for whom Dionysius
  was a 36-year devotional patron) against critics charging Monophysite/pantheist tendencies
  (Le Quien, F. C. Baur, Vacherot, Dorner, Bach, Westcott). See
  [[dionysius-christian-or-neoplatonist|the controversy page]].
