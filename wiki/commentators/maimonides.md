Maimonides (1135–1204 CE), known in Hebrew as the **Rambam** (*RaMBaM* = Rabbi Moses ben Maimon), is the dominant figure in medieval Jewish philosophy and the most systematic Jewish legal codifier in history. His two magnum opera — the *Mishna Torah* (legal code) and the *Guide for the Perplexed* (philosophical theology) — define the poles of his achievement and have generated controversy in both directions for eight centuries.

## Biographical Context

Born in Córdoba under Muslim Almoravid rule, Maimonides fled Spain as a child following the Almohad conquest (c. 1148) and eventually settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), where he served as court physician. He supported the Jewish community of Egypt through responsa and eventually succeeded his father as the community's head. Despite a life under intermittent persecution and constant professional pressure, he produced works of extraordinary scope and originality.

## Major Works

**Commentary on the Mishna** (Arabic, c. 1168): An Arabic introduction to the Mishna for the general reader, containing the famous thirteen principles of Jewish faith (*Thirteen Ikkarim*).

**Mishna Torah** (*Yad ha-Chazakah*, c. 1180): A complete, systematic codification of the entire Talmudic law in clear, unencumbered Hebrew. The *Mishna Torah* covers every subject in Talmudic law — including laws that no longer apply (Temple service, Sanhedrin) — organized logically rather than following Talmudic arrangement. It is the first code to present law "without sources or citations," giving it directly in clear and comprehensible form. This unprecedented clarity and completeness immediately evoked admiration and opposition in equal measure.

**Guide for the Perplexed** (*Moreh Nevukhim*, Arabic, c. 1190): Addressed to educated Jews troubled by the apparent conflict between Aristotelian philosophy and Torah. Maimonides argues that correct interpretation of both Torah and Aristotle dissolves most contradictions. He employs extensive allegorization of the Torah's anthropomorphic language about God, arguing that all such language is metaphorical.

## Hermeneutical Method

Maimonides employs multiple hermeneutical registers depending on the audience and genre:

**In the Mishna Torah**: Purely legal (halakhic) reasoning; no philosophy, no homiletics. The goal is practical legal clarity. The Rambam asserts in the Mishna Torah's introduction that a scholar who knows only the Written Torah and his code will need no other legal text — a claim that provoked accusations of making the Talmud redundant.

**In the Guide**: Philosophical allegorization (*ta'wil*-influenced). Following the Islamic rationalist tradition (al-Farabi, Ibn Sina), he argues that the Torah's narratives encode philosophical and scientific truths accessible only to the educated elite. Anthropomorphic passages about God must be read allegorically; to read them literally is, for Maimonides, idolatry of a sophisticated kind.

**On the imago dei (Gen 1:26–27)**: Maimonides reads the *tselem* (image) as referring to the intellect — specifically the divine intellect that humanity uniquely shares. He explicitly rejects any corporeal interpretation. This places him in tension with traditional reading and with later commentators (Nahmanides, Radak) who gave the term a broader sense.

## Controversies

**The Mishna Torah controversy**: [[rabbi-abraham-ben-david|Rabbi Abraham ben David of Posquières]] (Rabad, 1125–1198) wrote sharp critical glosses on the Mishna Torah, objecting to specific rulings and to the code's authority-claim. The broader opposition feared that the Mishna Torah would weaken Talmud study — a fear history has not validated; Talmudic study intensified after the code's publication.

**The philosophical controversy**: The *Guide for the Perplexed* polarized Jewish intellectual life. Anti-rationalists (centered in southern France and Spain) sought to ban philosophical studies for those under 25. This controversy contributed indirectly to the [[paris-trial-1240|Paris trial of 1240]]: Solomon of Montpellier sought Dominican help in condemning the *Guide* as heretical, which led to the burning not of the Guide but of the Talmud itself.

**The thirteen principles**: Maimonides' thirteen *ikkarim* (including belief in divine incorporeality, the eternity of the Torah, bodily resurrection) have been contested by later authorities who objected to the very concept of fixed dogmatic articles in Judaism.

## Doctrine of the Guide for the Perplexed

The following is drawn from a full reading of the [[guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide for the
Perplexed*]] (Friedländer trans.). All claims are grounded in the text by Part and chapter.

**Audience and esoteric method.** The Guide is addressed not to the multitude but to the single
perplexed believer trained in philosophy: "I prefer to address myself to the one man, and to
take no notice whatever of the condemnation of the multitude" (Introduction). Truths are
deliberately scattered and partly concealed so as not to oppose "the Divine Will … which has
withheld from the multitude the truths required for the knowledge of God" (Introduction). He
lists **seven causes of contradiction** in a text, the fifth (pedagogical) and seventh
(deliberate concealment of metaphysics) being those operative in the Guide itself.

**The parable (*mashal*).** Scripture's husk is silver, its inner sense gold (Prov. 25:11);
two kinds of parable are distinguished — those where every detail signifies, and those where
only the whole does (Introduction). This is [[allegorical-reading|allegoresis]] put to
systematic philosophical use, governed by the rule *dibberah Torah bi-leshon benei adam* ("the
Law speaks in the language of man").

**Incorporeality and the equivocal terms (Part I).** Most of Part I is a lexicon dissolving
[[anthropomorphism|anthropomorphic]] language: *tselem*, *panim*, *regel*, *leb*, *ayin*, etc.
are homonyms. Belief in God's corporeality is graded *worse than idolatry* (I:36). He commends
**Onkelos** as the gold standard of anti-corporeal translation (I:27–28). On Gen 1:26 he reads
*[[image-of-god-tzelem|tselem]]* as the **intellect**, not bodily form.

**Negative theology (Part I:50–60).** God is "one simple substance, without any composition" (I:51);
positive essential attributes would import plurality and so are denied. Only **negations** and
**attributes of action** describe him; terms like existence, wisdom, power are "perfectly
homonymous" between God and creatures (I:56). The summit of knowledge is the knowledge that he
cannot be known — "Silence is praise to Thee" (I:59). See [[negative-theology]],
[[divine-attributes]], [[divine-names]].

**Critique of the Kalam (Part I:71–76).** Maimonides traces [[kalam|kalam]] to Greek/Syrian
Christian apologetics adopted by the Mutakallimun, faults its method — they "first considered
what must be the properties of the things which should yield proof for … a certain creed" and
then asserted those properties (I:71) — and refutes its twelve atomist propositions (atoms,
time-atoms, the constant recreation of accidents, "admissibility") and its seven proofs of
creation, five of unity, three of incorporeality.

**Proofs and cosmology (Part II).** Part II opens with **25 granted Aristotelian propositions**
plus a withheld 26th (eternity); on these he proves God's existence, unity, and incorporeality
*on the hypothesis of eternity itself* — "not because I believe in the eternity of the Universe,
but because by the aid of this method these three principles can be fully proved" (I:71; II:1).
The cosmos is the nine spheres and the [[separate-intellects|separate Intelligences]] (identified
with the angels), proceeding by **[[emanation|emanation]]** (*shefa*).

**Creation vs. eternity (Part II:13–25).** Three views: Moses (*[[creation-ex-nihilo|creatio ex
nihilo]]*), Plato (eternal matter), Aristotle (eternal universe). Neither creation nor eternity
is demonstrated; Aristotle "never intended to prove" eternity (II:15), so Maimonides accepts
creation "on the authority of Prophecy" (II:16), holding it more defensible and required by the
Law. **Time itself is created** (II:13). The hermeneutical pivot (II:25): allegory of the
creation texts would be licit *only if* eternity were demonstrated — since it is not, the literal
sense stands (contrast the divine-corporeality texts, which *were* refuted by demonstration). See
[[eternity-of-the-world]].

**Prophecy (Part II:32–48).** Prophecy is the natural **overflow of the Active Intellect** upon a
man perfect in intellect and imagination — which God may miraculously withhold. Eleven degrees
are distinguished; **Moses' prophecy is homonymously distinct** from all others (II:35); prophetic
acts (Hosea's marriage, Ezekiel's siege) occur only within vision. See [[prophecy]].

**The Chariot, evil, providence (Part III:1–24).** The *[[maaseh-merkabah|Maaseh Merkabah]]*
(Ezekiel 1) is metaphysics taught only by hints (III: Intro–VII). **Evil is privation**, rooted
in [[matter-and-form|matter]] (III:8–12). The universe "does not exist for man's sake" (III:13,
25). **[[divine-providence|Providence]] is proportional to intellectual perfection** (III:17–18);
God's knowledge of particulars is categorically unlike human knowledge and does not determine the
possible (III:19–21). [[job|Job]] is read as a parable whose speakers voice the rival theories of
providence (III:22–23).

**Reasons for the commandments (Part III:25–49).** **Every** commandment has a rational cause
(III:26); the Law has two objects, "the well-being of the soul and the well-being of the body"
(III:27). Many precepts are intelligible only against the idolatry of the
**[[sabians|Sabians]]**, which the Law exists "to blot out" (III:29); the **sacrificial system
is a concession** to ingrained habit, not intrinsically desired by God (III:32). See
[[reasons-for-the-commandments]].

**Human perfection (Part III:51–54).** The **Parable of the Palace** ranks people by their
knowledge of God (III:51); "man's love of God is identical with His knowledge of Him" (III:51).
Of the four perfections, only the **intellectual** is true and abiding; it issues in the
imitation of God's *hesed, mishpat, tzedakah* (III:54). See [[intellectual-perfection]].
