# W. Montgomery Watt

**William Montgomery Watt** (1909–2006) was the foremost British Islamicist of the twentieth century
and, for decades, professor of Arabic and Islamic Studies at the University of Edinburgh. An ordained
Scottish Episcopal priest, he combined historical-critical rigor with an unusually sympathetic stance
toward Islam, and his works became standard textbooks in the English-speaking academy.

## Major Works

- ***Muhammad at Mecca*** (1953) and ***Muhammad at Medina*** (1956) — the standard mid-century
  critical biography of [[muhammad|Muhammad]], reading his career in socio-economic and political
  context; abridged as *Muhammad: Prophet and Statesman* (1961).
- His revision of Richard **Bell**'s *Introduction to the Qur'ān* →
  **[[bell-watt-introduction-to-the-quran|*Bell & Watt, Introduction to the Qur'an*]]** (1970), the
  work ingested here.
- *Islamic Philosophy and Theology*, *The Formative Period of Islamic Thought*, and studies of
  Muslim–Christian relations.

## Method and Characteristic Positions

Watt works in the **modern historical-critical** tradition (the German *Geschichte des Qorāns*
lineage of Nöldeke and Schwally extended into English), but his conclusions are frequently
**moderate**:

- On the Qur'an's text, he treats the *traditions* about the Abu Bakr "collection" skeptically while
  affirming that the **Uthmanic recension** faithfully preserves what Muhammad delivered — "modern
  study … has not raised any serious question of its authenticity."
- On Muhammad, he accepts the prophet's **sincerity** (he genuinely believed his revelations divine,
  could not have knowingly forged them) while explaining the prophetic experience and the community's
  rise in historical terms — a stance that drew criticism from both secular skeptics and Muslim and
  Christian confessional readers.
- He preserved and extended **Bell's revision hypothesis** (Muhammad's own editing of the text;
  reused written scraps) — a theory the wider field never widely adopted.

## Reception and Controversy

Watt was respected by many Muslims as a fair-minded "orientalist," yet his historical method is
exactly the target of the traditionalist polemic of
**[[al-azami-history-of-the-quranic-text|M. M. Al-Azami]]**, who classes Bell, Watt, Jeffery, and
Wansbrough together as undermining the integrity of the Qur'anic text. The wiki records this dispute
without adjudication (Contradiction Protocol): Watt's *moderate* critical verdict on authenticity
sits between the radical revisionists (Wansbrough) and the traditionalists (Al-Azami).
