# William Buck

**William Buck** (1933–1970) was an American writer who devoted his life to making the great Sanskrit epics accessible to Western readers in modern English prose. He died at the age of 37, leaving as his legacy a prose retelling of the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]], a prose retelling of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], and an unfinished Harivamsa manuscript.

## Hermeneutical Method

Buck's method was explicitly that of the **storyteller rather than the scholar**: he began with literal English translations of the epics, studied Sanskrit (but did not work from Sanskrit directly), and then retold the stories in vivid modern English prose. His stated goal was to preserve the *spirit and flavor* of the original — the character of each actor, the texture of the land and times — while eliminating the repetition, theological digression, caste-system preachments, and interpolated material that he believed obscured the narrative core for modern readers. He acknowledged making deliberate changes to "tell the story better" and explicitly did not present his work as technically accurate scholarship. His frame: "Read the stories and you get the real spirit of the original once you're done."

This places Buck in a tradition of **adaptive retelling** — the same tradition that the original Rāmāyaṇa itself endorses (van Nooten's introduction notes it has always been "part of [the Ramayana's] own tradition to be retold in different times and places"). Buck was therefore doing in English what Southeast Asian court poets, Tulsidas in Hindi, and regional oral traditions had done across two millennia.

## Major Works

- **Ramayana: King Rama's Way** (1976, University of California Press) — prose retelling of Valmiki's Ramayana. See [[buck-ramayana|source summary]].
- **Mahabharata** (1973, University of California Press) — prose retelling of the Sanskrit Mahabharata.
- **Harivamsa** — unfinished at death.

## Characteristic Positions and Divergences from Sources

Buck read all available English translations but found none fully satisfactory. His key interpretive moves in the Ramayana:
- Streamlines the narrative to the essential story, cutting theological interpolations and repetitive battle catalogs
- Softens the fire ordeal episode: removes Rama's harsh rejection of Sita; instead has Agni lead Sita to Rama
- Adds a posthumous stone letter from Ravana to Rama announcing reconciliation — an invention with no Sanskrit precedent
- Frames the narrative with a meta-story (Kusa and Lava reciting the Ramayana in Naimisha Forest) rather than beginning in Ayodhya

## Reception

B. A. van Nooten (Sanskrit scholar, University of California) called Buck's Ramayana "an extraordinary accomplishment" that captured "the simple religious tone that pervades the Indian original." Ram Dass's foreword (1978 Mentor edition) places the work within a bhakti devotional framework and uses Buck's Hanuman portrait as a vehicle for teaching *seva* (selfless service). Critics noted it is "a fascinating tale of adventure, heroism, exciting plots" — accessible popular literature that has introduced the Ramayana to generations of Western readers.
