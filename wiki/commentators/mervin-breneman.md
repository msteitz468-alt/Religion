Mervin Breneman contributed the volume on Ezra, Nehemiah, and Esther to the **New American Commentary** (NAC) series, published by Broadman & Holman in 1993. The NAC is a Southern Baptist confessional series affirming biblical inerrancy, aimed at pastors and serious students rather than technical specialists. Breneman's approach is traditional evangelical theological exposition: historical context, textual explanation, and practical-theological application, with lighter critical apparatus than NICOT volumes. He makes no methodological innovations and does not engage social-scientific frameworks; the value of his commentary lies in solid theological synthesis and Esther coverage.

## Background and Series Context

The NAC series (general editor E. Ray Clendenen; associate OT editors Kenneth Mathews and L. Russ Bush) is explicitly "unapologetically confessional" — distinguishing it from NICOT's broader ecumenical stance and from critical commentary series. Authors affirm "the divine inspiration, inerrancy, complete truthfulness, and full authority of the Bible." The commentary targets expository preaching and teaching.

Breneman's volume is 29 years older than [[hannah-harrington|Harrington]]'s NICOT Ezra-Nehemiah (2022), reflecting a different scholarly moment: less engagement with social-scientific methods, Dead Sea Scrolls comparative studies, or postcolonial readings.

## Hermeneutical Method

Breneman practices **theological-expository exegesis**: reading the texts for their theological structure, historical setting, and Christian application. His engagement with critical scholarship is selective — he responds to the major historical-critical objections but does not systematically engage source criticism, social anthropology, or Second Temple comparative literature. He uses the NIV as his base translation.

## Ezra-Nehemiah: Theological Themes

Breneman identifies six major theological themes across Ezra-Nehemiah:

**1. The Continuity of God's Plan and People**: The primary theological purpose of the book — demonstrating that the returned exiles are the continuation of God's covenant community. Continuity is established through: second-exodus allusions, celebration of the Passover after temple completion (Ezra 6:19-22), the Feast of Tabernacles after the Torah reading (Neh 8), and the persistence of covenantal identity even without national sovereignty.

**2. Separation**: Breneman defends Ezra's and Nehemiah's intermarriage decrees as necessary for covenant purity. His position is more apologetically traditional than Harrington's: "Unchecked assimilation would have meant the end of the community." He explicitly invokes the later Maccabean period as evidence that the "conservative and exclusivistic groups... preserved Judaism and led the way toward Christianity." His stance: the strictness was justified in context, and while later Judaism exaggerated some emphases, the core principle of covenant separation was sound. See [[intermarriage-ezra-nehemiah|The Intermarriage Controversy]].

**3. Scripture**: Ezra did not introduce a new Torah — he recalled the people to the law they had neglected. The Protestant Reformation analogy: "The Bible's impact on people's lives awaited only its availability, not its existence." Ezra 9:4: revival among those who "trembled at the words of the God of Israel."

**4. Worship**: The priority of worship over physical security — the altar was built before the temple, and the temple before the walls. The temple is "a symbol of God's presence and a reminder that they were to be 'a kingdom of priests and a holy nation' (Exod 19:6)."

**5. Prayer**: Prayer is "taken for granted throughout" — Ezra's prayer in Ezra 9, the people's prayer in Neh 9, and Nehemiah's pattern of combining brief intercessory prayer with action (Neh 2:4-5; 5:19; 13:14, 22, 31). "Nehemiah presents us with an admirable example of combining prayer with action."

**6. Theocentric Historiography**: God acts through human agents, including foreign kings (Cyrus, Artaxerxes). God's sovereignty encompasses all nations. History is to be interpreted as the arena of God's redemptive action.

## Ezra and Nehemiah as Figures

**Ezra**: Breneman follows the talmudic tradition (b. Sukkah 20a) that Ezra reestablished the law in Israel. He cites the tradition that Ezra and the Torah mattered more than the temple (b. Megilla 16b). "He realized, as perhaps no one else had since Moses and the prophets, that man cannot live by bread alone, only by and through the words that proceed from the mouth of God."

**Nehemiah**: "A genuine leader, an excellent administrator, and a man of prayer." His single-mindedness, attention to detail, willingness to delegate, dedication to service, and dependence on God are held up as models. Breneman notes: "Because this community persevered, our Old Testament was completed and preserved."

## Esther: Theological Themes

Breneman's Esther commentary centers on four theological claims:

**1. The Providence of God**: The governing theme of the entire book. God is never named — but this is *intentional theology*. "The author chose to show how human decision and action are the instrumentation of divine purpose." Before Haman's plot is devised, God has already installed the instruments of deliverance. The hiddenness of God's name is a pastoral teaching: "When we are most tempted to think that God has forgotten us... we can be sure that he is at work." Application especially to situations in which God appears least visible.

**2. The Conflict of Worldviews**: Haman's lot-casting (*pûr*) represents the fatalistic/magical worldview of the ancient world — divination, astrology, omens. Israel's covenant monotheism rejected this. The supreme irony: Haman's lot-casting provides the very name of the feast celebrating his defeat (*Purim* = lots). "The auspicious day on which Haman had depended for the destruction of the Jews turned out to be the day of his destruction and of their triumph."

**3. Obedience to God's Will**: Esther's "If I perish, I perish" (4:16) and Mordecai's "for such a time as this" (4:14) as models of faithful courage under threat — acting in obedience without certainty of outcome, trusting divine providence.

**4. The Danger of Anti-Semitism**: The planned pogrom of Haman as a historical type of systematic ethnic genocide. Breneman treats this as a permanent warning applicable beyond the original context.

## Esther's Canonical Status

Breneman discusses the canonical debates around Esther without extensive engagement. He defends its inclusion in the canon against:
- The absence from Qumran (explained by the non-Mosaic character of Purim and the absence of God's name)
- Luther's famous dislike (not engaged in depth)
- Critical claims that it is fiction (he defends historicity)

See [[esther-canonicity|Esther: Canonical Disputes and the Hidden God]].

## Comparison with Harrington (NICOT, 2022)

| Feature | Breneman (NAC, 1993) | Harrington (NICOT, 2022) |
|---|---|---|
| Ezra-Nehemiah method | Traditional evangelical exposition | Social-scientific (Douglas, Turner) + Second Temple comparative |
| Intermarriage decrees | Defended as necessary for covenant survival | Explained sociologically; "drastic but understandable" |
| Torah renewal | Protestant Reformation analogy | Midrashic method; synagogue embryo |
| Esther | Full commentary | Not covered |
| Critical engagement | Moderate; apologetic | Substantial; accepts some critical results |
| Second Temple parallels | Minimal | Extensive (DSS, Jubilees, Josephus, Mishnah) |
| Series ethos | Inerrancy-affirming, confessional, pastoral | Broadly evangelical, ecumenical, academic |

Breneman's Ezra-Nehemiah is a useful complement to Harrington as a more theologically focused, less methodologically complex perspective from the same evangelical tradition — but three decades earlier and from a more strictly confessional Baptist ethos. His primary unique contribution to the wiki is **Esther**.
