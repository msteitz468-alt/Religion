# Leontius of Jerusalem

Leontius of Jerusalem (fl. early 7th c.) — long confused with [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]] until Marcel Richard established him as "his own person with a distinct theology" — is for Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §8.3) the great representative of a **"philosophy of the hypostasis."** Where the Leontian school (Pamphilus, Theodore) marginalized the hypostasis in favour of ousia, Leontius of Jerusalem did the reverse, making hypostasis the primary ontological principle. Zachhuber follows Krausmüller's early-7th-century dating; his main work, ***Against the Nestorians*** (AN), engages opponents "of a sophistication unknown in Nestorian theology prior to... Babai the Great."

## Reconsidering the Hypostasis

Leontius "was uninterested" in the *enhypostaton*, investing his creativity instead in the notion of **hypostasis**. Answering a Nestorian claim that *hypostasis* has three meanings, he offers **sixteen definitions**; the proper one names "the concrete individual thing... set off from all other things... by its specific mark; it is, as such, a detachment (ἀπόστασίς τις) and a limitation of unlimited being (διορισμὸς τῶν ἀδιορίστων οὐσιῶν)." Though consciously based on the Cappadocian *Epistle 38*, the key modification is that he speaks of "unlimited *ousiai* (plural)" realized in a hypostasis — opening the door to **a hypostasis carrying more than one nature**. The hypostasis "is first and foremost itself, whereas its connection with nature is of secondary importance and, ultimately, accidental." Substances depend on hypostasis, not vice versa. (Against Richard's "radical break" claim, Zachhuber stresses the Cappadocian basis.)

## Insubsistence and Inherent Natures

Leontius is an "unequivocal proponent of insubsistence Christology": Christ's human nature "does not subsist in a hypostasis of his own but from the beginning took its existence in the hypostasis of the Logos" — so the Son's hypostasis is now distinguished "also by its being from more than one nature." Natures are treated almost like accidents (though "Leontius never states that natures and accidents are the same"): substances are "parts of other substances, not of hypostases. Only particular properties (idiomata) are parts of hypostases." His analogies — humans receiving spiritual nature in the resurrection, wool dyed red, wood gilded, a man putting on armour — "make sense only on the basis of an ontology in which the distinction between substances and accidents is less important."

## The Composite Property

Leontius's "most original contribution" is the **composite property (σύνθετον ἰδίωμα)**: a hypostasis's many simple properties are "synthesized into a single, overarching super-property." Unlike the Stoic *idia poiotēs* (the final stage completing an individual), for Leontius "something is initially and intuitively identified as 'Peter' and only secondarily... as a human being" — abandoning the notion of hypostasis as individuated nature. Christologically, Christ's composite property refers to "divine and human natures as such": "all the properties of the Lord's flesh are comprised by one more universal property... namely the very fact that it... never subsisted in its pure nature by itself." Critics charge this yields a Christ "neither fully divine nor fully human"; Zachhuber regards the result as "inevitable once [natures] were understood as merely inhering in the individual."

## Divine Nature

Leontius **concedes** the WT/WHI argument (unlike John the Grammarian and Pamphilus) and, with his foundation in concrete hypostases, allows little role for universal being. His doctrine of individual natures is "fundamentally different from the miaphysite theory of 'particular natures'": where miaphysites preserved unity of nature and hypostasis, Leontius introduced "a strict separation of ousia and hypostasis." He classifies the trinitarian unity as "a union of things... into one nature" alongside soul's powers and fire's elements — "remarkable" — and bases generic unity on "similarity," "quite simply abandon[ing]" the Cappadocian human analogy for the Trinity. Zachhuber concludes: "there is no cogent trinitarian theology emerging from Leontius' extensive anti-Nestorian writing," but his "philosophy of the hypostasis" is "one of the most radical revisions of the Cappadocian framework."
