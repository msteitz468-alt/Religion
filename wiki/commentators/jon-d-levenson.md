# Jon D. Levenson

**Jon D. Levenson** (b. 1949) is an American Jewish biblical scholar, the Albert A. List
Professor of Jewish Studies at Harvard Divinity School, and one of the most influential
practitioners of **Jewish biblical theology**. He is distinctive for combining
rigorous **historical-critical** scholarship with serious **theological** reading and with
close attention to **classical rabbinic interpretation** — and for his sharp critiques of
the way both Christian biblical theology and modern liberal Judaism have read (or
misread) the Hebrew Bible. The source ingested here is *Resurrection and the Restoration of
Israel* (2006); see the book summary
[[levenson-resurrection-and-the-restoration-of-israel|here]].

## Hermeneutical Method

Levenson occupies an unusual middle ground that defines his whole project:

- **Historical criticism *and* theology together.** He fully accepts source-, form-, and
  tradition-critical methods, but rejects the assumption that critical scholarship must be
  theologically mute or that the only "real" meaning is the reconstructed earliest layer.
  He reads the Bible's final form and its afterlife in living communities as theologically
  serious.
- **Rabbinic exegesis as data, not decoration.** Where much critical scholarship dismisses
  *derash* as fanciful, Levenson treats midrashic readings as often exposing genuine
  features of the biblical text that historical critics have missed (e.g. that a promise
  can be fulfilled *after* its recipient's death). He carefully distinguishes *peshat* from
  *derash* while refusing to rank the plain sense as the only legitimate one.
- **Against anachronistic individualism.** A recurring move: modern readers impose a
  deracinated, individualist frame ("Will *I* survive death?") on texts whose categories
  are **corporate, familial, and national** (the people Israel as a natural family). Much
  misreading, he argues, follows from this anachronism.
- **Comparative and polemic-aware.** He reads Israelite religion against its **Canaanite
  and ancient Near Eastern** background (the Divine Warrior, Baal–Mot), and he is acutely
  attentive to how Jewish–Christian and intra-Jewish **polemics** have shaped scholarly
  conclusions (e.g. casting resurrection as a "foreign" doctrine to distance Judaism from
  Christianity and align it with modern science).

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Resurrection is centrally Jewish, not a Christian innovation or a foreign import.** It
   is a "weight-bearing beam in the edifice of rabbinic Judaism," tied to the redemption and
   peoplehood of Israel — see [[resurrection-of-the-dead|Resurrection of the Dead]].
2. **Resurrection ≠ immortality of the soul.** The bodily, communal, God-wrought
   overcoming of a real death is sharply distinct from the Greek idea of a naturally
   indestructible soul; modern Judaism's drift to the latter is **assimilation**.
3. **Zoroastrian influence on resurrection is indirect at most.** The chief root is the
   **Canaanite-Israelite Divine Warrior**, not Persia — a measured qualification of the
   [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|influence thesis]].
4. **The Bible's categories are corporate and familial.** Promise, blessing, and life are
   realized across generations of a natural family; infertility functions as death and
   restored progeny as resurrection.
5. **Creation is combat, not just fabrication.** (From his earlier work, undergirding the
   resurrection book.) God's creative and redemptive power is the *mastery of chaos and
   death* — see [[the-combat-myth|the Combat Myth]].

## Place in This Wiki

Levenson is the wiki's first dedicated **Jewish biblical-theology** commentator, and its
source on the **biblical pre-history of resurrection** ([[sheol|Sheol]];
[[resurrection-of-the-dead|resurrection of the dead]]). On the Zoroastrian question he sits
**beside [[cohn-cosmos-chaos-and-the-world-to-come|Norman Cohn]]** (who credits Zoroaster
with the linear-eschatological frame) but **pulls in the opposite direction** on
resurrection specifically, rooting it in the Canaanite Divine Warrior — a productive tension
recorded on the [[zoroastrian-influence-on-abrahamic-eschatology|comparison page]].
