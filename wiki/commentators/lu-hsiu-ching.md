# Lu Hsiu-ching (406–477 CE)

Lu Hsiu-ching is, in [[russell-kirkland|Kirkland's]] analysis, the **practical founder of
organized Taoism** (*tao-chiao*) as a coherent, self-identified religious community. Before
Lu's organizational work in the 5th century, the traditions we call "Taoist" were a diverse
collection of practices and textual communities without a shared institutional identity.

## Significance

Lu Hsiu-ching organized the existing body of **Ling-pao** texts into the first systematic
Taoist canon — his *Catalogue of the Three Caverns* (*San-tung mu-lu*, c. 471 CE). This act of
canon-formation was the moment when "Taoism" (*tao-chiao*, "the teachings of Tao") became a
self-identified religion with a defined scriptural corpus, analogous to other world religions.

By doing this, Lu accomplished several things simultaneously:
1. He gave the Ling-pao revelations institutional authority alongside the T'ien-shih and
   Shang-ch'ing traditions
2. He created a framework ("Three Caverns") that could accommodate diverse texts under one identity
3. He aligned the Taoist community with the Liu-Sung dynasty (420–479), providing religious
   legitimation for the rulers (recognizing them as the legitimate continuation of the Han) and
   securing official patronage in return

## Political Role

Lu Hsiu-ching's religious establishment performed ceremonies and provided auspicious portents
in support of the Liu-Sung dynasty. He recognized the Liu-Sung rulers as heirs to the Han
dynasty (both dynasties shared the surname Liu), and Taoist manifestos of the period declared:
"the descendents of the house of Liu, in possession of the Tao, have revived the tradition, and
Heaven has bestowed the Chart [a token of political legitimacy] in response."

This political engagement was not opportunism but the expression of a long Taoist tradition of
providing divine legitimation to worthy rulers — a tradition traceable to the *T'ai-p'ing ching*.

## The Lieh-tzu Attribution

Lu Hsiu-ching apparently intended to include the *Lieh-tzu* (the *Chung-hsü chih-te chen-ching*)
in his canon (*Catalogue of the Three Caverns*). This suggests that the *Lieh-tzu* — a text that
emphasizes the finality of death and enjoyment of life (*carpe diem*) rather than cultivation —
was considered an authentic Taoist voice by the tradition's organizer.

## Hermeneutical Method

Lu worked through the **Ling-pao** interpretive framework: communal liturgy, vocalization of
primordial *chen-wen* texts, and a universalistic soteriology (salvation available to all beings).
His approach was fundamentally **organizational and liturgical** — creating institutional
structures for preserving and transmitting diverse Taoist practices — rather than
exegetical-philosophical.
