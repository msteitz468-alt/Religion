# Jeffrey A. D. Weima

## Biographical and Institutional Context

Jeffrey A. D. Weima is Professor of New Testament at Calvin Theological Seminary in Grand Rapids, Michigan — the academic institution of the Christian Reformed Church. In the Author’s Preface to *1–2 Thessalonians* (BECNT, Baker Academic, 2014), Weima reports that the commentary was the product of nearly twenty years of specialized research. Intermediate steps he names include *An Annotated Bibliography of 1 & 2 Thessalonians* (Leiden: Brill, 1998) co-authored with Stanley E. Porter; journal articles and book chapters on Pauline letter-writing and the Thessalonian correspondence; an annual Calvin Theological Seminary elective on the letters; participation (later as cochair) in the Studiorum Novi Testamenti Societas (SNTS) “Thessalonian Correspondence” seminar; and an interim contribution to the *Zondervan Illustrated Bible Background Commentary* (2002). Weima states that the delays matured his reading and that he remains sober about remaining shortcomings.

The BECNT (Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament) series, whose series preface is by Robert W. Yarbrough and Robert H. Stein, presents informed-evangelical commentaries for pastors and serious readers: focus on problems bearing on the meaning of the text; stress on argument development; engagement with scholarly research without either dismissive proof-texting or assimilation that erases theological distinctives. Contributors share a framework of the trustworthiness and essential unity of Scripture and regard historic creeds and Reformation documents as a legitimate interpretive frame. Weima’s volume within this series combines intensive historical-background research, careful grammatical analysis, and explicit pastoral concern — his reading consistently asks how Paul’s words function as comfort and correction for a specific congregation. In the Author’s Preface he prays that the commentary clarify both what God was saying through Paul to first-century Thessalonian Christ-followers and how these letters continue to communicate God’s will for Christ-followers today.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Modern critical: Form criticism (Epistolary-literary approach).** Weima’s defining methodological commitment is to interpret Paul’s letters as letters — governed by the conventions of ancient Greco-Roman epistolary practice rather than by classical rhetorical theory or purely thematic categories. In the Structure section of the Introduction he surveys three approaches (thematic, rhetorical, epistolary) and argues that “the most important source for understanding the apostle’s letters must naturally be the letter-writing practices of his day rather than the rules for oral discourse.” He concedes rhetoric as broad persuasion and everyday devices (e.g., paraleipsis in 1 Thess 4:9; 5:1) but objects — with Porter, Stamps, Classen, and Porter & Dyer — to “direct and wholesale” application of Greco-Roman speech rhetoric to the letters (Weima 1997b).

Each passage begins with a **Literary Analysis** triad that addresses: (1) the literary *character* of the unit (thanksgiving, paraenesis, apostolic parousia, disclosure formula, autobiography, body benediction, etc.); (2) its *boundaries* / extent (where the unit begins and ends, often against rival paragraphings such as Schubert’s mega-thanksgiving 1:2–3:13 or majority cuts of 2 Thess 2 that stop at v. 12); and (3) its *internal structure* (the logic of Paul’s argument and sub-units). This approach, developed in Weima’s prior work on Pauline letter-writing conventions and letter closings (*Neglected Endings* / Weima 1993, 1994a, 1995, 2010b), proceeds from the conviction that the form of the text is an important supplementary key to its meaning. Pauline closings are treated as carefully constructed units that reverse the thanksgiving’s foreshadowing function by encapsulating body themes.

**Grammatico-historical exegesis / Modern critical: Historical-critical.** In keeping with the BECNT mandate, Weima operates firmly within a grammatico-historical framework, taking into account text-critical analysis and historical-critical background research while maintaining a conservative theological stance. He subjects every clause to detailed syntactical analysis, attending closely to verb tense, mood, and voice; to the force of specific particles and conjunctions; and to text-critical variants (half-brackets signal variants in the series presentation). His work is particularly attentive to emphatic constructions (the emphatic future negation *ou mē* + aorist subjunctive; the emphatic personal pronoun; the divine passive voice) as rhetorical and theological signals. On obscure passages (notably the restrainer of 2 Thess 2:6–7) he articulates a discipline of proportion: shout where Scripture is frequent and clear; whisper where it is infrequent and unclear — yet the *comfort* purpose of the text must still be shouted.

**Pastoral-consolatory framework.** A recurring insistence in the commentary is that both Thessalonian Letters are primarily *comfort* documents addressed to a persecuted congregation, not eschatological blueprints for predicting the future. Weima states the overriding concern of 2 Thessalonians as “not to calculate the future but to comfort the church,” and gives 2 Thess 2:1–17 the alternate title “The Slaying of Satan’s Superman and the Sure Salvation of the Saints: Paul’s Word of Comfort” (Weima 2006). This pastoral orientation shapes his reading of every major passage, including 1 Thess 4:13–18, 5:1–11, and 2 Thess 1:3–12 and 2:1–17, where he consistently asks what Paul is trying to reassure his readers about. Defense material (1 Thess 2:1–3:13) is read as reestablishing trust so that the later paraenesis (4:1–5:22) will be accepted.

**Anti-imperial / socio-historical background.** Weima reads both letters against the backdrop of Thessalonica’s aggressive pursuit of good standing with Rome — free-city (*civitas libera*) status after 42 BC, coins and inscriptions, cult of Roma and Roman benefactors, imperial temple and priesthoods, and the city’s distinctive *politarch* office (~70 nonliterary references, 28 from Thessalonica). Terms like *parousia* (royal visit), *apantēsis* (formal reception of a visiting official), and “peace and security” (*eirēnē kai asphaleia*, 1 Thess 5:3) are read as deliberate counter-claims to imperial ideology rather than primarily as OT prophetic echoes. Paul’s proclamation of another King and another coming is not incidentally political; Acts 17:7’s “another king, Jesus” charge and Judge’s reading of imperial bans on predicting ruler-change are brought into the same frame.

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Undisputed Pauline authorship of 1 Thessalonians.** Universal modern scholarly consensus (Jewett, Kümmel); Weima addresses only the 1 Thess 2:13–16 interpolation debate (Pearson; Schmidt), which he rejects on theological, historical, form-critical, linguistic, and textual grounds (universal MS support; no floating location). The aorist *ephthasen* (“has come upon”) in 2:16 need not refer to the AD 70 destruction; other pre-50 calamities or gospel-rejection as wrath remain options; evidence for Jewish Christian suffering in Judea predating 1 Thessalonians is ample (Paul’s own past; Agrippa I; late-40s/early-50s Zealot pressure). *Eis telos* is read temporally as “until the end” (until Christ’s return), leaving room consistent with Rom 11:25–32 rather than forever-closed judgment on Israel as a whole. Restrictive “Judeans” / limited-group readings of *Ioudaioi* also blunt anti-Semitism charges.

2. **Pauline authorship of 2 Thessalonians.** Weima defends against four standard arguments (colder tone; authenticating comment of 3:17; eschatological contradiction with 1 Thess; literary dependence on 1 Thess), with Marshall, Wanamaker, Still, Malherbe, Foster, and others. His counters: (a) the “myth of the cold tone” (Marshall) — “we ought to give thanks” intensifies affection; high “brothers” rate; “our Father” and obligatory-thanks language are warm and common, not distant; firmer tone fits crisis (false prophecy; idle rebellion); (b) 3:17 is not unique emphatic autograph and stresses *authority* / personal presence at least as much as authenticity, especially given the rebellious idlers of 3:6–15 who may ignore written commands; if a forged letter is feared from 2:2, Weima prefers reading 2:2’s triad as error *attributed* to Paul (instrumentality), often via suspected prophecy, more than as a simple counterfeit letter “from us”; (c) eschatological differences are minimal and situation-driven — 1 Thess 5 addresses unbelievers surprised by the day vs. sons of light who know accurately; 2 Thess 2 corrects the false claim that the day *has come* (*enestēken* = presence, not mere imminence); (d) close parallels only to 1 Thess and not to later Paul actually *undermines* the pseudepigraphy hypothesis (Fee’s illogic of a forger who knows only 1 Thess). Additional obstacles: specific local crises; standing-temple assumption (2:4); rarity of pseudonymous *letters*; early reception as Pauline (Polycarp already cites it as such). Cumulative stacking of weak arguments does not yield a strong case (Jewett, Wanamaker).

3. **1 Thess 4:13–18 is not about a rapture to heaven.** The *apantēsis* (Hellenistic formal reception; E. Peterson 1930) metaphor is the interpretive key: citizens exit city gates to meet a visiting king and escort him into the city; so believers meet the descending Lord “in the air” and escort him the remainder of the way to earth. Movement is Christ-toward-earth, not believers-to-heaven. *Harpazō* (“snatched up”) is not intended to teach a “secret rapture of the church”; Weima (with Fee) notes such an event is otherwise unknown in the church until the mid-nineteenth century and rejects Plevnik/Lohfink assumption-to-heaven and Harnisch/Schmithals gnostic readings. Preferred problem diagnosis: fear that the dead are at a *disadvantage* relative to the living at the parousia (coordination of resurrection with return), answered by creed (4:14) + “word of the Lord” (4:15–17). Apocalyptic sequence is poetry for pastoral comfort (4:18), not a predictive timetable.

4. **The restrainer (2 Thess 2:6–7) is the archangel Michael.** Weima’s preferred solution (with Hannah 2000; Nicholl 2004): neuter *to katechon* = Michael’s restraining *activity*; masculine *ho katechōn* = Michael as *person*. Allusions: man of lawlessness // Dan 12:10; self-exaltation/temple // Dan 11:31, 36; restrainer // Michael in Dan 10–12 (including LXX *parerchomai* “stand aside” at Dan 12:1). *Katechō* = restrain/hold back (positive), not “hold sway” or Giblin’s “seize/possess.” He surveys and finds wanting Rome/emperor, law-and-order, gospel/Paul, church+Spirit/rapture, Satan, and false-prophet identifications. Paul emphasizes function over name because readers already know the identity from prior teaching; pastorally what matters is the *sequence* — the restrainer is still operative, therefore the day of the Lord has not yet come. Even with Michael, Weima insists the conclusion is not settled enough to “shout.”

5. **2 Thess 2:1–17 as comfort, not apocalyptic calendar.** The unit runs through v. 17 (not stopping at v. 12) so that the passage closes on comfort, not judgment. Five units: crisis (2:1–2) → correction (3–12) → comfort (13–14) → command (15) → prayer (16–17). The false claim that “the day of the Lord has come” is corrected by prerequisite sequence (apostasy + revelation of the man of lawlessness); future–present alternation is proof the day is not present, not a prophecy-expert timetable. Elliptical “shorthand” reflects prior oral teaching (*elegon*, imperfect). Judgment language against followers of the lawless one and “strong delusion” (2:11) is framed pastorally as vindication of the persecuted.

6. **Workshop ministry as Paul’s primary evangelistic setting.** Paul worked as a leatherworker/tentmaker (*skēnopoioi*, Acts 18:3; “with our own hands”) in a local workshop — plausibly that of his host Jason. The workshop, not the marketplace, was a conventional setting for discourse in antiquity (Hock, Malherbe). Labor and preaching are linked in 1 Thess 2:9 and 2 Thess 3:7–9; self-support models against idleness and avoids burdening new converts while still accepting later support from established churches (Phil 4:15–16 while in Thessalonica).

7. **Thessalonica’s unique politarch office and usable Acts framework.** Acts 17:6, 8 accurately identifies city officials as *politarchs* (πολιτάρχης) — once questioned as Lukan invention, now verified by ~70 nonliterary references (Horsley, Riesner), 28 from Thessalonica; pre-Roman office; number varied. Weima rejects Knox-school methodological discounting of Acts and Koester’s “legendary” individual events: Riesner finds strong verification rates between Acts 17:1–9 and 1 Thessalonians; Bruce’s framework may “confidently be accepted.” Letters retain absolute priority where they speak, but Acts is a usable historical complement (founding itinerary, bail/security deposit possibly behind “Satan has hindered,” Timothy’s later eligibility to return).

8. **“Peace and security” as Roman imperial slogan (1 Thess 5:3).** Weima doubts the traditional OT false-“Peace, peace” (Jer 6:14 etc.) reading: anomaly of OT citation in the Thessalonian correspondence; Gentile audience; intro “whenever they say”; OT lacks “security.” Prefer imperial propaganda pairing *eirēnē kai asphaleia* — coins (Pax/Securitas), Ara Pacis, Pompey inscription at Ilium, Praeneste twin altars, Res gestae, OGIS, Josephus/Velleius/Tacitus/Plutarch (*Ant.* 40.4 exact parallel). Apt for Thessalonica’s favored free-city relationship with Rome; fellow citizens may have countered Christian “day of the Lord” / “coming wrath” by appealing to Rome’s peace and prosperity. Those who buy the slogan “will certainly not escape” (*ou mē*) the destruction of the day.

9. **Cabirus cult as patron deity of Thessalonica.** From the Flavian period at latest, Cabirus was the chief tutelary deity (Edson; Witt’s “Kabeiric monotheism”); “most holy and ancestral god” (IT 199). Documented on ~100 coins (youthful Cabirus with hammer + rhyton); rare inscriptions; possible influence on later St. Demetrius hagiography. Weima warns against uncritical transfer from the twin Cabiri of Samothrace (Thessalonica’s cult is singular). Together with Dionysus, Egyptian (Serapis/Isis — second center after Delos), and imperial cult, Cabirus frames the radical social cost of “turning to God from idols” (1:9).

10. **2 Thess 2:4’s temple reference and pre-70 setting.** “Temple of God” (*naos*) almost certainly means the Jerusalem sanctuary, used metaphorically/typologically for the height of usurpation (Bruce, Marshall endorsed) rather than heavenly temple or church-as-temple (against Chrysostom, some Reformers, Giblin, Beale). Caligula’s AD 40 plan to place his statue in the Jerusalem temple was a “close call” ~11–12 years before 2 Thessalonians, keeping temple-desecration expectation alive. The reference situates the letter while the temple stood and is one of Weima’s strongest historical obstacles to post-70 pseudepigraphy; he does not treat a rebuilt future temple as required for predictive fulfillment.

11. **νήπιοι (“infants”) not ἤπιοι (“gentle”) at 1 Thess 2:7.** Excursus 2: external evidence decisively favors *nēpioi* (𝔓65, א*, B, C*, D*, early versions and Fathers; NA/UBS shift; Fee concedes ordinary circumstances would not accept *ēpioi*). Four internal arguments for “gentle” fail (dittography non-decisive; rarity claims overstated; *nēpios* not always pejorative in Paul; mixed-metaphor problem solved by correct punctuation — full stop after “infants among you,” then nursing-mother metaphor as a new clause). The infant image concludes the denials of flattery, greed, and honor-seeking (2:5–7b) as a claim of innocence; orphan metaphor at 2:17 (Paul as orphaned *child*) coheres with the infant reading. Most modern translations still print “gentle.”

12. **Rebellious idlers (*ataktoi* / *ataktōs*).** *Tous ataktous* (1 Thess 5:14) and the *atakt-* root in 2 Thess 3:6, 7, 11 name not mere “disorderly” persons but “rebellious idlers” — laziness compounded by refusal to obey leaders and Paul (Frame: “deliberate, disobedient idleness”). Trajectory: founding-visit command (1 Thess 4:11; 2 Thess 3:6, 10) → brief appeal/admonition in 1 Thessalonians (4:11–12; 5:14) → third, much longer treatment in 2 Thess 3:1–15 as the problem worsened. Maxim: “If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat” (3:10) targets *unwillingness*, not inability; discipline is redemptive non-association that shames toward repentance while still treating the person as a “brother” not an enemy (3:6, 14–15 inclusio). Cause of idleness (eschatological excitement vs sociological freeloading) is uncertain; if forced, Weima leans slightly eschatological but judges the question largely non-decisive for meaning. 1 Thess 5:27’s solemn charge that the letter be read to *all* the brothers (likely autograph) may already fear that idlers would defy written teaching.

## Relationship to Other Commentators

Weima interacts extensively with the full range of Thessalonian scholarship:
- **Against pseudepigraphy of 2 Thess**: Aligns with Marshall, Wanamaker, Malherbe, Fee, Still, Foster, and Jewett (the last of whom accepts Pauline authorship despite acknowledging the different tone). Foster’s BNTC 2011 survey (109 respondents: 63 yes / 13 no / 35 uncertain on Pauline authorship) is cited as evidence that majority opinion remains Pauline.
- **For pseudepigraphy of 2 Thess**: Engages Trilling (1972, the watershed monograph for a growing minority), Wrede, Schmidt (1801 onward), Bailey, Krodel, Menken, Furnish, Richard, and others; Hollmann (1904) already summarized the four classic anti-authenticity arguments.
- **On 4:13–18**: Engages Plevnik (assumption hypothesis), Harnisch/Schmithals (gnostic hypothesis), Cosby (objections to *apantēsis*), Malherbe, Fee, Holmes, Gaventa, Nicholl, Luckensmeyer, and dispensational secret-rapture readings.
- **On the restrainer**: Heavily reliant on Hannah (2000) and Nicholl (2004) for the Daniel 10–12 / Michael argument; interacts with Giblin’s false-prophet reading, Tertullian/Hippolytus Rome, Cullmann/Munck gospel-and-Paul, dispensational church+Spirit, and all seven major proposals surveyed in Excursus 3.
- **On letter form**: Adopts (with caveats) Jervis’s five-unit thanksgiving analysis over Schubert’s types; prefers Aune’s loose “topos” for apostolic parousia over rigid Funk/Jervis unit grids; cites his own prior work against wholesale rhetorical outlines.
- **I. Howard Marshall** ([[i-howard-marshall|I. Howard Marshall]]) is cited approvingly throughout, especially on 2 Thessalonians authorship, pastoral character, temple motif, and judgment language that does not set God’s love against justice.

## Controversies and Debates (as Weima engages them)

Weima does not invent controversies so much as take clear attributed positions inside existing ones:
- **2 Thess authorship** — live scholarly dispute; Weima affirms Pauline authorship (see Characteristic Positions §2 and [[2-thessalonians-authorship]]).
- **1 Thess 2:13–16 interpolation** — rejects Pearson/Schmidt (Characteristic Positions §1).
- **Identity of the restrainer** — prefers Michael while insisting on “whisper” epistemic humility ([[restrainer-2-thess-2]]).
- **Direction of the parousia meeting** — *apantēsis* escort-to-earth vs secret rapture / heavenward assumption ([[parousia-1-thess-4]]).
- **1 Thess 2:7 text** — *nēpioi* vs *ēpioi*; Weima for infants against most modern translations.
- **Function of 1 Thess 2:1–12** — defensive/apologetic against the modern consensus (Malherbe, Lyons) that the unit is exclusively paraenetic modeling.
- **“Peace and security” background** — imperial slogan vs OT prophetic echo.
- **Temple in 2 Thess 2:4** — historic Jerusalem *naos* used metaphorically vs heavenly temple, church-as-temple, or required future rebuilt temple.

## Influence and Reception Notes

Within the ingested BECNT volume, Weima presents himself less as founding a school than as consolidating a mature epistolary-literary and pastoral-consolatory reading of the Thessalonian letters for pastors and scholars. His long SNTS seminar leadership, the Porter co-bibliography, and repeated self-citation of prior form-critical work on letter closings and Thessalonian apologetic function (Weima 1996, 1997a, 2000b, 2006, etc.) mark him as a specialist voice in late-twentieth / early-twenty-first-century Thessalonian studies, especially among Reformed evangelical and broader evangelical commentary audiences served by BECNT. Claims about wider reception history beyond this commentary’s own self-positioning are not extracted here.
