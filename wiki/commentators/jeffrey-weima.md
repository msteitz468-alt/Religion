# Jeffrey A. D. Weima

## Biographical and Institutional Context

Jeffrey A. D. Weima is Professor of New Testament at Calvin Theological Seminary in Grand Rapids, Michigan — the academic institution of the Christian Reformed Church. His landmark commentary *1–2 Thessalonians* (BECNT, Baker Academic, 2014) was the product of over twenty years of specialized research. Weima cochaired the "Thessalonian Correspondence" seminar of the Studiorum Novi Testamenti Societas (SNTS) for five years, representing sustained engagement with the international Thessalonian scholarship community before the commentary's publication.

The BECNT (Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament) series grounds exegesis in the Greek text, aiming at both scholarly depth and ecclesial usability. Weima's work within this series is notable for its combination of intensive historical-background research, careful grammatical analysis, and explicit pastoral concern — his reading consistently asks how Paul's words function as comfort and correction for a specific congregation.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Modern critical: Form criticism (Epistolary-literary approach).** Weima's defining methodological commitment is to interpret Paul's letters as letters — governed by the conventions of ancient Greco-Roman epistolary practice rather than by classical rhetorical theory or purely thematic categories. Each passage is analyzed under a "Literary Analysis" heading that addresses: (1) the literary *character* of the unit (thanksgiving, paraenesis, apostolic parousia, disclosure formula, etc.); (2) its *boundaries* (where the unit begins and ends); and (3) its *internal structure* (the logic of Paul's argument). This approach, which Weima has developed in prior publications on Pauline letter-writing conventions, proceeds from the conviction that the form of the text is an important supplementary key to its meaning.

**Grammatico-historical exegesis / Modern critical: Historical-critical.** In keeping with the BECNT mandate, Weima operates firmly within a grammatico-historical framework, taking into account text-critical analysis and historical-critical background research while maintaining a conservative theological stance. He subjects every clause to detailed syntactical analysis, attending closely to verb tense, mood, and voice; to the force of specific particles and conjunctions; and to text-critical variants. His work is particularly attentive to emphatic constructions (the emphatic future negation *ou mē* + aorist subjunctive; the emphatic personal pronoun; the divine passive voice) as rhetorical and theological signals.

**Pastoral-consolatory framework.** A recurring insistence in the commentary is that both Thessalonian Letters are primarily *comfort* documents addressed to a persecuted congregation, not eschatological blueprints for predicting the future. This pastoral orientation shapes Weima's reading of every major passage, including 1 Thess 4:13–18 and 2 Thess 2:1–12, where he consistently asks: what is Paul trying to reassure his readers about?

**Anti-imperial hermeneutics (Socio-historical background).** Weima reads both letters against the backdrop of Thessalonica's aggressive pursuit of good standing with Rome — a relationship documented by coins, inscriptions, and the city's distinctive governmental structure (the *politarchs*). Terms like *parousia* (royal visit), *apantēsis* (formal reception), and "peace and security" (1 Thess 5:3) are read as deliberate counter-claims to the imperial ideology that permeated every level of Thessalonian public life. Paul's proclamation of another King and another coming is not incidentally political.

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Undisputed Pauline authorship of 1 Thessalonians.** Universal scholarly consensus; Weima addresses only the 1 Thess 2:13–16 interpolation debate, which he rejects on theological, historical, form-critical, and linguistic grounds. The aorist *ephthasen* ("has come upon") in 2:16 need not refer to the AD 70 destruction; evidence for Jewish Christian suffering in Judea predating 1 Thessalonians is ample.

2. **Pauline authorship of 2 Thessalonians.** Weima defends against four standard arguments (colder tone; authenticating comment of 3:17; eschatological contradiction with 1 Thess; literary dependence on 1 Thess). His counter: (a) the tone difference is exaggerated and explicable from the changed situation; (b) 3:17 stresses *authority* rather than authenticity, given the rebellious idlers; (c) the eschatological difference between the letters is minimal and addressed to different audiences (unbelievers vs. believers in 1 Thess 5; believers under eschatological confusion in 2 Thess 2); (d) close parallels to only 1 Thess and no other Pauline letter actually *undermines* the pseudepigraphy hypothesis.

3. **1 Thess 4:13–18 is not about a rapture to heaven.** The *apantēsis* (formal reception) metaphor is the interpretive key: just as citizens would exit their city gates to meet a visiting king and then escort him into the city, so believers meet the descending Lord "in the air" and escort him the remainder of the way to earth. The movement is Christ-toward-earth, with believers joining him — not believers-to-heaven. Paul's concern is pastoral: to assure Thessalonians that their deceased members will not miss the parousia or be at a disadvantage compared to living believers.

4. **The restrainer (2 Thess 2:6–7) is the archangel Michael.** Weima argues this from Daniel 10–12 allusions. The "man of lawlessness" title draws on Dan 12:10; his self-exaltation and temple desecration draws on Dan 11:36; 11:31. The restraining entity therefore also derives from the same Danielic vision: Michael, the patron archangel of Israel, currently restrains evil (Dan 10:20–21), but will "stand aside" (Dan 12:1 LXX: *parerchomai*) at the time of eschatological tribulation. The shift from neuter (*to katechon*, restraining force) to masculine (*ho katechōn*, restraining person) parallels the Johannine usage of *pneuma* (neuter) and personal references to the Spirit. Paul emphasizes function (restraint) over name, because what matters pastorally is the *sequence*: the restrainer is still operative — therefore the day of the Lord has not yet come.

5. **2 Thess 2:1–12 as comfort, not apocalyptic calendar.** Weima consistently reads this passage as reassurance to persecuted believers, not as a timetable for end-time enthusiasts. The sequence of events (apostasy → man of lawlessness → day of the Lord) corrects the false claim that the day has already come, and the description of the lawless one's destruction (2:8) and the judgment of his followers (2:11–12) is meant to vindicate the suffering believers who are being persecuted by those same followers.

6. **Workshop ministry as Paul's primary evangelistic setting.** Paul preached while working as a leatherworker in a local workshop — probably that of his host Jason. The workshop, not the marketplace, was the ordinary setting for intellectual discourse in antiquity. This explains why Paul's labor and preaching are closely linked in 1 Thess 2:9 and why the Corinthian critic Celsus complained that Christians were being instructed at wool-dressers' and cobblers' shops.

7. **Thessalonica's unique politarch office.** Acts 17:6, 8 accurately identifies the city officials as *politarchs* (πολιτάρχης) — a rare office initially questioned by scholars as Lukan invention but now verified by ~70 inscriptions, 28 from Thessalonica itself. This is a concrete vindication of Acts' historical reliability, which Weima cites in support of using Acts alongside Paul's letters as complementary sources.

8. **Imperial cult as direct backdrop.** The goddess Roma, the cult of Julius Caesar and Augustus, the divine titles on Thessalonian coinage, the moved archaic temple dedicated to Aphrodite/Aeneas — Weima documents in exhaustive detail how deeply Thessalonica had invested in imperial religious identity. The "peace and security" slogan of 1 Thess 5:3 echoes the Roman imperial propaganda disseminated through coins, monuments, and inscriptions. Paul's gospel of another King and another *parousia* was heard in this context as a direct political as well as religious challenge.

9. **Cabirus cult as patron deity of Thessalonica.** Weima gives special attention to the mysterious Cabirus — the single most important deity of Thessalonica, documented on over one hundred coins. A murdered young man who became the patron deity, linked with craftsmen (hammer) and conviviality (drinking horn): Weima follows Edson and Koester in noting the persistent scholarly discussion about whether the Cabirus iconography influenced the later hagiography of Saint Demetrius, the city's Christian patron.

10. **2 Thess 2:4's temple reference undermines post-70 pseudepigraphy.** The reference to "the temple of God" in 2 Thess 2:4 almost certainly means the Jerusalem temple. If 2 Thessalonians were written after AD 70, a pseudonymous forger would not have described the man of lawlessness as still needing to desecrate a standing temple. The reference presupposes the temple's existence — one of Weima's strongest arguments for pre-70 Pauline authorship.

## Relationship to Other Commentators

Weima interacts extensively with the full range of Thessalonian scholarship:
- **Against pseudepigraphy of 2 Thess**: Aligns with Marshall, Wanamaker, Malherbe, Fee, and Jewett (the last of whom accepts Pauline authorship despite acknowledging the different tone).
- **For pseudepigraphy of 2 Thess**: Engages Trilling (1972, the watershed monograph), Bailey, Krodel, Menken, Furnish, Richard, and others.
- **On 4:13–18**: Engages Plevnik (assumption hypothesis), Harnisch (gnostic hypothesis), Malherbe, Fee, Holmes, Gaventa, and Nicholl.
- **On the restrainer**: Heavily reliant on Hannah (1999) and Nicholl (2004) for the Daniel 10–12 argument; interacts with Giblin's false-prophet reading and all seven major proposals.
- **I. Howard Marshall** ([[i-howard-marshall|I. Howard Marshall]]) is cited approvingly throughout, especially on 2 Thessalonians authorship and the pastoral character of the letters.
