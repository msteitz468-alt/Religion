# Tichonius (Tyconius) the Donatist

## Identity and Affiliation

Tichonius was a member of the [[donatism|Donatist]] church in North Africa, active in the latter fourth century (fl. c. 370–390 CE). He is introduced by [[augustine]] in [[on-christian-doctrine]], Book III, Ch. 30, §42 (line 3171) as:

> "One Tichonius, who, although a Donatist himself, has written most triumphantly against the Donatists (and herein showed himself of a most inconsistent disposition, that he was unwilling to give them up altogether)."

Augustine thus marks a paradox at the center of Tichonius's career: he polemicized against his own communion while remaining within it rather than converting to catholic Christianity.

## The *Book of Rules* (*Liber Regularum*)

Tichonius wrote a work he titled the *Book of Rules* (*Liber Regularum*), in which he "laid down seven rules, which are, as it were, keys to open the secrets of Scripture" (lines 3173–3174). Tichonius himself introduced the work with the claim (quoted by Augustine at lines 3197–3203):

> "Of all the things that occur to me, I consider none so necessary as to write a little book of rules, and, as it were, to make keys for, and put windows in, the secret places of the law. For there are certain mystical rules which hold the key to the secret recesses of the whole law, and render visible the treasures of truth that are to many invisible. And if this system of rules be received as I communicate it, without jealousy, what is shut shall be laid open, and what is obscure shall be elucidated, so that a man travelling through the vast forest of prophecy shall, if he follow these rules as pathways of light, be preserved from going astray."

The seven rules are: (1) Of the Lord and His Body; (2) Of the Lord's Bipartite (True and Mixed) Body; (3) Of the Promises and the Law; (4) Of Species and Genus; (5) Of Times; (6) Of Recapitulation; (7) Of the Devil and His Body. See [[tichonius-seven-rules]] for detailed summaries.

## Augustine's Qualified Praise

Augustine's treatment is consistently two-handed — high praise for the hermeneutical value of the rules combined with explicit corrections on both exegetical and doctrinal grounds.

**Praise:** "These rules, as expounded by their author, do indeed, when carefully considered, afford considerable assistance in penetrating the secrets of the sacred writings" (lines 3178–3179). The *Liber Regularum* is "of very great assistance in understanding Scripture" (lines 3209–3210).

**Limitation on scope:** Augustine corrects Tichonius's overreach in claiming the rules unlock *all* scriptural obscurity: "He would have said what was true, and he would not, by attributing more than is warranted by the facts to his very elaborate and useful work, have led the reader into false expectations" (lines 3207–3208). Many passages require none of the seven rules for their elucidation.

**Theological error — Rule 3:** On the third rule (promises and law), Augustine finds Tichonius's treatment "good, but not complete" (line 3275). The defect: Tichonius held that "works were given us by God as the reward of faith, but that faith itself was so far our own that it did not come to us from God" (lines 3276–3278). Augustine regards this as an inadvertent anticipation of the Pelagian error, corrected by Eph 6:23 and Rom 12:3. Tichonius's excuse is that he "had not come into contact with this heresy [Pelagianism], which has arisen in our time" (lines 3279–3280).

**Caution regarding Donatist heresy:** The *Liber Regularum* "must be read with caution, not only on account of the errors into which the author falls as a man, but chiefly on account of the heresies which he advances as a Donatist" (lines 3211–3213).

## Hermeneutical Method

Tichonius's rules are primarily figural and ecclesiological: they systematize the way prophetic and apostolic texts move between literal referent and wider figural scope (head/body, species/genus, sequential/recapitulatory narrative, typological numbers). Rule 6 (recapitulation) is particularly original — identifying narrative analepsis as a deliberate scriptural technique. The rules collectively belong to what Augustine in Book III calls the interpretation of **figurative** or **metaphorical** signs. See [[figurative-interpretation]].
