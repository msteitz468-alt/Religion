# Ko Hung (c. 283–343 CE)

Ko Hung was a scholar-aristocrat of the Six Dynasties period, best known for his *Pao-p'u-tzu*
("The Master Who Embraces Simplicity"), which discusses both external alchemy (*wai-tan*) and
Confucian-style ethics. He has long been treated as the paradigmatic advocate of "physical
immortality" through alchemical means — but [[russell-kirkland|Kirkland]] argues this
characterization is seriously misleading.

## Life and Social Context

Ko Hung came from the same social milieu that produced the [[shang-ching|Shang-ch'ing]] and
[[ling-pao|Ling-pao]] traditions. He was related (granduncle relationship is asserted in
tradition) to Ko Ch'ao-fu, who produced the Ling-pao revelations. Ko Hung held minor military
and clerical positions before retiring to Mount Lo-fu in the south (331 CE).

Kirkland describes Ko Hung as "a scholar of aristocratic heritage" and notes that the "outer
folios" (*wai-p'ien*) of his *Pao-p'u-tzu* express such strong "rational humanism — along
Confucian and Legalist lines — that their translator calls him 'a conservative defender of
common sense.'"

## The Pao-p'u-tzu

The *Pao-p'u-tzu* consists of two parts:
- **Nei-p'ien** ("Inner Folios"): discusses alchemy, immortality techniques, and the pursuit
  of a deathless state through the T'ai-ch'ing tradition
- **Wai-p'ien** ("Outer Folios"): Confucian-style discussions of ethics, governance, and
  social responsibility — making Ko Hung a complex figure who does not fit neatly into
  either "Taoist" or "Confucian" categories

## Kirkland's Reassessment

Kirkland argues that Ko Hung was NOT the representative of "religious Taoism" that 20th-c.
scholars made him. Specifically:
1. His interest in alchemy does not represent "Taoism" as a whole — the overwhelming majority
   of Taoists across all periods did not focus on wai-tan (external alchemy)
2. Even within alchemy, T'ao Hung-ching later clarified that Taoist alchemy was about
   *spiritual elevation requiring loss of bodily life* — not mere indefinite physical extension
3. Ko Hung's "hagiographical" persona as an immortality-monger was largely a later
   *appropriation* by other writers; the historical Ko Hung, as seen in his texts, was a
   different and more complex figure
4. H.G. Creel and Holmes Welch's elevation of Ko Hung as the "ablest theoretician of
   Hsien Taoism" was ideologically motivated — it allowed them to dismiss all "religious Taoism"
   as foolish by caricaturing it through Ko Hung

## Relationship to the Ling-pao Tradition

Ko Ch'ao-fu (the Ling-pao founder) was reportedly Ko Hung's grandnephew. Yet Ko Ch'ao-fu's
tradition had "no hint that 'being Taoist' was primarily an effort to achieve 'a perpetuation
of the physical body.'" The co-existence of both figures in the same aristocratic family
illustrates that there was no uniform Taoist position on death and immortality.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ko Hung operated primarily within the **T'ai-ch'ing** (Outer Alchemy) tradition, reading Taoist
ideas about the transformation of matter and spirit through the lens of laboratory and physical
cultivation. His method is distinctive and not representative of the broader tradition.
