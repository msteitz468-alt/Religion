# Theodore of Raïthu

Theodore of Raïthu (fl. early 7th c.), known from a single work — the ***Praeparatio*** (Introduction) — is treated by Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §8.2) as a close systematic ally of [[pamphilus-the-theologian|Pamphilus]]. Following Werner Elert, Zachhuber accepts his identification with **Theodore of Pharan**, a leader of the seventh-century monothelite movement anathematized at the Sixth Ecumenical Council (680/81) — though Nikas and Dell'Osso dispute the identification. The *Praeparatio* has two parts: a heresiological survey of Christological heresies (popular in the West, quoted in the Lutheran *Formula of Concord*) and a terminological primer — "a Christian version of Porphyry's *Isagoge*."

## Ousia as Universal and Individual

More than Pamphilus, Theodore tries to **harmonize** the Patristic and Aristotelian traditions (rather than running them on parallel tracks). Like Pamphilus he makes the substance–accident binary fundamental: *ousia* = "independent being (αὐθυπόστατον)... whatever exists by itself and not in another" (body vs. colour). But he combines this with a **strong universal realism**: ousia is "said of many and numerically distinct things," present in each hypostasis "in the same way and in the same rank... without partition or division." Peter, Paul, and John fully contain human substance and "are rightly called 'one man'" — language likely inspired directly by [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]]'s *To Ablabius*. He nonetheless cautions that "every physis and ousia after the Holy Trinity is only seen as one by conception, whereas their plurality... exist[s] in reality."

Theodore concedes a hypostasis can be called "a single, particular, individual ousia," but only as ousia individuated in a person, indicating its **universal properties only**, not the particular ones — predication is not reversible ("Peter is a human being, but a human being is not Peter").

## Substance Ontology and the Diminished Hypostasis

Zachhuber calls Theodore's system a **substance ontology**: "ousia is the foundation of all being and the basis of concrete existence." Theodore is "clearly aware of the dilemma" — either hypostasis is the principle of subsistence, or ousia is self-subsistent (leaving little room for hypostasis) — and "chose the latter," accepting "a largely diminished role for hypostasis": "hypostasis is only added to indicate that 'the accidents subsist in it alone.'" He is, however, more reluctant than Pamphilus to push this, insisting ousia and hypostasis "refer to 'the same thing'... considered in different ways."

## Dependence on Pamphilus and Doctrinal Background

Theodore's text "almost entirely refrains from references to doctrinal controversies," though his Christology is a "far-reaching version of... neo-Chalcedonianism" (Christ's humanity subsists in the Logos). Zachhuber argues he **depends literarily on Pamphilus** (not vice versa): Theodore shows no echoes of Leontius's language — notably the term *enhypostatos* is absent — most plausibly because he read Pamphilus "without making [the ideas'] origin in Leontius recognizable." Against tritheism he appeals to Gregory of Nyssa's realism and (like his contemporaries) to ps.-Dionysian language for the absolute oneness of the divine substance.
