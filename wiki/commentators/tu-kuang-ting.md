# Tu Kuang-t'ing (850–933 CE)

Tu Kuang-t'ing is, in [[russell-kirkland|Kirkland's]] assessment, **"perhaps the greatest of
all historians who wrote from within the Taoist tradition."** He served at the T'ang court
through its twilight years and survived into the Five Dynasties period. His principal works
preserve crucial data about Taoism's political history and its female practitioners — data
largely ignored by 20th-c. scholarship.

## Major Works

- ***Yung-ch'eng chi-hsien lu*** ("Records of the Assembled Transcendents of the Fortified
  Walled City"): apparently the **first comprehensive collection of accounts of female Taoist
  divinities and exemplary women**. Contains accounts of Huang Ling-wei, Pien Tung-hsüan,
  the empress Wu's daughter, and many other Taoist women, with genuine historical data
  interspersed among idealizing hagiography. Primary source for women in early medieval Taoism.
- ***Li-tai ch'ung-tao chi*** ("Records of Reverence for Taoism over the Ages"): an account
  of how rulers — from Han through T'ang — patronized Taoism over the centuries. Remains
  untranslated and largely unstudied. Kirkland calls it an essential source for the
  political history of Taoism.
- Writings on the **Ling-pao** and **Shang-ch'ing** traditions, and on the new ritual
  movements that emerged in his day (Ch'ing-wei, Shen-hsiao)

## Significance

Tu Kuang-t'ing's significance is partly in what he preserved:
- He wrote about the Ching-ming tradition, tracing it to the T'ang figure Hsü Sun
- He provides most of the surviving data about Huang Ling-wei ("the Flower Maid")
- His records demonstrate that emperors regarded Taoist rituals as genuinely efficacious
  political acts, not mere personal piety

His significance is also in what he represents: a highly educated, politically sophisticated
Taoist scholar who saw his tradition's institutional and historical life as worthy of careful
documentation. The fact that his major works remain unstudied and untranslated as of Kirkland's
writing is one of the clearest examples of 20th-c. sinology's neglect of Taoist source material.

## Political Context

Tu Kuang-t'ing witnessed the collapse of the T'ang dynasty — the high-water mark of Taoist
political prominence — and the chaos of the Five Dynasties period. His historical writing is
in part an effort to preserve the memory of what Taoism had been at its height and to argue
for its continued relevance to political order.

## Hermeneutical Method

Tu Kuang-t'ing worked primarily as a **historian and compiler** rather than a philosophical
commentator. His method is collection, preservation, and narrative interpretation of Taoist
institutional and biographical data. He belongs to a tradition of Taoist historiography that
Kirkland treats as an essential but neglected genre.
