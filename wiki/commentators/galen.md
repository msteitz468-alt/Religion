# Galen of Pergamon

Physician, anatomist, and philosopher; the most consequential medical writer of antiquity and the first pagan intellectual to engage Christianity as a *philosophical school* rather than dismissing it as a foreign superstition.

## Biographical Overview

Galen was born ca. 130 CE on an estate outside Pergamum in western Asia Minor, into a wealthy and educated family. His father Nicon was an architect formally trained in mathematics and the physical sciences, but with broad interests in literature, the arts, and philosophy. Nicon "insisted that Galen not only receive a liberal education but also develop a genuinely critical spirit toward all intellectual matters" (Wilken, Ch. IV, ~line 721).

Galen's philosophical education was deliberately non-partisan. He studied successively with a Stoic, a Platonist, a Peripatetic, and an Epicurean, never affiliating with any single school. "Never abandoning his critical approach, as a mature thinker Galen criticized all the philosophical schools for their dogmatism and unwillingness to entertain opinions different from their own" (~line 733). He then turned to anatomy and medicine, studying at Pergamum and Alexandria, producing his first book (on the movements of the lungs and thorax) while still a student. He also continued philosophical study under the Platonist Albinus at Smyrna.

After returning to Pergamum he served as physician at the great Asclepieion, the shrine to Asclepius and medical center of the cult. In 161–62 CE he traveled to Rome just as Marcus Aurelius was beginning his reign. "Marcus was so impressed with the young doctor that he asked him to become private physician to his son Commodus" (~line 757). Galen remained in Rome until near the end of the century, then returned to Pergamum for his final years. His complete works, compiled in the nineteenth century, comprise twenty-two volumes, covering medicine, anatomy, pharmacy, logic, philosophy, philology, and pathology.

## Galen as Philosophical Commentator on Christianity

Galen did not write a treatise on Christianity. His observations appear embedded in medical works, especially *De pulsuum differentiis* (On the Differences of Pulses) and *De usu partium* (On the Usefulness of the Parts of the Body). Nevertheless, he "had clearly given thought to Christianity and made an attempt to understand the new movement and to place it within the context of his intellectual and social world" (~line 787).

Galen's key interpretive move was unprecedented: he categorized Christianity (alongside Judaism) as a **philosophical school** (*haeresis*) rather than a foreign superstition (*superstitio*). "He dignified Christianity (and Judaism) with the term 'school,' by which he meant a philosophical school, and he offered philosophical criticism of Christian and Jewish beliefs" (~line 844). This gave Christianity a measure of intellectual respectability it had not received from earlier observers such as Pliny or Tacitus. As scholar Richard Walzer (whom Wilken cites) observes, Galen is "the first pagan author who implicitly places Greek philosophy and the Christian religion on the same footing" (~line 1531).

## Christianity as Philosophical School: Faith vs. Demonstration

Galen's first recorded references to Christians occur in *De pulsuum differentiis*, where he compares dogmatic physicians who refuse to reason carefully with the followers of Moses and Christ:

> "For one might more easily teach novelties to the followers of Moses and Christ than to the physicians and philosophers who cling fast to their schools." (*De pulsuum differentiis* 3.3; ~line 803)

And again:

> "If one offers no reasons for one's views it would be 'as if one had come into the school of Moses and Christ and heard talk of undemonstrated laws.'" (*De pulsuum differentiis* 2.4; ~line 813)

A third fragment (from *On Hippocrates's Anatomy*) presses the same point: Moses "wrote without offering proofs, saying 'God commanded, God spake'" (~line 819).

Galen's criticism is epistemological. By his day the philosophical schools themselves had grown dogmatic, appealing to faith and the authority of their founders rather than to reasoned demonstration. Galen does not single Christianity out uniquely on this score; he makes the same complaint about Platonists, Stoics, and Epicureans. "People admire this or that particular physician or philosopher without proper study of their subject and without a training in scientific demonstration" (*Libr. ord.*; ~line 963). What Galen faulted in the Christians was their general refusal to submit beliefs to philosophical scrutiny — a flaw he found, to varying degrees, in most schools.

## Praise of Christian Moral Practice

Despite his epistemological criticism, Galen praised the Christian *way of life* as rivaling that of genuine philosophers. In one passage (the precise work is not identified in Wilken's text, but translated at ~line 1071):

> "Most people are unable to follow any demonstrative argument consecutively; hence they need parables, and benefit from them just as we now see the people called Christians drawing their faith from parables and miracles, and yet sometimes acting in the same way as those who practice philosophy. For their contempt of death and of its sequel is patent to us every day, and likewise their restraint in cohabitation. For they include not only men but also women who refrain from cohabiting all through their lives; and they also number individuals who, in self-discipline and self-control in matters of food and drink, and in their keen pursuit of justice, have attained a pitch not inferior to that of genuine philosophers."

This is the decisive judgment. In Galen's world, philosophy had become less a system of metaphysics than a **way of life** (*bios*), centered on moral discipline (*askesis*) and the pursuit of virtue. It was in this sense that Galen identified Christianity as a philosophy: "Christians led people to embrace lives of discipline and self-control, to pursue justice, to overcome the fear of death" (~line 1149). Though Christians lacked intellectual foundations, "they did achieve a way of life not inferior to that led by 'those who are truly philosophers'" (~line 1153).

Galen's positive evaluation was not universal among his contemporaries. Marcus Aurelius apparently regarded the Christians' willingness to face death as arising from "mere obstinacy based on irrational ideas" rather than from genuine philosophical self-mastery (*Meditations* 11.3; ~line 1160). The Stoic Epictetus similarly dismissed "the Galileans" as facing death out of "habit" without any appeal to reason or demonstration (*Discourses* 4.7.6; ~line 1167).

## The Arbitrary God of the Christians: Critique of Creation

Galen's most philosophically sustained criticism of Christianity (and Judaism) appears in *De usu partium* (On the Usefulness of the Parts of the Body, written in Rome ca. 170 CE). The work is a study in anatomy — the structure of hands, eyes, muscles, nerves — but the harmony of the body's design leads Galen to reflect on the nature of creation.

The immediate example is the hair of the eyelashes. Galen asks why eyelashes maintain a constant length rather than growing continuously. His answer involves the cartilaginous body at their base. This leads him to contrast the Greek view of creation with the Mosaic view:

> "Did your demiurge simply enjoin this hair to preserve its length always equal, and does it strictly observe this order either from fear of its master's command, or from reverence for the God who gave this order, or is it because it itself believes it better to do this. Is not this Moses's way of treating nature? ... For our demiurge created it to preserve a constant length, because this was better. When he had determined to make it so, he set under part of it a hard body as a kind of cartilage... For it was certainly not sufficient merely to will their becoming such; it would not have been possible for him to make a man out of a stone in an instant, by simply wishing so." (*De usu partium* 11.4; ~line 1236)

Galen then elaborates the contrast directly:

> "For the latter [Moses] it seems enough to say that God simply willed the arrangement of matter and it was presently arranged in due order; for he believes everything to be possible with God, even should he wish to make a bull or a horse out of ashes. We, however, do not hold this; we say that certain things are impossible by nature and that God does not even attempt such things at all but that he chooses the best out of the possibilities of becoming. We say therefore that since it was better that the eyelashes should always be equal in length and number, it was not that he just willed and they were instantly there... it was altogether impossible for them to stand erect unless fixed on something hard." (*De usu partium* 11.14; ~line 1327)

Two philosophical objections are bundled here:

1. **The will vs. reason objection.** The Mosaic account implies that God creates solely by an act of will, without reference to the reasons why things should be as they are. In the Greek view — codified in Plato's *Timaeus*, which Galen draws on — God is a craftsman (*demiurgos*) who fashions existing matter according to reason, choosing the *best* possible arrangement. "Nature does nothing without a reason" (*De usu partium* 11.2.3; ~line 1225). Creation by sheer divine fiat short-circuits rational explanation.

2. **The material cause objection.** The Platonic demiurge works with pre-existing matter (the "material principle"). The Mosaic account implies that God created matter itself — what would later be called *creatio ex nihilo* (creation out of nothing). Galen finds this philosophically inadmissible: "nothing can come out of what does not exist" (citing Aristotle, ~line 1440). For Galen, God is embedded within the cosmos and subject to its laws, not sovereign over them from outside.

Wilken notes that Galen's criticism anticipates a major fault line in the encounter between Christianity and classical culture. Galen sensed that the Christian God was "capricious, arbitrary, even whimsical, subject to no laws other than his own will, and beyond the bounds of nature, a rule unto himself" (~line 1551). The same criticism was later pressed by Celsus, who objected to the Christian doctrine that God could reconstitute bodies from corruption: "'anything is possible to God' ... But, indeed neither can God do what is shameful nor does He desire what is contrary to nature" (Celsus, *c. Cels.* 5.14; ~line 1457).

At the time Galen wrote, Christian doctrine on creation was not yet settled. Justin Martyr followed the Platonic model (God fashioning pre-existing matter: *1 Apol.* 10, 59). The Gnostic Basilides had begun to articulate *creatio ex nihilo*, and Theophilus of Antioch, a contemporary of Galen, stated it explicitly. Galen was "the first critic of Christianity to see the implications of the emerging Christian doctrine" (~line 1433).

## Hermeneutical Method

Galen's approach to Christianity is **philosophical and empirical**, not theological. He treats Christian and Jewish texts (especially Genesis) as he would a philosophical treatise: examining their claims for logical coherence, rational demonstration, and consistency with natural science. He does not engage Christian ritual, ecclesiology, or scriptural interpretation in the traditional sense. His framework is that of Platonic/eclectic philosophy and Aristotelian natural science, with a strong commitment to demonstrative reasoning (*apodeixis*) as the criterion of knowledge.

## Influence on Christian Thought

Galen's categorization of Christianity as a philosophical school had positive effects on Christian self-presentation. Melito of Sardis described Christianity as "our philosophy" (*Frag.* 7), and Justin Martyr presented his conversion as a turn to philosophy (~line 1183). Galen's intellectual challenge also provoked a direct response: a group of Christians in Rome (active during the episcopate of Victor, 187–89), described in the *Little Labyrinth* fragment preserved in Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 5.28.13–15), took Galen's rationalist agenda seriously, studying Euclidean geometry, Aristotle, and Theophrastus, and "some of them almost worship Galen" (~line 1027). Their opponents condemned them for tampering with Scripture. This group represents the first known instance of Galen's direct influence on Christian intellectual life.
