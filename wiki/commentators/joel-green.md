Joel B. Green is Professor of New Testament Interpretation at Asbury Theological Seminary (Wilmore, Kentucky), a leading Lukan scholar and one of the most methodologically innovative commentators in the NICNT series. His Ph.D. was completed at Aberdeen (1985) under I. Howard Marshall, whose influence on Luke-Acts scholarship he has inherited and redirected. His NICNT Luke (1997) represents the most complete departure from traditional redaction criticism in the entire NICNT series — it is self-consciously a post-Conzelmann, narrative-critical reading of the Third Gospel.

## Background

Green is Methodist — working within the Wesleyan-holiness theological tradition, which shares with Roman Catholicism and liberation theology a stronger emphasis on embodied, present, social dimensions of salvation than the Reformed evangelical tradition dominant elsewhere in NICNT. This theological background is congruent with and perhaps partly shapes his approach to Luke's holistic soteriology. He joins Oswalt (Isaiah, Wesleyan) and Hartley (Job, Wesleyan) as the wiki's third commentator in this tradition.

He was a founding co-editor of the *Dictionary of Jesus and the Gospels* (IVP, 1992) and has been a prolific contributor to interdisciplinary NT studies combining narrative, social-scientific, and theological methods.

## Hermeneutical Method: Discourse Analysis

Green's stated method is **"discourse analysis"** — his umbrella term for correlating narratological concerns (how the text is structured as a story) with culture-critical concerns (how the text assumes and subverts the social world of first-century Mediterranean culture). This is a deliberate departure from the dominant twentieth-century approaches:

- **Not redaction criticism**: Green makes no attempt to identify Luke's sources (Mark, Q, special L material) or to describe how Luke "changed" his sources. He treats this as both methodologically problematic and exegetically unproductive — "it is not what Luke's original readers would have done."
- **Not source criticism**: The existence of Q is presupposed but never exploited. Green does not cross-reference the Lukan version of double-tradition material against Matthew.
- **Not form criticism**: Individual pericopes are not analyzed for their Sitz im Leben in pre-Lukan communities.

**What Green does instead**: He reads Luke as a flowing narrative (co-text, intertext, context) in which each episode derives its meaning from its narrative location:

- **Co-text**: how each pericope is shaped by what immediately precedes it and what the narrative has established up to that point
- **Intertext**: how Luke draws on the LXX — explicit citations, type-scenes, allusions, linguistic echoes — as an "echo chamber" for Israel's sacred stories
- **Context**: what socio-cultural scripts (honor-shame, patron-client, purity, household codes) Luke's audience would have activated in reading each scene

This methodology has an important limitation Green acknowledges: it does not offer independent verification of historical accuracy, and it does not aspire to. "Our reading of the Third Gospel is concerned above all with the narrative side of this equation."

## Key Contributions

**Luke-Acts as one story**: Green's foundational insistence that the Gospel of Luke is incomplete without the Acts of the Apostles, and that reading Luke without Acts is like reading the first half of a sentence. The gospel anticipates the Gentile mission and the gift of the Spirit that are only realized in Acts. This reading recovers the perspective of Luke's original audience, for whom the two volumes circulated together before the canonical placement separated them.

**Theocentric narrative**: Green's most characteristic claim about Lukan theology — God is the primary "character" in Luke-Acts, not Jesus in any biographical sense. The divine purpose (δεῖ — "it is necessary") drives every plot development; the Holy Spirit is the agent through whom God's will is executed. Jesus is God's agent, not the ultimate referent of the narrative.

**The Nazareth sermon as programmatic text**: Green's close reading of Luke 4:16–30 as the master statement of Luke's theological program. The threefold definition of "the poor" (= those of low-status honor — not only economic poverty), the Jubilee content of "year of the Lord's favor" (Lev 25 + Isa 61), and the Elijah/Elisha examples (God's grace to Gentile outsiders) together provide the interpretive key to all of Luke-Acts.

**Salvation as holistic and present**: Against any reading of Luke that spiritualizes, privatizes, or defers salvation to the afterlife, Green insists on salvation as the restoration of the whole person — physical, social, economic, spiritual — occurring in the present encounter with Jesus. "Luke knows nothing of dichotomies between social and spiritual or individual and communal."

**Possessions and the kingdom**: Green's extended treatment of Luke's possessions theme shows that Luke is neither a simple renunciation mandate nor a merely spiritual metaphor. Wealth cannot be neutral; the patron-client system is subverted by kingdom economics; giving "expecting nothing in return" (6:35) is programmatically anti-reciprocity.

**Women as full disciples**: Green documents Luke's systematic integration of women as models of faithful response — Mary, Elizabeth, Anna, the women who fund the ministry (8:1–3), Mary/Martha, women at the cross and empty tomb — and argues this is not incidental but integral to the gospel's reversal theology.

**Social-scientific cultural analysis**: Green applies first-century Mediterranean social codes — honor/shame dynamics, purity systems, patron-client obligations, household economics — as interpretive tools for understanding both what the text assumes and how Jesus subverts it. Green's debt to Halvor Moxnes (*The Economy of the Kingdom*, 1988), Philip Esler (*Community and Gospel in Luke-Acts*, 1987), and Jerome Neyrey is explicit.

## Relation to Other Wiki Commentators

- **Same series (NICNT)**: France on Matthew approaches Luke's sibling Gospel through a very different method (fulfillment/OT citation analysis, redaction-critical assumption of Marcan priority). Green explicitly does not engage this. The NICNT now has two methodologically opposite commentaries on adjacent Gospels by scholars of the same generation.
- **Luke Johnson and Robert Tannehill**: Green's primary literary-critical predecessors for narrative readings of Luke-Acts; he synthesizes their work with social-scientific analysis.
- **Bock (BECNT Acts)**: Bock's commentary on Acts (Luke's second volume) takes a more traditional evangelical-historical approach, prioritizing historical reliability and Lukan authorship — methodological contrast to Green's approach to the first volume.
- **Wesleyan tradition**: Green (Luke), Oswalt (Isaiah, NICOT), Hartley (Job, NICOT) now represent three NICNT/NICOT commentators from the Wesleyan-holiness tradition, the strongest single denominational strand in the wiki after the broadly Reformed evangelical mainstream.
