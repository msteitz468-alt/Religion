# Wang Bi

**Wang Bi** (王弼, 226–249 CE) is the most important **philosophical** commentator on the
[[daodejing|Daodejing]], the leading figure of 3rd-century **[[xuanxue|Xuanxue]]**
("Dark/Profound Learning"), and one of the most precocious minds in Chinese intellectual history —
dead at 23 in the epidemic of 249.

## Major Works

- **Commentary on the Daodejing** — the standard philosophical reading of the text that shaped all
  subsequent literati reception; accompanied by the programmatic essay *Laozi weizhi lüeli* (see below).
- **Commentary on the Yijing** — a philosophical systematization of the *Book of Changes* that
  grounds the hexagrams in an ontology of *wu* (non-being).
- ***Laozi weizhi lüeli*** (老子微旨略例, "The Structure of the Laozi's Subtle Pointers") —
  Wang Bi's own methodological preface on how to read the Daodejing. Wagner (2003) calls it
  "the most important surviving Chinese philosophic treatise of the third century."

## The *Laozi weizhi lüeli* — Wang Bi's Reading Program

The LZWZLL establishes four interlocking principles:

### 1. The Dao as Featureless/Nameless Root
The Dao is *wu xing* (featureless) and *wu ming* (nameless). This negative ontology is not a
deficiency but the precondition of the Dao's universality:

> "It is generally true with regard to that by which things are created — that [things] are
> necessarily created out of the 'featureless'; and that by which achievements are brought about —
> that [achievements] are based on the 'nameless.' The featureless and nameless is [what the Laozi
> calls] the 'ancestor of the ten thousand kinds of entities.'" (LZWZLL 1.1–6; trans. Wagner)

The argument: if the Dao warmed, it could not cool; if it produced the note *gong*, it could not
produce *shang*. Only by being without any determinate feature can the Dao be the root and master
of ALL features. The Dao's "nothing" is more fecund than any "something."

### 2. The Laozi Uses "Subtle Pointers," Not Definitions
The Daodejing's paradoxes, reversals, and "I call this X" formulae are philosophically necessary.
The Dao **cannot be defined** (definition imposes a determinate form). It can only be **pointed
at**. Every chapter is a *zhi* — a "subtle pointer" toward the same unnameable center.

Wang Bi distinguishes two registers of naming:
- ***ming*** (名) — definition: the fixed essence of a thing
- ***cheng*** (稱) — inferred designation: an approximative name that gestures toward what resists definition

The Dao can only be *cheng*, never *ming*. Translations that treat *dao* as a mere name for a
doctrine have already missed the point.

### 3. The Anti-Fragmentation Rule
Wrong readings of the Laozi: (a) imposing discursive analysis on its textual patterns (*zhangju*
commentary — word-by-word paraphrase); (b) Zhuang Zun's binary Yin/Yang reading of the text's
structure; (c) reducing it to any particular school's doctrine (Daoist, Confucian, etc.).
Wang Bi's rule (trans. Wagner):

> "He who imposes discursive analysis upon the textual patterns of the Laozi will miss what he
> points at; and he who wishes to put the weight on the particular term will deviate from the
> overall meaning."

The correct reading is **implosive**: read every chapter in light of every other, all passages
converging on the single unnameable center.

### 4. Political Application
The LZWZLL embeds a theory of sage rulership. The ruler who embodies *wu* (featureless
non-being) does not impose one standard on the myriad entities. Each entity can then flourish in
its own nature (*ziran* — of itself, all on its own). Imposing one's particular will creates
dissonance; ruling from the featureless creates harmony.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Commentary-as-transformation** in its *philosophical* mode (cf. CLAUDE.md hermeneutical
framework): Wang Bi's commentary trains **metaphysical insight** — calibrating the reader to see
all determinate being as grounded in, and returning to, indeterminate non-being. This is the
opposite of [[heshang-gong|Heshang Gong]]'s bodily/longevity-cultivation reading.

**Interlocking Parallel Style (IPS)**: Wagner identifies this as Wang Bi's formal compositional
method. Claims are made in paired chains (a/b, a/b…) that mirror the ontological binary of
*you* (being) and *wu* (non-being). Reading Wang Bi without recognizing IPS produces misreadings.

## The Wang Bi Receptus Problem

A critical textual finding from Wagner (2003): the **"Wang Bi Laozi"** printed in most editions
is **not the text Wang Bi himself used**. In 79 passages, Wang Bi's commentary quotes the Laozi
differently from the superimposed printed text. The Wang Bi Laozi Receptus is a later Heshang
Gong text that was overlaid on Wang Bi's commentary during transmission.
Wagner reconstructed Wang Bi's actual working text from: Wang Bi's own commentary quotations;
the Guodian, Mawangdui, and Beijing University manuscript traditions; and Fu Yi's and Fan
Yingyuan's "Old Manuscripts." See [[wagner-wang-bi]] for the full analysis.

## Relationship to Other Commentators

- **[[heshang-gong|Heshang Gong]]**: The other great Laozi commentator — reads the same text as
  a manual for bodily cultivation and longevity. Completely different register. See
  [[wang-bi-vs-heshang-gong|the controversy]].
- **[[brook-ziporyn|Ziporyn]] (2022)**: His working text follows the Wang Bi received version as
  default, noting variants explicitly. Ziporyn's A/B structure resonates with Wang Bi's point
  about the Dao's strategic valorization of the *B* (non-being, weakness, emptiness).

## Historical Significance

Wang Bi's reading became the standard for the Chinese literati tradition and shaped:
- The Neo-Daoist movement
- The reception of Buddhism in China (rapprochement between *wu*/non-being and *śūnyatā*/emptiness)
- Neo-Confucian metaphysics (the ontological structure of *ti/yong* — substance and function)
- All subsequent philosophical commentary on the Daodejing
