# Andrew Delbanco

**Andrew Delbanco** (b. 1952) is an American literary scholar and cultural historian, long
based at Columbia University, best known to the general public for *The Death of Satan: How
Americans Have Lost the Sense of Evil* (1995) — the source ingested here. The son of
German-born Jews who fled Hitler, Delbanco writes as a self-described member of the "party
of secular liberalism" who is nonetheless unconvinced that a culture can do without some
language for evil. He is included in this wiki as a **commentator** under the project's
convention for modern trade-book authors of religious and cultural synthesis (alongside
[[karen-armstrong|Karen Armstrong]], [[stephen-prothero|Stephen Prothero]], and
[[john-hick|John Hick]]), here treating the **reception history of evil and the Devil** in
the modern West. His later *The Real American Dream* (1999) and the award-winning *Melville:
His World and Work* (2005) extend the same project.

## The Source: *The Death of Satan* (1995)

The book is "a kind of national spiritual biography": the story of how the **personified
Devil and the category of evil were gradually 'unnamed'** in America as the culture
secularized. It does not argue that evil ceased; it argues that the *symbolic and moral
language* for naming evil withered, leaving moderns confronting atrocity with "inarticulate
dread." Its structure is a declension narrative in two parts:

| Part | Chapters | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| I. **The Age of Belief** | The Old Enemy Comes to the New World; The Devil in the Age of Reason; The Birth of the Self | The Puritan Devil as a vivid daily presence → his erosion under Enlightenment reason → the Romantic relocation of moral authority into the self |
| II. **Modern Times** | The Loss of Providence; The Age of Blame; The Culture of Irony; Prospects | Providence dissolves → evil becomes a matter of "blame" and victimhood politics → the ironic sensibility makes the very idea of evil seem naïve → the closing reckoning |

It is told **through American literature** — with **Melville** as its "polar star" — and
through figures from **Jonathan Edwards** and **Emerson** to **Hawthorne**, **Lincoln**,
and the modern "culture of irony," because Delbanco takes literature to be "the most
sensitive record we have of American historical experience."

## Hermeneutical Method

Delbanco is not a theologian or a philosopher of religion arguing a doctrine; he is a
**literary-cultural historian** reading the disappearance of a religious idea. His method:

- **Cultural history through literature.** He reconstructs a culture's moral imagination
  from its imaginative writing, not its formal theology — the "dark mirror" of the national
  mind (Tocqueville).
- **The secularization narrative.** He adopts (while humanizing) Richard Rorty's compressed
  history: a culture that moved from worshipping God, to worshipping scientific truth, to
  worshipping the self, to "treat[ing] everything... as a product of time and chance." The
  loss of a transcendent order is "the central and irreversible fact of modern history."
- **Tracking dead metaphors.** He follows specific words and images ("awful," "bless you,"
  the comet as portent, the Devil himself) as they decay from urgent religious meaning into
  rote or ironic usage — a method of **lexical-cultural archaeology**.
- **Belief vs. irony.** His master-distinction for the present is not science vs. religion
  but two *sensibilities* — **belief** and **irony** — whose conflict he judges potentially
  more rancorous than any earlier cultural division.

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Evil outlives its vocabulary.** "We feel something that our culture no longer gives us
   the vocabulary to express." The crisis is semantic and moral, not a simple decline in
   wrongdoing.
2. **The personified Devil is "dead" — and this is a loss for the imagination.** Following
   Wallace Stevens's epigraph ("The death of Satan was a tragedy / For the imagination"),
   Delbanco mourns the loss of the figure even as he refuses to literally re-enchant it.
3. **Against both nostalgia and complacency.** He explicitly disavows wanting to restore the
   inquisitorial Satan "of priestcraft" (the Devil has been put to "monstrous uses"); yet he
   insists the rationalist "party" is "prematurely convinced that people can endure life
   without the old metaphors."
4. **The banality of evil and the vanishing perpetrator.** Modern bureaucratic and "blame"
   cultures (Arendt's Eichmann; the corporate diffusion of responsibility) make evil hard to
   *see* and to *name* — evil threatens to become "an epistemological problem."
5. **We cannot live without metaphor.** Against Susan Sontag's call to purify illness "of
   metaphoric thinking," Delbanco argues (with Isaiah Berlin) that the "deep metaphysical
   need" is ineradicable; even scientific description smuggles in norms and hierarchies of
   value.

## Criticisms

- **A declension narrative.** Like all secularization theses, the book can be charged with
  romanticizing the coherence of the "Age of Belief" and overstating the linearity of
  decline (Delbanco himself concedes secularization "has always been resisted").
- **America as synecdoche.** The argument generalizes from American literary culture to "the
  West"; critics question how far the U.S. case travels.
- **Elegy vs. analysis.** The book is as much a literary essay and lament as a work of
  social science, and is sometimes read as nostalgia despite its disclaimers.

the Devil** — the late, secular chapter in the long story carried by the figure page
