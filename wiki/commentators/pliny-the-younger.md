# Pliny the Younger

Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus, known as Pliny the Younger, was a Roman advocate, administrator, and man of letters whose correspondence with the emperor Trajan contains the earliest mention of the Christian movement in any Roman writer. He provides an invaluable window into how Roman officialdom first categorized and responded to Christianity.

## Career and Formation

Pliny was born c. 62 CE in Comum (near modern Milan) and entered public life in 79–80 CE. His mother's brother, the elder Pliny (author of the *Natural History*), adopted him and provided both wealth and social standing (Wilken, *Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, range ll. 50–70, hereafter cited by offset line). From his adopted uncle Pliny "inherited the family's Tuscan estate, worth 400,000 sesterces a year" (ll. 68–69).

His education was thoroughly rhetorical: private tutors, advanced study under Quintilian in Rome ("the greatest rhetorician in Roman history"), and immersion in Latin literary culture (ll. 77–91). "His greatest pleasure came from discussing the literary qualities of speeches . . . reading poetry to his wife in the evening, or spending long afternoons walking about the gardens of his villa listening to Greek or Latin verse or prose" (ll. 87–91). Pliny harbored no interest in philosophy or travel for intellectual adventure; his world was "the privileged upper class of Rome, his values those of the political and moral traditions of his ancestors" (ll. 139–141).

His career traced the conventional *cursus honorum*: advocate before the centumviral court; military tribune in Syria (the only time before his final posting he lived outside Italy); quaestor under Domitian; tribune; praetor; prefect for military finances; prefect of the state treasury; consul (100 CE, alongside his friend Cornutus Tertullus); augur (103 CE, the same priesthood once held by Cicero—a comparison Pliny explicitly savoured: "I hope I may attain to something of his genius at least in later life," Ep. 4.8, ll. 223–226); curator of the Tiber; and finally, c. 109–110 CE, imperial legate to Bithynia-Pontus (ll. 110–255).

His letters—nine books of personal correspondence plus a tenth book of official letters to Trajan—are "the chief source for Pliny's life and our surest guide to the man, the world he inhabited, and the scope of his interests" (ll. 96–98). Wilken cites Ronald Syme's characterization: Pliny "displays people in their daily pursuits or confronting the important events of upper and middle class life in a stable society characterized by ease and refinement" (ll. 100–109).

## Governorship of Bithynia-Pontus

Pliny arrived in Bithynia-Pontus on 17 September 111 CE as the emperor's personal legate—a special appointment reflecting Trajan's judgment that the province needed closer supervision than a senatorial governor could provide (ll. 34–44, 302–304). His assigned tasks: auditing city finances; examining municipal administration; suppressing political disorders; adjudicating criminal cases; assessing military needs (ll. 365–371).

A recurrent theme in his provincial letters is the problem of **associations** (*collegia*, *hetaeriae*). When a fire in Nicomedia devastated the city, Pliny proposed forming a "company of firemen" (*collegium fabrorum*), only to have Trajan reject it: "If people assemble for a common purpose, whatever name we give them and for whatever reason, they soon turn into a **political club** (*hetaeria*)" (Ep. 10.34, ll. 437–445). Wilken notes: "The term used in this letter for 'club,' hetaeria, is the same word Pliny was to use later when he wrote to Trajan about the Christians" (ll. 446–448). At Amisus, dealing with "benefit societies" (*eranos*), Pliny again deferred to Trajan's general suspicion of private associations, though Trajan allowed the Amisene societies given the city's treaty privileges (ll. 490–520).

## The Letter on Christians (Ep. 10.96)

Pliny's encounter with Christians occurred in a coastal city of northern Pontus in the fall of 112 CE, probably prompted by complaints from local merchants—likely butchers and others in the sacrificial trade—who noted that business was poor because "people were not making sacrifices" (ll. 39–43). "Toward the end of the letter, written after Pliny had dealt with the problem, he observed that the 'flesh of sacrificial victims is on sale everywhere, though up till recently scarcely anyone could be found to buy it'" (ll. 41–43).

Pliny was candid about his ignorance: "I have never been present at an examination of Christians. Consequently, I do not know the nature or the extent of the punishments usually meted out to them, nor the grounds for starting an investigation" (Ep. 10.96, ll. 64–74).

### Procedure

Pliny used the simplified *cognitio extra ordinem* procedure—no jury, no multiple judges, the governor alone hearing and adjudicating (ll. 307–315). He asked each accused three times whether they were Christian, warning that a yes answer meant death. Those who admitted it he executed. Those who denied it were required to:
1. Repeat a "formula of invocation to the gods"
2. Offer wine and incense to a statue of the emperor Trajan (alongside statues of Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva)
3. "Revile the name of Christ" (ll. 391–398)

Roman citizens in the group were exempt from summary execution and were instead imprisoned to be sent to Rome for trial (ll. 333–340).

### What Pliny Found

Contrary to his expectation of criminal "crimes" (*flagitia*), Pliny discovered only "a superstition" (*superstitio*), a foreign cult (l. 276). Arrested Christians described their practice:

> "They had met regularly before dawn on a fixed day to chant verses alternately among themselves in honor of Christ as if to a god, and also to bind themselves by oath, not for any criminal purpose, but to abstain from theft, robbery, and adultery, to commit no breach of trust and not to deny a deposit when called upon to restore it. After this ceremony it had been their custom to disperse and reassemble later to take food of an ordinary harmless kind." (Ep. 10.96, ll. 267–275)

Wilken observes that Pliny's note that the food was "ordinary harmless kind" implies "rumors were already circulating" about sinister Christian rites (ll. 244–246), though the accusations of ritual murder and promiscuity found in later sources (Minucius Felix, Epiphanius) do not appear in pagan writers and may have spread from fringe groups such as the Carpocratians (ll. 176–238).

### How Pliny Categorized Christianity

Pliny employed two key Roman administrative-religious categories:

- **Superstitio**: a depraved, foreign superstition ("a depraved superstition carried to extravagant lengths," later in the letter). See [[superstitio]].
- **Hetaeria** / political club: the same term used for Nicomedia's proposed firemen's association. "All Pliny found was a superstition, a foreign cult" (l. 276). The Christians had abandoned their meetings after Pliny's edict banning all *hetaeriae* in the province (Ep. 10.96, ll. 60–65 in Wilken's rendering).

Pliny treated the Christians as an independent sect: "There is no hint that the Christians had anything to do with Jews or that they came from Jewish background. It is likely that some were converted Jews, but Pliny treated the Christians as an independent sect" (ll. 300–302).

On the social composition of the group: "The majority of the group came no doubt from humble backgrounds, freedmen and slaves, working people engaged in menial tasks, artisans" (ll. 303–305).

### Pliny's Uncertainty and Trajan's Rescript

Pliny was unsure whether "the mere name of Christian . . . is punishable, even if innocent of crime, or rather the crimes (*flagitia*) associated with the name" is the ground for punishment (Ep. 10.96, ll. 318–320). He ultimately acted on the principle that the name alone was sufficient. His stated grounds were also contumacy: "I am convinced that their stubbornness and unshakeable obstinacy ought not to go unpunished" (ll. 324–326).

Trajan's rescript (Ep. 10.97) endorsed Pliny's approach while setting limits: Christians "must not be hunted out; if they are brought before you and the charge against them is proved, they must be punished, but in the case of anyone who denies that he is a Christian, and makes it clear that he is not by offering prayers to our gods, he is to be pardoned" (ll. 488–495). Anonymous pamphlets naming Christians were to play no part in any accusation. Wilken summarizes the logic: the Christians are "not to be sought out" but must be punished if brought and proven.

### The Offering Test and Roman Religious Practice

The "test" of offering wine and incense to the emperor's image was unusual—Wilken notes "the procedure followed by Pliny has few, if any, real precedents in Roman history" (ll. 406–407). He traces its background to the Roman practice of *supplicationes* (unbloody offerings of wine and incense on national occasions), which became regularized under Augustus: "On the first day that he entered the senate after the death of Augustus, [Tiberius] offered sacrifice . . . with incense and wine" (ll. 435–437). The innovation in Pliny's action was "the use of a ritual act of offering incense and wine as a **test of religious allegiance**" (ll. 464–465). He may have drawn on proceedings under Domitian (the condemnation of Flavia Domitilla and Flavius Clemens on a charge involving "atheism" possibly linked to Judaism, ll. 467–470).

## Hermeneutical Note

Pliny did not study Christianity; he processed it through Roman administrative-religious categories. His framework was that of a conservative senatorial official whose intellectual horizon was "Latin rhetorical education" (ll. 140–141) and whose conception of religion was intrinsically civic: "In Rome the practice of religion was a public matter" (ll. 214–215). When he encountered the Christians, he automatically mapped them onto existing Roman typologies—foreign cult, private association, potential *superstitio*—without attempting to understand Christian self-description. This makes his letter a document of Roman *perception*, not Christian reality.

## Legacy and Death

Pliny died in office c. 113 CE, before returning to Rome, probably between January and September of that year (ll. 521–530). He was succeeded by Cornutus Tertullus, his longtime friend. His public inscription records a career of impressive civic generosity: public baths, a library endowment, support for 100 freedmen, an annual dinner for the people of Comum (ll. 543–564).
