Peter J. Leithart is a Reformed theologian, writer, and president of the Theopolis Institute (Birmingham, Alabama). He earned his doctorate from Cambridge (Jesus College) and is known for combining traditional Reformed theology with patristic and liturgical interests — a position sometimes called "Reformed Catholicism" or "paleo-orthodoxy." He has published extensively on typology, Christology, and political theology. His commentary on 1–2 Chronicles (Brazos, 2019) completes a trilogy of OT historical commentaries following 1–2 Samuel (*A Son to Me*, Canon Press) and 1–2 Kings (Brazos, 2006).

Leithart is associated with the Federal Vision movement (though he does not embrace that label), the liturgical theology of James B. Jordan, and a broadly "catholic" approach to Protestant exegesis. The Brazos series editors include a deliberately ecumenical roster (Reformed, Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran), and Leithart's own commentary reflects engagement across patristic and medieval tradition.

## Background: The Brazos Theological Commentary Series

Leithart writes in the **Brazos Theological Commentary on the Bible** (BTC), which represents the most radical methodological departure from the academic commentary traditions in the wiki. The BTC premise:

- **Doctrine clarifies rather than obscures**: Against Jowett's claim that doctrine distorts exegesis, the series argues that the Nicene tradition provides the proper basis for reading scripture.
- **Theologians, not philologists**: Commentators were chosen for doctrinal formation, not biblical scholarship. "War is too important to leave to the generals."
- **Post-critical doctrinal interpretation**: The series is explicitly "not anti-critical but post-critical" — after the historical-critical project, not before it. The editors acknowledge uncertainty about how doctrine and historical-critical method interact; the series is "exploratory."
- **Ecumenical**: Series editors include Reformed, Roman Catholic, Anglican, and Lutheran voices.

This means Leithart's commentary has **no survey of secondary literature, no detailed text-critical apparatus, no engagement with the synoptic problem** of Chronicles vs. Samuel-Kings. It is a theological reading aimed at making Chronicles preachable.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Typological/Figural Reading

Leithart's primary method. Every episode, character, and structural feature is read for its typological significance within the canonical whole. His central claim: the Chronicler has structured his history as a **recapitulation of all prior OT history** — genealogies = Genesis, Saul's death = Egyptian slavery, David = Moses, Solomon = Joshua, divided kingdom = Judges, Cyrus = new Davidic commission. This is not imposed from outside; Leithart traces specific verbal and thematic connections the Chronicler himself built into the narrative.

### 2. Chiastic Structure Analysis

Leithart identifies chiastic (AXB) and concentrically structured patterns at every scale: individual verses, passages, chapters, entire sections. He uses these patterns to identify theological centers of emphasis. The temple dedication (2 Chr 5–7) has nested chiasms at three levels; the genealogy (1 Chr 1–9) is a large chiasm with Levites at the structural center; the organization of temple personnel (1 Chr 23–27) is organized architecturally, mirroring movement from court to inner sanctuary.

### 3. New-Creation Theology

Leithart consistently reads major events in the historical books through the lens of Genesis 1-2. Temple construction = a new creation; its completion (*kalah/shalam*) echoes Gen 2:1-4; the fire from heaven at dedication = Pentecostal consecration of the new world. The Levitical division (*badal*) of personnel in 1 Chr 25 recapitulates God's creative divisions in Genesis 1. This approach owes much to G.K. Beale's temple-cosmos theology and James Jordan's liturgical cosmology.

### 4. Ecclesiological Application

Leithart consistently draws the line from Chronicles to the NT church. The assembly (*qahal*) of Israel = the assembled church; the Levitical officers = church offices; the musicians = the singing congregation. The Chronicler's institutional detail is ecclesiologically instructive: "Churches sometimes denigrate the more material dimensions of the church's ministry, but the Chronicler teaches us that the church secretary or administrative pastor is as essential to God's work as preachers and teachers."

### 5. Christological Genealogies

Leithart develops a distinctive reading of the genealogies' orientation. Biblical genealogies are eschatological, not protological — they trace what a person *produces*, not merely where they came from. The Chronicler's phrase "heads of the fathers" (used of sons) hints that sons are heads *of* their fathers — the son's being actualizes the father's paternity. Stated christologically: the Son is "head of the Father" (in the Chronicler's sense) because without the eternal Son, the Father would not be Father. The genealogies ultimately trace a line to the last Adam, Jesus, who is the "head" of all the fathers.

## Key Theological Positions

**Cyrus as new David**: The Chronicler's typological pattern (Judges → new David) leads the reader to expect a Davidic king after Josiah. Instead Cyrus the Persian inherits the Davidic commission. This is not a failure of the covenant but its transfer. Isaiah 44-45 grounds the claim prophetically; the Chronicler's verbal parallels between Cyrus's speech and David's make it explicit. Chronicles narrates the transition from the Davidic world-order to the Gentile oikoumenē that provides the stage for the Messiah.

**Music as priestly-royal-prophetic**: The most extended contribution of the commentary. Levitical song is priestly (offering breath as sacrifice), royal (singing = dominion over body and creation), and prophetic (singing can be prophecy; 1 Chr 25:1). God created Adam to produce a race of singers. Israel's calling = to be God's choir. The musicians' structural centrality in both the genealogies (1 Chr 1-9 centers on Levitical singers) and the temple organization (1 Chr 25 at the center of chs. 23-27) signals this is Israel's fundamental vocation.

**The temple as axis mundi and house of prayer**: Solomon's temple is where heaven and earth intersect. Solomon's dedicatory prayer (2 Chr 6) is read as a theological masterpiece — the chiastic structure shows that the center of the prayer is Yahweh's eyes and ears open to the temple; the expansion of prayer from temple outward (house → city → far countries → exile) depicts the temple as a prayer amplifier connecting earth to the God who transcends heaven itself.

**David's bloodshed as land-pollution**: The Chronicles explanation for David's exclusion from temple-building (not in Samuel-Kings) is read through Num 31:19-20 and 35:33-34. Killing in war generates corpse-defilement that requires atoning cleansing (*shaphak dam*); if this pollution is not cleansed, YHWH threatens to abandon the land. This is not a moral condemnation of David's wars but a legal-cultic matter: the temple must not have blood-pollution built into its foundations.

**"Sapiential imperialism"**: Solomon's rule extends Israel's territory to Abrahamic boundaries (Gen 15:18), but the Gentile kings submit *voluntarily*, bringing tribute and seeking his wisdom. This is the church's vocation: "Something greater than Solomon is here, the eternal Wisdom of God in flesh... As we keep step with the Spirit, we expect kings to start knocking on our doors, bearing gifts and looking for help."

**The Chronicler is not whitewashing David**: Despite deleting the Bathsheba narrative, the Chronicler depicts David's census-sin as *equivalent* to adultery and murder — "he seizes the host of Yahweh and treats it as if it were his own," analogous to seizing a man's wife. The sin is a "rape of the bride of Yahweh." This is theological condensation, not sanitizing.
