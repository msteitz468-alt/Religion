# Madhva

**Madhvacharya** is the founder of [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita Vedanta]] (dualism), the third of the three
classical Vedanta commentators alongside [[shankara|Shankara]] and [[ramanuja|Ramanuja]]. Now grounded on
[[dasgupta-history-of-indian-philosophy|Dasgupta's *History of Indian Philosophy*]], Vol. IV (*Indian
Pluralism*) — Madhva's own *Brahma-Sūtra-bhāṣya* still having no complete free English translation.

## Life and Names

Born near **Uḍupi** in the **Tuḷuva** country (coastal Karnataka — still the heartland of Madhvism),
Madhva was a disciple of **Acyutaprekṣa**, from whom he received the initiation name **Pūrṇaprajña** and
later **Ānanda-tīrtha**; he is also called **Nanditīrtha** and **Vāsudeva**. His tradition regards him as
an **incarnation of Vāyu** (the wind-god), sent to destroy [[shankara|Śaṅkara's]] doctrine — which the
hagiography *Maṇi-mañjarī* casts as a demon's covert Buddhism. (Mādhva tradition more fully counts him the
*third* avatāra of Vāyu, after Hanumān and Bhīma — a detail not in Dasgupta's account.)
He toured South and North India debating Advaitins, met **Vyāsa** at Badarī (by tradition), and is said to
have refuted **twenty-one prior Brahma-Sūtra commentaries**.

> **Dating dispute** (Contradiction Protocol): Dasgupta — following Bhandarkar and Grierson, from the
> Maṭha lists and the Narahari-tīrtha inscriptions — places Madhva at **saka 1119–1198 = c. 1197–1276 CE**
> (lived 79 years). The also-current traditional dating is **1238–1317**. Both are recorded; neither is
> adjudicated here.

## Major Works

Dasgupta enumerates **thirty-seven works**. The pillars: the **[[brahma-sutras|*Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya*]]**
and its independent restatement the ***Anuvyākhyāna***; a **[[bhagavad-gita|*Gītā-bhāṣya*]]** and
*Gītā-tātparya-nirṇaya*; *bhāṣyas* on ten principal [[upanishads|Upaniṣads]]; an **Ṛg-bhāṣya** (on
Ṛgveda I.1–40); the *Mahābhārata-tātparya-nirṇaya* and *Bhāgavata-tātparya-nirṇaya*; and a cluster of
polemical tracts (*Māyāvāda-khaṇḍana*, *Tattva-viveka*, *Viṣṇu-tattva-nirṇaya*, the *Dvādaśa-stotra*).

## Characteristic Positions

The **fivefold eternal difference** ([[panchabheda|*pañca-bheda*]]): God–soul, God–matter, soul–soul,
soul–matter, matter–matter are all real and beginningless. Beneath it lies a twofold ontology — the
**independent** (*svatantra*) = **[[brahman|Brahman]] = Viṣṇu** (Nārāyaṇa/Vāsudeva), the sole self-standing
reality, "the fullness of infinite qualities" — and the **dependent** (*asvatantra*) = souls and matter,
fully real but never self-standing. Souls fall into **three classes** — those *mukti-yogya* (fit for
emancipation: gods, sages), the transmigrating, and a third class Dasgupta describes as "the demons,
ghosts and the like," the worst **cursed eternally in hell** (later Mādhva terminology: *tamo-yogya*) — so
Madhva uniquely affirms an **eternal damnation** singular in Indian thought. The path is **bhakti**;
[[moksha|liberation]] is graded, blissful service — never identity.

## Hermeneutical Method

Strict realism, fought out *adhikaraṇa by adhikaraṇa* against Śaṅkara (Dasgupta, Ch. XXVI). Madhva's
master move is the **_samanvaya_** sūtra (BS I.1.4): *every* word of the Upaniṣads ultimately denotes
**Viṣṇu**, so across the contested terms (*ākāśa*, *prāṇa*, *jyotiḥ*, *sat*, *akṣara*…) where Śaṅkara
reads "Brahman/Īśvara," Madhva reads "Viṣṇu specifically" — his *vidvad-rūḍhi*, the learned convention by
which "Brahman" always means Viṣṇu. On the identity-texts (*tat tvam asi*; *satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ
brahma*), his lever in Dasgupta's account is **semantic, not grammatical**: because the texts *admit a
direct (mukhyārtha) reading on the basis of difference*, the Advaitin's non-difference reading — which
must resort to figurative *lakṣaṇā* — is illegitimate. (The popularly-cited *re-parsing* of *tat tvam
asi* into *atat tvam asi*, "thou art *not* that," is a separate later-Mādhva move and is **not** the
argument Dasgupta reports here.)

## Controversies and Influence

The realist pole of [[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the Advaita/Vishishtadvaita/Dvaita triad]]; accused
his opponents (especially Advaita) of disguised nihilism. His school became a sharp dialectical
tradition: **Jaya-tīrtha** (*Tattva-prakāśikā*, *Nyāya-sudhā*) and **Vyāsa-tīrtha** systematized Dvaita
logic, and Vyāsa-tīrtha's *Nyāyāmṛta* provoked the great Advaitin reply, **Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's
*Advaita-siddhi*** — the classic **dualist–monist controversy**. Madhva also inspired the Haridāsa
*bhakti* singers of Karnataka.
