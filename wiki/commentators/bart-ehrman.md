# Bart D. Ehrman

A prominent American New Testament scholar and historian of early Christianity,
writing from an explicitly **historical-critical** standpoint. In the wiki he is
represented chiefly through [[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]
(2014), his developmental account of early Christology.

## Biographical and Methodological Stance

Ehrman describes himself as "a historian of early Christianity, who for nearly
three decades has studied the New Testament and the rise of the Christian religion
from a historical perspective." A **former evangelical believer** (he recounts
having confessed the Nicene Creed and pondered John 1:1–2,14 as the "mystery of
faith"), he writes that "I had changed over the years, and now in middle age I am
no longer a believer." His scholarly question is "the precise opposite" of his
believing friend's: not *how God became a man* (theology) but **how a man became
God** (history).

He explicitly **brackets theological truth claims** — "I do not take a stand on
the theological question of Jesus's divine status" — aiming his work at "secular
historians of religion … but also … believers." On miracles such as the
resurrection he holds that "historical sciences simply are of no help in
establishing exactly what happened," because history can work only from
presuppositions shared by investigators of every faith. He distinguishes "the
past" (everything that happened) from "history" (what can be established by shared
evidence).

## Hermeneutical Method

- **Historical-critical / criteria of authenticity.** For reconstructing the
  historical [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]] he applies independent attestation
  (Mark, Q, M, L, John as largely independent streams), **dissimilarity** (traditions
  cutting against Christian interest are likely authentic — e.g. Jesus's baptism by
  [[john-the-baptist|John]]), and **contextual credibility** (sayings must fit a
  first-century Palestinian Jewish setting).
- **Form criticism / tradition-history.** He recovers **pre-literary creeds, hymns,
  and poems** embedded in the New Testament (Rom 1:3–4; Phil 2:6–11; the Johannine
  prologue; 1 Cor 15:3–5) by their poetic structure, anomalous vocabulary, theology
  differing from the embedding author, and removability — dating some to "within a
  decade" of the resurrection belief. He stresses this is "not simply a 'skeptical'
  view," citing the Catholic scholar Raymond Brown.
- **Redaction and text criticism.** He distinguishes an author's own theology from
  inherited tradition (e.g. Luke's birth Christology vs. the resurrection-exaltation
  traditions Luke quotes) and uses manuscript variants to recover earlier readings
  (e.g. Luke 3:22).
- **History-of-doctrine / [[bauer-thesis|Bauer-thesis]] framing.** Orthodoxy and
  heresy are treated as historical, not theological, categories — "a 'heresy' … is
  simply a view that lost the debate."

## Characteristic Positions

- The confession of Jesus as divine is **early** (not a fourth-century invention)
  but **developmental**: Jesus "was not originally considered to be God in any sense
  at all" and "eventually became divine for his followers."
- The historical Jesus was an **apocalyptic prophet** who did not call himself God.
- The engine of Christology was the disciples' **belief in the resurrection**,
  grounded in **visionary experiences** — a belief the historian can establish even
  though the event itself lies beyond historical proof.
- Earliest Christology was **[[exaltation-christology|exaltation]]** ("from below"),
  progressively pushed back toward **[[incarnation|incarnation]]** ("from above").

## Influence and Reception

Ehrman is a leading popularizer of mainstream critical scholarship and a frequent
foil for conservative apologists (he engages [[larry-hurtado|Larry Hurtado]],
N. T. Wright, Michael Licona, and Richard Bauckham). His [[bauer-thesis|diversity-first]]
account of early Christianity aligns with the trajectory associated with Walter
Bauer; he is contested by scholars such as [[michael-kruger|Michael Kruger]]
([[kruger-christianity-at-the-crossroads|*Christianity at the Crossroads*]]).
