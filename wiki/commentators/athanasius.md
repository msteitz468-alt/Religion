# Athanasius of Alexandria

**Athanasius** (c. 296/298 – 373 CE), bishop of [[alexandria|Alexandria]] from 328, was the
foremost defender of Nicene orthodoxy in the fourth century and one of the most influential of the
Greek Fathers. His later epitaph — *Athanasius contra mundum*, "Athanasius against the world" —
captures his decades of conflict with the [[arianism|Arian]] party, which saw him exiled five times.
This page is based so far on his early treatise **[[on-the-incarnation|*On the Incarnation*]]**
(c. 318); his anti-Arian corpus (the *Orations against the Arians*, *De Decretis*, the *Letters to
Serapion*) is not yet ingested.

## Biographical Context

Athanasius was formed in the church of Alexandria, the leading center of Greek Christian learning
(the catechetical school of Pantaenus, Clement, and Origen). He attended the **Council of Nicaea
(325)** as a young deacon and secretary to bishop Alexander, and succeeded him as bishop in 328. His
episcopate was dominated by the struggle over the relation of the Son to the Father; his repeated
depositions and exiles, and his eventual vindication, made him the symbol of the pro-Nicene cause
that triumphed at Constantinople (381). *(Biographical frame beyond the treatise drawn from the
wiki's [[alexandria]] and [[arianism]] pages; the C. S. Lewis introduction bound with* On the
Incarnation *supplies the* contra mundum *characterization and the note that the "Athanasian Creed"
is neither a creed nor by Athanasius.)*

## Hermeneutical Method

In *On the Incarnation* Athanasius reads Scripture **prophetically and typologically** within a
**[[logos-christology|theology of the Word]]**:
- The Old Testament is a system of **shadows and types** fulfilled in Christ — once "the Truth had
  come, what further need was there of the shadow?" (§40). Against the Jews he argues *entirely from
  their own Scriptures* (Isa 7, Isa 53, Ps 22, Dan 9's seventy weeks), treating fulfilled prophecy
  as decisive proof.
- **Creation itself is a text**: the ordered cosmos reveals the Word who made it, so that natural
  apologetics ("from reason and from facts") runs parallel to scriptural argument against the Greeks.
- A strong **hermeneutic of spiritual prerequisite** (§57): "for the searching and right
  understanding of the Scriptures there is need of a good life and a pure soul"; one must "cleanse
  his own life, and approach the saints by copying their deeds" to grasp the writers' meaning.

## Characteristic Positions

- **Creation *ex nihilo* through the Word**, against Epicurean chance, Platonic pre-existent matter,
  and the [[gnosticism|Gnostic]] Demiurge (*On the Incarnation* §§2–3).
- **The "Divine Dilemma"** — the tension between God's goodness (which will not let his creatures
  perish) and his truthfulness (which will not revoke the sentence of death) — resolved only by the
  Word's assuming a body and dying "instead of all" (§§6–10). See [[atonement]], [[incarnation]].
- **The physical/participatory model of redemption**: humanity's solidarity in the defaced
  [[imago-dei|Image]] is restored when the Word, indwelling one body, makes it a "sufficient exchange
  for all" and re-draws the portrait on the same panel (§§9, 14).
- **Deification (theosis)**: the famous exchange formula, "He, indeed, assumed humanity that we might
  become God" (§54) — unpacked as incorruption and immortality, not merger of essences. See
  [[theosis-divine-nature]].
- **"Instrument/temple" Christology**: the body is the Word's *organon* and temple (§§8–9, 20), a
  hallmark of the Alexandrian tradition.

## Known Controversies

Athanasius is the great opponent of **[[arianism|Arianism]]**, defending the Son's full divinity and
the *homoousion* ("of one substance with the Father") of Nicaea against Arius and his successors. *On
the Incarnation*, however, is an **early work that predates the open controversy**: its declared
opponents are Jews and Gentiles, not Arians, and it nowhere argues the *homoousion*. Its later use as
ammunition for the Nicene cause is a matter of reception, not of the treatise's own polemic — a point
worth keeping distinct (Contradiction Protocol: do not retroject the mature controversy onto the
early text).

## Influence on Later Tradition

Athanasius is a primary patristic source for **Eastern Orthodox [[theosis-divine-nature|theosis]]**
and a fixture of Western Christology; later scholastic treatments of the [[incarnation]] and
[[atonement]] (e.g., [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]) inherit the "God became man that man might become
God" topos through Augustine and the conciliar tradition. He is also remembered for promoting
**monasticism** (his *Life of Antony*, not yet ingested) and for his **Festal Letter of 367**, an
early witness to the 27-book New Testament canon (not yet ingested).

## Ayres on Athanasius (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) credits Athanasius with
the **rhetorical creation of "Arianism."** His *Orations Against the Arians* (c. 339–345) "sold
to his readers" the sprawling controversy "as a conspiracy by a group of 'Arians' seeking to
perpetuate the novel theology of a condemned heretic" — a "masterpiece of rhetorical art," not
an accurate description, lumping in theologians (e.g. Asterius) "whose theologies were distinct
from Arius'." Ayres calls this "a great difference between an attempt to shape ecclesiastical
affairs in the light of a theological trajectory and… an overt conspiracy to perpetuate the
theology of Arius."

Athanasius's positive contribution was the slow defence of Nicaea's language: in *De decretis*
(c. 353–356) he reads *[[homoousion|homoousios]]* as a cipher for the Son being "from God,"
grounded in [[divine-simplicity|divine simplicity]] and the Creator/creation boundary — though
"he never bothers to claim this terminology as credal; such a style of reference did not yet
exist." Ayres places him among the [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene]] forerunners (with
[[hilary-of-poitiers|Hilary]]) who "show many of the features of these later theologies but not
all"; his *De synodis* reached out to the [[homoiousians|Homoiousians]]. On Ayres's account,
direct Athanasian influence on the [[cappadocian-fathers|Cappadocians]] "is difficult to prove"
— against the older "one original Nicene theology" narrative.
