# I. Howard Marshall

British New Testament scholar; one of the leading evangelical NT exegetes of the late twentieth century. Graduate of the Universities of Aberdeen and Cambridge; also studied under Joachim Jeremias at Göttingen. Long career at the University of Aberdeen, where he specialized in NT exegesis.

## Biographical Context

Marshall's scholarly career is primarily organized around Lukan studies (Luke: Historian and Theologian, 1970; NIGTC Luke) and Johannine studies (The Epistles of John, 1978). He also produced important works in NT theology, particularly on the doctrine of perseverance (*Kept by the Power of God*, 1969) and NT Christology (*The Origins of New Testament Christology*, 1976). The NICNT Johannine Epistles volume was written at the invitation of series editor F.F. Bruce.

## Primary Work Ingested

**I. Howard Marshall, *The Epistles of John*, New International Commentary on the New Testament (NICNT), Eerdmans, 1978.**

Series context: NICNT (Eerdmans) — evangelical commentary series combining scholarly rigor with pastoral intent; English-text base (Marshall uses the NIV). Series editors at time of publication: F.F. Bruce (1962–1990).

Notable structural choice: Marshall reverses the canonical order and treats 2 and 3 John before 1 John, on the grounds that the shorter letters are easier to grasp and illuminate 1 John when read first. He was also concerned to rescue 2 and 3 John from being treated as appendices.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Framework**: Evangelical historical-grammatical exegesis with a consistent pastoral-theological orientation. Marshall engages Continental European scholarship (Schnackenburg, Bultmann, Haenchen, Conzelmann, Klein) substantively and critically, mediating it for English readers without being captured by any single methodological school.

**Authorship stance**: Defends common authorship of Gospel and Epistles (or at least very close proximity). On "the elder": probably John the apostle known as "the elder" given his age and revered status — or a close follower. Rejects Revelation as being by the same author as the Epistles on linguistic grounds.

**Approach to the opponents**: The secessionists held views related to Docetism and Cerinthianism — specifically, denial of a lasting union between the divine Christ and the human Jesus, and a corresponding denial of the saving significance of the cross. The Docetic/Cerinthian identification is not exact (Schnackenburg's objections are noted) but represents the closest known parallel. Rejects Kasemann's reversal (that the elder was the Gnostic, Diotrephes the orthodox defender).

**Approach to the structural problem of 1 John**: Rejects all large-scale outlines as forcing the text. The epistle is "a series of connected paragraphs governed by association of ideas" — spiral or chiastic patterns are suggestive but never watertight. Follows Schnackenburg on major paragraph divisions.

**Approach to the sinlessness paradox**: The 3:6/9 passages describe the habitual directional orientation of the person born of God; sin is incompatible with that orientation but individual acts of sin remain real (hence 2:1–2). The 1:8/10 passages address the secessionist claim to sinlessness.

**Propitiation vs. expiation**: Defends *hilasmos* (1 John 2:2; 4:10) as propitiation (turning away divine wrath) against Dodd's expiation reading. "God is a righteous judge as well as a loving Father, and he cannot be indifferent to sin; hence there must be something that corresponds to the wrath of a righteous God and the removal of that wrath."

**Spirit and truth**: The anointing (*chrisma*, 2:20, 27) refers primarily to the Holy Spirit who has implanted the truth in believers, not to the truth itself or to the Word as such.

## Characteristic Positions

- Eyewitness claim in 1:1–4 is to be taken at face value; the author claims direct sensory contact with the incarnate Word.
- 1 John's purpose is *assurance*, not self-testing: John is not asking his readers to test whether they qualify for eternal life but assuring them that they do qualify.
- "Water and blood" (5:6) is specifically anti-Cerinthian: Christ came not only by baptism (water) but also by the atoning crucifixion (blood), against the view that the divine Christ departed before the cross.
- The three "tests of life" (right belief, righteousness, love) function as interconnected marks of authentic Christianity, not as independent criteria.
- Diotrephes (3 John 9–10) is an ecclesiastical problem, not a theological heretic. He represents the early development of local monarchical episcopacy against the elder's itinerant apostolic-type oversight.
- Hostility toward false teachers (2 John 10–11: refusing hospitality) is addressed to the community, not to individuals, and pertains to providing a base for their mission — not to withdrawing all love.

## Quoted Observation

Luther on 1 John (cited by Marshall as epigraph to the commentary's "Invitation"): "I have never read a book written in simpler words than this one, and yet the words are inexpressible." The observation captures the exegetical challenge Marshall takes up: the simplicity of Johannine vocabulary (303 different words in all three letters) conceals profound theological depth.

## Relation to Other Commentators

- **Kruse (PNTC, 2020)**: [[colin-kruse|Kruse]] is the other ingested commentator on the same letters. Kruse's second-edition work cites Marshall extensively; both are evangelical, both defend apostolic authorship, both read the eyewitness language literally. Kruse goes further in providing excursuses on 24 exegetical topics, a more detailed rhetorical analysis, and a fuller engagement with scholarship from 1978 to 2020.
- **Schnackenburg**: Marshall's most important German interlocutor; his Herder commentary (in German) is "the fundamental work for the serious student." Marshall frequently engages Schnackenburg critically.
- **Bultmann**: Noted but found to have little fresh to offer beyond Schnackenburg; his source-critical theories for 1 John (a "revelatory discourse" source + ecclesiastical redactor) are rejected by Marshall as methodologically insufficiently grounded.
