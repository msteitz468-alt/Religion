# John Scotus Eriugena

**John Scotus Eriugena** was the Carolingian philosopher-theologian who first made the
[[pseudo-dionysius|Dionysian corpus]] available to the Latin West and built its principles into
his own speculative system. He is the single most important channel by which Dionysian apophatic
theology entered Western Christian thought.

> **Scope note.** This page is grounded in the reception essay appended to
> [[rolt-dionysius|Rolt's edition]] of Dionysius — W. J. Sparrow-Simpson, *The Influence of
> Dionysius in Religious History* (1920) — which treats Eriugena chiefly as an *expositor of
> Dionysius*. A dedicated ingest of Eriugena's own works (*Periphyseon / De Divisione Naturae*)
> would warrant a fuller page.

## Role in the Transmission of Dionysius

The Greek writings of the Areopagite had been sent to the Gallican Church by Pope Paul in 757
and lay "for nearly a century unread in the Abbey of St. Denis." At the request of **Charles the
Bald**, Eriugena translated all four principal treatises into Latin — the act that "in reality
popularized Dionysius for Latin Christendom" (Sparrow-Simpson). His translations made the Greek
Master's principles familiar to the West and fed them into [[thomas-aquinas|Scholasticism]] and
the monastic mystical tradition (e.g. [[hugh-of-saint-victor|Hugh of St. Victor]]).

## Hermeneutical Method and the *De Divisione Naturae*

Eriugena did "vastly more than merely act as translator": he incorporated Dionysian principles
into his treatise **_De Divisione Naturae_** (the *Periphyseon*), a work as representative of his
mind "as the *De Principiis* is for Origen or the *Summa* for St. Thomas." Cast as a dialogue
between Master and Disciple, it attempts to reconcile theology with philosophy on a Dionysian
basis:

- **Divine incomprehensibility as first principle.** "We can only say that God *is*; but what He
  is we are unable to affirm." Since the First Cause cannot be understood, it cannot be defined.
- **Affirmative vs. negative theology** (taken directly from Dionysius): negative theology denies
  predicates of God; affirmative asserts them. But even the "divinest" attributes — goodness,
  truth, justice, wisdom — are only "figuratively transferred from man to Deity." Strictly, God
  is not goodness but *plus quam bonitas* / *super bonus*, not truth but *plus quam veritas*, and
  so on. Negations are "more correct than our affirmations."
- **The Absolute beyond Being.** Eriugena interprets *essentia* as involving spatial/temporal
  relations, which cannot apply to God; "Deity as Erigena contemplates it is simply the Infinite
  and the Absolute." He "did not hesitate to deny Being to Deity" — Being is "a defect," and God
  "in virtue of His excellence, is not undeservedly described as *Nihil* — nothingness."
- **Authority and reason.** Scripture furnishes "certain symbols and signs, by condescension to
  our infirmities"; "true authority does not contradict right reason, nor right reason true
  authority," both springing from one Divine source. Yet, even while professing the speculative
  method, Eriugena "falls back on the authority of Dionysius" — a measure of the weight he gave
  the Areopagite.

## Controversy: Christian or Pantheist?

Eriugena's exposition pushed the Dionysian doctrine to the edge of, or over into,
**pantheism**, and his standing in his own age was uneasy: "He was by no means a *persona grata*
to the Church of his age," "a metaphysician, without the mystical tendencies of Dionysius," who
"roused suspicion and resentment by the boldness of his conclusions."

The reception essay reports **Theodor Christlieb's** assessment that Eriugena's reconciliation of
theology with philosophy "ends in the supremacy of the latter, and in the abolition of the
essential characteristics of the Christian Revelation":

- His conception of Deity "precludes any firm hold on the Divine self-consciousness."
- On his principles "the doctrine of the Trinity is not really tenable" — the Persons "have no
  actual substantial existence whatever… mere names and not realities"; God is "neither unity nor
  trinity, but an incomprehensible somewhat which transcends them both."
- "The teaching of Dionysius in the exposition of Erigena became scarcely distinguishable from
  Pantheism."

Eriugena thus raises in acute form the question that recurs throughout the Dionysian reception:
whether **the Absolute is the proper conception of Deity**, or whether the God of religion is
"rather spirit, self-consciousness, and perfect personality." See the
[[dionysius-christian-or-neoplatonist|Christian-or-Neoplatonist controversy]] and
[[apophatic-theology|apophatic theology]].
