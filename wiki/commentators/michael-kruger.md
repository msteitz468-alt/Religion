# Michael J. Kruger

## Biographical Context

Michael J. Kruger is President and Samuel C. Patterson Professor of New Testament and Early Christianity at Reformed Theological Seminary (Charlotte, North Carolina). He holds a PhD from the University of Edinburgh. He represents the conservative evangelical wing of early Christian scholarship and engages seriously with historical-critical methods while defending orthodox positions on canon formation and theological continuity.

He is among the leading evangelical scholars of NT canon formation, patristics, and second-century Christianity. His work is notably irenic in engaging revisionist scholarship (Bauer, Ehrman, Koester) while mounting substantive historical rebuttals.

## Major Works

- *The Question of Canon: Challenging the Status Quo in the New Testament Debate* (IVP Academic, 2013) — argues for functional definition of canon and early emergence
- *Canon Revisited: Establishing the Origins and Authority of the New Testament Books* (Crossway, 2012)
- *The Heresy of Orthodoxy* (Crossway, 2010, with Andreas Köstenberger) — extended rebuttal of Bauer's thesis
- *The Gospel of the Savior: An Analysis of P.Oxy. 840* (Brill, 2005) — text-critical work on an apocryphal gospel fragment
- *Gospel Fragments* (Oxford, 2007, with Nicklas and Kraus) — edition of fragmentary gospel texts
- *The Early Text of the New Testament* (Oxford University Press, 2012, edited with Charles Hill) — collected scholarly essays on manuscript evidence
- *[[kruger-christianity-at-the-crossroads|Christianity at the Crossroads: How the Second Century Shaped the Future of the Church]]* (IVP Academic / SPCK, 2016/2017) — accessible survey of five transitional axes of 2nd-century Christianity

## Hermeneutical Method

**Primary approach**: Social-historical and text-critical, within a canonical-critical framework. Kruger's distinctive methodological contribution is the "functional definition" of canon: a canon exists when books function as Scripture in a community's life, not only when a formal closed list is produced. This distinguishes him from the Sundberg-Gamble "exclusive definition" school that places canon formation in the 4th century.

**Relationship to tradition**: Kruger situates himself within the tradition of scholars like J.N.D. Kelly, Oscar Cullmann, and Jaroslav Pelikan who argue for early credal unity. He engages Bart Ehrman, Helmut Koester, Elaine Pagels, and the Bauer tradition as his primary revisionist foils.

**Evaluative stance on sources**: Kruger distinguishes (a) primary patristic sources as reliable even for groups they attack (the writer has an interest in accurate representation for his polemic to be effective); (b) argument from silence as an unreliable tool for demonstrating diversity; (c) inexact patristic citations as typically reflecting literary convention rather than evidence of oral tradition.

**Stance on the Bauer thesis**: Bauer was correct that early Christianity was theologically diverse; but wrong in claiming heresy was numerically dominant and wrong in denying the possibility of distinguishing orthodoxy from heresy by means of the apostolic kerygma. See [[bauer-thesis|Bauer Thesis]].

## Characteristic Positions

1. **Canon formation**: The NT "core canon" (4 Gospels + Pauline letters + several others = ~22-23/27 books) was functionally in place by the *middle* of the second century — well before Irenaeus, well before the alleged "Marcionite catalyst." Marcion *reduced* a pre-existing larger corpus rather than stimulating the creation of the canon.

2. **Christology**: High Christology is present in the earliest accessible layer of Christianity — Paul's seven undisputed letters — and is embedded in pre-Pauline creedal formulas (1 Cor 8:5-6; Phil 2:6-11; 1 Cor 15:3-11) that take us into the 30s-40s of the first century. This confirms [[larry-hurtado|Hurtado's]] "mutation within Judaism" model and Bauckham's "divine identity" Christology.

3. **Monepiscopate**: The singular bishop office was a second-century development, not an apostolic institution; but the principle of apostolic succession (preserving the teaching of the apostles) was ancient even if Irenaeus applied it anachronistically.

4. **Textual culture**: Christianity's "bookish" orientation (codex, nomina sacra, public reading, publication networks) was distinctive in the Greco-Roman world and provides the best explanation for the rapid standardization of the NT text.

5. **The "Great Church"**: Celsus — Christianity's harshest pagan critic — himself identified a mainstream "Great Church" as the primary target of his attack, confirming that orthodox Christianity was the dominant form in the 2nd century.

## Influence on Commentary Tradition

Kruger's canon scholarship has become a standard reference point for conservative NT scholarship on canon formation, functioning as an accessible and well-documented alternative to Ehrman's revisionist narrative. *The Heresy of Orthodoxy* is widely used in evangelical seminaries as a response to the Bauer tradition.
