# Plato

The Athenian philosopher (c. 428–348 BCE), founder of the Academy, treated in
[[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Book VIII** as the philosopher who "came nearest to us" — to Christian
truth — and whose followers (the [[middle-platonists|Platonists]] and Neoplatonists) are
[[augustine|Augustine]]'s chosen interlocutors for *natural theology*. (Plato also appears in Book I as
a witness against suicide and in Book II as morally superior to the gods who demanded obscene plays.)

## Augustine's Portrait

Augustine sketches a history of philosophy culminating in Plato: greatest disciple of Socrates, he
"perfected philosophy" by uniting its contemplative (Pythagorean/Italic) and active-moral
(Socratic/Ionic) parts. Plato's God is **immaterial, immutable**, the cause of all being, the light of
the understanding, and the end of life — so that "to philosophize is to love God, whose nature is
incorporeal," and the final good is "to live according to virtue" by knowing and imitating God.

Augustine entertains the tradition that Plato, traveling in Egypt, may have encountered the Hebrew
scriptures, and finds the closest pagan parallel to Platonic immutability in the divine name of
**Exodus 3:14, "I AM WHO AM."** He frames the Platonists' achievement and failure through Romans 1: they
"knew God" through creation but "did not glorify Him," lapsing into polytheism and the worship of demons.

## The Decisive Failure

For all their insight, the Platonists conceded that sacrifice should be offered to *many gods*, and
their later masters ([[apuleius|Apuleius]], [[porphyry|Porphyry]]) made demons the mediators between gods
and men. Plato's own dictum that "no god holds intercourse with men" is precisely the premise Christ's
incarnation overturns: the true [[two-cities|Mediator]] is God become man.
