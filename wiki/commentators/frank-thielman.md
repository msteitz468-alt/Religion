# Frank Thielman

Evangelical NT scholar at Beeson Divinity School (Samford University, Birmingham, Alabama). Specialist in Pauline theology, particularly the theology of the law in Paul, and author of the Baker Exegetical Commentary on Ephesians (2010) as well as a Theology of the New Testament (2005). His work is characterized by careful grammatico-historical exegesis, sustained engagement with both patristic commentators and modern historical-critical scholarship, and defense of conservative positions on authorship and historical setting within a framework that takes scholarly objections seriously rather than dismissing them.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Grammatico-historical exegesis** within an evangelical theological framework. Thielman's approach in the BECNT Ephesians volume:

- Takes text-critical questions with full seriousness (e.g., the "in Ephesus" problem in 1:1 receives sustained treatment against the evidence of 𝔓46, א, and B)
- Engages the patristic tradition extensively: Chrysostom, Origen, Theodoret, Basil, Jerome, and Theophylact are regular interlocutors — used both for grammatical parallels (native Greek speakers' instincts about syntax) and theological tradition
- Works carefully through Greek syntax and vocabulary: the long, broken sentences of Ephesians receive clause-by-clause analysis
- Emphasizes OT background: the *berakah* tradition, Isaiah allusions in 2:14–18 and 6:14–17, Qumran parallels for 1:3–14, LXX lexical data
- Attends to Greco-Roman cultural context: the Artemis cult, magical practice in Ephesus, and especially the imperial cult (the counter-imperial dimensions of Ephesians' vocabulary receive significant treatment)
- Defends Pauline authorship while engaging pseudonymity arguments (Lincoln, MacDonald) seriously

The BECNT series positions itself as accessible to pastors while engaging scholars, and Thielman fits this profile: the commentary neither descends into technical minutiae (treated mostly in additional notes) nor sacrifices exegetical depth.

## Characteristic Positions on Ephesians

**Pauline authorship (c. AD 62)**: Thielman mounts one of the more methodologically careful defenses. He accepts that Ephesians is stylistically unusual — the extreme density of long, complex sentences and digressions is distinctive. But he argues: (1) The Paul of undisputed letters could write in a variety of styles (1 Cor 13; Phil 2:6–11; 2 Cor 6:14–7:1; Rom 16:25–27). (2) 2 Thess 1:3–12 shows that long, redundant, torrent-of-words sentence openings are not without Pauline precedent. (3) The broken syntax of Eph 2:1–7 and 3:1–19 is not unlike Rom 5:12–18 and 2 Cor 2:12–7:7. (4) A plausible historical explanation — dictation under light Roman military custody without opportunity for revision — accounts for the unusual concentration of such features in a single short letter.

Thielman's dating (c. AD 62, end of Roman imprisonment described in Acts 28) is earlier than Lincoln's (AD 80–90 pseudonymous) and allows the historically plausible conditions — 7+ years of absence from Ephesus, the natural diversification of house churches, the separation of Jewish and Gentile Christianity — without requiring post-Pauline anachronism.

**The "in Ephesus" problem (1:1)**: Defends inclusion despite its absence from 𝔓46, א, B. His argument: omitting the phrase produces "almost unintelligible Greek" (*tois ousin* requires a place name in Pauline epistolary convention, as in Phil 1:1, 2 Cor 1:1, Rom 1:7). The Alexandrian omission probably reflects the same scribal tendency visible in Codex G's erasure of "in Rome" from Rom 1:7 — an effort to universalize a geographically specific letter.

**The "middle wall" (*mesotoichon*) = the Mosaic law**: The exegetically central decision of 2:11–22. Thielman rejects: (1) the Jerusalem temple balustrade reading (too architecturally specific for a general Asian audience); (2) the gnostic-cosmic-barrier hypothesis (rests on texts postdating Ephesians by centuries). He settles on the Mosaic law as both "middle wall" and "fence" (*phragmos*) — using Letter of Aristeas 139 as evidence that the Jewish law was explicitly compared to "iron walls" and a "fence" that "enclosed" Israel and kept it from mingling with other nations. In first-century Ephesus, the law was in fact the primary social indicator of Jewish identity: observance of Jewish rites (*hiera Ioudaika*) was what distinguished Jews from others (Josephus, Ant. 14.228). See [[ephesians-middle-wall|The Middle Wall in Eph 2:14]].

**The Mosaic law's abolition**: In 2:14–15, Christ "set aside" (*katargeō*) the Mosaic law. Thielman reads this as a real abolition — not merely its "ceremonial" elements (pace Calvin), not merely its commanding character (pace Robinson) — while maintaining that the Mosaic law remains authoritative Scripture. The distinction: believers who "obey the parts of the Mosaic law that Paul incorporates into his ethical teaching" are not obeying them as the Mosaic law. The law has passed its appointed end (*telos*, cf. Rom 10:4), but as Scripture it still reveals God's character, the nature of humanity, and the centrality of faith.

**Imperial cult as counter-narrative**: Thielman gives more weight than typical evangelical commentaries to the political register of Ephesians' language. The province of Asia redesigned its calendar around Augustus's birthday c. 9 BC; coins, inscriptions, and altars proclaimed his birth "the beginning of good tidings (*euangelia*) to the world." The language of savior, god, and lord was the public vocabulary of imperial worship. Paul's proclamation of Christ as *kyrios* with "all things under his feet" (1:22), as lord of "the administration of times" (1:10), and as "eternal" victor (1:21) would have been heard as a direct counter-claim in Asia Minor.

**The *berakah* structure of 1:3–14**: The 202-word Greek sentence is analyzed as a Jewish-style benedictory prayer (*berakah*) — the form central to Jewish piety, governing both liturgical and daily prayer. The closest structural parallel may be 4Q434 from Qumran. The passage combines careful pre-planned structure (four sections divided by *en Christō/en hō* hinges; three leading aorist active participles; the refrain "for the praise of his glory") with small inconsistencies that suggest extemporaneous dictation. Thielman argues Paul dictated from a set form of prayer he had prepared or knew, filling in the subordinate clauses spontaneously.

**The church's cosmic role (3:10)**: "That through the church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places." The church's unity across ethnic lines is not simply a social achievement — it is a cosmic proclamation addressed to the hostile spiritual powers that sustain division. This is the most distinctively Ephesian ecclesiological claim: the church is a witness to the cosmos, not just a community of the redeemed.

## Known Controversies and Tensions

- His defense of Pauline authorship is rigorous but remains a minority position among critical NT scholars. Lincoln's pseudonymity case (ICC) retains considerable scholarly influence.
- His reading of the "middle wall" as the Mosaic law is exegetically the most defensible single option but still debated; the Jerusalem temple balustrade reading has ancient proponents (Anselm of Laon, some modern scholars).
- His reading of *katargeō* in 2:15 as a real (not just partial) abolition of the Mosaic law creates some tension with his use of Deut 5:16 in 6:2–3 — he resolves this by distinguishing the law *as* the Mosaic covenant from the law *as* authoritative Scripture.

## Influence and Tradition

Thielman stands in the mainstream of evangelical Pauline scholarship, in conversation with Moo (BECNT Galatians), Schreiner (BECNT Romans), and the broader Reformed-evangelical tradition represented by the BECNT and NICNT series. His work on Paul and the law (*Paul and the Law*, 1994) provides the exegetical basis for his reading of 2:14–15; his earlier NT theology work grounds the broader ecclesiological reading.
