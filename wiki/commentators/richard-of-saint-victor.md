# Richard of Saint Victor (c. 1110–1173)

**Richard of Saint Victor** is the mystical theologian and exegete who, together with
[[hugh-of-saint-victor|Hugh of Saint Victor]], defines the Victorine school's mature voice. He is
"a warm partisan of the fourfold exposition" (de Lubac) and a great allegorist, despite having been
claimed by some historiography as another step toward twelfth-century "literalism." His commentary
on the Apocalypse and his treatment of Ezekiel's Temple are the two loci most discussed in this
connection.

## Biographical Context

Born in Scotland; entered the Abbey of Saint Victor in Paris by the mid-1140s; became sub-prior
(1159) and prior (1162), the office he held until his death. His major works include *Benjamin
minor* (*De praeparatione animi ad contemplationem*), *Benjamin maior* (*De gratia contemplationis*,
his great mystical-theological summa), the *De Trinitate*, and commentaries on the Apocalypse and on
Ezekiel.

## Hermeneutical Method

Richard is a committed practitioner of the [[fourfold-sense|fourfold sense]] and inherits
[[hugh-of-saint-victor|Hugh's]] orientation without the historiographical ambiguity surrounding
Hugh's emphasis on history.

### Commentary on the Apocalypse

Richard's Apocalypse commentary is explicitly spiritual and follows the tradition of Bede's seven
visions. The seven seals reveal "the manifold tribulations of holy Church from the beginning of grace
up to the very end." His sub-divisions (the seven sections, the seven seals, the dragon's seven heads
etc.) are consistently *qualitative and spiritual*: the content is "not all at once, once and for
all" but progressively manifest through the life of the Church — yet these "following events" are
always categories of spiritual experience (preaching, persecution, heresy, temptation) rather than
specific datable events. The outpouring of the seven vessels of wrath designates categories of the
reprobate (the land = Judea; the sea = persecuting Gentiles; rivers and mountains = heretics; the
sun = the Antichrist himself; etc.), not a chronological sequence of historical events. Richard's
usage is therefore entirely within the tradition and entirely opposed to Joachim's "concordia."

### The Temple of Ezekiel: A Particular Case, Not a New Program

Richard's commentary defending the literal architectural coherence of Ezekiel's visionary temple
(against Gregory the Great, who dissolved its details into pure allegory) was "a very particular
case" — Richard thought Gregory had mishandled the specific literary form of Ezekiel's vision, not
that the literal sense generally trumped the spiritual. Richard explicitly opposed **Andrew of Saint
Victor's** "Judaizing" tendency and was a "warm partisan of the fourfold exposition" throughout his
other commentaries.

## Position on Allegory and Mystical Theology

Richard's *Benjamin* works are among the great classics of Western Christian mystical theology:
they describe the stages of contemplation through the figures of Leah, Rachel, Bilhah, and Zilpah
(the wives and concubines of Jacob in Genesis), and Rachel's son Benjamin ("son of the right hand")
as the figure of ecstatic contemplation. This is thoroughgoing, sustained *allegoria facti* —
reading the literal-historical narrative of Genesis as the figure of the soul's ascent to God. His
exegesis of the spiritual senses is therefore not merely theoretical but the operative framework of
his most important works.
