# John the Grammarian of Caesarea

John the Grammarian of Caesarea (fl. c. 514–518) — not to be confused with [[john-philoponus|John Philoponus]] (also called "the Grammarian," of Alexandria) — was, for Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], Chs. 4 & 7), "a crucial, early voice of an intellectually and philosophically ambitious Chalcedonianism" and the originator of the strategy later called **insubsistence Christology**. His *Apology for the Council of Chalcedon* provoked [[severus-of-antioch|Severus of Antioch]]'s *Contra impium Grammaticum*, from whose citations Marcel Richard reconstructed John's Greek. His identity (Caesarea in Cappadocia or in Palestine) is disputed.

## Applying the Classical Theory to Christology

John's "striking and unexpected claim" was that in the Incarnate we meet "two substances (οὐσίαι) united in one hypostasis" — terminology "absent from the Chalcedonian formula... and from practically all earlier Christological writing." He grounded it in the [[double-homoousion|double homoousion]]: Christ is consubstantial with the Father in divinity and with us in humanity, so each *homoousion* refers to a **universal ousia** in the Cappadocian sense (citing Basil, *Ep.* 236). With this "half-forgotten thinker... utterly changed... the direction of Patristic philosophy": within decades Chalcedonians and non-Chalcedonians shared "a unified conceptual framework for trinitarian and Christological doctrines." The move had soteriological appeal (Christ's humanity as the universal nature shared with all). But for it to work, the classical theory "had to be reduced to... its abstract dimension," severing the bond between universal and particular.

## Answering the Anti-Chalcedonian Objections

- **WT/WHI** ("whole Trinity / whole humanity incarnated"): John replied this follows only "if ousia was understood as being only partially present in its particulars, which was not at all his view" — "the same substance is recognized entirely (ὁλοτελῶς) in three persons."
- **NNWH** ("no nature without a hypostasis"): John affirmed the axiom in its Cappadocian sense ("a universal is seen in particular individuals... such as divine nature in Father, Son, and Holy Spirit") — Zachhuber argues NNWH is "an inference of the classical theory, specifically of its concrete dimension," not merely the miaphysite identification of physis and hypostasis.

## Insubsistence Christology

John is "the earliest source" for the claim that Christ's human nature needed no hypostasis of its own because it "took hypostatic existence in the hypostasis of the Logos." His humanity "subsisted not in another but in himself... has its individual existence (τὰ ἰδικά) only in the divine Logos." Two moves: hypostatic realization is defined as **inherence in another**, and individuation is tied to that inherence — Christ's humanity is individuated by its assumption into the Logos, its "only individual property." Drawing on Aristotle's *Categories* (he probably taught it), he treats the assumed humanity "in effect, as an accident" that "gains hypostatic reality 'in' another hypostasis."

Zachhuber stresses a terminological point: John uses *enhypostatos* in its **traditional sense** (= *kath' hypostasin* = hypostatic union), **not** for the insubsistence relation. The technical sense of [[enhypostaton|*enhypostaton*]] is a separate, rival development in [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]]: "It is only [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] who will bring these two traditions together."

## Two Strategies and Their Legacy

Zachhuber identifies two strategies in John, "both... far-reaching": (1) **decoupling individuation from hypostatization** (the body/soul analogy: two universal natures jointly constituting one individual, so hypostatic realization is "accidental to the individuation of universal natures"); and (2) the **accident model** (Christ's humanity realized "in a manner that normally characterizes" an accident, blurring the substance/accident boundary). His argument that natures must be universal — a "single nature of Christ" would imply a class of saviours — became popular among Chalcedonians (adapted by [[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus]] and the Damascene) but is, Zachhuber judges, "more polemical than philosophical."
