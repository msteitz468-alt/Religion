# Hugh of Saint Victor (c. 1096–1141)

**Hugh of Saint Victor** is the foremost theologian and exegete of the Abbey of Saint Victor (Paris),
"the greatest thinker of his time" (de Ghellinck), and one of the two greatest allegorists of the
twelfth century alongside [[bernard-of-clairvaux|Bernard of Clairvaux]] (Ford Lewis Battles). He is
the central figure in [[de-lubac-medieval-exegesis|de Lubac's Vol. 3]] of *Medieval Exegesis*,
precisely because the modern historiographical tradition (Spicq, Mandonnet, and Beryl Smalley) had
misread Hugh as a proto-literalist preparing the way for modern historical-critical method. De Lubac's
analysis reverses this: Hugh is a **conservative defender of the traditional fourfold synthesis**
against the new threats of twelfth-century rationalism.

## Biographical Context

Little is known of Hugh's early life; he entered Saint Victor around 1115 and remained there until
his death in 1141. His principal works include the *Didascalicon* (a theory of the arts and sacred
reading), *De sacramentis Christianae fidei* (Hugh's major theological synthesis), *De arca Noe
morali*, *De arca Noe mystica*, and commentaries on Ecclesiastes and the Celestial Hierarchy of
Pseudo-Dionysius. He shaped the entire generation of Victorine theologians and exegetes who followed
him, including [[richard-of-saint-victor|Richard of Saint Victor]] and (more ambiguously) Andrew of
Saint Victor.

## Hermeneutical Method

### Against the "Litterati": Defense of Proper Order, Not of Literalism

Hugh's famous warnings to the "litterati" (rash allegorizers who skip the historical foundation and
move immediately to spiritual sense) were **not anti-allegorical**. They targeted the
Abelardian-style dialecticians and rationalists who used logical argument rather than faithful
reception of revealed history as the ground of their theology. Hugh defended the *order* of reading:
history first, then allegory, then tropology — precisely *because* the historical sense is the
necessary foundation of the spiritual senses, not because he preferred it over them.

### The "Over-Squeezed Udders": Against Allegorical Intemperance

Hugh's warning against excessive allegory (the image from Proverbs 30:33) is similarly traditional —
found in Gregory the Great, Bede, Rabanus Maurus, Rupert of Deutz, Paschasius Radbertus, Hugh Metel,
Peter Lombard, and Peter the Eater, all going back ultimately to Origen via Gregory. Hugh criticized
*both* dialectical/rationalist excess and allegorical excess with equal force.

### Noah's Ark: Mystical, Not Historical-Critical

Hugh's elaborate descriptions of Noah's Ark have been read as early historical-critical literalism.
De Lubac shows this to be a misreading. Hugh's *De arca Noe mystica* announces itself as a "mystical
description." Hugh states explicitly: "you ought to know that they have been set down so that in them
you may learn wisdom, discipline, and virtue." De Ghellinck diagnosed Hugh with "an exaggerated
predilection for allegory."

### On Anagogy and the Fourfold Scheme

Hugh preferred the **tripartite** scheme (history / allegory / tropology) for symbolic reasons (the
Victorines' love of triads), but he did *not* eliminate anagogy. He either treated it as a
subdivision within allegory (contemplative mystical allegory) or as the ultimate contemplative state
beyond both allegory and tropology. The *Speculum de mysteriis Ecclesiae* (dependent on Hugh) counts
four senses explicitly. Hugh cited Gregory's *Moralia* as "sweeter than all others" (*mihi prae
caeteris dulcia*) — the "authentic Gregorian spirit" is what Hugh manifests, not an innovation
against it.

### History as Defensive Priority

Hugh's emphasis on "history" as the indispensable foundation was **defensive** — protecting the
historical reality of revelation against:
- Abelardian dialecticians treating theology as logical inference
- Cosmologists reading natural philosophy into Scripture
- Nominalist logic that might "de-existentialize" the facts of salvation history

A key passage in Hugh's *De sacramentis* on the progressive development of revelation was taken
"word for word" from a letter of Bernard of Clairvaux. Hugh *maintained* the historical sense against
new threats; he did not introduce a new hermeneutics.

## Relation to the Tradition and to His School

Hugh was faithful to Gregory the Great's theological synthesis and to the Augustinian tradition of
the spiritual senses. The contrast with his successor **Andrew of Saint Victor** (who genuinely did
make exegesis a "separate science" and was accused of "Judaizing") is stark. Hugh's promise was *not*
fulfilled in Andrew; it was better fulfilled in [[richard-of-saint-victor|Richard of Saint Victor]],
Godfrey, Absalon, and Adam of Paris, all of whom maintained the traditional allegorical orientation.
