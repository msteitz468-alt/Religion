# Justin Martyr (Commentator)

*See also [[justin-martyr|Justin Martyr (Figure)]] for biographical and reception data.*

Justin Martyr (c. 100–165 CE) is the most significant surviving 2nd-century Christian apologist and the first whose works survive in substantial quantity. As a commentator he reads two bodies of text: (1) the Hebrew Bible (in the Septuagint) as predictive prophecy fulfilled in Christ, and (2) the "memoirs of the apostles" (i.e., the Gospels) as authoritative narrative witness to that fulfillment. His hermeneutical method combines prophetic-typological exegesis with philosophical rationalism drawn from Middle Platonism.

## Biographical Context

Born c. 100 CE in Flavia Neapolis (modern Nablus), Samaria, to pagan Greek-speaking parents. Trained in Stoic, Aristotelian, Pythagorean, and finally Platonic philosophy before converting to Christianity through encounter with an old man who pointed him to the Hebrew prophets. He continued to wear the philosopher's *pallium* and ran what amounted to a philosophical school in Rome. Executed c. 165 CE under Marcus Aurelius after refusing to sacrifice to the gods (*Acta Justini*).

## Hermeneutical Method

### Prophetic-Typological (Primary Framework)

Justin's dominant mode is **prophetic fulfillment reading**: the entire Hebrew Bible is forward-pointing prophecy whose meaning is only unlocked by the events of Jesus' life, death, and resurrection. His exegetical moves:

1. **Prophetic past tense** = divine certainty about the future: "The things which He absolutely knows will take place, He predicts as if already they had taken place" (*First Apol.* Ch. XLII). This explains why passages in the perfect or aorist in Hebrew prophets are read as predictions.

2. **Discerning the prophetic speaker**: "When you hear the utterances of the prophets spoken as it were personally, you must not suppose that they are spoken by the inspired themselves, but by the Divine Word who moves them. For sometimes He declares things that are to come to pass; sometimes He speaks as from the person of God the Lord and Father; sometimes as from the person of Christ; sometimes as from the person of the people answering the Lord or His Father" (*First Apol.* Ch. XXXVI). This fourfold voice-discernment (Father / Son / Spirit as predictor / people responding) is Justin's systematic hermeneutical principle for reading prophecy.

3. **Demonic pre-emption**: Pagan myths that resemble Christian claims (virgin births, resurrection, heavenly ascent) are explained as demonic anticipations of prophecy, not independent parallels. The demons heard the prophets and fabricated counterfeit myths to preempt credibility (*First Apol.* Chs LIV–LVI).

4. **Plato as dependent on Moses**: Greek philosophical insights (Plato's creation from shapeless matter, the "second power placed crosswise") derive from Moses, whom Justin identifies as older than all Greek writers. Philosophy borrows from prophecy, not the reverse (*First Apol.* Chs LIX–LX).

### Philosophical-Rationalist (Secondary Framework)

Justin presents Christianity as the "true philosophy" and employs Platonic and Stoic concepts to defend it before educated pagans. His philosophical hermeneutic includes:

- **Logos theology**: Christ = the divine Logos (Reason/Word), first-born of God, through whom God made all things, who was partially present in all rational creatures as the *logos spermatikos* (spermatic/seed of the Word). Philosophers and prophets each grasped fragments of the Logos; Christ is the Logos in fullness (*Second Apol.* Chs VIII, X, XIII).
- **Free will against fate**: Against Stoic determinism and fatalism, Justin argues that moral accountability requires free choice. Prophecy describes what God foreknows; it does not constrain human decision (*First Apol.* Chs XLIII–XLIV; *Second Apol.* Ch. VII).
- **Rational worship**: Christian rejection of sacrifice and idol worship is presented as philosophically superior — not as eccentric or antisocial (*First Apol.* Chs IX–X, XIII).

## Major Works Consulted

1. **[[first-apology-justin|First Apology]]** (c. 155–160 CE) — 68 chapters; the primary source for Justin's hermeneutical method, Logos theology, prophetic fulfillment argument, and sacramental descriptions.
2. **[[second-apology-justin|Second Apology]]** (c. 160 CE) — 15 chapters; most developed statement of *logos spermatikos*; case of Ptolemaeus; demonology.
3. **Dialogue with Trypho** — not yet ingested; lengthy dialogue with a Jewish interlocutor; extensive OT interpretation.

Lost works: *Treatise against All Heresies*, *Against Marcion*, *On the Soul*, *Discourse to the Greeks* (referenced by Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* 4.11, 4.16–17).

## Characteristic Positions

- **Christian universalism of truth**: "Whatever things were rightly said among all men, are the property of us Christians" (*Second Apol.* XIII) — all partial truths derive from the implanted Logos.
- **Pre-Christian Logos-participants**: Socrates, Heraclitus, Abraham, Ananias, Azarias, Misael, and Elias were "Christians" before Christ in virtue of living according to the Logos (*First Apol.* Ch. XLVI).
- **Triadic theology**: Father, Son/Logos, prophetic Spirit are consistently grouped as the objects of Christian worship, with the Son in "second place" and the Spirit in "third" — an early subordinationist triadic formula.
- **Sacramental realism**: The Eucharistic food "is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" — not common food but transformed by "the prayer of His word" (*First Apol.* Ch. LXVI). Justin does not elaborate a mechanism but insists on the identity.
- **Anti-Marcionite**: The God of the Hebrew prophets is the same as the God and Father of Jesus. The prophets foretell Christ. Marcion's "other god greater than the Creator" is a demonic deception (*First Apol.* Ch. XXVI, LVIII).

## Controversies

- **Subordinationism**: Justin's repeated placement of the Son "in the second place" and the Spirit "in the third" (*First Apol.* XIII) was read by later critics (and some modern scholars) as proto-Arian. Others read it as pre-Nicene orthodoxy before the technical vocabulary existed.
- **Relationship of the Logos to the Father**: Justin uses the analogies of fire kindling fire (the Son comes forth from the Father without diminishing the Father, *Dialogue* 128), which orthodox tradition affirms and Arianism denies — but his language is imprecise by Nicene standards.
- **Authenticity of the Marcus Aurelius letter** appended to the First Apology: widely considered spurious or significantly interpolated.

## Influence

Justin is cited by Athenagoras, Theophilus of Antioch, Tertullian, and Irenaeus within decades of his death; Origen and Clement of Alexandria continue his Logos-philosophy integration; Eusebius treats him as the definitive 2nd-century orthodox apologist. His Sunday worship description (*First Apol.* 67) and Eucharist description (*First Apol.* 65–66) are among the most cited passages in early church historical scholarship.

## Christology in Ehrman's account

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) presents Justin
as the first trained philosopher of the church, combining two frameworks: Christ as the
pre-incarnate **[[angel-of-the-lord|Angel of the Lord]]** (the OT theophanies) and Christ as the
**[[logos-christology|Logos]]** of God made flesh — divine reason present in all humans (Socrates
a "pre-Christian Christian") but fully incarnate in Christ. Crucially, Justin's Christ is still
"numerically distinct from" *and* "subordinate to" the Father — a way-station, not yet the
[[trinity|Nicene]] equality. See [[arian-controversy]].
