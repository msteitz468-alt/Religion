# Leontius of Byzantium

Leontius "the monk" of Byzantium (fl. 530s–540s), a younger contemporary of [[john-the-grammarian-of-caesarea|John the Grammarian]], "became one of the most respected voices of sixth-century Chalcedonianism." In Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §7.2) he is the author of the technical concept of the **[[enhypostaton|*enhypostaton*]]** and the fountainhead of a "Leontian School" later developed by [[pamphilus-the-theologian|Pamphilus]]. An early sympathizer of the Antiochene cause who moved to mainstream Chalcedonianism, he "never embraced the more radically Cyriline... neo-Chalcedonianism." His major works (*Contra Nestorianos et Eutychianos*, *Epilyseis*) date to the 530s–early 540s.

## The Classical Theory, Abstract Side Only

Leontius took Cappadocian philosophy "for granted as the incontrovertible foundation of Patristic thought," framing the Chalcedonian settlement as a mirror of the Nicene: Eutychians and Nestorians parallel Sabellians and Arians. His definitions are strictly **abstract** — even more one-sided than John the Grammarian: "Physis contains the account of being (τὸν τοῦ εἶναι λόγον); hypostasis also the principle of being on its own... the former signifies the character of a universal thing, whereas the latter distinguishes the particular from the common" (*CNE* 1). He makes "no mention... of the necessarily concrete realization of these natures or of the collective unity of their individuals."

## The Enhypostaton

Confronting **NNWH** ("no nature without a hypostasis"), Leontius granted that natures are not *anhypostatoi* but denied it follows they are hypostases — rather, they are *enhypostatoi*. His famous passage (*CNE* 1): "Hypostasis... and the enhypostaton are not the same thing, just as ousia and enousion are different. For the hypostasis signifies the individual, but the enhypostaton the ousia... the enhypostaton signifies that something is not an accident which has its being in another and is not perceived by itself." Zachhuber reads the *enhypostaton* as the **individuated universal** — the universal as embodied in the individual, distinct from both the universal as such and from the hypostasis (pure existential realization). It is not an accident (Leontius is far from John the Grammarian's accident model) but it inheres in the particular.

The genuine novelty is **distinguishing the *enhypostaton* from the hypostasis**: in the classical theory's concrete dimension "individuals simply were the concrete realizations of natures and in this sense their hypostases." Leontius breaks that identity, making it "feasible to differentiate between the individuated nature and the concrete realization of the individual."

## Individuation and the Diminished Hypostasis

Zachhuber argues that, against Grillmeier's reading, Leontius "more often appears hesitant to define a hypostasis through properties," preferring "language referring to its separate existence." In *CNE* 4 the "nature of the Logos" contains both universal and individual properties: "hypostasis is reduced to existence pure and simple, whereas all properties... can be seen as part of the nature." Properties migrate from the hypostasis side to the (enhypostatic) nature side.

In the later ***Epilyseis***, asked whether the Logos assumed human nature as species or as individual, Leontius answers: Christ assumed an individual nature (*τὴν τινά*) that "is the same as the species" (*τὴν αὐτὴν οὖσαν τῷ εἴδει*). The individuating properties merely "make a subject distinct (ἄλλον), not different (ἀλλοῖον)" (alluding to Gregory of Nazianzus, *Ep.* 101) — they do not require a human hypostasis. Crucially, **Leontius rejects insubsistence**: his Christology is symmetric (divinity and humanity jointly constitute one hypostasis), and the *Epilyseis* closes with explicit criticism of the neo-Chalcedonian position. "Leontius' enhypostaton... has nothing to do with the neo-Chalcedonian view of the inexistence of Christ's humanity in the Logos."

## The Tension He Bequeathed

If the *enhypostaton* = individual nature = universal nature + individuating properties, it corresponds exactly to one definition of *hypostasis* (Porphyry's *athroisma*, the bundle of properties). So affirming two individual natures in Christ might imply two hypostases. Later readers had to choose: "either retain the classical definition of hypostasis and, consequently, modify Leontius' concept of the enhypostaton, or... accept the necessity of a radical revision of the Cappadocian concept of hypostasis."
