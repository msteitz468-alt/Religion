# Karen Armstrong

**Karen Armstrong** (b. 1944) is a British writer on comparative religion, a former
Roman Catholic nun (she spent seven years in the Society of the Holy Child Jesus before
leaving in 1969), and one of the most widely read popular historians of religion in the
English-speaking world. Her *A History of God: The 4,000-Year Quest of Judaism,
Christianity and Islam* (1993) — the source ingested here — narrates not the history of
God but **the history of the human idea and experience of God** across the three Western
monotheisms. She is included in this wiki as a **commentator** (per the project's
filing convention for modern comparative authors, alongside [[john-hick|John Hick]] and,
forthcoming, Stephen Prothero), occupying a **developmental-experiential** position in
[[religious-pluralism-debate|the pluralism debate]].

## Hermeneutical Method

Armstrong is not a philosopher arguing a thesis (as Hick is) but a **narrative historian
of religious experience**. Her method has several defining commitments:

- **The idea of God has a history.** "This book will not be a history of the ineffable
  reality of God itself … but a history of the way men and women have perceived him from
  Abraham to the present day." There is no single, unchanging referent of the word "God";
  the word "contains a whole spectrum of meanings, some of which are contradictory or even
  mutually exclusive." Each generation "has to create the image of God that works for it."
- **Religion is pragmatic, not propositional.** "It is far more important for a particular
  idea of God to work than for it to be logically or scientifically sound. As soon as it
  ceases to be effective it will be changed." When an idea of God stops working it is
  "quietly discarded and replaced by a new theology." This is a deliberately
  **functionalist** account of doctrine.
- **[[mythos-and-logos|Mythos vs. logos]].** Following Eliade, Otto, and the study of
  myth, Armstrong reads the foundational narratives (the *Enuma Elish*, Genesis, the
  resurrection) as **mythos** — symbolic, non-literal attempts to articulate transcendence
  — not as **logos**, the rational-empirical mode proper to science and history. Reading
  myth as though it were logos (i.e. literal fact) is, for her, the characteristic modern
  error and the root of fundamentalism.
- **The privilege of the [[apophatic-theology|apophatic / mystical]] God.** Armstrong's
  governing distinction is between the **personal God** of popular monotheism — an
  anthropomorphic "God out there," prone to becoming an idol and a projection of human ego,
  cruelty, and nationalism — and the **God of the mystics**: ineffable, beyond personality
  and even beyond "existence," deliberately *created within* the self through disciplined
  contemplative practice. She consistently sides with the second. The mystics, she argues,
  "insist that human beings must deliberately create this sense of God for themselves, with
  the same degree of care and attention that others devote to artistic creation."
- **Suspicion of the [[apophatic-theology|God of the philosophers]].** The Greek
  metaphysical God (immutable, impassible *apatheia*, First Cause) absorbed into
  Christianity, *Falsafah*, and scholasticism is treated as an arid, alien intrusion — a
  "God of the philosophers" that the mystics and prophets would scarcely recognize.

## Characteristic Positions

1. ***Homo religiosus*.** Human beings "are spiritual animals"; they "started to worship
   gods as soon as they became recognizably human." Religion is natural to humanity, and
   modern secularism is "an entirely new experiment, unprecedented in human history." Even
   liberal humanism is "a religion without God."

2. **God as a deliberate creation of the imagination.** "Ever since the prophets of Israel
   started to ascribe their own feelings and experiences to God, monotheists have in some
   sense created a God for themselves." She reports approvingly that "a few highly respected
   monotheists would have told me quietly and firmly that God did not really exist — and yet
   that 'he' was the most important reality in the world." This brings her to the edge of
   **[[religious-realism|religious non-realism]]** (see Contradiction with Hick, below).

3. **Fundamentalism as a modern aberration.** Fundamentalism is "antihistorical": it
   wrongly believes Abraham, Moses, and the prophets "experienced their God in exactly the
   same way as people do today," and it reads mythos as logos. "The idols of fundamentalism
   are not good substitutes for God."

4. **A recurring, near-perennial mystical convergence.** Despite mutual polemic, the three
   faiths "produced their own versions" of even their most divisive doctrines (Trinity,
   Incarnation), and their mystics arrive at "a striking similarity" — an ineffable,
   apophatic Reality reached by contemplative discipline. This convergence is what aligns
   her with the experiential wing of [[religious-pluralism|pluralism]] and with
   [[the-real|the Real]].

5. **The pragmatic test of a healthy faith.** A society that stops "deliberately creating
   a faith" falls into "aimlessness, alienation, anomie and violence." The question of her
   final chapter — *Does God Have a Future?* — is answered not metaphysically but
   functionally: a viable God for the future would be the apophatic, compassion-centred God
   of the mystics, not the personal idol of the literalists.

## Relationship to Other Positions in This Wiki

- **vs. [[john-hick|John Hick]] — the realism fault line.** Hick is a **critical realist**:
  [[the-real|the Real]] is a mind-independent transcendent reality to which the traditions
  are genuine cognitive responses. Armstrong, by contrast, repeatedly frames God as a
  *symbol deliberately created by the imagination*, "not … a reality 'out there,'" and cites
  Don Cupitt's non-realism approvingly in her further reading. She thus drifts toward the
  very **[[religious-realism|non-realism]]** Hick wrote *An Interpretation of Religion* to
  refute. Both privilege mysticism and the ineffable, but Hick keeps a robustly realist
  referent where Armstrong is studiedly ambiguous. **Flagged on both pages per the
  Contradiction Protocol.**
- **vs. perennialism.** Armstrong is *close* to perennialism (a common mystical core) but
  more historicist: she stresses that each idea of God is culturally constructed and
  discardable, where the Traditionalists posit a timeless *philosophia perennis*.
- **vs. Prothero (forthcoming).** Stephen Prothero's *God Is Not One* is the **particularist
  counter-case**: he argues precisely against the "all religions are one mountain" framing
  that Armstrong's mystical-convergence narrative exemplifies. Their opposition will be the
  developmental-experiential vs. particularist axis of the
  [[religious-pluralism-debate|pluralism debate]].

## Criticisms

- **Scholarly looseness.** Academic reviewers (and specialist historians) have charged her
  popular syntheses with selective sourcing, smoothing of contested history, and a
  tendency to read a congenial liberal-mystical conclusion back into the evidence.
- **The non-realism slide.** Confessional critics object that "God did not really exist —
  and yet was the most important reality" empties theism of content; sympathetic realists
  (Hick's side) make the same complaint from the other direction.
- **A normative thesis dressed as history.** Her clear preference for the apophatic/mystical
  God over the personal God is a theological *advocacy*, not a neutral historical finding —
  the narrative is built to vindicate it.

## Influence

Armstrong is among the most influential popularizers of comparative religion since Huston
Smith and Joseph Campbell; *A History of God* was an international bestseller and shaped a
generation of lay readers' picture of monotheism. Her *The Case for God* became a standard
foil to the New Atheists. Through the **Charter for Compassion** (2008) she turned the
book's thesis — that the test of religion is the move beyond ego — into an activist
program. She also helped re-popularize the [[axial-age|Axial Age]] thesis (her *The Great
Transformation*).
