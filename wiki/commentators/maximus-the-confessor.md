# Maximus the Confessor

Maximus the Confessor (c. 580–662) is, in Johannes Zachhuber's account ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §9.1), "the first Chalcedonian Church Father in the full sense of that word" — the practitioner of what Zachhuber calls **"assertive Chalcedonianism."** Unlike Chalcedonians of the previous 150 years, Maximus refuses to develop his theology apologetically in response to [[oriental-orthodoxy|miaphysite]] objections, achieving instead "the first comprehensive philosophical synthesis based on the Chalcedonian formula" (Balthasar). His public rejection of imperial monenergism/monotheletism (following Sophronius) led to his condemnation and mutilation; "within a generation of his death, Maximus' position was entirely vindicated" at the Sixth Ecumenical Council (680/81).

## Maximus and the Classical Theory

Maximus takes the Cappadocian abstract theory for granted ("ousia and nature are the same, as are hypostasis and individual," using [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]]'s account of the hypostasis). But distinctively he **revives the concrete dimension** developed by [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]] — "arguably, of more importance for Maximus than the largely logical theory" of earlier Chalcedonians. Universals and particulars are mutually necessary; their relationship is dynamic, "a permanent upward and downward movement comparable to that of Plato's dialectic," inscribed in the divine economy (creatures from God, returning to God). The **Logos and the logoi**: "the one Logos is many logoi." Zachhuber hypothesizes — without claiming unequivocal textual proof — that Maximus's *logoi* may derive from an *ontological* reading of the Cappadocian *logos tēs ousias*, which, if accepted, would unify the abstract and concrete dimensions into one concrete-ontological theory.

## Wholes and Parts

In Maximus "the more fundamental category was the relationship of a whole and its parts" — a *reversal* of the classical theory's priority of universal/particular logic. (Tollefsen makes whole/part the "ontological foundation" of Maximus's thought.) The Church (*Mystagogy* 1) exemplifies the whole that gives its members "a single, simple, whole and indivisible condition." Applying whole/part to the Trinity "was obviously problematic" — miaphysites who pressed the concrete account had come "close to affirming a Trinity composed of its Persons as parts" — which is why earlier authors kept universal/particular primary.

## The Christology of Whole and Part

Maximus reframes the Christological problem: Christ's two natures are his "two 'parts' joined as *homoousioi* with God and humanity respectively," and "Christ's single hypostasis is a whole consisting of the natures as its parts." The Incarnation is "the true hypostatic union of each of the parts that are joined together for the composition of a whole"; the natures are "consubsistential" (ὁμοϋπόστατος). He uses the body/soul analogy: a human being is "a hypostatic whole consisting of parts that are each of a different substance." Although Maximus "accepted the identification of the single hypostasis with the divine hypostasis of the Logos and the insubsistence of Christ's human nature," his whole–part Christology was "at least indirectly, a repudiation" of building the union on humanity's inherence in the Logos; his insistence on **two energies and two wills** (dyothelitism) extends this orientation.

## Assessment

Maximus "hearkened back in an unprecedented way to... the Cappadocians, read through the spectacles of ps.-Dionysius the Areopagite while bypassing many of the intricate Christological puzzles," prioritizing the mereological dimension so that Christ becomes "the ontological focal point of the universe." Zachhuber considers him "an outlier despite his undoubted influence." His alignment with Rome meant John Scotus Eriugena later drew on him: Maximus "stands... at the beginning of the impact Patristic philosophy made on later Western thought." Residual problems (the whole that transcends its parts; whether his union satisfies Cyriline strictures) "may explain why the development of Patristic philosophy did not stop with Maximus."
