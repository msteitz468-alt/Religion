# Marcus Terentius Varro

Rome's greatest antiquarian scholar (116–27 BCE), known here entirely through
[[augustine|Augustine]]'s extensive engagement in [[city-of-god|*City of God*]] Books IV and VI–VII,
where Varro is the single most important pagan interlocutor on Roman religion. Augustine relays
Cicero's verdict that Varro was "unquestionably the acutest of all men, and without any doubt the most
learned."

## Major Work (as Augustine presents it)

Varro's lost **_Antiquities of Human and Divine Things_** (*Antiquitates Rerum Humanarum et
Divinarum*) — **forty-one books**, twenty-five on human things and sixteen on divine things, the divine
section closing with a triad on *certain*, *uncertain*, and **select gods**. Augustine treats it as the
authoritative record of Roman civic religion. He pointedly notes Varro placed the human books *before*
the divine — and quotes Varro's own simile: "as the painter is before the painted tablet, the mason
before the edifice, so states are before those things which are instituted by states" — reading this as
Varro's tacit admission that the civic gods are human institutions, not divine realities.

## The Three Theologies

Varro's organizing scheme, which Augustine adopts and turns against him ([[three-theologies]]):

- **Mythical / fabulous** — the poets' gods, "adapted to the theatre," full of fictions unworthy of the
  divine;
- **Natural / physical** — the philosophers' theology, "fitted for the world," debated inside the
  schools;
- **Civil** — the state's theology, "fitted for the city," administered by priests and magistrates.

Augustine's counter: the civil theology merely incorporates the fabulous (the same obscene gods are
worshipped in the same city that stages the plays), so it is no better. Varro's physical/allegorical
readings of the select gods as natural forces (Janus = the world, Saturn = seeds) are exposed as
mutually contradictory and as terminating in mutable, temporal things.

## Augustine's Reading of Varro

Augustine credits Varro with covertly *exposing* civil religion by laying it bare, while lacking the
freedom to condemn it openly — "overpowered by the custom and laws of his state… not made free by the
Holy Spirit." Varro himself confessed uncertainty: "I will state what I think, not what I am prepared to
maintain: it is for man to think those things, for God to know them." Varro is also Augustine's source
for the story of **Numa Pompilius's** buried books of sacred causes, burned by the senate.

## Influence

Through *City of God*, Varro became the principal channel by which the structure of Roman religion — and
the three-theologies scheme — was transmitted to the Christian West.
