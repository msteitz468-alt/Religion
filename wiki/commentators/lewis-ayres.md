## Scholarly Context

Lewis Ayres is a modern historical theologian and patristics scholar, author of *Nicaea and Its Legacy: An Approach to Fourth-Century Trinitarian Theology* (OUP, 2004). The work is his major contribution to rethinking the narrative frameworks used to interpret fourth-century Trinitarian controversy. His argument is self-consciously addressed to theologians "in communions that hold Nicaea to be a normative statement" (Ch. 16, ~line 43), though he notes others may find it "intriguing, insofar as they have been convinced by my argument that pro-Nicenes offer a plausible reading of Scripture" (~line 47).

## Central Theses of *Nicaea and Its Legacy*

### 1. Against "Arianism" as a Coherent Movement
Ayres argues that "Arianism" as a theological movement is a rhetorical construction — largely Athanasius's polemical invention — rather than a distinct and unified school. In the Epilogue's teaching summary he notes that after 325 "Arius himself is of little significance in the years that follow" (~line 2275) and that Athanasius's "rhetorical construction of 'Arianism' seems to have begun before Athanasius' exile" and was taken up by western theologians (~line 2297). The term "Arian" obscures the actual diversity of eastern theologies in the period.

### 2. "Pro-Nicene Theology" as the Proper Category
In place of the Arian/orthodox binary, Ayres proposes the concept of [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene theology]] — the theologies that, in the 360–80 period, "came during the 360–80 period to recognize each other as orthodox and to interpret Nicaea within the context of confessing God to be mysteriously one and three" (Epilogue, ~line 2415–18). Pro-Nicene theology is defined by shared commitments: "God was one power, nature, and activity; that there could be no degrees in divinity; that the divine persons were irreducible although all sharers in the divine being without any ontological hierarchy; that human beings would always fail to comprehend God and that one could only make progress towards knowledge and love of God through entering a discipline and practice that would reshape the imagination" (Epilogue, ~line 2421–26). Key pro-Nicene theologians include [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil of Caesarea]], [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]], Gregory Nazianzen, Hilary, Ambrose, and [[augustine|Augustine of Hippo]].

### 3. Critique of the East/West Narrative and the De Régnon Paradigm
Ayres directly challenges the widespread narrative of a fundamental split between eastern/Greek and western/Latin Trinitarian theologies: "the narrative about the separation between eastern and western theologies finds its origin in Theodore De Régnon's history of Trinitarian theology in the late nineteenth century" (Ch. 16, ~line 1399–1402). He follows Michel Barnes ("De Régnon Reconsidered") in showing that De Régnon was not primarily concerned with Greek/Latin difference but with historical periodization, and that his argument was substantially transformed in later reception. Neo-Thomist writers then attributed to Augustine a failure of "psychological" analogy on the basis of readings shaped by this same neo-Thomist tradition. See also [[controversies/de-regnon-paradigm|De Régnon paradigm controversy]].

### 4. Defence of Augustine
Ayres explicitly defends [[augustine|Augustine]] against the charge — widespread in the modern Trinitarian "revival" — that Augustine's western/psychological Trinitarianism is defective. He acknowledges that Michel Barnes's "Augustine in Contemporary Trinitarian Theology" overlaps significantly with his own critique (~line 25–28). The "psychological analogy" as attributed to Augustine in much modern theology is itself shaped by a neo-Thomist projection.

### 5. Critique of the Modern Trinitarian "Revival"
Ayres identifies the late twentieth-century Trinitarian "revival" as deeply shaped by Hegelian and idealist dynamics it rarely acknowledges. Key figures he engages critically include: Pannenberg, Moltmann, Jüngel, Robert Jenson (Protestant); Karl Rahner, Walter Kasper, Catherine LaCugna, Elizabeth Johnson, Miroslav Volf (Catholic); John Zizioulas (Orthodox). His critique is not merely that their positions diverge from pro-Nicene theology, but that "modern Trinitarians do not offer arguments for these dynamics that are or should be convincing" (~line 1985–86): they lack "a theological culture in sufficient continuity with Nicene thought to show how these modern trends might be read alongside careful attention to foundational credal formulae and alongside the text of Scripture" (~line 1987–89).

He identifies three "meta-narrative strategies" enabling this evasion:
1. Modern theological method *must* differ from premodernity due to post-Enlightenment rationality (~line 147–200).
2. Classical Christianity is unsustainable because of its debt to "Greek metaphysics" (~line 201–215).
3. Philosophies function as self-enclosed systems that overwhelm theologians who use them piecemeal (~line 277–320).

All three, Ayres argues, are historically questionable and serve to insulate modern systematics from the genuine challenge pro-Nicene theology poses.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ayres proposes what he calls a "theology of theology" grounded in the reading practices of pro-Nicene theologians themselves. Key elements, stated in Ch. 16:

- **Theology as contemplation of Scripture**: "by saying that theology is fundamentally a contemplation of Scripture I mean that it should form the penultimate focus of the theologian's attention: ultimate attention must be reserved for the mystery of God revealed in Christ" (~line 1567–70).
- **Plain sense + figural/grammatical reading**: The plain sense of Scripture, read grammatically and figurally, governs the theologian's attention; this is not Barthian Scripture-as-propositional-deposit but a practice of attention that leads into mystery.
- **Creeds as guides for reading Scripture**: Credal formulae organize and summarize the plain sense; attention to them requires historical investigation of their circumstances and language.
- **Piecemeal appropriation of philosophy**: Pro-Nicene theologians used philosophical resources (Stoic, Platonic, Aristotelian) selectively and eclectically to elucidate Scripture, not as self-enclosed systems. "Both Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine adapt themes from Plotinus: neither, however, makes any extensive use of the complex discussions concerning the interrelationships between the three primary hypostases that so fascinated the latter" (~line 350–56).
- **Epistemological reserve**: Pro-Nicene theology is marked by "the epistemological reserve intrinsic to pro-Nicene thought" (~line 1218) — the recognition that human speech about God always fails the divine mystery.

This method implies that modern Trinitarian theology cannot simply borrow pro-Nicene formulae while rejecting the exegetical and ascetic practices that produced and sustained them: "the Fathers, and creeds, and councils claim to be interpreting Scripture. How can one accept their results if one does not accept their methods?" (Andrew Louth, cited approvingly at ~line 115–22).

## On the Development of Doctrine

In section V Ayres argues that modern theories of doctrinal development — whether vitalist (Newman, Tübingen) or rationalist (Thomist) — share the problematic assumption that "success in such a theory involves providing a way in which the paths of continuity in doctrine can be traced and understood by the human observer" (~line 2115–18). This assumption is "both theoretically problematic for anyone who has read a little about the structure and problems of narrative" and "theologically problematic" (~line 2121–22). Christians should "no more expect to be able to trace the paths of that continuity with certainty than we expect to be able to locate the history of grace in the Church with certainty" (~line 2125–27). He follows Henri de Lubac's 1948 essay in insisting that "revelation" is the action of God in Christ, and all subsequent reflection is abstraction from mystery. Ayres's own account of fourth-century development is "strongly revisionist" in presenting pro-Nicene theology as a gradual, contested, politically entangled emergence — while maintaining that the Church's "maintenance of this faith down the centuries, in spite of Hegel, fire, and sword, may itself be something of an answer" to challenges about doctrinal continuity (~line 2188–90).

## Positive Program

Ayres does not claim to offer "a vision of theological practice as clearly defined as that of many 'systematic' methodologies" (~line 1995–96). He calls instead for: (a) recovery of close reading of pro-Nicene texts and the exegetical practices embedded in them; (b) attention to creeds as historically shaped guides for reading Scripture rather than abstract propositions; (c) a different appropriation of modern historical consciousness — one that resists epochal summary narratives and is attentive to cultural complexity. He notes sympathy with ressourcement theologians (de Lubac, Congar) on the Scripture/tradition relationship, and names as broadly congenial "post-liberal" writers (Hans Frei, George Lindbeck, Stephen Fowl, Bruce Marshall), British theologians (Sarah Coakley, John Webster, Rowan Williams), and certain Catholic writers (Michael Buckley, Nicholas Lash, Matthew Levering) — while explicitly distancing himself from "Radical Orthodoxy" as still too bound to the structures of modern systematics (~line 925–35).
