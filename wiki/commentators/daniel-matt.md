# Daniel C. Matt

Contemporary American scholar of Jewish mysticism, best known as the translator and annotator of the **Pritzker Edition** of the [[zohar|Zohar]] — the first complete scholarly English translation of the Zohar from a reconstructed critical Aramaic text. The edition (Stanford University Press, 12 volumes, 2004–2017; Matt translated the first nine, covering the Torah portions) is the standard scholarly English Zohar and the source for this wiki's Zohar pages.

---

## Biographical and Scholarly Context

Daniel Matt is the son of the Conservative rabbi and scholar Hershel Matt. He earned his doctorate at Brandeis University, writing on the early kabbalistic text *Sefer Mar'ot ha-Tsove'ot* of David ben Yehudah he-Ḥasid, and taught for over twenty years at the Graduate Theological Union in Berkeley, California. He has also taught at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Stanford.

His major works:
- **The Zohar: Pritzker Edition** (2004–2017) — the monumental annotated translation, named for benefactor Margot Pritzker, who commissioned and underwrote the multi-decade project. Matt translated the first nine volumes, which comprise the main running Torah commentary (Genesis through Deuteronomy, the primary theosophic and theurgic parashah exegesis); Volume X (Midrash ha-Ne’lam, the earliest Zoharic stratum) and later volumes were translated by other scholars (e.g., Nathan Wolski). **Volumes 1–3 cover the whole of Genesis** (Vol 1: Be-Reshit–Noaḥ / primeval; Vol 2: Lekh Lekha–Va-Yetse patriarchal middle; Vol 3: Va-Yishlaḥ–Va-Yḥi close of Jacob + Joseph cycle), with **Volume 4** (ingested 2026-06-07) covering the first half of Exodus (Shemot through Yitro: enslavement, birth and call of Moses, plagues, deliverance, crossing of the Red Sea with revelation of the 72-letter Name, wilderness trials including Amalek, Jethro’s counsel, and the Sinai theophany / Ten Commandments). **Volume 5** (ingested 2026-06-07) continues with Parashat Mishpatim (civil and ritual laws, Exod 21–24) including the embedded *Sava de-Mishpatim* ("Old Man of Mishpatim" — first extensive Jewish treatment of gilgul/reincarnation, linked to levirate marriage, and the famous parable of Torah as a beautiful maiden gradually revealing herself), Parashat Terumah (beginning of Tabernacle instructions, Exod 25–27), and *Sifra di-Tsni’uta* (the "Book of Concealment"). The volume centers on the Dwelling (*mishkan*) as symbol of Shekhinah and the intimacy of "Kallat Moshe" (Bride of Moses). **Volume 6** (ingested 2026-06-07) covers Parashat Ki Tissa (the Golden Calf crisis, Exod 32, read as rejection of Shekhinah leading to Her departure; Moses intercedes by invoking the patriarchs), and Parashat Va-Yaqhel / Pequdei (the actual construction and completion of the Tabernacle under Bezalel; the glory of YHWH filling the Dwelling as the full indwelling of Shekhinah). **Volume 7** (first-pass 2026-06-07; **standards reingest 2026-07-18** — source page [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol7|Matt Pritzker Vol VII]]; body ~3:3b–80a) covers early-to-mid Leviticus: Va-Yiqra (*qorban* as crown-binding; married *adam*; garden/bride banquet), Tsav (perpetual fire; *zeh*/*zot*), Shemini (Nadab/Abihu multi-cause dossier: alien fire, unmarried, Assembly unlinked, forced hour), Tazri’a/Metsora (*Al mishkavi*; *tsara’at*/evil speech), Aḥarei Mot (YK goats/Azazel; golden knotted cord ~3:67a; three rungs Holy One–Torah–Israel at 3:73a; Lilith; Lev 18 as Father/Mother exposure). Matt preface: *olah* as Shekhinah ascending; folk “all one” saying never exact in Zohar — body has “three rungs, interlinked.” (The wiki has now ingested **Vols 1–7**; Vols I–VII standards reingests complete.) **Volume 8** (first-pass 2026-06-07; **standards reingest 2026-07-18** — source page [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol8|Matt Pritzker Vol VIII]]; body ~3:80b–156b) covers Qedoshim (midnight *zivvug*; Torah tears decrees), Emor (*ushpizin*; festivals), Be-Har/Be-Ḥuqqotai (Jubilee; exile as Shekhinah’s pledge), Be-Midbar, Naso (sotah; nazirite beard; ***Idra Rabba*** Arikh Anpin / Ze’eir Anpin; three Companions die by kiss), Be-Ha’alotekha (Torah not mere story; *El na refa na lah*). (The wiki has now ingested **Vols 1–8**; Vols I–IX standards reingests complete.) **Volume 9** (first-pass 2026-06-07; **standards reingest 2026-07-18** — source page [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol9|Matt Pritzker Vol IX]]; body ~3:157a–299a) completes Matt’s main running commentary on the Torah: the remainder of Numbers (beginning with Parashat Shelach Lekha—the spies/scouting of Canaan read as a mystical drama in which the bodies of the generation die in the desert while the souls resemble “denizens of the Garden”; subsequent parashot on rebellion, leadership, and preparations for the land) and the entirety of Deuteronomy (Moses’ farewell addresses as theurgic covenant renewal and transmission of the divine mystery; the visionary Rav Metivta section with its journey to the Garden of Eden, revelations about the afterlife, the tripartite soul, and secrets of the righteous; the colorful Yanuqa/Child narrative as a model of surprising wisdom and intergenerational teaching; theurgic readings of the Shema, the land as sefirotic inheritance, blessings/curses, and Moses’ death/ascent). With Volume 9, the Pritzker Edition Volumes 1–9 (translated by Matt) cover the Zohar’s full Torah commentary from Genesis through Deuteronomy. (The wiki has now ingested **Vols 1–9**; the main Torah commentary is complete.)
- **Zohar: The Book of Enlightenment** (1983) — an earlier annotated anthology of selected passages in the Classics of Western Spirituality series
- **The Essential Kabbalah** (1995) — a popular thematic anthology of Kabbalistic texts
- **God and the Big Bang** (1996) — a constructive work relating Kabbalah and modern cosmology

## Method and Significance of the Pritzker Edition

Matt's edition is significant for several reasons documented on the [[zohar|Zohar]] text page:

1. **A reconstructed critical text**: There was no reliable critical edition of the Zohar's Aramaic. Working from roughly twenty of the best manuscripts (14th–16th centuries) against the standard printed editions (Mantua 1558–60, Cremona 1559–60), Matt constructed an eclectic Aramaic text — published separately online (the "Pritzker Aramaic text") — restoring readings the printers had altered, smoothed, or censored. His translation thus often reads passages unavailable in earlier English versions (Sperling-Simon, Tishby).

2. **Dense scholarly annotation**: Each page carries extensive footnotes tracing the Zohar's biblical and rabbinic sources, parallel kabbalistic passages, and the sefirotic symbolism encoded in each image — making the cryptic text navigable for the first time in English.

3. **Attention to literary artistry**: Following his teacher's generation (Scholem) and especially the work of **Yehuda Liebes** and **Arthur Green** (who wrote the introduction to Volume I), Matt presents the Zohar not as a chaotic anthology but as a work of profound literary and mystical creativity.

Matt operates as a **historical-critical and philological** scholar (see the modern critical frameworks in the wiki). He treats the Zohar as a medieval Castilian composition (the work of [[moses-de-leon|Moses de León]] and his circle), not as an ancient work of [[shimon-bar-yochai|Shim'on bar Yoḥai]] — i.e., he stands on the modern side of the [[zohar-authorship|authorship controversy]]. At the same time, his translation is animated by deep sympathy for the text's spiritual vision, a combination characteristic of the Berkeley/GTU school of Jewish mysticism scholarship.

## Translator’s Introduction Method (Pritzker Vol. I)

*Grounded in Matt’s own Translator’s Introduction to [[matt-zohar-pritzker-vol1|The Zohar: Pritzker Edition, Volume I]]. Historical/literary/authorship context Matt explicitly **defers** to [[arthur-green|Arthur Green]]’s introduction in the same volume.*

### Critical text (~20 MSS; Mantua/Cremona; eclectic, not diplomatic)

Matt states that earlier English translations rest on the standard printed editions, “nearly all” from **Mantua (1558–60)** with variants from **Cremona (1559–60)**. Examining fourteenth- through sixteenth-century manuscripts, he reports superior readings that the first printers had rejected or revised, and signs of an editorial process *within* the manuscript tradition: “certain manuscripts of older lineage reflect an earlier version of the Zohar, which was then reworked in manuscripts of later lineage.” On that basis he abandoned sole reliance on the printed base and undertook to reconstruct a “new-ancient version” of the Aramaic as the foundation for the translation.

He frames the editorial choice as **diplomatic** (exact reproduction of the “best” MS per Torah portion) versus **critical/eclectic** (selecting among a wide range of variants). After consulting the Academic Committee for the Translation of the Zohar, Matt chose a **critical text**, arguing that even for individual sections no single “best” manuscript exists. He notes the sixteenth-century precedent: Mantua’s editors drew on **ten** manuscripts, Cremona’s on **six**.

For the first two volumes he identifies **approximately twenty reliable manuscripts**, selected by provenance, age, lack of scribal errors, and legibility (originals in Oxford, Cambridge, London, Paris, Munich, Rome, the Vatican, Parma, Toronto, and the Jewish Theological Seminary; microfilms at the Institute for Microfilmed Hebrew Manuscripts, JNUL/Hebrew University). Workflow: a research assistant collates variants from roughly half the corpus (and further MSS for difficult loci), plus Mantua, Cremona, and the edition used by Moses Cordovero in *Or Yaqar*. Matt begins from **Reuven Margaliot’s *Sefer ha-Zohar*** (Vilna line, itself Mantua-based) as a “relatively reliable starting point,” then substitutes better readings when shared and confirmed by several reliable witnesses.

**Older-lineage preference.** Matt describes centuries of scribal/editorial smoothing (explanatory phrases, “corrected” syntax, bland replacements for wild neologisms). Relying on variants, he often removes those accretions “to recover the Zohar’s primal texture and cryptic flavor,” preferring unusual, striking wording in early manuscripts when later witnesses “correct” it, and preferring the Zohar’s creative Talmudic paraphrases over later restorations to exact Talmudic form (the Talmudic base is then noted in the commentary). He **disclaims** full restoration of “the original text of the Zohar,” allowing that none may ever have existed because the text “probably emerged over many years, written and distributed piecemeal,” and that no complete reliable manuscript exists (booklet/section circulation from the start; complete printed book as a sixteenth-century collation). Variant analysis, he claims, can still “scrape away some seven hundred years of accretion and corruption” and approach that hypothetical original. The Aramaic base of the translation is made available for scholarly examination.

### Translation: literal yet poetic; commentary against over-sefirotization

Matt’s stated style is **“literal yet poetic”**: a literal rendering is, in his view, both the most accurate and the most colorful way to transmit the “lyrical energy of the Aramaic.” He aims simultaneously to make the Zohar accessible and to convey its “strangeness, potency, and rich ambiguity,” with commentary essential to that dual aim. When translation cannot carry multivalence, the notes unfold the range; when the English is as cryptic as the Aramaic, the commentary “rescues the stranded reader.”

On the commentary’s hermeneutical restraint: Matt seeks to elicit meaning from the Zohar’s own language “without being heavy-handed,” allowing and compelling the reader to **wrestle** with a text “not intended to be easily understood but rather to be deciphered.” He resists the later tendency “to overinterpret, with commentators often insisting on assigning sefirotic significance to nearly every image and metaphor,” while still marking sefirotic correspondences when called for.

**Critique of Sperling–Simon–Levertoff (1930s).** Matt judges the previous standard English (Harry Sperling, Maurice Simon, Paul Levertoff) as reading smoothly but often misunderstanding; its “genteel prose is more a paraphrase than an accurate translation—avoiding unfamiliar terms, censoring erotic material, skipping difficult passages and even entire sections,” subduing the “unruly Aramaic,” and lacking commentary so that symbolism remains impenetrable. He acknowledges learning from consulting it and other translations, but takes a “significantly different” approach. Worked examples in the intro include *qumrin tehirin* rendered as “hooded, hunchbacked, dazzling demons of defilement” and *ḥavilei teriqin* as “ravaging bands of truculent stingers.”

Linguistic notes Matt foregrounds: Castilian composition mostly in a neo-Aramaic blended from the Babylonian Talmud and Targum Onqelos, with neologisms, solecisms, and traces of medieval Hebrew and Castilian; grammar-flouting play on roots (e.g. *tqn*, *slq*); oxymoron **“new-ancient words”** glossed as secrets “recently composed yet ascribed to ancient sources.” Supporting apparatus per volume: glossary, bibliography, index of biblical/rabbinic citations; sefirot diagram; standard Aramaic pagination in running heads.

### How to Read the Zohar (Matt’s guidelines)

Matt offers features rather than a single “right” way: dynamic narrative frame of the Companions (*Ḥavrayya*) on the Galilean way, accompanied by Shekhinah; the work as fundamentally **biblical commentary** (keep a Bible at hand; “Rabbi X opened” opens the verse); rootedness in tradition joined to innovation (“This verse has been discussed, but come and see!”; “Innovations of Torah are required here!”); **questioning** as supreme value; hyperliteral and radical reorderings (e.g. *Lekh lekha* as “Go to yourself!”; Gen 1:1 as “With beginning, It created Elohim”). The wheat parable warns against reducing teachings to a prior “essence”: grasping the principle without the “delectable delights” that diverge from it. Reader posture: open imagination, active engagement, “Come and see!”

### Authorship wording in *this* introduction only

In the Translator’s Introduction itself Matt situates *Sefer ha-Zohar* as having “emerged mysteriously in medieval Spain (Castile) toward the end of the thirteenth century,” speaks of “author(s)” concocting the neo-Aramaic, and glosses “new-ancient words” as recently composed secrets ascribed to ancient sources; he models production as multi-year and piecemeal. He does **not** name Moses de León here; the “complex question of authorship” is deferred to Green. See [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]] for how these phrasings sit among recorded positions.

## Relation to the Scholarly Tradition

Matt's work stands in the lineage of modern academic Kabbalah scholarship:
- **Gershom Scholem** (1897–1982) — founder of the academic study of Jewish mysticism; established the de León authorship thesis on rigorous philological grounds
- **Isaiah Tishby** — produced *The Wisdom of the Zohar*, a thematic anthology with translation and commentary that Matt's edition supersedes in completeness
- **Yehuda Liebes** — argued for the Zohar as the product of a creative circle and illuminated its literary mythopoesis
- **Arthur Green** — wrote the scholarly introduction to Pritzker Volume I, situating the Zohar historically and theologically
