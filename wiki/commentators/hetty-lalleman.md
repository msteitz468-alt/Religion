Hetty Lalleman-de Winkel is a Dutch evangelical Old Testament scholar. Her TOTC commentary *Jeremiah and Lamentations* (IVP, 2013) is the wiki's first Tyndale Old Testament Commentary volume. In the author's preface she states that Jeremiah has been "part of [her] life for more than thirty years," the subject of her Master's thesis and of a PhD written under Gordon McConville (raw 417–418). She has taught OT at Spurgeon's College since 2000 (raw 427). Prior related publications listed in her select bibliography include *Jeremiah in Prophetic Tradition* (Peeters, 2000), a Dutch *Jeremia* commentary (2004), and essays on judgment/creation and on Paul and Jeremiah (raw 630–640).

The TOTC series (Series Editor David G. Firth; Consulting Editor Tremper Longman III) structures each unit as **Context → Comment → Meaning**: larger literary blocks rather than verse-by-verse minutiae, with Meaning carrying "the message that the passage seeks to communicate within the book" (Firth/Longman series preface, raw 405–411). Main English base in the volume is TNIV 2005 (raw 424–426).

## Hermeneutical method

### 1. Literary-context priority, MT base

Lalleman writes that she "pays attention to the historical context of passages, in so far as we can know anything about the events. More attention, however, is devoted to the interpretation of passages in their literary contexts" (raw 950–951). She takes the Masoretic Text as "the main source for exegesis" (raw 922–923). She resists portraying Jeremiah as an unstructured jumble: themes and catchwords link units (examples: chs. 16–17; the "Go…" pattern in 17–20) (raw 951–970).

### 2. Skepticism of heavy Deuteronomistic redaction theories

She objects that theories heavily defined by "Deuteronomistic" rewriting lack hard proof of such a group (raw 935–936). She affirms **influence of Deuteronomy** (Book of the Law / Huldah; priestly-family Torah familiarity) without making hypothetical exilic Deuteronomists the authors of the poetry (raw 938–941). Consistency of *šûb*, plant/uproot, and medical language across poetry and prose supports, for her, "a carefully edited work with a clear message: from doom through judgment to hope" (raw 1121–1123).

### 3. The 604 BC scroll-burning as structural key

Load-bearing thesis of the volume:

> "The present commentary is based on the assumption that this event (604 BC) marked the 'point of no return' in Judah's history and the dynasty of David." (raw 870–871)

After Jehoiakim burns the scroll (Jer 36; public reading December 604; first scroll written 605, Carchemish year), Lalleman holds that the prophet is no longer hopeful of the kind of repentance urged in chs. 2–6; judgment becomes inescapable and Babylon is identified as the "enemy from the north" (raw 870–871, 976–977). Passages forbidding intercession (11:11, 14; 14:11; 15:1) she dates after this hinge (raw 976–977). In the ch. 36 Meaning unit she stresses that destroying the scroll is total disregard of the LORD's word; Jehoiakim is "cut off" from God's future plans while "God reigns for ever" (raw 4818–4819).

### 4. Word-study theology (*šûb*; metaphor clusters)

**Verb *šûb***: basic sense "to turn"; dense wordplay in 3:1–4:4 and 8:4–5; early ministry = call to human repentance; later God is grammatical subject — restore fortunes, cause return from exile (24; 29:11–14; Book of Comfort 30–33) (raw 997–1036). Linked to the 604 hinge: before, return still possible; after, hope only via God's restoring initiative through judgment and exile (raw 1034–1035).

**Two metaphor sets** (raw 1040–1124):
1. Uproot / tear down / destroy / overthrow ↔ build / plant (1:10) — four negative vs two positive verbs ≈ more judgment than hope; both applied to Judah and nations; for Judah, building/planting through exile.
2. Sickness / incurable wound / healing — judgment, hope, and laments (people, prophet, God, nations); Hosea influence and priestly-family familiarity with purity language suggested as background (raw 1118–1119).

### 5. Pastoral confessional frame (Meaning sections)

Author's preface: Jeremiah's life models "the suffering of God and the people"; Jeremiah "may be regarded as a forerunner of the Suffering Servant, Jesus Christ"; Nouwen's "downward mobility" (raw 419–422). New-covenant fulfilment "far beyond Jeremiah's days" inaugurated at the Last Supper (Luke 22:20) (raw 1484–1486). Meaning sections apply passages to Christian discipleship; they remain Lalleman's pastoral frame, not primary-text claims.

## Structure of Jeremiah she adopts

She declines the common major break at ch. 25 and keeps **23–29** together as the true-vs-false-prophecy centre (raw 1542–1543):

1. Prologue (1)  
2. Calls to repentance (2–6)  
3. Covenant broken; laments (7–20)  
4. Concerning kings (21–22)  
5. True versus false prophecy (23–29)  
6. Book of Comfort (30–33)  
7. Zedekiah and Jehoiakim (34–38)  
8. Final days (39–45)  
9. Oracles Against the Nations (46–51)  
10. Epilogue (52)

Supporting frames: 1//52 inclusio; 30:3 // 33:26 "restore the fortunes" inclusio for the Book of Comfort; MT preferred over LXX for base text (raw 912–923, 1404–1405).

## Lamentations in this volume

Lalleman treats Lamentations as **anonymous**, traditional and LXX attribution to Jeremiah noted but not adopted; hapax legomena, vocabulary shared with Isaiah/Psalms but not Jeremiah (e.g. expected *šeqer* missing at Lam 2:14), and more polished poetic style weigh against Jeremianic authorship (raw 5857; preface raw 423). Likely eyewitness near 587 BC, Jerusalem/Judah setting, possible priestly/Levitical influence (raw 5857–5859). Form: alphabetic acrostics in chs. 1–4 (triple in 3); ch. 5 non-acrostic 22 verses; communal/individual lament with ANE city-lament parallels and monotheistic contrast (God reigns for ever, 5:19) (raw 5944, 5952). Theology: covenant-curse judgment without the word "covenant"; God righteous, enemy, and faithful; hope at 3:21–24 does **not** close the book; ch. 5 ends open (raw 5965, 6178, 6508–6512 in digest; verify on cache). Christian Tenebrae use noted; she cautions that no modern nation-state is God's covenant people as Israel was (Lam intro pastoral frame).
