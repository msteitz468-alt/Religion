Hetty Lalleman-de Winkel is a Dutch evangelical OT scholar who has published on Jeremiah's theology, Ezra-Nehemiah, and the prophets. Her TOTC commentary on Jeremiah and Lamentations is the wiki's first Tyndale Old Testament Commentary — a pastoral series distinct from NICOT in being briefer, more accessible, and structured explicitly for pastors and serious students (Context → Comment → Meaning three-part format).

The TOTC series (InterVarsity Press) was recently revised under Series Editor David G. Firth and Consulting Editor Tremper Longman III. Longman's involvement brings methodological connections to his own NICOT work on Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs in the wiki.

## Background

Lalleman's doctoral work focused on Jeremiah's theology, and she reads the book with a degree of literary sensitivity and theological organization that goes beyond the accessibility typical of pastoral commentaries. Her key contribution — the *šûb* analysis and the 604 BCE turning point — is a substantive exegetical and theological argument, not merely a devotional observation.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Final Form with Literary Sensitivity

Lalleman follows the shift in Jeremiah scholarship from diachronic (source-critical) to synchronic (literary) readings. Rather than attempting to identify "Deuteronomistic" layers or reconstructing the book's compositional stages, she pays attention to literary structures, catchwords, chiasms, and thematic connections across the book. She notes that scholars like Lundbom have demonstrated more coherence in the book's structure than the "unorganized jumble" tradition assumed.

### 2. The 604 BCE Turning Point as Structural Key

Lalleman's most distinctive contribution: reading the burning of Jeremiah's scroll by Jehoiakim in 604 BCE (Jer 36) as the theological hinge of the entire book. Before this event, Jeremiah's message holds out the possibility of human repentance and the averting of judgment; after it, judgment is inevitable and the only hope is God's unilateral act of restoration. This structural reading gives the book a clear narrative arc: from conditional threat to unconditional promise, with the terrible middle of the 604 turning point.

### 3. Word-Study Theology

Lalleman uses thorough word studies as the primary means of tracing theological development across the book. The *šûb* analysis is the exemplar: she tracks every occurrence of the word through the book's development, showing how the same word shifts function as the theological moment shifts. Similarly, the two metaphor clusters (build/plant and medical) are traced as organizers of the book's coherence.

## Key Theological Contributions

**The verb *šûb***: The most important single-word theological analysis in the wiki. Occurring over 110 times in Jeremiah, *šûb* marks the book's entire theological development:
- Phase 1: Human *šûb* — the urgent call "Repent! Turn back!" (chs. 2-6; ca. 626-604 BCE)
- Phase 2: Divine *šûb* — God as subject "I will make them return; I will restore" (chs. 29-33; ca. after 604 BCE)
- The shift from human initiative to divine initiative = the 604 turning point

**The 604 BCE turning point**: Jehoiakim's burning of the scroll (Jer 36) is "the point of no return." Before: conditional threats that the people can still respond to. After: unconditional judgment, then unconditional hope through God's initiative alone. This hermeneutic helps explain why the Book of Comfort (chs. 30-33) with its New Covenant promise is so surprising: it comes only *after* judgment has become certain.

**Two metaphor clusters**:
1. Build/plant vs. tear down/uproot (from 1:10): four verbs of judgment, two of hope — reflects the proportions of the book as a whole; both clusters appear in judgment AND salvation contexts
2. Medical metaphors: wound, healing, sickness, balm; Judah as "incurably wounded" nation; Egypt and Babylon as fatally ill; God as the only physician who can heal what is humanly incurable

**Hosea as primary prophetic predecessor**: *šûb* vocabulary, marriage metaphors, medical language, and challenge to empty cult all connect Jeremiah closely to Hosea. Lalleman suggests direct literary influence.
