# Celsus

Celsus was a Greek philosopher who wrote *The True Doctrine* (*Alēthēs Logos*) — "about the year 170 C.E." — the first comprehensive intellectual attack on Christianity by a pagan author (Wilken, tool-file l.7–8). The work is lost as an independent text; it survives only in extensive fragments quoted by [[origen|Origen]] in his eight-book reply *Contra Celsum* (c. 248 CE), wherein Origen "cited Celsus's book at length, sometimes sentence by sentence, before offering his own views" (l.14–16). Through generations of scholarship the "main ideas" and "actual wording" of the *True Doctrine* can be reconstructed (l.17–19).

Nothing is known about Celsus beyond what Origen and the fragments themselves record. Even in Origen's day "nothing was known about Celsus except that he had been dead for a long time" (*c. Cels.* Pref. 4; l.26–27). Origen initially labeled him an Epicurean — a polemically useful tag since "Epicureans were thought to be atheists who undermined society" — but gradually shifted to identifying him as closer to a Platonist (l.28–40). Close examination of the fragments shows he "cannot be identified with any of the major philosophical schools of his time"; he is "closest to the Platonists, but his own philosophical stance was eclectic" (l.42–45). Wilken characterizes him as "a conservative intellectual" who "supports traditional values and defends accepted beliefs" while approaching them "as an intellectual prepared to offer philosophical and religious arguments in support of the traditional political and social order" (l.47–55).

## Hermeneutical Method and Interpretive Stance

Celsus's interpretive approach to Christian writings is distinctive and multi-layered:

1. **Literary and historical criticism** of the Gospels — he reads the gospel accounts of Jesus' life critically, questioning the reliability of witnesses and applying criteria of historical verifiability comparable to those used for Greek legends (l.549–553, 600–615).
2. **Philosophical critique** — he tests Christian doctrines against Middle Platonic axioms (divine immutability, the relation of God to reason and nature) and finds them deficient.
3. **Comparative religion** — he deploys parallels from Greek myth and other cults to show that Christian claims about Jesus are neither unique nor well-attested.
4. **Appeal to *Nomos*** — his deepest critical lens: truth is identified with antiquity; the "true doctrine" (*alēthēs logos*) is identical with the "ancient doctrine" (*palaios logos*) (l.1026–1027). Whatever is traditional is therefore presumptively true.

He is described by Wilken as writing from "a consistent point of view" — not "planless polemic" — arising out of "his views about the society in which he lives, the intellectual and spiritual traditions that animated this society, and the religious convictions on which it was based" (l.856–860).

## The True Doctrine: Major Arguments

### Lumping Christians with Disreputable Cults

Celsus compared Christians to "the begging priests of Cybele and soothsayers, and to worshippers of Mithras and Sabazius, and whatever else one might meet, apparitions of Hecate or of some other daemon or daemons" (*c. Cels.* 1.9; l.99–102). He also compared Christian worship to "superstitious practices of the Egyptians" and to "those in the Bacchic mysteries who introduce phantoms and terrors" (*c. Cels.* 3.17, 4.10; l.103–106). This strategy linked Christianity to marginal, "foreign cult or superstition" categories already disfavored in Roman public opinion (l.98–99).

He caricatured Christian proselytizing as targeting the credulous and uneducated: "wool-workers, cobblers, laundry-workers, and the most illiterate and bucolic yokels" who approach "children in private and some stupid women" and urge them to disobey their fathers and schoolteachers (*c. Cels.* 3:55; l.130–142).

### Deficiencies of Christian Doctrine

**The Incarnation.** Celsus argued that the claim "God came down from the heavens to live on earth among men" was "most shameful" and required little argument to refute (*c. Cels.* 4.2; l.313–315). God by definition is immutable: "It is the nature only of a mortal being to undergo change and remoulding, whereas it is the nature of an immortal being to remain the same without alteration. Accordingly, God could not be capable of undergoing this change" (*c. Cels.* 4.14; l.322–325). If God already knows all things, why descend? And if God appeared only in a particular historical moment, "Is it only now after such a long age that God has remembered to judge the human race? Did he not care before?" (*c. Cels.* 4.8; l.348–350) — presenting a "capricious God who acts willfully without regard to what is best for all creatures" (l.353–354).

**The Resurrection.** Celsus "often reproached" Christians about the resurrection (*c. Cels.* 8.49; l.378). His philosophical objection was that a God who is himself "the reason of everything that exists" cannot act "contrary to reason, or to his own character" (*c. Cels.* 5.14; l.391–392). Restoring a decomposed body would be contrary to reason and nature; the Christian appeal that "anything is possible to God" is "a most outrageous refuge" (*c. Cels.* 5.14; l.372–374). He also dismissed the resurrection testimony: "who says this? A hysterical female, as you say, and perhaps some other one of those who were deluded by the same sorcery, who either dreamt in a certain state of mind and through wishful thinking had a hallucination" (*c. Cels.* 2.55; l.635–638).

**Worship of Jesus and Monotheism.** Celsus defended what Wilken calls "henotheism — belief in one god without excluding belief in other lesser gods" (l.524–526). He held that "it makes no difference if one invokes the highest God or Zeus or Adonai or Sabaoth or Amoun, as the Egyptians do, or Papaios, as the Scythians do" (*c. Cels.* 5.41; l.465–467). The divine hierarchy descends from the one high God through lesser deities and *daimones* to heroes. Worshipping lesser gods enhances, not diminishes, devotion to the high God: "the worship of God becomes more perfect by going through them all" (*c. Cels.* 8.66; l.499–500). Christian worship of Jesus, however, elevated him to near-parity with God and excluded all other divine beings, thereby "rob[bing] the one God of his proper due" (l.497–498) and setting up "a rival to the one high God" (l.430). Christians make Jesus "not because they are paying very great reverence to God but because they are exalting Jesus excessively" (*c. Cels.* 8.14; l.433–434).

### Demythologizing the Story of Jesus

Celsus is "the first critic to call Jesus a magician and charge the Christians with practicing magic" (l.164–165). He was willing to grant that Jesus actually performed wonders — "cures or resurrections or a few loaves feeding many people . . . related by his disciples" (*c. Cels.* 1.68; l.232–234) — but explained them as sorcery learned in Egypt: Jesus "was brought up in secret and hired himself out as a workman in Egypt and after having tried his hand at certain magical powers he returned from there, and on account of those powers gave himself the title Son of God" (*c. Cels.* 1.38; l.238–241). Jesus belonged among the "sorcerers who profess to do wonderful miracles . . . who for a few obols make known their sacred lore in the middle of the market-place" (*c. Cels.* 1.68; l.246–249).

On the **virgin birth**, Celsus claimed Jesus "fabricated the story of his birth from a virgin" (*c. Cels.* 1.28; l.557–558). His mother was a "poor country woman who earned her living by spinning" who became pregnant by a soldier named Panthera and "was driven out by her husband, who was a carpenter by trade, since she was convicted of adultery" (*c. Cels.* 1.32; l.560–562). Wilken notes this tradition may derive from Jewish circles and that "Panthera" is close to the Greek *parthenos* (virgin) (l.577–581).

On the **baptism**, Celsus imagines a Jewish interlocutor questioning Jesus: "What trustworthy witness saw this apparition, or who heard a voice from heaven adopting you as son of God? There is no proof except for your word and the evidence which you may produce of one of the men who were punished with you" (*c. Cels.* 1.41; l.597–599). Historical verifiability is the criterion.

On the **resurrection**, Celsus pointed to parallel claims in Greek religion (Zalmoxis, Pythagoras, Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus) and suggested the witnesses were unreliable: "a hysterical female" whose report could be explained by dreaming, wishful thinking, or deliberate fabrication (*c. Cels.* 2.55; l.619–642).

### Christianity as Apostasy from Judaism

This was among Celsus's most pointed structural charges. He put the accusation in the mouth of a Jewish interlocutor: "Why do you [Christians] take your origin from our religion [Judaism], and then, as if you are progressing in knowledge, despise these things, although you cannot name any other origin for your doctrine than our law?" (*c. Cels.* 2.4; l.687–690). See [[christianity-apostasy-from-judaism|Controversy: Was Christianity an Apostasy from Judaism?]] for the full argument.

### Religion and the Social Order

Celsus's deepest framework is his concept of *Nomos* — "the accumulated wisdom and practices of a particular culture" (l.1002–1003). He identified the "true doctrine" (*alēthēs logos*) with the "ancient doctrine" (*palaios logos*): "There is an ancient doctrine (logos) which has existed from the beginning that has always been maintained by the wisest nations and cities and wise men" (*c. Cels.* 1.14; l.1067–1069). Among these nations he included "the Egyptians, Assyrians, Indians, Persians, Ödrysians, Samothracians, and Eleusinians" (l.1069–1070). Truth is coterminous with antiquity because "men of earlier times, especially those who lived very long ago, were thought to have been closer to the gods" (l.1055–1057).

Celsus tolerated wide diversity in traditional religious practice: "There is nothing wrong if each people observes its own laws of worship" (*c. Cels.* 5.35; l.1111–1112). He could even praise Jewish custom — "they observe a worship which may be very peculiar, but it is at least traditional" (*c. Cels.* 5.25; l.1089–1091) — while condemning Christianity for having no tradition of its own: "I will ask them where they have come from, or who is the author of their traditional law. Nobody, they will say" (*c. Cels.* 5.33; l.1100–1104).

Politically, Celsus urged Christians to "help the emperor with all [their] power, and cooperate with him in what is right, and fight for him" (*c. Cels.* 8.73; l.866–868). He accused Christians of refusing military service and public office, creating "an obscure and secret society" (*c. Cels.* 8.17; l.973–974) that functioned as a revolutionary "counterculture" (l.978). He tied monotheism to imperial order: "If you overthrow this doctrine [one God = one king], it is probable that the emperor will punish you . . . while earthly things would come into the power of the most lawless and savage barbarians" (*c. Cels.* 8.68; l.1153–1156).

Wilken notes that Celsus saw Christianity as introducing a new "privatizing" of religion — transferring "religious values from the public sphere to a private association" (l.1143–1146) — which was in principle incoherent to an ancient mind for whom "religion belonged to a people" (l.1139).
