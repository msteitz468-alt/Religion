Victor P. Hamilton is an American evangelical Old Testament scholar, best known for his two-volume commentary on [[genesis|Genesis]] in the New International Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT) series. The volumes cover Genesis 1–17 (1990; Intro covers the whole book) and Genesis 18–50 (1995; commentary only), both published by William B. Eerdmans. He accepted the project at R. K. Harrison’s invitation and Dennis Kinlaw’s encouragement, noting that “the last and definitive word on Genesis has not yet been said, and probably never will be said” (V1 Intro/Preface).

## Hermeneutical Method

Hamilton occupies a position best described as **canonical evangelical**. He engages historical-critical scholarship — the [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]], form criticism (Gunkel), tradition history (Noth, Rendtorff), and archaeologically-grounded historical research — with genuine seriousness, but treats the **holistic canonical text** as the primary object of theological interpretation. Controlling conviction (Intro §IV Theology): “Theology must be based on the text as we have it, not on what it might once have been.”

**Mosaic authorship decoupled from evangelical conviction.** Hamilton does not wed an evangelical view of Scripture to Mosaic authorship: “We do not believe that an evangelical view of Scripture is necessarily wedded to the Mosaic authorship of Genesis… ‘Composite’ is not a synonym for ‘errant.’” He notes post-Mosaic contemporization (“until this day”) while judging that recent discourse grammar, rhetorical, and literary criticism tend to support essential unity of Genesis (V1 Intro §III Composition).

**On JEDP and composition.** He summarizes classic criteria (divine names; duplications; anachronisms; style/ideology) and the staged consensus (oral units → J/E → JE → P → JEP). He describes Cassuto’s case against JEDP as “the most convincing,” notes Kitchen’s critique that fragmentary composition-and-conflation is anomalous in the ANE, and engages Rendtorff’s six-block model — but judges Rendtorff largely renames multilayered Genesis and that stripping promises makes barren-heir narratives “pointless.” Against Van Seters’s Neo-Babylonian kerygmatic Abraham, Hamilton holds Van Seters has not replaced the Wellhausen consensus. He welcomes Kikawada/Quinn (Gen 1–11 rhetorical unity vs J+P patchwork), Rendsburg (purposeful structure; DH “untenable”), and Radday/Shore (computer study of narrative vs speech, not J vs E). Practical stance: source identification is legitimate scholarly work but must not displace attention to the canonical text’s theological coherence. On Gilgamesh/SP multiediting analogies: “A multiedited Gilgamesh Epic is empirically verifiable. A multiedited Genesis remains, at best, a hypothesis.”

**Literary/narrative criticism.** Hamilton draws substantially on Alter (type-scenes), Sternberg (narrative irony, characterization), and Fokkelman (stylistic analysis). He treats *tôlĕdôt* architecture, chiasmus, epic resumption (vs assumed doublets), heptads, and phonetic wordplay as theologically load-bearing, not merely aesthetic. In body commentary he repeatedly resists source-critical fragmentation when literary unity works (e.g., Flood YHWH/Elohim shifts as thematic participants; one-pair/seven-pair as general→specific; Gen 7 epic resumption; Table of Nations chiastic whole).

**ANE comparative method.** He engages Atrahasis, Enuma Elish, Gilgamesh, Ugaritic El-tradition, Mari, Nuzi, and Sumerian King List extensively — to establish cultural plausibility **and** theological distinctiveness (anti-mythical polemic: no theogony; luminaries serve not served; Flood moral motivation vs overpopulation/noise; rainbow as warrior-bow reinterpreted as peace sign). He resists Speiser/Nuzi parallelomania where evidence fails (with Van Seters/Thompson’s best critiques), while rejecting the inference that collapsing the Amorite Hypothesis falsifies Genesis itself: “what has fallen by the wayside is a scholarly hypothesis, not the Biblical text.”

**Promise theology on the holistic text.** Against Hoftijzer, Westermann, Rendtorff et al. treating promise speeches as secondary/late accretions, Hamilton remains “unconvinced”; even if redactional, theology must rest on the holistic text. Clines-style tallies (descendants, relationship, land) structure his reading of Gen 12–50; Abraham’s first and last divine words (12:1–3; 22:15–18) and Jacob’s (28:13–15; 46:3–4) bracket lives with promise.

**NT appropriation as standing section.** Each major unit includes explicit NT appropriation (John 1 / Col 1 on creation; Rom 5 / 1 Cor 15 on Adam; Rom 4 / Gal 3 / Jas 2 on Gen 15:6; Heb 11 on Akedah and Joseph; Rom 9 on Jacob/Esau; 1 Pet 3 / 2 Pet 2 / Matt 24 on Flood; Luke 10 seventy and Gen 10 nations, etc.) — reception history as a formal part of the commentary, not an afterthought.

## Characteristic Positions

**Generation → degeneration → regeneration (book arc).** Hamilton reads Genesis as moving “progressively from generation (chs. 1–2), to degeneration (chs. 3–11), to regeneration (chs. 12–50).” Primeval history shows individuals who have land and lose it; patriarchal history shows individuals who lack land and move toward it. Four-fifths of the book treats four generations (Abraham–Joseph) vs one-fifth on twenty (Adam–Abraham). Election of Abraham is not isolation but vehicle for all families of the earth: chs. 12–50 are “Reconciliation Regained” / “Hope Regained,” resolving 3–11 without recapturing 1–2 (V1 Intro §II–IV).

**Tôlĕdôt as prospective titles.** Ten *’ēlleh tôlĕdôt* formulae demarcate the book (2:4a; 5:1a; 6:9a; 10:1a; 11:10a; 11:27a; 25:12a; 25:19a; 36:1a/9a; 37:2a). Five introduce narrative (“this is the story of X”); five head genealogies. Hamilton prefers 2:4a as **superscript** to 2:4bff. (not subscript to 1:1–2:3): genitive is always of the progenitor, never the progeny; elsewhere the formula is introductory. He engages but rejects the Wiseman–Harrison eleven-tablet colophon theory (V1 Intro §II Structure). See [[toledot|Tôlĕdôt]].

**Gen 1:1 absolute beginning; creatio orientation.** Among four translation options for *berēʾšîṯ*, Hamilton defends absolute independent clause on lexical (Isa 46:9–10), versional, Masoretic (*ṭiphâ*), syntactic (Enuma Elish closer to 2:4b than 1:1–3), and stylistic (parataxis) grounds — not theology alone. Stakes: one (God) vs two (God + preexistent chaos). “The concept of the creation of chaos would be a contradiction in terms.” Gap theory and day-age concordism rejected; prefers literary/anti-pagan reading that may retain 24-hour *yôm* as analogy of divine work-week (V1 Gen 1; Intro §V.A). See [[creation-ex-nihilo|Creation ex Nihilo]], [[genesis-creation-accounts|Genesis: Two Creation Accounts]].

**Imago dei as holistic personhood / democratized royalty.** Gen 1:26 does not define the image as a faculty (reason, conscience, etc.): “to be human is to bear the image of God” — man as unity. *ṣelem/demût* democratizes ANE royal “image of God” language to all humanity; dominion is responsible care, not exploitation. Male and female both image-bearers; sexuality gift though foreign to God’s person. Preferred reading of “let us”: Hasel’s “plural of fullness” / Clines’s “duality within the Godhead” (God speaking to the Spirit of 1:2) — approaches trinitarian terminology without claiming Moses as trinitarian monotheist (V1 Gen 1:26–28). See [[imago-dei|Imago Dei]].

**Two creation accounts as complementary poles.** 1:1–2:3 stresses divine image, endowments, authority (“you shall,” 1:29); 2:4ff. stresses mundane origins and creation under authority (“you shall not,” 2:17). *Yahweh ʾĕlōhîm* compound joins sovereign material and moral control; serpent alone uses only *ʾĕlōhîm*. Neither account is more truthful. Knowledge of good and evil = **moral autonomy** (legal idiom of deciding good/evil), not sexuality or omniscience (V1 Gen 1–2).

**ʿēzer kĕnegdô as equality.** The “helper corresponding to him” (2:18) is neither superior nor inferior; *ʿēzer* most often describes Yahweh’s help to Israel — not subordinate status. Woman delivers man from solitude (V1 Gen 2:18).

**Fall and protevangelium.** Serpent is *nāḥāš*, creature made by God — no dualistic origin of evil. Gen 3:15: Hamilton keeps the traditional *protevangelium* label while urging caution on full messianic-prophecy claims; *šûp* same both times (“strike at”); *zeraʿ* flexible singular/collective. Woman’s *tešûqâ* illuminated by 4:7 as desire to dominate (contest for rule), not romantic Canticle sense. Grace before judgment: coats of skin before expulsion (// Cain’s mark; covenant before Flood). Penalty language centers expulsion/toil, not *môṯ* “die” in 3:17–19 (V1 Gen 3–4).

**Sons of God (6:1–4): deliberate non-dogmatism.** Three main views (angels; dynastic rulers; Sethite–Cainite intermarriage); Hamilton states options and concludes “it is impossible to be dogmatic… the evidence is ambiguous.” Marriage language not rape; 120 years preferred as Flood grace period; Nephilim distinct from *gibbôrîm* offspring (V1 Gen 6:1–4). See [[sons-of-god|Sons of God (Gen 6:1-4)]].

**Flood: moral, universal-leaning, anti-ANE-random; Noachian covenant unilateral.** Clear moral motivation vs Gilgamesh vagueness and Atrahasis overpopulation/noise. *ʾereṣ/kol-hāʾāreṣ* leans universal while local-land possibilities acknowledged. Flood un-creates (waters above/below); Noah finds (not wins) favor; covenant announced before Flood (grace precedes wrath); Hiphil *qûm* with animals included proves unilaterality; rainbow = warrior *qešet* reinterpreted as peace sign for God’s remembering. Noah as second Adam vs Atrahasis birth-control ending. Resists J/P doublets (pairs; divine names; dual dry reports; 8:20–22 // 9:1–17 as incipient → amplification) (V1 Gen 6–9). See [[flood-universality|The Flood's Scope]], [[covenant|Covenant]].

**Gen 15 and 17: one covenant, not two.** Against critical consensus (J 15 / P 17 as two accounts), Hamilton reads ch. 17 as **reconfirmation/maintenance** (Hiphil *qûm*, not *kāraṯ*) of the unilateral commitment of ch. 15. Gen 15: only the deity-symbol (smoking pot / flaming torch) passes between pieces — “That only something representing deity passes between the rows of flesh shows that this covenant is unilateral.” Thrice *ʿôlām* in ch. 17 (with behavioral expectations: walk before me, blameless, keep covenant, circumcise) emphasizes that expectations do **not** shift the covenant into a bilateral pact whose grounds are Abraham’s compliance: “the covenant remains a personal commitment by God in which he binds himself to this open-ended promise.” Circumcision is *ʾôṯ* of the covenant (// Sabbath; rainbow as three great covenant signs). Ishmael receives blessing/nationhood **without** covenant — “Divine sovereignty dictates the selection of one son over the other, quite apart from personal life-styles” (V1 Gen 15; 17). See [[covenant|Covenant]], [[circumcision|Circumcision]].

**God never clearly rebukes the patriarchs.** Despite wife-sister lies, Hagar initiative, Jacob’s exploitation/deceit, Judah–Tamar, Joseph’s framing: “God never clearly rebukes any of the patriarchs… So there is a ‘Why did Sarah laugh?’ but no ‘Why did Abraham lie?’ or ‘Why did Jacob deceive?’ or ‘Why did Joseph frame?’” From Babel (Gen 11) to Exod 32, followers of God largely lack judgment stories (except Er/Onan). Pre- vs post-Sinai accountability (Exod 16 manna without plague vs Num 11 plague) illustrates covenant signing changing responsibility. Gen 12–50 illustrates **God’s faithfulness**, not patriarchal morality models: “Even if the bearers of those promises represent the greatest threat to the promises, the individual lives of the promise bearers cannot abort those promises.” Childs’s “theocentric interpretation” of Gen 12–36 by the rest of the OT canon. At Bethel: “Far from fulminating against Jacob, Yahweh bestows on Jacob a catena of unconditional promises” (V1 Intro §IV; V2 Jacob cycle).

**Election as gratuitous sovereign will.** Younger over firstborn (Isaac/Ishmael, Jacob/Esau, Ephraim/Manasseh, Perez/Zerah) and unloved Leah over loved Rachel: “All of these bear witness to God’s gratuitous choice in which the factor in the choice is not age but God’s sovereign will.” Paul (Rom 9:10–13): election before works — “‘Jacob I loved’ … means ‘Jacob I have chosen.’” Moral consequences still operate (nemesis patterns), but election is not reward for virtue (V2 Gen 25–30; 48).

**Akedah (Gen 22): divine test of surrendering the promise-bearer.** Primary frame: *hāʾĕlōhîm* tested Abraham (not demonic temptation); reader knows, Abraham does not. Real intensity: not only beloved son but “the one person who can perpetuate the promises of God.” Hamilton insists the **whole narrative** (ram, stay-of-knife, subsequent promises) frames God as mercy + obedience, not v. 2 alone as cruelty. Resists making Isaac-Christ typology the interpretive center (though notes John 19:17 wood-bearing; Heb 11:17–19 resurrection faith on “we will return”). Yahweh-yireh names **God’s provision**, not Abraham’s obedience: “The reader will come away from this story more impressed with God’s faithfulness than with Abraham’s compliance.” Defends 22:15–18 as original climax balancing 12:2–3 (V2 Gen 22; V1 Intro). See [[akedah-interpretation|The Akedah: Interpretive Controversies]].

**Providence without dissolving human will (Gen 50:20).** Climax of the Joseph story: brothers *ḥăšaḇtem ʿālay rāʿâ*; God *ḥăšābāh leṭôbāh* — same verb *ḥāšaḇ*, double intention. Joseph refuses to play God (*hataḥat ʾĕlōhîm ʾānî* — contrasts Adam/Eve who sought God’s place); he is instrument never substitute. “God took their evil plan and turned it to good” for survival of many. Earlier: 45:5–8 (God sent me). Neither human malice nor divine purpose cancels the other (V2 Gen 45; 50:19–20). See [[divine-providence|Divine Providence]].

## Key Scholars Engaged

Hamilton interacts most substantially with: Cassuto (anti-JEDP), Von Rad (promise theology; *ḥāšaḇ* cultic-declaratory), Westermann (form criticism; Gen 1–11 themes), Gunkel (oral prehistory, saga), Speiser (ANE parallels/Nuzi — engaged and limited), Kitchen (Egyptology/chronology; DH anomalous in ANE), Rendtorff (tradition-history blocks), Cross (P as redactor), Van Seters and Thompson (patriarchal historicity challenge), Alter and Sternberg (narrative criticism), Kikawada and Quinn (rhetorical criticism/Atrahasis parallel), Rendsburg (literary structure), Clines (promise tallies), Childs (theocentric canonical reading; phenomenological myth), Hasel/Clines (plural of fullness / duality in Godhead on 1:26).
