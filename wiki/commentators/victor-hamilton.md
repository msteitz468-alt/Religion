Victor P. Hamilton is an American evangelical Old Testament scholar, best known for his two-volume commentary on [[genesis|Genesis]] in the New International Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT) series. The volumes cover Genesis 1–17 (1990) and Genesis 18–50 (1995), both published by William B. Eerdmans.

## Hermeneutical Method

Hamilton occupies a position that can be described as **canonical evangelical**. He engages historical-critical scholarship — including the [[documentary-hypothesis|Documentary Hypothesis]], form criticism, tradition history, and archaeologically-grounded historical research — with genuine seriousness but reads the canonical text as the primary object of interpretation. His controlling conviction: "Theology must be based on the text as we have it, not on what it might once have been."

**On Mosaic authorship**: Hamilton explicitly decouples evangelical conviction from the question of Mosaic authorship. "We do not believe that an evangelical view of Scripture is necessarily wedded to the Mosaic authorship of Genesis... 'Composite' is not a synonym for 'errant.'" This makes him unusual among NICOT contributors: he does not defend Mosaic authorship as a theological requirement.

**On JEDP**: He describes Cassuto's case against the Documentary Hypothesis as "the most convincing" yet does not fully adopt it. He notes Kitchen's critique that fragmentary composition-and-conflation is anomalous in ancient literature. He engages Rendtorff's block-of-tradition model as a viable alternative to standard JEDP. His practical stance is that source identification is legitimate but must not displace attention to the canonical text's theological coherence.

**On literary/narrative criticism**: Hamilton draws substantially on Alter (type-scenes), Sternberg (narrative irony, characterization), and Fokkelman (stylistic analysis). He treats the narrative artistry of Genesis as theologically significant, not merely aesthetic.

**On ANE parallels**: He engages them extensively and positively — Atrahasis, Enuma Elish, Gilgamesh, Ugaritic El-tradition, Mari — to establish cultural plausibility and theological distinctiveness. Genesis 1–11's monotheistic polemic against polytheistic cosmologies is a recurring emphasis.

## Characteristic Positions

**Primeval history as problem → solution**: The arc from Gen 1 to Gen 11 is degeneration (Fall, Cain, Flood, Babel). Gen 12 (Abraham's call) is God's answer to universal human failure. The two halves of Genesis are theologically interdependent.

**Imago dei as holistic personhood**: The image/likeness terminology in Gen 1:26–27 designates the whole human being as bearing God's image — not a specific faculty like reason or conscience. See [[imago-dei|Imago Dei]].

**Ezer kenegdo as equality**: The "helper" designation for the woman in Gen 2:18 does not connote subordination; the same Hebrew root (*ʿzr*) describes God's help to Israel. *Kenegdo* ("corresponding to him") signals complementarity, not hierarchy.

**God never rebukes the patriarchs**: A notably consistent theological observation: despite the patriarchs' moral failings (Abraham's deception, Jacob's fraud), God never directly rebukes them for these acts. Hamilton reads this as evidence that God's decretive will cannot be aborted by human unfaithfulness — a theocentric reading that resists moralizing.

**Providence without dissolving human will (Gen 50:20)**: Both human malice and divine purpose are simultaneously real in the Joseph narrative. The Hebrew *ḥāšaḇ* (to intend) is the same word used for both Joseph's brothers and God: they "intended" evil; God "intended" good. Neither intention cancels the other.

**The Akedah (Gen 22) as test of obedience, not Isaac typology**: Hamilton resists typological readings of Isaac as prefiguring Christ. The narrative's focus is Abraham's faith and willingness to surrender the one through whom the promises flow. NT typological use (Hebrews 11) is acknowledged but not the interpretive center.

## Key Scholars Engaged

Hamilton interacts most substantially with: Cassuto (anti-JEDP), Von Rad (promise theology), Westermann (form criticism), Gunkel (oral prehistory), Speiser (ANE parallels/Nuzi), Kitchen (Egyptology/chronology), Rendtorff (tradition-history), Alter and Sternberg (narrative criticism), Kikawada and Quinn (rhetorical criticism/Atrahasis parallel).
