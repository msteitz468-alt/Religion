Darrell L. Bock (b. 1953) is an American evangelical New Testament scholar, holder of a PhD from the University of Aberdeen. He is Research Professor of New Testament Studies at Dallas Theological Seminary and is widely regarded as a leading evangelical voice in Lukan scholarship. His *Acts* commentary in the Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament (BECNT) series (Baker Academic, 2007) is the largest single-volume Acts commentary in English.

## Hermeneutical Method

Bock positions himself within **informed evangelical thought** — committed to both scholarly rigor and theological conviction, neither dismissing critical scholarship nor uncritically adopting skeptical conclusions. His core conviction about Acts: Luke is a **careful ancient historian** comparable in precision and method to Thucydides, writing with "considerable restraint." Luke writes theology, but he remains interested in what actually happened. This combination — historical seriousness plus theological reading — defines Bock's approach throughout.

He explicitly defends Acts' **historical reliability** against the skeptical school (Dibelius, Haenchen) while engaging seriously with moderate-critical scholars (Barrett, Fitzmyer). He insists history and theology are not competing categories: a careful ancient historian can also be a committed theologian.

## Characteristic Positions

**Luke-Acts as a two-volume work**: Bock treats the [[luke-acts-unity|Luke-Acts]] unity as a non-negotiable starting point. Acts is volume 2 of one story, and failing to honor this two-volume architecture is a fundamental interpretive error. His motivation for writing the commentary was partly that previous exegetical work had failed to read Acts through this lens consistently.

**Lukan authorship**: Bock argues from external evidence — unanimous attribution by AD 200 — that Lukan authorship is "statistically improbable if invented," given the number of Pauline companions who could have been named. Internal evidence (the "we passages" indicating eyewitness participation) supports this conclusion.

**Dating (late 60s CE)**: Bock prefers composition before the fall of Jerusalem (70 CE), noting that Acts seems to sense the approaching crisis without yet describing it. He holds this with appropriate tentativeness; the 80–100 CE range remains viable.

**The speeches as authentic summaries**: Against Dibelius and Haenchen, who treat the speeches as Lukan literary compositions with little connection to apostolic tradition, Bock argues they are compressed but faithful summaries: their brevity (minutes to read aloud), theological distinctiveness across speakers, and integration into narrative settings argue against wholesale invention.

**Acts 11 = Galatians 2 (minority position)**: Bock holds that Acts 11 (Paul's famine visit to Jerusalem) rather than Acts 15 (the Jerusalem Council) corresponds to the Jerusalem visit Paul describes in Galatians 2. This reshuffles early Pauline chronology significantly: it means Galatians was written before the Jerusalem Council, likely from Antioch, to address a pre-Council situation. He follows Witherington's minority position here.

**Acts and Paul's letters**: Apparent tensions (Paul's temple participation, Timothy's circumcision, Paul's harmonious relationship with Jerusalem leaders) are resolvable by attention to genre and context. Paul's letters are occasional and address local crises; Acts presents a biographical and ecclesial narrative. Both are valid historical sources; neither is simply reducible to the other.

**Pentecost and tongues**: Tongues at Pentecost demonstrate the Spirit's "revelatory, declarative role" inaugurating the new messianic age. Bock does not engage the cessationist/continuationist debate explicitly but frames tongues as evidence of the Spirit's empowering rather than as a permanent requirement for all believers.

## Exegetical Method in the Acts Commentary

Across the BECNT *Acts*, Bock's procedure is consistent. Each pericope moves from an author's translation, through an "Additional Notes" section applying reasoned-eclectic text-criticism (he holds the **Alexandrian text** likeliest closest to the original, assessing Western/Byzantine variants "one at a time"), to a verse-by-verse exegesis attentive to Greek grammar and Greco-Roman/Jewish background, closing with an "In sum" section that carries the application.

His handling of the **Old Testament in Acts** is a signature. Bock reads the speeches' OT citations as **typological-prophetic** (a text about an individual "in a way that extends beyond that individual"), identifies first-century interpretive techniques — **gezerah shewa** (linking texts by shared catchwords, e.g. the chain across Peter's Pentecost sermon) and the **"this-is-that" (*touto estin*) pesher** form familiar from Qumran — and insists the christological readings do not require a late Hellenistic or LXX-only setting (a recurring rebuttal to Haenchen and Conzelmann). He argues Peter's Pentecost use of Joel 2, Psalm 16, and Psalm 110 yields a "high" Christology grounded only in belief in the resurrection-ascension, explicitly **not adoptionist**: "the resurrection made evident what Jesus always was."

Bock also foregrounds **narrative characterization** (following Gaventa and Brawley): the most important characters are God, Jesus, and the Spirit, and human figures are sorted into witnesses (Peter, Stephen, Philip, Barnabas, Paul), foils (Gamaliel, Sergius Paulus, Festus, Agrippa), God-fearers (the Ethiopian eunuch, Cornelius, Lydia), and opposers. On soteriology he frames Luke's emphasis as **forgiveness and the Spirit** rather than Pauline justification language, though he reads Paul's Pisidian-Antioch statement (13:38–39) as a genuine, if compressed, critique of the law's inability to justify.
