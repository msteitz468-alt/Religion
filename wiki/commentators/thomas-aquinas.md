# Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), the "Angelic Doctor" (*Doctor Angelicus*), was an Italian
Dominican friar, philosopher, and theologian, the foremost figure of medieval
**scholasticism** and the author of the [[summa-theologica|Summa Theologiae]]. Canonized
in 1323 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567, he gives his name to **Thomism**, the
theological-philosophical school that became, after Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879),
the normative framework of Catholic intellectual life.

## Biographical context

Born to a noble family near Aquino in the Kingdom of Sicily, Thomas studied at Monte
Cassino and the University of Naples, where he encountered Aristotle and the Dominican
Order, which he joined against his family's wishes. He studied under
Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus) at Paris and Cologne, taught at the University of
Paris and in Italy, and produced an immense body of work before his death en route to the
Second Council of Lyon in 1274. After a mystical experience in December 1273 he ceased
writing, leaving the *Summa* unfinished, reportedly saying that all he had written
"seems like straw" compared to what he had seen.

## Hermeneutical method

Aquinas's exegesis operates within the medieval **fourfold sense** of Scripture (the
Quadriga: literal/historical, allegorical, tropological/moral, anagogical — see
[[fourfold-sense]]), but he subordinates the spiritual senses to the **literal sense** in
a way that marks a turn in the tradition: at *ST* I, Q1, A10 he holds that the literal
sense is what the author (ultimately God) intends, that *all* the spiritual senses rest
upon it, and that "nothing necessary to faith is contained under the spiritual sense which
is not elsewhere put forward by the Scripture in its literal sense." Theological argument
may therefore proceed only from the literal sense.

His broader method is the synthesis of **Aristotelian philosophy** ("the Philosopher")
with Christian revelation: reason and faith are distinct but harmonious, since both derive
from God; philosophy is the "handmaid" that clarifies and defends what revelation
delivers. He draws constantly on [[augustine|Augustine]], Pseudo-Dionysius the
Areopagite, [[boethius|Boethius]], John Damascene, and the Arabic and Jewish
philosophers — [[avicenna|Avicenna]], [[averroes|Averroes]] (the "Commentator"), and
[[maimonides|Maimonides]] (often "Rabbi Moses").

## Major works

- **[[summa-theologica|Summa Theologiae]]** (c. 1265–1274) — his systematic masterwork,
  unfinished.
- *Summa contra Gentiles* — an apologetic synthesis for use with non-Christians.
- *Scriptum* (Commentary on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard) — his earlier systematic work.
- Aristotelian commentaries (on the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *Nicomachean Ethics*,
  *De Anima*, etc.).
- Scriptural commentaries (on Job, the Psalms, the Pauline epistles, the Gospel of John,
  the *Catena Aurea*).
- Disputed questions (*De Veritate*, *De Potentia*, *De Malo*).

## Characteristic positions

- **Existence of God demonstrable, not self-evident to us** (*ST* I, Q2): the proposition
  "God exists" is self-evident in itself but not to us; it is demonstrated *a posteriori*
  from effects, via the **Five Ways**.
- **Divine simplicity** (Q3): God is wholly without composition — his essence is his
  existence (*ipsum esse subsistens*).
- **Analogical predication** (Q13): terms applied to God and creatures are neither
  univocal nor purely equivocal but **analogical**. See [[analogy]].
- **Primacy of the literal sense** in scriptural interpretation (Q1, A10).
- **Eternity of the world not demonstrable either way** (Q46): that the world began is
  held by faith, not proved by reason — a position distinguishing him from Bonaventure, who held
  the world's temporal beginning to be rationally demonstrable.
- **The rational soul as the single substantial form of the body** (Q76), against
  pluralist accounts.

## Influence on later tradition

Thomism became the dominant Dominican theology and, through the Neo-Thomist revival
inaugurated by *Aeterni Patris*, the de facto official philosophy of the modern Catholic
Church. His thought shaped Cajetan, John of St. Thomas, the Salamanca school, and
twentieth-century figures (Maritain, Gilson, Garrigou-Lagrange). The Protestant Reformers
engaged him selectively — appreciative of his doctrine of God, critical of his
sacramental theology.
