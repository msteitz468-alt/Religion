# Gregory of Nazianzus

Gregory of Nazianzus ("the Theologian," c. 329 – c. 390) was the third of the [[cappadocian-fathers|Cappadocian Fathers]] and the one who, after [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]]'s death, presented the Cappadocian vision in orations of such rhetorical brilliance that they became fundamental points of reference for later Eastern theology. In Johannes Zachhuber's analysis ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]]), Nazianzen represents a distinct strand within Cappadocian thought: more deeply shaped by the **Origenist** concern to preserve the Father's primacy than by the anti-Eunomian logic that drove Basil and [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]].

## Biographical Context

Born c. 329; a close friend of Basil from their student years (they are said to have jointly edited the *Philocalia* of Origen, though Zachhuber notes this tradition has been questioned). Their relationship was never easy and broke down after Gregory felt strong-armed by Basil into ordination as bishop of the obscure see of Sasima (372). After Basil's death and Theodosius I's accession, Nazianzen was suddenly elevated to the centre of the imperial Church and presided briefly over the Council of Constantinople (381) as the recognized architect of the new anti-homoian settlement — owed, Zachhuber suggests, largely to the plausibility of his self-presentation as guardian of Basil's legacy. After failing to steer the Council to a successful completion he withdrew, spending his last years preparing his literary legacy.

## Hermeneutical and Philosophical Method

Nazianzen is primarily a **rhetor and theologian** rather than a system-builder; his philosophy appears in defensive, polemical orations against Eunomians and Pneumatomachians, above all the five **Theological Orations** (Or. 27–31).

- **Abstract account, when confronting Eunomians.** In *On the Son* (Or. 29) he uses Basil's logic: as there is one defining account (*logos*) of "horse," "ox," "human," so there is one *ousia*, one nature, and one appellation "God," predicated univocally — against the anomoian claim that "God" applies to the Son only equivocally.
- **Origenist emphasis on the Father as cause.** Yet Nazianzen consistently foregrounds the asymmetry of the persons: the Father is "greater" *as cause* (τῷ αἰτίῳ μείζων, glossing John 14:28) though not "greater in nature." He fears the word "origin" (*archē*) lest it make the Father the origin of *inferior* beings. Where Basil's particularizing properties often seem merely formal, Nazianzen finds the ultimate significance of the Trinity precisely in these relational distinctions.
- **Symmetric, imagistic language too.** He can also write of the Trinity in nearly symmetric terms — "three in individualities or hypostases... but one in respect of the substance," united without confusion, divided without separation, "equally far from the confusion of Sabellius and the division of Arius" — using his famous variation on the sun-and-ray image: one light flashing forth in three suns/beams.

Zachhuber reads this variety not as inconsistency to be explained away but as illustrating the **integrative power of Basil's abstract theory**: it was flexible enough that Nazianzen could accept it while retaining major elements of his preferred Origenist-Apollinarian (derivative) account, on the understanding that the persons' individual properties characterize them *as persons* without impinging on the identical divinity each possesses. He walked a fine line and, by Zachhuber's account, occasionally strayed to both sides of it.

## Major Works

- **The five Theological Orations** (Or. 27–31, delivered in Constantinople, 380) — the classic defense of the full divinity of Son and Spirit against Eunomians and Pneumatomachians; foundational for later Eastern Trinitarian theology.
- **Orations 2, 20, 39, 40** and others — further Trinitarian statements (the "greater as cause" texts; the baptismal address).
- Poems and letters.

## Characteristic Positions

- One *ousia* / nature of God expressed by a common defining account; the persons co-ordinate in divinity.
- Strong relational asymmetry: the Father as the one ultimate origin/cause of the Godhead (his defense against tritheism), within the limits of full consubstantiality.
- The Trinity as a mystery to be confessed without "fusing" (Sabellius) or "cutting up" (Arius) the Godhead.

## Reception and Title

Honored in the East as "the Theologian" — one of only three figures so titled (with John the Evangelist and Symeon the New Theologian). His orations were enormously influential and heavily commented; Zachhuber notes that previous interpreters' concentration on the Theological Orations has shaped (and perhaps skewed) the modern picture of his thought.

## Ayres on Nazianzen (Nicaea and Its Legacy)

Lewis Ayres ([[nicaea-and-its-legacy|*Nicaea and Its Legacy*]], 2004) treats the five
**Theological Orations** (summer/autumn 380, preached at the Anastasia) as "a manifesto" of
Gregory's [[pro-nicene-theology|pro-Nicene]] mission in Constantinople. Gregory's distinctive
move is to make the **generative divine nature** the eternal point of departure: "Monotheism…
is what we value — not… the sovereignty of a single person… but the single rule produced by
equality of nature (φύσεως ὁμοτιμία), harmony of will, identity of action… a one eternally
changes to two and stops at three" (*Or.* 29.2). He develops a near-**perichoresis** — a
"convergence towards the source" (σύννευσις, drawn from Plotinus) by which the persons "cleave
together… not so as to coalesce" — and treats **relation** (σχέσις) as the category upholding
"paradoxical unity in distinction": "'Father' is a name neither of substance nor of activity,
but of relationship" (*Or.* 29.16). His running through and rejecting of analogies (*Or.* 31)
exemplifies pro-Nicene **analogical reserve**. Ayres notes Gregory's parallel to
[[gregory-of-nyssa|Nyssa's]] anti-tritheist argument (*Or.* 31.13–15, "structurally identical")
— and that Gregory later openly disapproved of the council's reticent pneumatological clauses.
