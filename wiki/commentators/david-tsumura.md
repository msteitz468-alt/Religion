David Toshio Tsumura is a Japanese evangelical Old Testament scholar at Japan Bible Seminary in Tokyo. He was trained in Semitic philology — especially Ugaritic and Canaanite religion — under the late Cyrus H. Gordon, whose work on East Mediterranean cultural unity fundamentally shaped the comparative-contextual approach to the Hebrew Bible. Tsumura's NICOT commentary on 1 Samuel (2006) is the most philologically specialized volume in the series; he has also published on 2 Samuel and on the creation account in Genesis. He acknowledges especially the commentaries of P. K. McCarter (AB) and R. P. Gordon as his primary dialogue partners, noting that he "often disagree[s] with them."

## Background

Tsumura works in the context of an East Asian evangelical academy — Japan Bible Seminary, which has a broadly Reformed evangelical orientation, and Hamadayama Church. His formation under Gordon connected him to the school of comparative Semitics that reads the Hebrew Bible in the light of Ugaritic literature, Akkadian texts, and the broader ANE literary tradition. This is a different lineage from both the German historical-critical tradition (Noth, Wellhausen) and the Anglo-American literary-critical school (Alter, Fokkelman). He dedicates the commentary with 2 Cor 12:9 in Greek — "my power is perfected in weakness."

## Hermeneutical Method

Tsumura's method is best described as **historical-philological with holistic literary sensitivity**. He is not a postmodern literary critic and explicitly rejects reader-response and deconstructive approaches (Miscall, Derrida) as producing readings that cannot be checked against authorial intention. At the same time, he endorses the insights of the new literary criticism (Alter, Fokkelman, Sternberg) for attending to the final form of the text. His own distinctive contribution lies in several specific techniques:

### 1. MT Prioritization

Tsumura wages a sustained argument against the dominant practice of emending the MT Samuel based on the LXX, 4QSam^a, and other versions. His position: many proposed emendations are unnecessary because they mistake three categories of authentic MT features for scribal error:

**Phonetic spellings**: The narrative was composed as an "aural" text — written to be heard. Unusual spelling forms sometimes record actual pronunciation rather than copying mistakes. Example: *wattazrēnî* (2 Sam 22:40) — usually emended — is explicable as a sandhi spelling of *watte'azzerēnî*.

**Brachylogy**: Idiomatic ellipsis of expected elements. When an idiom has become fixed, its standard component can be omitted while the idiomatic meaning is retained. Example: *happîlû* ("decide/cause to fall," 1 Sam 14:42) omits *gôrāl* ("lots") by brachylogy — the verb alone carries the idiom's meaning.

**AXB patterns (literary insertion)**: A rhetorical device in which an element (X) is deliberately inserted between the two parts of a grammatically unified expression (AB), creating suspense and slowing narrative tempo. Critics who do not recognize this technique mistake the disruption for displacement and propose needless emendations. Example: 1 Sam 10:2 — "Rachel's tomb [A] in the territory of Benjamin [X] in Zelzah [B]" — the territorial qualifier is inserted between "Rachel's tomb" and its geographic modifier.

This position places Tsumura further right than any other ingested NICOT commentator on textual matters — more conservative than Woudstra, who does not develop text-critical arguments explicitly.

### 2. Semitic Philology

Using Ugaritic, Akkadian, Old Aramaic, and other Northwest Semitic cognates, Tsumura makes lexical contributions unavailable from grammar alone:

- *ḥămôr leḥem* (1 Sam 16:20) — a calque of Akkadian *imēru* ("ass-measure"), an archaic dry measurement already obsolete by the LXX's era; a postexilic editor would not have preserved it
- *ṣemed* (1 Sam 14:14) — the Akkadian *ṣimdu* land-area measure, not "yoke" in the sense of oxen
- *ṭeḥōrîm* vs. *'ăpālîm* for "tumors" (1 Sam 5-6) — *ṭeḥōrîm* is the technical list-formula term; *'ăpālîm* is the narrative-register term
- "Band of prophets" (*ḥebel nebî'îm*, 10:5) — compared with the Mari *āpilum* group, whose oracular activity was collective and non-ecstatic; Tsumura resists characterizing the prophesying as irrational

The ability to read Ugaritic literature directly allows Tsumura to identify verbal and structural parallels unavailable to commentators without this training.

### 3. Discourse Grammar Analysis

Tsumura applies supra-sentential grammatical analysis — examining how narrative discourse is structured at the paragraph and section level:

- **Wayqtl vs. weqtl forms**: *wayqtl* (waw-consecutive + "imperfect") marks narrative mainline; *weqtl* (waw + perfect) marks predictive discourse. The analysis of 1 Sam 10:2-8 as predictive discourse (Samuel's predictions) vs. 10:9-12 as narrative mainline (fulfillment) is based on this distinction.
- **Background vs. foreground clauses**: Parenthetical information is signaled by non-*wayqtl* forms; recognition of these signals resolves many apparent chronological contradictions.
- **AXB and other insertion patterns**: See above.

### 4. ANE Comparative and Contextual Approach

Tsumura follows his mentor Gordon and W. W. Hallo's "contextual approach" — using every available piece of ANE evidence (Assyrian inscriptions, Hittite apologies, Mari letters, Ugaritic epics, Akkadian chronicles) to illuminate and illustrate the biblical text. He is explicitly "maximalist" on historicity: "The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." He notes that Assyrian royal inscriptions use the same phraseology over four centuries — this directly undermines the argument that particular phrases must be late because they appear in "late" texts.

## Relationship to the Deuteronomistic History

Tsumura is strongly skeptical of the DH hypothesis. His objections:

1. **Circularity**: Expressions are labeled "Deuteronomistic" based on their appearance in Kings, then any similar expression in Samuel is said to be a late Deuteronomistic interpolation — but the reasoning is circular.
2. **Archaic vocabulary**: Terms in Samuel that were obsolete by the exilic period (see Semitic philology section) would not have been used or preserved by a postexilic editor.
3. **ANE analogy**: Assyrian scribes used standard phraseology over centuries without being "late." The presence of recurring phrases does not establish late redaction.

His tentative conclusion: the major compositional blocks of 1-2 Samuel existed during the United Monarchy era; the final editing was done no later than the late 10th century BCE. This aligns him closer to Woudstra's pre-critical skepticism toward the DH than to Bruckner's or even Webb's modified acceptance.

## Theology

**YHWH's holy sovereignty**: YHWH is king by nature and creation — not by conquest over chaos (unlike Marduk or Baal). His sovereignty is not contested within the narrative; it is the frame within which all human kingship is evaluated.

**The king as vice-regent**: The proper title for the Israelite king is *nāgîd* ("prince/designated one") — YHWH's earthly deputy ruling YHWH's estate (*naḥălāh*). Even after the establishment of the monarchy, Israel remains a theocracy in principle. The failure to grasp this is Saul's fundamental failing.

**Obedience to the prophetic word**: The stated purpose of 1 Samuel: "the king in Israel is to be subject to the prophet through whom God conveys his word." Both of Saul's rejections are structured by this principle. The Messianic climax: "This is what Jesus the Messiah-king did in his life of obedience to God the Father, 'even unto the death of the cross' (Phil. 2:8)."

**Providence in the ordinary**: God guides through mundane events (Saul's donkey search; David's food errand), not in "virtual reality." Romans 8:28 is Tsumura's NT summary of 1 Samuel's providential theology.

**Reversal of fortune**: Hannah's song announces the pattern that drives every major character arc in 1-2 Samuel. The Lord brings low and lifts up; human expectation is consistently inverted by divine choice.

**Messianic trajectory**: Samuel, Saul, and David are "forerunners and heralds of the real King." The Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7) becomes the theological pivot that Matt 1:1 names explicitly: "Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham."

## Characteristic Positions

- **1 Sam 8 (monarchy request)**: The sin is not in wanting a king per se but in the motive — "like all the nations" — and the implied transfer of trust from YHWH to a human military figure. YHWH grants the king as an accommodation; the grant does not validate the motive. See [[monarchy-request|The Monarchy Request]].
- **1 Sam 10:10-12 ("Is Saul also among the prophets?")**: The proverb is originally neutral (surprise at transformation); it accrues a negative valence at 19:24 through contextual shift — not through a negative view of prophecy itself.
- **1 Sam 15 (Saul and Agag)**: Samuel's execution of Agag is not "sacrificial butchering" (contra McCarter) but the completion of the *ḥerem* that Saul neglected. The theological climax of the chapter: disobedience to YHWH's word = the sin of divination = wickedness and idolatry (15:23). See [[herem-canaanite-conquest|The ḥerem and Canaanite Conquest]].
- **God's "regret" (15:11 vs. 15:29)**: Two distinct senses of *nḥm*: (a) emotive grief at Saul's failure; (b) irrevocable judicial decision. The apparent contradiction is contextually resolved, not glossed over.
- **"The spirit of the LORD rushed upon"**: The *rûaḥ YHWH* appears in 1-2 Samuel exclusively in connection with YHWH's anointed (Saul, then David). Its departure from Saul (16:14) is YHWH's enacted rejection made visible.

## Additional Contributions from 2 Samuel (2019)

The 2 Samuel volume (published 2019, 13 years after 1 Samuel) refines and defends the same methodological approach. Tsumura explicitly acknowledges critics of the 1 Samuel volume (Chapman: "lacks theological depth"; Klein: "ignored LXX/DSS evidence") and doubles down on his position. New contributions include:

**Genre classification**: 1–2 Samuel as **intermediate between epic (Judges) and annalistic (Kings)**. Judges uses round numbers for judgeships; Kings records regnal years systematically; Samuel combines both registers. This is a distinctive literary judgment.

**Discourse analysis of 2 Sam 7**: The oracle of Nathan (7:8b-16) has traditionally been rendered entirely in future tense. Tsumura demonstrates via discourse grammar that vv. 9b-11aβ are **past procedural discourse** ("how-it-was-done" — explaining YHWH's ongoing past dealings with David), with the actual future promise beginning only at v. 11b. This resolves the contradiction between v. 1b ("the LORD had given him rest") and the conventional future rendering of v. 11a.

**Speaker-oriented *ʿal-kēn*** in v. 22: Grammatical analysis showing the particle introduces a comment on a different discourse level ("because of A, I say B"), not a logical consequence — with theological implications for YHWH's greatness.

**David's repentance in Psalm 51**: Tsumura defends the psalm title's historical authenticity and analyzes it as a literary unity (personal confession + royal Zion prayer), arguing against postexilic dismissal.

**Intertextuality critique**: Engaged but critically. The wise woman of Tekoa (2 Sam 14) is Tsumura's test case against postmodern intertextuality (Lyke's Bakhtinian reading): "Any given narrow constituent element can be found in any corpus of ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature" — the danger is "word association games" uncontrolled by context. He insists on reading ambiguity only where the author intended it.

**"Human desires and divine plans"** as the governing theme of all 1-2 Samuel — Tsumura's most theological synthesis in the 2 Samuel volume, extending his previous theme of providential guidance.
