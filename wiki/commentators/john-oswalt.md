John N. Oswalt is Professor of Old Testament at Asbury Theological Seminary, representing the Wesleyan-Arminian / Holiness theological tradition — a significant departure from the Reformed tradition that has characterized most of the wiki's other NICOT commentators (Waltke, Woudstra, Craigie, Hamilton, Webb). His two-volume NICOT Isaiah commentary (1986 and 1998) is the largest commentary in the wiki and covers the most NT-quoted OT book.

Oswalt's theological tradition emphasizes the transformative sanctification of believers (the Wesleyan doctrine of "entire sanctification") — a concern that influences his reading of chs. 56-66 as the demanding outworking of servanthood in real life.

## Background

The commentary represents over two decades of work at Asbury, where Oswalt also taught at Trinity Evangelical Divinity School. Unlike Waltke (Reformed, Westminster Theological Seminary) or Longman (broadly Reformed, Westminster and Westmont), Oswalt comes from the Wesleyan revival tradition which prizes both scriptural authority and the transforming work of the Holy Spirit in producing holy character — themes that run through his reading of Isaiah's call to servanthood.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Single Authorship as Hermeneutical Commitment

Oswalt's most distinctive and contested methodological commitment: the book of Isaiah comes from one human author, Isaiah of Jerusalem (ca. 740-701 BCE), who wrote predictively by divine inspiration. This is not merely a conservative instinct but a carefully argued position:
- The multiple-authorship hypothesis requires "Second Isaiah" to manufacture evidence for the very theology he proclaims (God's ability to predict the future)
- No independent fragment of "Second Isaiah" has ever been found in any form
- Recent critical scholarship increasingly admits the ideological unity of the book while struggling to explain it
- Single authorship requires accepting predictive prophecy — which Oswalt argues is not arbitrary supernaturalism but is embedded in the book's own theological claims

This hermeneutical commitment leads to reading chs. 1-66 as a single theological composition organized around the theme of servanthood.

### 2. Holistic Theological Interpretation

Oswalt argues forcefully that Isaiah cannot be interpreted piecemeal. Each section must be read in light of the whole, and the whole must be read as the theological framework for each part. The fragmentation of the book into "I, II, and III Isaiah" — interpreted separately as if they were independent books — has done serious damage to the book's canonical function.

Specifically: chs. 1-39 cannot be fully understood without chs. 40-66 (because the questions raised by chs. 7-39 require chs. 40-55 for answers), and chs. 40-66 cannot be understood without chs. 1-39 (because the theology of chs. 40-55 is organically derived from chs. 1-39).

### 3. The "Holy One of Israel" as Theological Center

The phrase *qĕdôš yiśrāʾēl* (29 occurrences in Isaiah vs. 7 elsewhere in the OT) is Oswalt's governing theological concept. Holiness = ontological otherness + moral/ethical perfection. Both dimensions are inseparable: God's transcendence is expressed in his justice; his justice is expressed through his compassion; his compassion presupposes his holiness. This theological center provides the interpretive key for understanding Isaiah's sarcasm about idolatry, his view of sin as rebellion, and his vision of redemption as the sharing of God's own character with his people.

### 4. Servanthood as the Book's Unifying Theme

Every major section of the book — from the call of chs. 1-5 to the eschatological promises of chs. 60-66 — is organized around the question: How can sinful Israel become the servant of God? The answer requires working through three problems: trustworthiness (chs. 7-39), motivation (chs. 40-48), and means (chs. 49-55), before the servant life can be lived out (chs. 56-66).

## Key Theological Positions

**Single authorship of Isaiah**: Defended on the grounds of internal logical necessity, no contrary manuscript evidence, and the self-defeating nature of the multiple-authorship hypothesis for the book's central theological claims.

**The Holy One of Israel**: The foundational theological concept with two dimensions — ontological otherness and moral/ethical perfection. The book's pervasive sarcasm about idolatry follows logically from God's holiness.

**Sin as pride/rebellion**: All human evil reduces to the refusal to accept creatureliness and dependency on God. This produces the invariant sequence: alienation → unfaithfulness → oppression → destruction.

**Substitutionary atonement in 52:13-53:12**: The Servant's suffering is substitutionary and divinely planned, not merely exemplary. "Many will be accounted righteous because he bore their iniquities" (53:11). Without ch. 53, the free grace of chs. 40-48 cannot be realized — there must be a moral and judicial satisfaction.

**The Servant's identity**: "Both/and" — not collective Israel (the Servant restores Israel; Israel cannot suffer to redeem itself) and not a purely individual figure separate from Israel's identity. The Servant is the One who incarnates both ideal servanthood and divine power (53:1 = "arm of the LORD"), making it possible for the nation to become what they were called to be.

**Cyrus as *māšîaḥ***: The prediction of Cyrus by name (44:28; 45:1) is central to the book's theology, not an embarrassment to be explained away. This is the book's proof text for God's ability to predict — if it is not predictive, the theology built on it is groundless.

**Holistic canonical function**: Isaiah is "the bridge between the Already and the Not Yet" — the book that demonstrates most comprehensively that the Messiah is not a contradiction of the Law and Prophets but their fulfillment.

## Volume 1 Profile (from the 2026-07-18 fidelity re-ingest)

Positions and habits documented verbatim from the chs. 1–39 volume (see
[[oswalt-isaiah-1-39-nicot|source page]] for loci):

- **Text-as-received rule**: "Our mandate is to interpret the text as it is before us unless
  there is manuscript evidence to correct that text. To do anything else is to build our
  interpretations upon air." He repeatedly rejects excision/rearrangement proposals (2:6–22;
  5:25; 22:15–25; chs. 24–27 stratifications) while accepting 1QIsaᵃ-supported corrections
  (14:4; 21:8; 33:8; 37:27–28).
- **ANE comparanda without myth-adoption**: Leviathan/Lotan, Yam, the Baal cloud-rider, the
  *ǵlmt* cognate, Athtar — the prophet "was appropriating well-known emotive language into
  which [he] could put new meaning," never "adopting a mythological worldview."
- **Signature exegetical resolutions**: dual-focus 7:14 (initial fulfillment "perhaps"
  Maher-shalal-hash-baz; ultimate, the Messiah); 8:16 sealing as attestation of Scripture
  rather than withdrawal from ministry; 14:12–15 as human pride, with the Reformers against
  the patristic Satan-reading; 28:16 cornerstone as "the whole complex" of God's revealed
  faithfulness "summed up in Jesus Christ"; chs. 24–27 as eschatological, not apocalyptic;
  26:19 with 25:8 as "the highest conception of resurrection in the OT."
- **Prediction as load-bearing**: "To insist that the predictions were written after the fact
  is to assert that the great exponents of ethical righteousness in human history falsified
  their evidence"; the 701 deliverance (37:36) is "the crux of the entire account."
- **Method polemic**: the failure to take the present form seriously "must stand as one of
  the modern critical movement's most serious defects"; dating by theology is "extremely
  tenuous and often betrays a distinctly circular flavor."
- **Homiletic register**: modern applications throughout (Hitler/Germany 1939–45, F-16s and
  Main Battle Tanks, "Modern humanism is simply ancient idolatry in a three-piece suit or a
  pair of designer jeans"), Wesley citations, biblical-authority asides (on 8:20).
- **Amanuensis concession** (endnote): he would accept amanuenses in the book's production,
  but "cannot conceive of the present unity being arrived at without the guiding hand of a
  single master."

## A Key Tension With Hartley

Hartley (NICOT Job) argued that Isaiah was influenced *by* Job — that Job's figure of the suffering righteous man provides the prototype for Isaiah's Servant Songs, which in turn provides the basis for NT Christology. Oswalt's commentary presents the Servant Songs as Isaiah's own prophetic revelation, without discussion of Job as a prior source. This tension — the direction of influence between Job and Isaiah — is documented in [[controversies/isaiah-job-servant-direction|Isaiah-Job: Direction of Servant Influence]] (to be created).
