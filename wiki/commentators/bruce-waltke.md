Bruce K. Waltke is among the foremost conservative evangelical OT scholars of the 20th–21st centuries. A Hebrew philologist and biblical theologian, he taught at Westminster Theological Seminary, then Regent College (Vancouver), then Reformed Theological Seminary (Orlando), all within the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition. He is a member of the NIV Committee on Bible Translation and co-author of *Biblical Hebrew Syntax* (with M. O'Connor), the standard reference for advanced Hebrew grammar in evangelical scholarship.

His NICOT commentary on Proverbs (two volumes, 2004 and 2005) is among the most technically ambitious in the series and represents over 25 years of research and teaching.

## Background

Waltke's approach is defined by three commitments:
1. **Grammatico-historical exegesis**: words and grammar must be interpreted in their historical context; theological conclusions must be grounded in careful philological and literary analysis
2. **Reformed canonical theology**: Scripture is a unified revelation from the triune God; wisdom literature belongs within, not alongside, Israel's covenant faith
3. **Ancient Near Eastern contextualization**: Israel's wisdom is best understood in comparison with the pan-oriental wisdom tradition, but that comparison must be theologically controlled

He distinguishes himself from critical scholars by accepting the Solomonic attributions as historically plausible (on the basis of Kenneth Kitchen's structural analysis) and from von Rad by rejecting the thesis that Woman Wisdom represents a divine cosmic order (*ma'at*) accessible apart from special revelation.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Grammatico-Historical with Canonical Integration

Each proverb is first interpreted on its own two feet (independently), then in its literary context (proverb pairs, units, sections, collections), then in the canonical context of the whole book and of Israel's Scripture. The prologue (Collection I) is the non-negotiable hermeneutical key: it defines the epistemological foundation (*yir'at YHWH*) within which all subsequent proverbs must be read.

### 2. Poetics as Prerequisite to Exegesis

Waltke provides the most systematic treatment of biblical poetics in the wiki. The hierarchy: sounds → words → versets → verses → proverb pairs → units → sections → collections. Before asking *what* a text means, the interpreter must know *how* it means — the grammar of literature (poetics) is prior to the grammar of meaning (hermeneutics). This applies especially to:
- **Terseness**: Proverbs are epigrammatic — each expresses one truth that must be qualified by counterproverbs; the genre effect of a primer for youth causes it to emphasize future vindication over present suffering
- **Parallelism**: B verset is never simply synonymous with A — it advances, specifies, intensifies, or qualifies
- **Concentric and chiastic structures**: Especially in Collection I and in Solomon I's groupings

### 3. ANE Comparative Contextualization

Waltke's ANE treatment is the most extensive in the wiki. He uses Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Ugaritic parallels extensively to illuminate form, structure, vocabulary, and social setting — while insisting on the theological distinctiveness of Israel's wisdom: "Pagan wisdom, though it, too, may be religious, has no anchor in the covenant-God."

**Text-critical base.** Waltke works primarily from the Leningrad Codex (following the Bomberg edition at 8:16), holding the MT "a reliable witness to the original text." He makes "little use" of the two fragmentary Qumran Proverbs manuscripts (4Q102, 4Q103), treats the LXX as "both free and creative" (with Tov and Childs he assumes the priority of the MT recension), and cites the Greek only where a literal rendering preserves an original reading against the MT (e.g. 1:15; 3:3; 8:5). He regards H. C. Washington's thesis that the LXX "created the fiction of Solomonic authorship" by rearranging the collections as evidence *for* the priority of the MT sequence.

### 4. Typological Christology

The theology section's most distinctive contribution: Woman Wisdom is *not* a direct identification with Christ but a **type** of Christ — similar in structure but inferior in degree. This positions Waltke between the Arian (Woman Wisdom = a creature, used to subordinate Christ) and the uncritical patristic position (Woman Wisdom = Christ directly) — both exegetically inadequate when applied to the original text of Proverbs 8. The typological reading maintains the hermeneutical legitimacy of the Christological reading while avoiding its overreach.

## Key Theological Positions

**"Fear of the LORD" as compound epistemological foundation**: Not natural theology, not mere moral intuition, but a unified rational + non-rational response to YHWH's special revelation. "Fear" and "love" are synonyms in Deuteronomy; both are dimensions of the same covenantal relationship with YHWH.

**Personal divine retribution**: Against K. Koch's "deed-destiny nexus" (which reduces God to a first cause within an impersonal order), Waltke argues that YHWH personally upholds justice. The structure of Solomon I places Yahweh sayings at the beginnings and ends of units to provide the theological frame for the adjacent "world-order" proverbs — an intentional editorial strategy.

**Life (*ḥayyîm*) = eternal fellowship with God**: "Life" in the majority of Proverbs texts refers to abundant life in fellowship with God, a relationship not envisioned as ending at clinical death. Proverbs teaches immortality (not resurrection). This requires justice to extend beyond clinical death — the righteous must be vindicated beyond the grave.

**Woman Wisdom as type, not hypostasis**: The prologue's Woman Wisdom is a literary personification of Solomon's revealed wisdom. She is not a heavenly being, not *ma'at*, not Christ directly identified — but a legitimate type of Christ, with the asymmetries Christ's superiority requires.

**Solomonic authorship**: Defended on Kitchen's structural grounds, linguistic evidence, and the coherence of the attributions. A final editor compiled the canonical book in the Persian period.

## Method across the Second Volume (15:30–31:31)

The second volume, being commentary only (the whole-book Introduction sits in Vol. 1), displays
Waltke's method most densely at the level of the individual unit. Recurring moves: demarcating
units by **catchword, janus verse, inclusio, and chiasm** (e.g. the "breached wall" inclusio
framing the "seven perverted types," 25:28–26:28; the righteous/wicked framing couplets
organizing chs 28–29); a consistently **conservative text-critical stance** that defends "the
more difficult MT" against LXX paraphrase and *metri causa* emendation (e.g. retaining "silver
dross" against the Ugaritic "glaze" reading at 26:23); and a habit of reading prophetic technical
terms as taking a **wisdom nuance** in Proverbs — *qesem* (16:10), *ḥāzôn* (29:18), and *maśśāʾ*
(30:1; 31:1) all denote the sage's inspired revelation rather than mantic or prophetic vision.
His two most consequential Vol. 2 judgments for the wiki are the disciplined account of the
**Amenemope** borrowing in Collection III (dependence limited to 22:16–23:11, theologically
controlled by trust in the covenant God) and his defense of the **historical** (non-allegorical)
reading of the [[valiant-woman|valiant wife]] as the counterpart, not the equivalent, of
[[woman-wisdom|Woman Wisdom]]. He treats [[agur|Agur]] and [[lemuel|Lemuel]] as genuine
non-Solomonic authors whose collections the "inspired editor" added to a "developing canon,"
against the LXX's rearrangement to preserve Solomonic authorship of the whole.
