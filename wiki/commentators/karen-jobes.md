# Karen H. Jobes

Karen H. Jobes (Ph.D., Westminster Theological Seminary, Philadelphia) is an American evangelical
New Testament scholar and Septuagint specialist. At the time of her BECNT *1 Peter* (2005) she was
associate professor of New Testament at Westmont College (Santa Barbara, CA); she later became the
Gerald F. Hawthorne Professor of New Testament Greek and Exegesis at Wheaton College Graduate School
(the same institution as [[douglas-moo|Douglas Moo]]). She is the wiki's first solo-authored woman
commentator, and one of relatively few women to write a major exegetical commentary in a flagship
evangelical series.

She is best known in two areas: **Septuagint studies** — she co-authored, with her doctoral mentor
Moisés Silva, the widely used *Invitation to the Septuagint* — and the **General/Catholic Epistles**
(commentaries on 1 Peter and on 1–3 John, plus an earlier NIVAC volume on Esther). Her LXX expertise
decisively shapes her NT exegesis. The principal source page for her 1 Peter commentary is
[[jobes-1-peter-becnt|Jobes, *1 Peter* (BECNT)]].

## BECNT Method Apparatus

Jobes writes within the **Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament** frame set out by series
editors Robert W. Yarbrough and Robert H. Stein. As she and the series present it:

- Blend scholarly depth with pastoral readability; primary audience is “pastors and others involved
  in the preaching and exposition of the Scriptures as the uniquely inspired Word of God.”
- Prefer **paragraph-level** exposition (argument flow, function in context) over atomistic
  verse-chopping when the discourse warrants it.
- Account for “the latest scholarly research, regardless of its source,” without either dismissive
  proof-texting of critical scholarship or assimilation that erases evangelical theological
  distinctives; traditional views are not sacrosanct, but not abandoned for fashion.
- Shared series commitments: trustworthiness and essential unity of Scripture; historic creeds and
  Reformation documents as legitimate readings; theological assumptions not treated as obstacles to
  interpretation.
- Apparatus conventions she inherits: shading for intro/concluding summaries; half-brackets for
  textual variants; author-date documentation (with standard exceptions: BDAG, LSJ, TDNT).

Her own Author’s Preface frames the constraint of writing after two millennia of reflection: hard
to say something “new enough to warrant another commentary but not so innovative as to be
heretical,” while presenting the “interpretive heritage of the Christian church in a fresh light.”
She dedicates the volume to her husband; Moisés Silva invited the project; Al Pietersma supplied a
pre-print of NETS (still forthcoming at writing).

## Three Distinct Contributions (Author’s Preface, denser evidence)

Jobes states three programmatic contributions of [[jobes-1-peter-becnt|the BECNT volume]]:

### 1. New historical-background theory (Roman colonization / displacement)

Interpretive tradition, she argues, assumed indigenous Christians of Asia Minor converted either by
Peter on travels or by anonymous evangelists from Pauline churches. Jobes proposes instead that the
readers were **converted elsewhere — probably Rome — and then displaced to Asia Minor**. Peter, with
whom they had some previous association, writes to these “foreigners and resident aliens,” using
their personal situation to lend power to his spiritual application of the motif.

**Supporting reconstruction** (Introduction, Recipients):
- **Claudius** (AD 41–54) established *coloniae* in **all five** regions of 1:1 (Pontus, Galatia,
  Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia) — Jobes: “perhaps only Claudius” did so across that exact set (e.g.
  Neoclaudiopolis, Claudiconium, Archelais, Claudiolaodicea, Bithynium-Claudiopolis).
- Colonies often populated by veterans, freedmen, volunteers, and sometimes deportations;
  noncitizens (*peregrini*; Gk. related terms, including *parepidēmos*) were common targets of
  suspicion.
- Claudian expulsion of Jews for disturbances at the instigation of “Chrestus” (Suetonius, *Claudius*
  25; Acts 18:2) could have included Christians still viewed as a Jewish sect (Scramuzza via Jobes).
- Explains: rapid spread without a named northern-Asia-Minor founder; deep LXX literacy among
  readers; Peter’s association via Rome; Greek as shared language; mild persecution profile;
  “elders among you” without city-*ekklēsia* addresses; lifestyle evangelism vs. overt proselytizing
  that may have triggered Roman expulsion.
- **Admitted weakness:** surviving evidence is meager; the letter “makes no direct reference to such
  an event”; only “possibilities and probabilities.” See [[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter: Authorship,
  Date, and the Colonization Theory]] and [[christian-exile-diaspora|Christian Exile / Diaspora
  Identity]].

### 2. LXX accessibility (not proof-texting)

“It was the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (OT) that formed the scriptural context
in which Peter wrote. Peter does not proof-text… but applies the context of the passage as it occurs
in the LXX.” Method “truer to the historical origin of the letter.”

**In the body** this is not slogan but practice: Isa 53 LXX woven through 2:21–25 in passion order
(not Isaiah’s order); Ps 33 LXX (34 Eng.) as ethical matrix (taste at 2:3; *paroikia* at 33:5 LXX //
1:17; full quote 3:10–12); Isa 40 co-text for 1:24–25; stone-complex from Ps 117 / Isa 8 / 28 / Exod
19 / Hos. Jobes treats the LXX translator’s Diaspora recontextualization (e.g. pointing yielding
*paroikian* “sojournings” at Ps 33:5) as material Peter can exploit. Primary literary source of the
letter = Greek OT; Schutter: more oral Christian sources than written NT dependence.

### 3. Bilingual-interference syntax analysis

Questions “the oft-repeated opinion about the high quality of the Greek of its author”; concludes
“the syntax exhibits elements consistent with a Semitic-speaking author for whom Greek was a second
language.” Full treatment in the end-of-book excursus (earlier form: *BBR* 13.2 [2003]: 159–73). See
**Syntax Excursus** below.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Grammatical-historical with a Septuagint-first lens.** Signature commitment: the **LXX is the
primary scriptural and literary context** of 1 Peter. Interpreting the letter means reading it
against the Greek OT as Peter and his readers would have known it — context, not isolated proof-texts
(e.g., saturating use of Ps. 33 LXX [34 ET]).

**Syntax criticism / bilingual interference.** Against the near-universal claim that 1 Peter’s Greek
is “too good” for a Galilean fisherman, Jobes analyzes *syntax* — which, unlike deliberate rhetorical
ornamentation, “flows almost subconsciously” — and finds **bilingual interference** consistent with a
Semitic L1 author for whom Greek was L2. See excursus section.

**Sociohistorical reconstruction.** Engages sociological readings (Elliott, Balch, Volf) and
proposes the colonization theory, while resisting reductive single-cause models of the social
setting. On culture: with Miroslav Volf, **differentiated acceptance and rejection** (not Elliott’s
pure identity-maintenance vs Balch’s pure accommodation binary); household codes “uphold and subvert”
Greco-Roman order.

**Evangelical-critical.** Interacts seriously with Achtemeier, Elliott, Goppelt et al. without
treating critical conclusions as foregone; defends traditional authorship where she finds the case
inconclusive. On Silvanus (5:12): *dia Silouanou … egrapsa* more likely **courier** idiom (papyri,
Ignatius, Acts 15:23) than amanuensis; amanuensis still possible for Silvanus or Mark but “must be
decided on other grounds.”

## Syntax Excursus: S-Number Method and Limits

*Excursus: “The Syntax of 1 Peter: How Good Is the Greek?”* — method adapted from Jobes’s dissertation
on LXX syntax (Jobes 1995: 29–47), building on R. A. Martin’s seventeen criteria of Semitic
interference (1974).

### Method

- **Problem:** opinion on 1 Peter’s Greek quality is often based on “subjective feel”; both sides of
  the authorship debate frequently assume the Greek is “too good for Peter himself.”
- **Aim:** quantitative comparison of syntax elements with other NT books, Josephus, and Polybius —
  **not** (at method stage) an attempt to defend or refute Peter’s authorship or to identify an
  amanuensis.
- **Why syntax first:** operates “at an unconscious level”; L1 interference appears frequently there;
  if Semitic interference can be shown, “possibilities for authorship would at least be circumscribed.”
- **Scale:** for each criterion, **–1 = composition-Greek norm**, **+1 = translation-Greek norm**.
  Composition norms from Plutarch, Polybius, Epictetus, Josephus, documentary papyri; translation
  norms from thousands of LXX lines with extant Semitic sources.
- **Profile + S-number:** seventeen (or available) criteria plotted; **S-number** = normalized average.
  Semitic source/interference → values toward 0 to +1; no Semitic influence → toward 0 to –1.
- Rhetorical ornamentation (anaphora, parallelism, etc.) is real but “not nearly as much as… Hebrews”;
  Latin rhetorical labels (*exordium*, *narratio*) do not prove formal training.

### Findings (four interference areas)

Only **14 of 17** Martin criteria occur with sufficient frequency in 1 Peter; of those, **nine on
the Semitic side**, five on the composition side. Jobes highlights four areas:

1. **Prepositions** (criteria 3–6, 8) — high *en* frequency vs native proficiency; prepositions
   “notoriously the most difficult element of a new language to master.”
2. **Genitive personal pronouns** (12–13) — Semitic suffix patterns; more dependent genitive
   pronouns and more on anarthrous substantives.
3. **Attributive adjective position** (14) — enough postpositive attributives to pull Semitic-ward
   (precede 22×, follow 16× in 1 Peter).
4. **Dative with *en*** (17) — higher *en* + dative frequency indicates Semitic influence.

**Controls:** LXX quotations **eliminated** from the primary counts still leave Semitic interference;
with quotations included, 1 Peter’s S-number rises to **0.28** (near some Greek OT books). Syntax is
“not nearly as ‘good’ as Polybius, or even as good as Josephus,” if “good” = native-proficient style.
1 Thessalonians shows **S-number 0.37** (more Semitic than 1 Peter); seven criteria similar in
frequency — interesting if Silvanus is linked to both, **but not** same-author proof. Hebrews 5–9
shows overall **negative** S-number (composition-side), closer to Polybius/Josephus.

### What Jobes claims — and does not claim

**Does claim:** (1) major conclusion — Semitic interference indicates an author whose **first
language was not Greek**; (2) quality of 1 Peter’s Greek “ought nevertheless not be exaggerated”
(with Achtemeier); (3) opens path to Semitic L2-Greek authors against Achtemeier’s perception of
“absence of influence by Hebrew or Aramaic”; (4) **unlikely** that the author was a native Greek
speaker; (5) favors quantitative profiles over subjective “feel.”

**Does not claim:** (1) does **not** prove Petrine authorship — “not an attempt to defend or refute
Peter’s authorship”; syntax “not sufficient for determining who the author… was”; (2) does not prove
Silvanus wrote 1 Peter or that 1 Peter and 1 Thessalonians share an author; (3) does not identify an
amanuensis; (4) formal vs informal rhetorical skill re: whether someone like Peter could have achieved
that level remains open.

**Integrative logic (still attributed):** the traditional chain “Greek too good for Peter → need
Silvanus amanuensis or pseudonymity” rests on contested bilingualism assumptions and subjective
quality judgments; quantitative syntax undercuts the “too good” premise **without** by itself
establishing apostolic authorship. See [[1-peter-authorship|1 Peter: Authorship, Date, and the
Colonization Theory]].

## Characteristic Positions (1 Peter, BECNT 2005)

**1. The Roman colonization theory.** Central new proposal: recipients not indigenous Asian converts
evangelized *in situ* but Christians converted *elsewhere — probably Rome —* displaced to Asia Minor
via Claudian colonization and/or expulsion. See denser evidence under Three Contributions §1 and
[[1-peter-authorship|the authorship/colonization page]].

**2. Petrine authorship and an early date.** Four classic anti-Petrine arguments (Greek quality;
persecution *Sitz*; church structure; Pauline dependence) plus “Babylon” as post-70 code are all
inconclusive on her reading. Epistolary pseudonymity in *personal correspondence* was not a clearly
accepted, victimless convention (3 Corinthians, Tertullian’s presbyter, Galen). Verba Christi
allusions, early theology, mild/sporadic ostracism, and undeveloped elder structure all lean early.
A date within Peter’s lifetime — early 50s or mid-60s — becomes plausible. Case against Petrine
authorship “no longer appears… ‘overwhelming.’”

**3. The “foreigner” motif as both literal and spiritual.** Against pure metaphor and against
Elliott’s pure socioeconomic *paroikoi*, Jobes holds real historical displacement *and* spiritual
truth that all Christians are resident aliens where gospel values clash with society. See
[[christian-exile-diaspora|Christian Exile / Diaspora Identity]].

**4. 3:18–22: ascension victory, not descent into hell.** Christ’s “going and preaching” to “the
spirits in prison” is *ascension proclamation of triumph* over the disobedient fallen angels of
Noah’s day (1 Enoch background), framed by Noah–baptism typology — not *descensus*, and not (against
Grudem) Christ preaching through Noah. See [[spirits-in-prison-1-peter-3|the controversy page]].

**5. 4:6: no postmortem second chance.** “The gospel preached to the dead” = those now dead who
heard it while alive; she severs it from 3:19 and rejects postmortem conversion.

**6. Peter’s creative, early Christology.** Not derivative of Paul; independently creative and
“antiquated” (Schlatter; R. P. Martin: emphases that “quickly dropped out”). Flagship: **original
extensive identification of Jesus with the Isaiah 53 Suffering Servant** (2:21–25) — passion sequence
orders LXX phrases, not Isaiah’s order; starts from Jesus’ death and searches the OT. Unique NT
contributions she lists: Servant Christology; 3:18–22 spirits/Noah; living-stones image (2:4–8). See
[[servant-songs|Servant Songs]].

**7. Closing (5:12–14).** “This is the true grace of God. Stand fast in it!” = letter’s exhortations
and teaching (including suffering as integral to Christian calling). Silvanus as likely courier;
“she in Babylon chosen with you” = Christian community sharing exile status (inclusio with Diaspora
1:1); Mark = John Mark, traditional interpreter (Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 3.39.15). Babylon = Rome as
referent is modern consensus, but significance is Diaspora-exile framing, not apocalyptic anti-Rome
code; composition at Rome “leans much more heavily on tradition than on exegesis.”

## Relationship to Other Commentators in the Wiki

- **Within BECNT**: she joins [[thomas-schreiner|Schreiner]] (Romans), [[douglas-moo|Moo]]
  (Galatians), and [[darrell-bock|Bock]] (Acts) as a BECNT contributor; her volume is the series'
  entry on 1 Peter. She later became Moo's colleague at Wheaton.
- **Septuagint method**: her LXX-first approach contrasts with the OT-template biblical theology of
  [[roy-ciampa-brian-rosner|Ciampa–Rosner]] (1 Corinthians) — both privilege the OT, but Jobes works
  specifically from the *Greek* OT's wording and context.
- **General Epistles**: alongside [[douglas-moo|Moo]] (James), [[gareth-lee-cockerill|Cockerill]]
  (Hebrews), and [[i-howard-marshall|Marshall]]/[[colin-kruse|Kruse]] (Johannine Epistles), she
  expands the wiki's coverage of the non-Pauline letters.
