Rashi (1040–1105 CE) is the most widely read Jewish biblical and Talmudic commentator in history. His commentaries on the [[talmud-bavli|Talmud]] and the [[genesis|Torah]] are printed in virtually every standard edition of those texts and remain the gateway commentary for students entering either. He was selected, writes [[isaac-unterman|Unterman]], "apparently by God to become the greatest and most beloved of all commentators."

## Biographical Context

Born in Troyes, France, Rashi studied at the yeshivot of Mainz and Worms under students of [[rabbenu-gershom|Rabbenu Gershom ben Judah]] ("Light of the Diaspora"), then returned to Troyes where he founded his own academy. He supported himself as a vintner. He had no sons, but three scholarly daughters whose husbands and children continued his interpretive work — their additions over two centuries are collected under the name [[tosafot|Tosafot]].

## Hermeneutical Method

Rashi's method combined two registers:

1. **Peshat** (plain meaning): He gave the *common-sense* meaning of a passage first, based on grammar, syntax, and narrative logic. He is thus a practitioner of peshat commentary, though never rigidly so.

2. **Derash** (midrashic/homiletical)**: Rashi drew extensively on midrashic and Talmudic interpretation, integrating it with the plain sense when it illuminated the text. He was honest about the distinction: when he used symbolic or homiletical meaning, he said so and did not pass it off as the plain sense.

Rashi describes his approach as seeking the "golden middle road between the common-sense meaning of a passage and its symbolic explanation." He was humble when he could not offer a satisfactory explanation and explicit when he gave symbolic meaning. This honesty made his commentary trusted by generations of students.

His Talmud commentary focuses on making the text accessible to readers who lack the oral interpretive tradition: he explains Aramaic vocabulary, clarifies technical legal discussions, and paraphrases compressed arguments. With Rashi's commentary, the Talmud "became an open book to everyone," in Unterman's formulation.

## The Tosafot

Rashi's three daughters married scholars of high ability. His grandchildren and their students made improvements and additions to his Talmud commentary over more than two centuries. These additions — critical engagements, alternative readings, and expansions — are collected as the **Tosafot** ("additions"). The greatest Tosafist was **Rabbenu Tam** (Rabbi Jacob ben Meir, c. 1100–1171), Rashi's grandson and one of the most incisive legal minds in Ashkenazic history. His main work is *Sefer ha-Yashar*.

## Influence

Rashi's Torah commentary was the first dated Hebrew book printed (Reggio di Calabria, 1475). The standard Talmud page layout positions his commentary on the inner margin, making it inseparable from the Talmud text itself in all standard editions. Christian Hebraists of the Renaissance and Reformation era (including Nicolas de Lyre and, through him, Luther) were heavily influenced by Rashi's interpretations.

In the Ashkenazic tradition, no commentary rivals Rashi's in pedagogical authority.
