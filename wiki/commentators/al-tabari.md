# Al-Tabari

**Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari** (c. 224/839 – 310/923) is the towering figure of
classical Sunni scholarship — at once the foremost early Qur'an commentator and the greatest early
Muslim historian. A Persian from Tabaristan (modern northern Iran) who mastered Arabic as a second
language, he settled in Baghdad and produced two monumental works that remain reference points to
this day: a Qur'an commentary and a universal history.

## Major Works

- **Jami' al-bayan 'an ta'wil ay al-Qur'an** ("The Comprehensive Exposition of the Interpretation of
  the Verses of the Qur'an") — the foundational work of *tafsir bi'l-ma'thur* and the single most
  influential Sunni commentary; it surveys, quotes, and weighs the interpretations of his
  predecessors verse by verse before giving his own judgment.
- **Tarikh al-rusul wa'l-muluk** ("History of the Prophets and Kings"), the **Annals** — a universal
  history from creation through 302/915, organized by the same *isnad*-based reporting; the principal
  narrative source for early Islamic history. *(The de Goeje Arabic critical edition of the Annals is
  held in `raw/`, but in Arabic and only partially; the wiki's coverage here derives from
  [[lawrence-quran-a-biography|Lawrence]]. The complete English [[quran|Qur'an]]-commentary translation
  and the SUNY translation of the Annals remain to be ingested.)*

## Hermeneutical Method

Al-Tabari is the classical exemplar of **[[tafsir|tafsir bi'l-ma'thur]]** — tradition-based exegesis
that interprets the Qur'an first **by the Qur'an** (collating verses that share vocabulary), then by
the **hadith** of the Prophet, then by the reports of the Companions. Lawrence's worked example: he
glosses the "those who incur wrath / those who go astray" of the [[quran|Fatiha]] (1:7) by cross-reading
Q 5:60 and 5:77, then *universalizes* the reference (Jews and Christians as representative of *all*
who might stray, including Muslims) rather than leaving it merely polemical.

Two methodological hallmarks the wiki should note:

- He treats **ta'wil and tafsir as effectively synonymous** — "going back to first principles" *is*
  expounding the wording — a quiet but pointed move against the Shi'ite use of *ta'wil* for a
  hidden, Imam-reserved sense (see [[allegorical-reading|allegorical reading]]).
- He sorts the verses into **Clear Signs** (*muhkam* — interpretable by any competent reader of
  Arabic) and **Ambiguous Signs** (*mutashabih* — e.g. the mysterious opening letters), insisting the
  ambiguous be approached only after mastering the clear, and condemning divinatory letter-mysticism.
  Crucially, the ambiguous category is **narrow** for him (a few passages), not the "quarter of the
  Qur'an" the Shia assign to the Imams' exclusive reading.

## Characteristic Positions

A committed **[[sunni-islam|Sunni]]**: he honors Ali and the Prophet's household yet upholds the
legitimacy of the full Rightly-Guided Caliphate (including Abu Bakr and Umar), and rejects the
Shi'ite claim of a hidden Imamic sense as flawed in its assumptions. As a Persian he read the
Qur'an partly as a non-Arab, stressing that divine favor flowed to Persian converts as well as Arabs,
and reading Muhammad as the successor and confirmation of **Abraham** (through Hagar and Ishmael).
He understood the Qur'an as **i'jaz** — inimitable in its order and composition, defeating all
imitators — and as "the Bible multiplied," fulfilling and surpassing earlier scripture.

## Influence on Later Tradition

*Jami' al-bayan* became the **backstop of mainstream Sunni exegesis**; later commentators
(including, in purged form, the reception of [[al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]]) define themselves in
relation to it. His method even reached the Latin West: **[[robert-of-ketton|Robert of Ketton]]**
consulted al-Tabari's commentary (and the Shi'ite al-Tabarsi) when making the first Latin translation
of the Qur'an. His own *Jariri* school of law did not survive him, but his scholarship did, totally.
