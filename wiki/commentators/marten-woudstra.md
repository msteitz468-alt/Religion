Marten H. Woudstra was a Dutch Reformed Old Testament scholar at Calvin Theological Seminary (Grand Rapids) and a member of the translation committee that produced the New International Version. His *The Book of Joshua* in the New International Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT; series editor R. K. Harrison; Eerdmans, 1981) is the wiki’s first full-scope ingested commentary on a book of the Former Prophets. It sits in the same conservative evangelical NICOT stream as [[victor-hamilton|Hamilton]] (Genesis) and [[peter-craigie|Craigie]] (Deuteronomy), but applied to a historical narrative with acute theological and ethical difficulties (conquest, *ḥerem*, land, miracles).

In the Author’s Preface, Woudstra places Joshua “at the crossroads” of an OT discipline he sees as in unprecedented flux, and states that he dialogues with critical viewpoints while sometimes disagreeing “fundamentally” on revelation, inspiration, and what biblical theism implies for historical science (Intro Preface). Interpreting Joshua, for him, means eliciting meaning already present in the work: the interpreter listens ultimately to God—“the great Someone Else”—and Spirit-guided exegesis gives access to what God says on the sacred page (Preface). Meaning in the comprehensive sense, he insists, inevitably involves contemporary relevance: “there simply is no such thing as irrelevant meaning” (Preface).

## Hermeneutical Method

### Theocentric, not anthropocentric, historiography

Woudstra’s overriding methodological principle is **theocentric, redemptive-historical historiography**. In his reading of Joshua as head of the Former Prophets, the books’ intent is interpretative (“prophetical”) history of God’s covenant dealings with Israel from Moses’ death to the Babylonian captivity—letting divine revelation illumine the events’ true meaning (Intro I).

He argues at length that biblical narrative is not a collection of moral examples (the “Bible story” approach) but a record of God’s redemptive acts. Human agents—including Joshua himself—are subordinate to the divine purpose; they are participants in a drama whose author is God, not paradigms primarily to imitate. Joshua 24’s prophetic survey, he holds, centers what the covenant Lord has done; that theocentric emphasis—not moral example—elicits loyalty (Intro I). Scripture presents historical facts to show history *en route* to a goal: dynamic movement from earlier to later and last (Intro I). Even passages often taken as pure example-models (1 Cor. 10; Heb. 11) he reads in “already / not yet” terms.

He applies this principle against two distortions he names:

- **Moralizing homiletics**: lifting characters and events out of redemptive-historical context to derive timeless “lessons.” The example method, he claims, cuts readers off from the true scriptural meaning of Joshua and the Former Prophets; morality becomes the watchword, but “morality as such cannot save” unless woven into redemption’s fabric (Intro I).
- **Liberation theology’s Exodus example-method**: treating the Exodus motif as mainspring for political change. Woudstra insists that in the Bible the Exodus is *redemption*—not mere physical deliverance from oppression but freedom from sin’s bondage; God’s triumph over Pharaoh manifests superiority over Egypt’s gods; Exodus vocabulary enters comprehensive, Christ-effected redemption language (Intro I).

This makes him more restrictive on the applicability of biblical narrative than most of his NICOT contemporaries and puts him in direct tension with [[james-bruckner|Bruckner]]’s sympathetic engagement with liberationist hermeneutics.

### Exegesis as grammatical-literary, historical, and theological (integral)

For Woudstra, biblical exegesis has three components—**grammatical-literary, historical, and theological**—and the theological is *integral*, not a later add-on, because the literature purports to be God’s word; the aim is the mind of the Spirit / knowledge of God (Intro VI.1). He rejects reducing a book’s “theology” to the intention of a secondary human author (e.g. a deuteronomistic school) as leaving out inspiration and revelation. He also rejects Stendahl’s descriptive-vs-normative dilemma as framing: Scripture does not merely “contain” theologies; one derives theology by reflecting in faith on revelation-contents; total exegesis aims at past, present, and future understanding (Intro VI.1).

Biblical historiography, though anchored in space and time, sets forth God’s dealings with Israel and the nations—not general national history—focusing progress of revelation and establishment of God’s kingdom. It must not be judged by alien scientific-historiography standards; Woudstra rejects von Rad–type cleavage between faith-picture and scientific reconstruction when that cleavage refuses Christian theism as regulatory for historical science (Intro V). Against Noth’s claim that Israel’s history is outwardly multi-interpretable (*vieldeutig*), Woudstra holds that Scripture knows no mute facts and does not allow more than one interpretation once facts are related to the God who manifests himself in them (Intro V). Emphasis in Joshua, he argues, lies chiefly on God’s “giving” the land, even while Joshua’s generalship is highlighted (Intro V).

### Literary-structural approach and unity of composition

Despite a traditionalist critical stance, Woudstra pays significant attention to **Hebrew narrative technique** as evidence of unity rather than failure of redaction:

- **Prolepsis and episodic construction**: What looks like a “seam” or “duplicate tradition” to source critics is often episodic narrative returning to a scene already summarized—e.g. spies’ pursuit (2:7/22), Rahab’s rope (2:15–16ff.), Ai burnings (8:19, 28) serving different purposes (signal vs final destruction) (Intro III).
- **Suspense and pacing**: Jordan-crossing narrative builds suspense through delayed disclosure; harvest-full banks parenthetically delay the miracle to highlight impossibility (Josh 3–4).
- **Chiastic and frame structures** (Intro III): hortatory first and last chapters as prophetical setting; southern campaign frame 9:1–2 // 10:40–43; promise 1:3, 6 // 11:16ff.; Transjordan charge 1:12–18 // 22:1–6; Achan’s *maʿal* (ch. 7) mirrored by potential *maʿal* at the altar controversy (ch. 22); Caleb materials framing Judah allotment (14:6–15 // 15:13–19); Caleb opens and Joshua closes allotment (19:49–50); faith at allotment’s first phase (14:6–12) vs hesitation later (18:2–3); Caleb’s claim vs Judah’s failure at Jebus (15:63).

He reports that he initially did not expect much structure in Joshua, but came to see materials arranged to support the book’s total message—warranting “a basic unity of composition of the book as it now lies before us,” *not* to the exclusion of diverse sources but by means of them (Preface; Intro III). His three-part outline of contents: (I) Promised Land Conquered (1–12); (II) Distributed (13–22); (III) to Be Kept in Covenant Obedience (23–24) (Intro IX).

### Critical stance: Hexateuch, documentary hypothesis, DH

Woudstra rejects both the **Hexateuch hypothesis** (extending J/E/P/D into Joshua as a six-book unit) and Noth’s **Deuteronomistic History** as the primary compositional control:

- He is “unable to accept” the documentary hypothesis; if Deuteronomy may be assigned to the Mosaic age (citing [[peter-craigie|Craigie]]), “the very foundation of the documentary hypothesis has been removed” (Intro II). Final D rather than P as dominant redaction in Joshua is, for him, an embarrassment to the documentary model (Intro II).
- He reports Noth’s traditio-historical model (etiological legends + hero legends; Gilgal sanctuary; continuing history from Deuteronomy through 2 Kings) as modifying but not radically breaking with the documentary hypothesis (Intro II).
- Against Hexateuch: Jewish tradition places Joshua first among the Former Prophets, not with the Pentateuch; Samaritans accept only the Torah—together, he argues, a “convincing argument against the critical position of an alleged Hexateuch” (Intro VIII).
- On date: he prefers a relatively early setting—history-view possibly product of days when Israel “served the Lord” (24:31), i.e. Joshua and the elders who outlived him—citing internal indicators (Gezer still Canaanite, 16:10; Jebus unconquered, 15:63; 13:6 omits Tyre; Kaufmann on Levitical cities antiquity; narrative vividness) while allowing use of sources (Jashar 10:13; allotment records 18:9) (Intro II). He does not make Mosaic authorship of Joshua a theological necessity.

On Judg. 1 vs Joshua, he refuses rival “true/false” conquest portraits: if Judg. 1 presents events *after* Joshua’s death (as 1:1 claims), both can be true; Joshua itself already holds complete-fulfilment language (11:23; 21:43–45) beside unfinished work (13:13; 15:63; 16:10; 17:12–13; 23:4–5) (Intro II).

### Archaeology stance

Woudstra grants archaeology value for authenticity of biblical traditions (customs, architecture, warfare, literary forms) but stresses hard limits: site identification uncertain; few inscriptions; layer chronology disputed; few sites fully excavated (Intro V). His stated preference “lies more with the ‘German’ school than with the archaeological school” (Albright / Bright / Wright)—greater concentration on biblical texts—**without** accepting German negative stance on Joshua traditions’ veracity. He believes there was a military conquest of Palestine (contra Alt/Noth gradual interpenetration), yet archaeological evidence is hard to regard as satisfying all questions (Intro V). On conquest dating he reports early (~1400) vs late (~1250) stalemate; Jericho (Garstang vs Kenyon) and Ai/et-Tell leave archaeology “silent where eloquence [is] wanted”; 13th-c. burn layers cannot prove Israelite agency (Intro V). At Jericho and Ai in the commentary body he repeats: archaeology’s role is valuable but limited. Text integrity is the prime historical datum; he believes Joshua’s concerted-onslaught picture “true and reliable” as prophetical historiography for exhortation, warning, and encouragement—not as exhaustive scientific chronicle (Intro V).

### Typology

Woudstra practices **restrained biblical typology**—grounded in symbolism already present for ancient Israelites, not coincidental verbal parallels or arbitrary allegorization (Intro VI.2):

- Joshua–Jesus name connection (*yehôšuʿa* / LXX–NT *Iēsous*, “the Lord is Salvation”) (Intro I).
- “Rest” typology in Heb. 4:8 as genuine—Joshua’s rest as partial fulfilment pointing to eschatological Sabbath rest; rest’s horizons expand through Pentateuch, Joshua, David/Solomon, then NT (Intro VI.2; Josh 21:43–45 / ch. 23).
- Canaan’s spiritual dimension as opening symbolico-typical significance (Christian hymnody’s heavenly rest)—while rejecting Liberation theology’s use of Canaan as paradigm for righting social wrongs as the controlling reading (Intro VI.2).
- Scarlet cord / Rahab-as-Church: Church Fathers’ readings “must be handled with great care”; color coincidence alone will not suffice; possible Exodus doorpost link noted with caution (Josh 2).
- He critiques patristic over-allegorization and insists typology is only one among links between Testaments, not the sole present use of Joshua’s revelation (Intro VI.2).

Joshua–Moses parallels he often treats as **narrated historical continuity** guaranteeing Joshua’s authority (Jordan // Red Sea; charge // Deut 31; 5:13–15 // burning bush; staff/javelin; farewell // Deut 31–32), not free-floating allegory.

### Text and canonicity (methodological implications)

Commentary based primarily on the Masoretic Text (generally good; few emendations); LXX and other versions consulted case-by-case, without general preference of LXX over MT (Intro VII). Canonicity, for him, follows divine inspiration as inherent datum—Church only recognizes; Jewish placement of Joshua among Former Prophets and Samaritan Torah-only canon serve his anti-Hexateuch argument (Intro VIII). He rejects labeling Joshua “pre-Christian” or “sub-Christian” (vs Bright et al.): the record of conquest and *ḥerem*, properly understood in inspired context, is “neither pre-Christian nor sub-Christian, but God’s Word for the ages” (Intro VI.1).

## Characteristic Positions

**On the *ḥerem***: Woudstra does not soften or evade the commanded destruction of Canaanite populations. Double sense of *ḥerem*: absolute irrevocable consecration (Lev. 27:28–29) and sentence to utter destruction (Deut. 13:16)—both intended; applied in degrees; Jericho receives the most rigorous form “by way of example”; temporal destruction foreshadows final judgment when unrighteousness is full (Gen. 15:16; Josh 6). Defense frame (Intro VI.3.c; also 11:19–20; C-D on hardening):

1. Canaanite sins demanded exemplary punishment after long patience—“Amorite iniquity” full (Gen. 15:16; Lev. 18:24–25; Deut. 9:1–6);
2. God used Israel’s sword as judicial instrument, as once natural forces at Sodom;
3. Divine behest (6:2; 8:1–2; 10:8, 40, 42) and sovereign hardening (11:20) sit beside Canaanite resolve to destroy invaders (9:1–2; 11:1–5)—human responsibility not cancelled; Gibeon shows “the other way was open”;
4. Purpose includes preventing religious-moral corruption of Israel (Deut. 7; 20).

Crucial methodological claim: while wholesale extermination practices **cease to be normative as practice**, “the revelation contained in the written record regarding such practices does not cease to be normative” (Intro VI.1). He resists both apologetic minimization and dismissal as “sub-Christian”: because *ḥerem* originates from the Lord’s order, one cannot call it sub-Christian without involving God in evolution from cruel to benign (Josh 6; Intro VI.1). Wipe-out language coexists in his reading with admitted survivors and unfinished subjugation (chs. 10–13). See [[herem|ḥerem]] and [[herem-canaanite-conquest|Controversy: ḥerem and Canaanite Conquest]].

**On land theology**: The promised land is not a real-estate transaction but the Lord’s own possession / *naḥalâ*; fundamental text Lev. 25:23 (“The land is mine…”) (Intro VI.2). Inheritance / stranger language enters NT vocabulary (1 Pet. 1:4; Heb. 11:13; Rom. 8:17)—not mere spiritualization; the OT idea is “already spiritual to the core” (Ps. 16:5–6). Allotment is a deeply spiritual occurrence for believing Israelites even while OT covenant happiness attaches to material goods (land, trees, houses) (Josh 13 intro; digest E). Lot expresses God’s sovereign domain (Prov. 16:33; de Vaux) (13:6–7; 14:1–2). **Explicit modern application restraint**: “Due care should be taken not to carry over the promise of the land into the modern era, as if somehow today the possession of the land is still part of what the people of the ancient covenant may claim”; possession may be forfeited (23:16); possible eschatological expansion Canaan → whole earth (Ps. 37:11 // Matt. 5:5) (Intro VI.2). Ideal/programmatic boundaries (13:2–6) are a program of action with the Lord’s help, not merely postexilic fiction (digest E). See [[promised-land|The Promised Land]].

**On the “already / not yet”**: The conquest and inheritance are simultaneously complete (11:23; 21:43–45; 23:1, 4, 14) and incomplete (13:1–7; 15:63; 16:10; 17:12–18; 18:2; 23:5, 7, 12). Woudstra holds both emphases theologically valid and complementary—neither sacrificed for logical coherence; in 23:4–5 the two lines “run side by side,” though completeness is often predominant (Intro VI.2). 21:43–45 is among the book’s key sections: Spirit’s revelational purpose is to cast full light on covenant faithfulness; v. 43 lands distribution (13–21), v. 44 conquest (1–12), v. 45 the whole book under God’s faithfulness—completing the cycle begun in 1:2–6 while covenant-obedience emphasis of ch. 1 must not be forgotten. Gift and task, rest and further possession, under covenant grace—not works-righteousness (Josh 13 intro; digest E). “Rest” expands horizons toward Messianic / eschatological rest (Heb 4).

**On Joshua and Moses**: Extensive parallel as narrated continuity: Jordan // Red Sea; “as I was with Moses, so I will be with you” (1:5; 3:7; 4:14); 5:13–15 // burning bush (outwardly different); javelin // Moses’ staff (8:26 // Exod 17); altars; farewell // Deut 31–32; only at death does Joshua receive Moses’ title “servant of the Lord” (24:29). Continuity of leadership is assured without slighting Moses.

**On Rahab’s lie**: Biblical narrator supplies no moral judgment on Rahab’s deception of the king’s messengers. Woudstra reports commentators who call it a lie and others who leave judgment to wider biblical context; he cites B. Holwerda: “truth” in Israel means “loyalty toward the neighbor and the Lord,” not mere agreement with fact—thus Rahab’s words need not be called a lie (Josh 2). He does not adjudicate a systematic ethics of deception, but does not condemn. Rahab’s confession (2:9–11) he takes as substance truly hers (possibly summarized by the narrator), climaxing in monotheistic acknowledgment. Her rescue is a singular instance of goodness to a Canaanite under *ḥerem*—faith that “worked” (Heb. 11:31; Jas. 2:25); purely racial components never defined the people of God (Josh 6).

**On liberation theology**: More explicitly hostile than any other ingested commentator on this point. Concedes Exodus has been paradigmatic for liberation movements but insists the example method “effectively cuts us off from the true scriptural meaning” and that Exodus in Scripture is redemption from sin’s bondage, not mere political oppression (Intro I). Liberation’s Canaan-as-righting-wrongs reading is noted as a use that becomes available once spiritual dimension is clear—but not endorsed as controlling (Intro VI.2). Woudstra would resist liberation readings even where [[james-bruckner|Bruckner]] accepts them critically.

**On sin and corporate solidarity**: Sin viewed objectively—Achan’s sin involves the whole community (7:1); communal “folly in Israel” (7:15) alongside individual non-transfer of guilt (Deut. 24:16; Ezek. 18:20); NT teaches both aspects (Intro VI.3.d). *Maʿal* frame links Achan (ch. 7) and the altar controversy (ch. 22): land truly possessed only under covenant adherence.

**On covenant occasion and frame**: Occasion of Joshua is the covenant between God and Israel and the need to keep past memories for present significance and future vistas (Intro IV). Apart from close reliance on ANE treaties (Kline), Woudstra safely claims covenant provided occasion and underlying unity of OT historical narratives (Intro IV). Chs. 1 and 23–24 form a hortatory frame around the book. Ch. 24 is not a perfect suzerainty-treaty specimen; if the scheme is applied, it is in narrative adaptation—Deuteronomy as a whole is a better model.
