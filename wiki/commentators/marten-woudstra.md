Marten H. Woudstra was a Dutch Reformed Old Testament scholar at Calvin Theological Seminary and a member of the translation committee that produced the New International Version. His *Joshua* commentary in the New International Commentary on the Old Testament (NICOT) series (Eerdmans, 1981) is the first ingested commentary on the Former Prophets and the Deuteronomistic History. It represents the same conservative evangelical NICOT tradition as Hamilton (Genesis) and Craigie (Deuteronomy), but applied to a historical narrative book with distinctive theological and ethical difficulties.

## Hermeneutical Method

Woudstra's overriding methodological principle is **theocentric, not anthropocentric, historiography**. He argues at length that biblical narrative is not a collection of moral examples (the "Bible story approach") but a record of God's redemptive acts. The human agents — including Joshua himself — are subordinate to the divine purpose; they are not paradigms to imitate but participants in a drama whose author is God.

He applies this principle against what he sees as two distortions:
- **Moralizing homiletics**: Lifting characters and events out of their redemptive-historical context to derive "lessons."
- **Liberation theology**: Using the exodus as a timeless paradigm for political liberation. Woudstra insists the exodus is about redemption from sin, not merely political oppression; it "enters the vocabulary of redemption in its more comprehensive, Christ-effected, sense."

This makes him more restrictive on the applicability of biblical narrative than most of his NICOT contemporaries and puts him in direct tension with [[james-bruckner|Bruckner]]'s sympathetic engagement with liberationist hermeneutics.

### Literary-Structural Approach

Despite being a traditionalist, Woudstra pays significant attention to **Hebrew narrative technique**:
- **Prolepsis**: Hebrew narrative frequently summarizes an event proleptically before reporting it in detail. What looks like a "seam" or "duplicate tradition" to source critics is often an episodic narrative returning to a scene already summarized.
- **Chiastic and frame structures**: The Achan story (ch. 7) and the eastern altar controversy (ch. 22) are framed with the same word (*maʿal*); the Caleb-Joshua stories frame the allotment section (14:6-15; 19:49-51); the hortatory chapters (1, 23-24) frame the entire book.
- **Suspense and pacing**: The Jordan crossing narrative builds suspense through delayed disclosure; the Rahab narrative concludes scenes proleptically.

He argues these literary features demonstrate a **unity of composition** that makes the source-critical fragmentation of the book unnecessary.

### Critical Stance

Woudstra rejects both the **Hexateuch hypothesis** (extending J/E/P/D into Joshua) and the **Deuteronomistic History hypothesis** (Noth's DH as the primary compositional unit). His grounds:
- The Samaritan canon (Torah only) argues against a Hexateuch
- The DH hypothesis requires that Joshua's literary seams are failures of redaction; Woudstra argues they are features of Hebrew narrative
- He prefers an early date (near Joshua's time, possibly shortly after) based on internal textual evidence

On the DH front, he is more conservative than Bruckner but less argumentative than Craigie — he does not make Mosaic authorship a theological necessity and is willing to allow for the use of sources.

### Typology

Woudstra practices **restrained biblical typology** — typology grounded in the OT's own symbolism, not in coincidental verbal parallels or arbitrary allegorization. He affirms:
- The Joshua-Jesus name connection (*Yehoshua* → *Iēsous*)
- The "rest" typology in Hebrews 4:8 as genuine — Joshua's *mĕnûḥâ* as the partial fulfillment pointing to eschatological Sabbath rest
- The Rahab typology (harlot-to-confessor) cautiously

He explicitly critiques patristic over-allegorization (Origen's Joshua homilies) and insists that typology must rest on genuine structural parallels in the redemptive-historical progress of revelation.

## Characteristic Positions

**On the *ḥerem***: Woudstra does not soften or evade the commanded destruction of Canaanite populations. His defense: (1) the Canaanites' "iniquity was full" (Gen 15:16) after centuries of divine patience; (2) God used Israel as a judicial instrument, as he had used natural forces at Sodom; (3) the *ḥerem* was a unique, historically bounded divine command — it ceased to be normative — but the revelatory record of it remains normative as Scripture. He resists both the apologetic minimization and the dismissal of the text as "sub-Christian." See [[herem|ḥerem]] and [[herem-canaanite-conquest|Controversy: ḥerem and Canaanite Conquest]].

**On land theology**: The promised land is not a real-estate transaction but a spiritual category — YHWH's own *naḥalâ* (inheritance), in which Israel dwells as a tenant-guest. The promise was "essentially spiritual to the core" from the beginning; the NT's extension from Canaan to "the whole earth" (Matt 5:5) is development, not spiritualization. He explicitly states that the OT land promise should not be applied to modern territorial claims. See [[promised-land|The Promised Land]].

**On the "already/not yet"**: The conquest is simultaneously complete (21:43-45) and incomplete (13:1; 15:63; 16:10). This is the structural pattern of eschatological promise — the book's "open-endedness" is intentional. The fullness of the inheritance awaits a future not yet described in Joshua.

**On Joshua and Moses**: Woudstra traces an extensive parallel between Joshua and Moses — the Jordan crossing parallels the Red Sea, the charge in ch. 1 parallels Deut 31, the encounter at Jericho (5:13-15) parallels the burning bush. This is not typology but narrated historical continuity: God guarantees that Joshua's authority is as legitimate as Moses' (3:7; 4:14).

**On Rahab's lie**: Woudstra notes that the biblical narrator does not supply a moral judgment on Rahab's deception of the king's messengers. He cites B. Holwerda's observation that "truth" in Israel means "loyalty toward the neighbor and the Lord" — suggesting the question may be misframed by modern categories of truth-telling. He does not adjudicate but does not condemn.

**On liberation theology**: More explicitly hostile than any other ingested commentator. He concedes that the exodus has been paradigmatic for liberation movements but insists the example method "effectively cuts us off from the true scriptural meaning." Woudstra would resist the liberation reading even where Bruckner accepts it critically.
