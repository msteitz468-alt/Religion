# Peter Kreeft

Peter Kreeft (b. 1937) is an American Catholic philosopher and prolific popular-Thomist author,
professor of philosophy at Boston College. A convert from Calvinism, he has published over
eighty books aimed at presenting classical Catholic philosophy and theology to general audiences.
Within the Neo-Thomist tradition inaugurated by Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879), Kreeft
occupies the popularizing wing — committed to the *philosophia perennis* but writing for
non-specialist undergraduates and the educated public rather than for academic philosophy
journals.

## Biographical Context

Kreeft studied at Calvin College and Fordham University, where he encountered scholasticism and
converted to Roman Catholicism. He has taught at Boston College since 1965. His intellectual
touchstones are Aquinas, Augustine, and C. S. Lewis; he regards Aquinas as "certainly the
greatest philosopher for the two thousand years between Aristotle and Descartes." He mentions
Norris W. Clarke, S.J. of Fordham ("the most Aquinas-like mind I know of all men living") as
a formative influence.

## Hermeneutical Method

Kreeft's approach is **pedagogical Thomism** — the *explicate texti* method applied in print.
He treats Aquinas as a primary source to be read directly, rather than through secondary
interpreters ("Thomas is clearer than Thomists"), and structures his anthologies around
copious explanatory footnotes that bridge the gap between primary text and commentary. He
explicitly refuses interpretive paraphrase ("the hubristic paraphrases of some subsequent
non-literal translators"), preferring the 1920 Dominican Province English translation's
formal equivalence.

His **selection criterion** for philosophical passages from the *Summa*:
(1) intrinsically important and (2) non-technical enough to be intelligible to modern readers
without professional philosophical background. He deliberately excludes revealed theology
(Part III: Christ, sacraments) from philosophical anthologies, treating the *Prima Pars* and
portions of the *Prima Secundae* as the properly philosophical core.

**Kreeft frames Aquinas against modern philosophy** rather than against rival medieval schools:
his introductions position Aquinas as the corrective to Cartesian method, Kantian critical
philosophy, Hegelian historicism, and contemporary analytic narrowness. He is not primarily
engaged in intra-scholastic disputes (Thomism vs. Scotism vs. Ockhamism) but in apologetics
for classical realism against modern idealism and nominalism.

## Characteristic Philosophical Positions

**Anti-nominalism**: Kreeft identifies nominalism (the Ockhamist doctrine that universals are
only names, not real essences) as "perhaps the most pervasive and destructive error in modern
philosophy" and traces modern reductionism — the tendency to explain the higher by the lower
(mind by brain, love by lust, man by ape) — to Ockham's fourteenth-century application of
nominalism.

**Classical realism and anti-reductionism**: Medieval/classical explanation proceeds from lower
to higher (matter is explained by form; body is explained by soul); modern reductionism inverts
this order. Kreeft presents Thomistic hylomorphism as the philosophical alternative to
materialist reduction.

**Faith-reason synthesis as the defining medieval achievement**: The *Summa*'s *exitus–redditus*
structure (procession from God and return to God) is for Kreeft the architectural expression of
a civilization that held together the seven essential syntheses: faith/reason, biblical/classical,
clarity/profundity, common sense/technical rigor, theory/practice, intuition/logic, unity/
multiplicity. The fracturing of these syntheses after Descartes is Kreeft's master narrative of
Western intellectual decline.

**Eternity of the world and Big Bang**: Kreeft endorses Aquinas's position that reason alone
cannot resolve the eternity question (contra Latin Averroism and contra Bonaventurian
rationalism alike), and suggests that modern cosmological evidence (the Big Bang) "fits in
nicely with the idea of creation of time" — confirming once again that "faith and reason never
really contradict each other."

**Will vs. intellect**: Kreeft expounds Aquinas's resolution of the Platonic-Aristotelian
primacy of intellect vs. the biblical primacy of the heart — the intellect is absolutely higher
(its object, truth, is more abstract and simple); the will is relatively higher when its object
is something nobler than the soul (God). Kreeft treats this as the philosophical hinge of the
faith-reason synthesis itself.

**Ontological dimension of virtue and vice**: Drawing on Aquinas (Section III, note 30), Kreeft
holds that "vice and virtue have an ontological dimension as well as a moral one; we diminish
our being when we sin and augment it by the virtues."

## The Euthyphro Problem

Kreeft's note on divine will explicitly engages the Euthyphro dilemma: Aquinas must avoid both
arbitrariness (God's will has no ground beyond itself) and heteronomy (God's will is constrained
by something external to God). The resolution is grounding the divine will in the divine nature
and intellect — which is itself identical with the Good.

## Influence and Standing

Kreeft stands squarely in the Neo-Thomist popularizing tradition, alongside G. K. Chesterton
and Josef Pieper (whom he cites alongside Gilson and Maritain as the best secondary sources on
Aquinas, but insists still cannot substitute for Aquinas himself). He has no significant
contribution to technical medieval studies or textual scholarship; his contribution is
*propagation* — making the *philosophia perennis* accessible to audiences who would not read
the Fathers of the English Dominican Province directly.
