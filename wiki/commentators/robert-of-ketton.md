# Robert of Ketton

**Robert of Ketton** (fl. 1136–1157) was a twelfth-century English polymath — astronomer, geometer,
and translator — who produced the **first complete Latin translation of the [[quran|Qur'an]]**
(completed 1143). He represents the wiki's first entry on the **Latin/Christian reception** of the
Qur'an: a translation born of crusade-era polemic that nonetheless became the channel through which
medieval and early-modern Europe knew the text.

## Biographical Context

An educated Englishman fluent in Latin (the language of theology and science), Robert went to Spain
to acquire Arabic learning, studying at Barcelona (from 1136, with Plato of Tivoli) and working
within the **Toledo school of translators** — the milieu that funneled Greek-via-Arabic science
(algebra, astronomy, geometry) into Latin Europe. His own preferred work was scientific translation;
the Qur'an commission was an interruption of it.

## The Qur'an Translation

In 1142 **Peter the Venerable**, Abbot of Cluny, visiting Toledo, commissioned Robert to lead a team
producing a Latin Qur'an as part of the **Cluniac corpus** (the *Collectio Toletana*) of texts on
Islam. Robert's version was titled **Lex Mahumet pseudoprophete** ("The Law of the Pseudo-Prophet
Muhammad") — its very title expressing the project's polemical frame: for Peter and Robert,
**[[muhammad|Muhammad]]** was a charlatan and the book a fabrication. Robert had earlier compiled an
anthology with the title *Lies and Ridiculous Tales of the Saracens*.

The context was the Crusades (the First ended 1099; Edessa fell to the Turks in 1144, triggering the
Second). Peter the Venerable, exceptionally, argued that Islam should be met "not by arms but by
words, not by force but by reason, not in hatred but in love" — battle by the pen — even while
intending to refute it.

## Method: A Polemical Title, a Sympathetic Translation

This is the page's central paradox, which the wiki records as Lawrence frames it. Despite the hostile
title, Robert did **not** produce a literal, debunking rendering. He worked as a *paraphraser* who
consulted **Muslim commentaries** — including **[[al-tabari|al-Tabari]]**'s and the Shi'ite
**al-Tabarsi**'s — to recover *what Muslims themselves understood the text to mean*. Lawrence's
examples:

- **Q 108 (al-Kawthar)**: Robert renders the rare word *al-kawthar* as "a fountain in Paradise,"
  following al-Tabari's gloss (a river of Paradise from the Night-Journey tradition), and expands the
  closing line about the enemy being "cut off" into "lacking helpers and progeny," apparently from
  al-Tabarsi.
- **Q 22:1 / Q 99**: he reads "the earthquake of the Hour" intertextually as the Day of Judgment.
- **Q 22:52** (the verse adjacent to the "[[quran|Satanic Verses]]" problem): his rendering
  *theologically* protects prophetic integrity, locating Satan's "obstruction" in the impulse
  preceding prophecy rather than in the revelation itself.

Robert thus grasped — ahead of many later European translators — that the **Arabic-Muslim commentary
tradition is indispensable** to translating the Qur'an across cultures. Lawrence reads his outward
antipathy as partly a protective pose in a crusading climate, masking genuine "inward sympathy."

## Influence

The *Lex Mahumet pseudoprophete* circulated widely in manuscript and was **printed in 1543** (edited
by Theodor Bibliander at Basel, with a preface by Luther's circle), remaining a principal European
access point to the Qur'an for centuries — the textual ancestor of much early Western Quranic
study, polemical and otherwise.
