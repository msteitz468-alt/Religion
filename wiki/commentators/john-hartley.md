John E. Hartley is an OT scholar and theologian who served on the faculty of Azusa Pacific University (Wesleyan evangelical institution). His NICOT commentary on Job (1988) is one of the major evangelical treatments of that book in the 20th century and represents his primary contribution to the wiki.

## Background

Hartley's approach to Job is explicitly **message-focused** rather than philological. He states in his preface: "The purpose of this work has been to concentrate on interpreting the book's message rather than to write a linguistic commentary." He works carefully with the Hebrew text, noting textual emendations and drawing on the major critical commentators (Dhorme, Pope, Dahood, Fohrer, Gordis), but subordinates technical analysis to theological synthesis.

This places Hartley between:
- [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] (maximally philological, minimally theological) 
- [[peter-leithart|Leithart]] (maximally theological, minimally philological)

Hartley represents the mainstream evangelical NICOT approach: careful with the text but driven by a concern for the book's theological witness within the canon.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Literary-Canonical Reading with Form-Critical Awareness

Hartley uses form-critical categories (lament, lawsuit, hymn, wisdom instruction, avowal of innocence) to identify speech types and their typical "life settings" (*Sitz im Leben*). But he consistently warns that in Job the *literary* setting is more determinative than the form's usual social context — genres are used freely and transformed by the author's artistic purpose.

### 2. Single-Author Defense

Against most critical scholars who find multiple layers, Hartley argues for a substantially single author who may have revised the work over a lifetime. His defense of the Elihu speeches as integral (following Gordis) and his defense of the two Yahweh speeches as original are sustained and detailed.

### 3. ANE Comparative Method

Hartley provides extensive comparison with Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts on suffering (the "Babylonian Job," the "Babylonian Ecclesiastes," Egyptian lament texts). His conclusion: the author was familiar with ANE Wisdom literature traditions and borrowed *format* (dialogue structure, lament genres, pattern of the righteous sufferer) but transformed the *substance* substantially — particularly in raising theodicy as a formal lawsuit against the personal God YHWH.

### 4. Canonical-Intertextual Connections

Hartley pays attention to the relationship between Job and other OT books, particularly Isaiah. His claim that **Isaiah was influenced by Job** (rather than vice versa) is a distinctive position: Job 7th-century → influenced Isaiah's Servant Songs → Servant Songs → NT Christology. This is a significant claim that affects both Job's theology and Isaiah's.

### 5. Holistic Reading of the Canon

Hartley's preface frames Job within the "theology of the canon" — the book plays a "vital role" by modifying simplistic retribution theology, and Job himself provides a model ("foundation") for believing that Jesus was truly righteous despite his shameful death.

## Key Theological Positions

**On the *haśśāṭān***: A title (heavenly "adversary/prosecutor") not a proper name; the definite article marks a function, not a being. The Job figure is closer to a heavenly quality-control function than to the cosmic evil being of later theology or Christian interpretation.

**On the Elihu speeches**: Integral to the book's structure; authored by either the original author in later life or a close disciple. Elihu's key contributions — disciplinary suffering (33:12-22) and the mediating angel/ransom (33:23-28) — are theologically essential and cannot be dismissed as clumsy interpolation.

**On Job's recantation (42:6)**: *Em'as* means Job surrenders his lawsuit, not that he confesses hidden sin. Job does not admit wrongdoing. He abandons his legal case after God has persuaded him that no human perspective is adequate to judge divine governance. God's subsequent vindication of Job ("you have spoken rightly") confirms this reading.

**On theodicy**: The book corrects a *simplistic* retribution doctrine without abandoning retribution altogether. Suffering can be punitive (in other texts), but also disciplinary, testing, or inexplicable. The Yahweh speeches offer grounds for trust in divine justice without providing an explanation for any particular case of suffering.

**On Isaiah's Suffering Servant**: Job served as Isaiah's model for the Servant Songs — a claim that, if correct, means Job is a foundational text for Christology through the Isaiah-NT trajectory.
