John E. Hartley is an OT scholar and theologian who served on the faculty of Azusa Pacific University (Wesleyan evangelical institution). His NICOT commentary on Job (1988) is one of the major evangelical treatments of that book in the 20th century and represents his primary contribution to the wiki.

## Background

Hartley's approach to Job is explicitly **message-focused** rather than philological. He states in his preface: "The purpose of this work has been to concentrate on interpreting the book's message rather than to write a linguistic commentary." He works carefully with the Hebrew text, noting textual emendations and drawing on the major critical commentators (Dhorme, Pope, Dahood, Fohrer, Gordis), but subordinates technical analysis to theological synthesis.

This places Hartley between:
- [[david-tsumura|Tsumura]] (maximally philological, minimally theological) 
- [[peter-leithart|Leithart]] (maximally theological, minimally philological)

Hartley represents the mainstream evangelical NICOT approach: careful with the text but driven by a concern for the book's theological witness within the canon.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Literary-Canonical Reading with Form-Critical Awareness

Hartley uses form-critical categories (lament, lawsuit, hymn, wisdom instruction, avowal of innocence) to identify speech types and their typical "life settings" (*Sitz im Leben*). But he consistently warns that in Job the *literary* setting is more determinative than the form's usual social context — genres are used freely and transformed by the author's artistic purpose.

### 2. Single-Author Defense

Against most critical scholars who find multiple layers, Hartley argues for a substantially single author who may have revised the work over a lifetime. His defense of the Elihu speeches as integral (following Gordis) and his defense of the two Yahweh speeches as original are sustained and detailed.

### 2b. Textual Conservatism and the Limits of Reconstruction

A full re-reading of the Job commentary (2026-07) sharpens what the single-author defense amounts to in practice: Hartley is a **minimal-intervention** interpreter. He works MT-default, holding that "the versions offer numerous variant readings, but only a few are useful for the restoration of the original reading," and he refuses emendation undertaken to serve a theory — against strophic analysis, "Those who arrange the speeches in strophes are often forced to emend the text quite freely and in so doing must omit many verses as secondary. Such wholesale reordering of the text does not seem warranted." His stated rule for the disordered third cycle is the clearest statement of the policy: "To regain a part of the third cycle and to avoid a highly speculative reconstruction that, if incorrect, would only be further from the original production, only a minor change is made in the commentary."

The result is that across the whole book Hartley **relocates exactly one block — 27:13–23, joined to Bildad's truncated third speech at 25:1–6 — and creates nothing.** He proposes no reconstructed verses, no conjectural speeches, no excisions. Even where he agrees a passage is misplaced (31:38–40b, which he prints before v. 35), he declines to claim a solution: "No consensus has been reached regarding the proper placement of these verses; they are placed before v. 35 simply because of their proximity to that location." He reports Gordis's and Hölscher/Duhm's third-cycle reconstructions, Fohrer's and Westermann's one-speech theophany reconstructions, and Buttenweiser's and Szczygiel's relocations of ch. 28, and declines all of them — often on the ground that the sheer variety of proposals is itself an argument against reconstructing: "The wide variety of the multiple reconstructions cautions further against major reapportioning of the verses."

He is also willing to leave his own questions open. On the Elihu speeches, having argued at length for their integration into the book's design, he still writes: "Whether they are the work of the author of Job or of a final redactor is difficult to ascertain."

### 2c. The "Aim" Apparatus

The commentary's distinctive structural device is an **"Aim" section closing each speech**, stating that speech's contribution to the whole. This is where Hartley's synthetic reading lives — the Aims carry his account of Elihu's function, the verdict of ch. 28 on the comforters, and the resolution of the patient-vs-defiant Job problem ("the persevering Job"). His stated purpose governs the whole: "The purpose of this work has been to concentrate on interpreting the book's message rather than to write a linguistic commentary."

### 3. ANE Comparative Method

Hartley provides extensive comparison with Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts on suffering (the "Babylonian Job," the "Babylonian Ecclesiastes," Egyptian lament texts). His conclusion: the author was familiar with ANE Wisdom literature traditions and borrowed *format* (dialogue structure, lament genres, pattern of the righteous sufferer) but transformed the *substance* substantially — particularly in raising theodicy as a formal lawsuit against the personal God YHWH.

### 4. Canonical-Intertextual Connections

Hartley pays attention to the relationship between Job and other OT books, particularly Isaiah. His claim that **Isaiah was influenced by Job** (rather than vice versa) is a distinctive position: Job 7th-century → influenced Isaiah's Servant Songs → Servant Songs → NT Christology. This is a significant claim that affects both Job's theology and Isaiah's.

### 5. Holistic Reading of the Canon

Hartley's preface frames Job within the "theology of the canon" — the book plays a "vital role" by modifying simplistic retribution theology, and Job himself provides a model ("foundation") for believing that Jesus was truly righteous despite his shameful death.

## Key Theological Positions

**On the *haśśāṭān***: A title (heavenly "adversary/prosecutor") not a proper name; the definite article marks a function, not a being. The Job figure is closer to a heavenly quality-control function than to the cosmic evil being of later theology or Christian interpretation.

**On the Elihu speeches**: Integral to the book's structure; authored by either the original author in later life or a close disciple. Elihu's key contributions — disciplinary suffering (33:12-22) and the mediating angel/ransom (33:23-28) — are theologically essential and cannot be dismissed as clumsy interpolation.

**On Job's recantation (42:6)**: *Em'as* means Job surrenders his lawsuit, not that he confesses hidden sin. Job does not admit wrongdoing. He abandons his legal case after God has persuaded him that no human perspective is adequate to judge divine governance. God's subsequent vindication of Job ("you have spoken rightly") confirms this reading.

**On theodicy**: The book corrects a *simplistic* retribution doctrine without abandoning retribution altogether. Suffering can be punitive (in other texts), but also disciplinary, testing, or inexplicable. The Yahweh speeches offer grounds for trust in divine justice without providing an explanation for any particular case of suffering.

**On Isaiah's Suffering Servant**: Job served as Isaiah's model for the Servant Songs — a claim that, if correct, means Job is a foundational text for Christology through the Isaiah-NT trajectory. Hartley credits the priority argument to **Pfeiffer and Terrien**, and concedes that the direction of dependence between Job and other OT texts is generally undeterminable: "Job may be dependent on other texts, other texts may be dependent on Job, or parallel texts may be mutually dependent on an unknown third source."

## Confessional Extensions — Explicitly Marked

Hartley's evangelical commitments surface at identifiable points, and he generally marks them as extensions rather than as the text's meaning. Recording them as *his*, not as Job's:

- **The NT Mediator analogy at 9:33.** Having judged Job's wish for an arbiter impossible within Job's own world, Hartley adds: "Job's thinking is working with the same inner logic that stands behind the core teaching of the NT, that Jesus Christ, God's Son, has become the Mediator, the Arbiter, between God and man" (p. 192). The claim is about inner logic, not about Job's intention.
- **Dating resurrection doctrine after Christ.** At 14:14 he holds that Job could not have envisaged resurrection — "His view here is closer to a concept of resuscitation than to resurrection" — because "this doctrine becomes central to biblical faith only after the resurrection of Christ, for it gains its validation in Christ's triumph over death."
- **The Sermon on the Mount trajectory in ch. 31.** Twice: "His moral insight is highly refined, pointing toward the Sermon on the Mount," and on 31:29, "Again Job's moral sensitivity points toward the ideal standard taught by Jesus (cf. Matt. 5:43–48; Luke 6:27–36)."
- **Where he declines the christological option.** At 33:23 Hartley lists six proposed identities for Elihu's mediating angel, the sixth being "the concealed Christ" — and does **not** adopt it. He identifies the mediator instead with "the angel of Yahweh," reading "one among a thousand" as "having very restrictive force." This is a useful control on how far his christological readings run: he takes the analogical extension at 9:33 but refuses the identification at 33:23.
