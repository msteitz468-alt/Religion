# Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

Ibn Rushd (Latin **Averroes**, 1126–1198), the Andalusian Aristotelian, "the most scholarly and
scrupulous commentator of Aristotle" and "the last great philosopher in Islam in the twelfth
century." For the Latin West he was simply **"the Commentator"** on Aristotle, and his influence
on medieval and Renaissance Europe "was immense" — far greater than in the Islamic East, "where
few of his works are still in existence and where he had no influence." For this wiki he is the
author of the **[[incoherence-of-the-incoherence|*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*]]** ("The Incoherence of the
Incoherence"), his point-by-point reply to **[[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]]**'s
*[[incoherence-of-the-philosophers|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]*.

> The biographical/reception detail in this opening is drawn from Simon van den Bergh's
> Introduction (the translator's commentary, not Averroes' own text).

## Hermeneutical Method

Averroes operates strictly within the *[[falsafa]]* framework of **demonstration (*burhān*)**,
graded above dialectic (*jadal*) and rhetoric. His characteristic moves in the *Tahāfut
al-Tahāfut*:

- **The triangular critique.** He defends "the philosophers" without sparing them: he judges that
  the philosophers' proofs (for the world's eternity, God's unity, the denial of attributes) are
  largely **dialectical, not demonstrative**, while Ghazālī's objections are largely
  **sophistical** — designed "to oppose difficulty with difficulty." Neither side, on his verdict,
  reaches "absolute evidence or… stringent proof"; the book becomes "the Incoherence of both
  parties together."
- **Aristotle against Avicenna.** Averroes' deepest polemic is *internal* to *falsafa*: he
  detaches the genuine Aristotelian tradition (and often al-Fārābī) from **[[avicenna|Avicenna]]**,
  whom he charges with non-Aristotelian innovations — the ten-intellect [[emanation]] cascade
  ("inventions fabricated against the philosophers by Avicenna, Fārābī, and others"), the
  necessary/possible distinction, treating existence as an accident "additional to the quiddity,"
  and missing analogical predication. Several of Ghazālī's objections, he grants, struck Avicenna
  but not Aristotle.
- **Equivocal / analogical predication of the divine names.** Terms such as "will," "knowledge,"
  "life," and "agent" apply to God and to creatures only **equivocally** — more exactly,
  analogically (*per prius et posterius*), the First being the *cause* of the quality in all
  secondary instances, not a co-species with them. This is his key tool against both Ghazālī's
  objections (Disc. 7) and the theologians' anthropomorphism: to deny that God "wills" because He
  does not will as a human does is the same error as denying He "knows."
- **God's knowledge is the cause of things, not their effect.** Human knowledge is passive
  (produced by its objects); divine knowledge is pure act and produces its objects. Hence God's
  knowledge is "neither universal nor individual." To conflate the two registers is to make "God an
  eternal man and man a mortal God." See [[gods-knowledge-of-particulars]].
- **The masses-versus-elect rule.** The deepest metaphysical truths (God's self-knowledge; "there
  is no reality besides Him") are forbidden topics for the masses — "this must not be written
  down… nor made an obligation of faith." See [[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy]].

## Characteristic Positions in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*

- **Eternal creation.** The world is eternal in both directions, in "everlasting becoming"; an
  eternal agent's act has no beginning ("a limited act can only be imagined of a limited agent").
  The Creator's priority is a third kind — "the priority of the unchanging timeless existence to
  the changing existence which is in time." He defends the **accidental (potential) infinite**
  while denying any actual or essential infinite. See [[eternity-of-the-world]].
- **The heavens are living rational animals** of distinct species, moved by their incorporeal
  movers "by command," all depending on one First Principle who, like a king with deputies or the
  soul of a single animal, distributes unity through the cosmic hierarchy. See
  [[separate-intellects]].
- **Real natural causation.** Against [[occasionalism|Ashʿarite occasionalism]], fire genuinely
  causes burning (though "not absolutely" — external conditions can impede it); to deny efficient
  causes is "sophistry," for "intelligence is nothing but the perception of things with their
  causes," and the denial uproots knowledge, definition, demonstration, and even the inference to a
  wise Maker.
- **Divine simplicity and the necessary existent.** He defends God's absolute simplicity but
  reformulates the proofs — preferring the argument from pure act over Avicenna's
  "necessary-through-another," which he calls "superfluous and erroneous." See
  [[necessary-existent]], [[divine-attributes-in-islam]].
- **The soul and the afterlife.** He defends the immateriality of the intellect (endorsing the
  proof that the receptacle of intelligibles is "not impressed" by them) and treats the
  **material intellect** as "absolutely immaterial." On resurrection he holds that what arises are
  **simulacra** of earthly bodies, not the numerically identical bodies — affirming the doctrine
  *specifically*, not *numerically*. See [[soul-in-islamic-philosophy]], [[bodily-resurrection]].
- **Religion as a necessary political art.** Revealed laws are "necessary political arts, the
  principles of which are taken from natural reason and inspiration"; the learned must uphold the
  best religion of their time. "Every prophet is a sage, but not every sage a prophet; the learned…
  are the heirs of the prophets." See [[harmony-of-religion-and-philosophy]].

## Known Controversies

He reads Ghazālī's whole anti-philosophical project with suspicion, suggesting Ghazālī did not
sincerely hold his Ashʿarite positions but wrote "to flatter the masses," and that "in metaphysics
he recurs to the philosophers… most clearly shown… in his book called *The Niche for Lights*."
Averroes also charges Ghazālī with confusing the demonstrative sciences and with self-contradiction
across his works (e.g. on whether spiritual resurrection is heresy — Ghazālī "erred in religious
questions as he erred in rational problems"). On the three doctrines for which Ghazālī declared the
philosophers infidels — [[eternity-of-the-world|eternity of the world]], God's ignorance of
particulars, denial of bodily resurrection — Averroes answers each: see [[takfir]].

## Influence on Later Tradition

In the Islamic world his philosophical project found few heirs; *falsafa* in the Sunni mainstream
remained under the shadow of Ghazālī. In the Latin West, through Calo Calonymus's 1328 translation
and his Aristotle commentaries, "Averroism" became a major and contested current (Siger of Brabant;
the 1277 condemnations), and his name reached the Renaissance largely apart from his Arabic texts
(Renan's *Averroès et l'Averroïsme* — written, van den Bergh notes, by an author who "had never
seen a line of Arabic by him").
