## Biographical Context

Aetius was a theologian whose biography Ayres sets aside as secondary to his theological contribution, noting only that "he was educated in theology by two or three figures who appear to have been among Arius' supporters in the 320s" (cache ~l.597–598). Even within those circles "he seems to have gained a reputation for pushing a strong subordinationism" (l.599–600). His career placed him in Antioch during the 350s, where his teaching under the patronage of Eudoxius (who became bishop of Antioch in 357) alarmed many who had broadly supported the emerging [[homoians|Homoian]] position; his emergence "was a significant factor in prompting some to reject it" (l.577–580).

Ayres explicitly cautions against calling Aetius's movement "neo-Arian": "there are . . . significant differences between Arius' theology and that of Aetius and Eunomius, and neither ever appears to have made any claim on Arius' legacy" (l.583–586). The term "Anomoians" (those who teach the 'unlikeness' of Father and Son) was coined by their opponents; Aetius and Eunomius "were keen to defend themselves against it, insisting that their concern was to teach 'unlikeness according to essence': there were many other ways in which Father and Son were alike" (l.589–595). Ayres therefore opts for **"Heteroousian"** as the most precise label (l.593–595). See [[heteroousians]].

## Theological and Dialectical Method

Aetius's theology is described as "highly dense and makes use of certain types of syllogism and logical argument" (l.626–627). His method is polemical and philosophical simultaneously: he targets as *χρόνιται* ("temporists") those who countenance either *homoousios* or *homoiousios*, i.e., those who speak of God in temporal terms (l.628–631).

## Major Work: the Syntagmation

Aetius's one surviving work is the *Syntagmation* ("little book"), preserved at Epiphanius, *Panarion* 76.11.1ff (l.627, footnote 335). In it he develops his core argument:

1. **Ingeneracy defines God's essence**: "the essence of God lies in being ingenerate (for which Aetius and Eunomius both consistently use [*agennētos*])" (l.631–634).
2. **God is uncomposite**: ingeneracy "also involves not consisting in a compound essence" (l.634–635).
3. **Logical consequence**: "if God is truly 'not generated', he argues, then no logical sense can be given to an act of generation that results in one who is either homoousios or homoiousios with God. All that is generated and all that generates from its own substance must be compound. God, not being compound, cannot generate in this way, but only by God's will or authority" (l.635–640).
4. **The Son as product of will**: "The Son is thus the product of God's will" (l.640).
5. **Father/Son language**: Aetius was "highly suspicious of using Father and Son language to show that the generation of the Word involves a generation from God's essence." In the *Syntagmation*, "Father" appears mainly toward the end and only "to indicate the relationship of Son to God"; "Son" is used much more frequently and is "taken to indicate that he is essentially subordinate" (l.641–646).

Key texts of the *Syntagmation*: §§5–7 give the core argument about compoundness and will (footnote 336).

## Controversies and Relation to Homoians

Aetius operated at first within the broad Homoian alliance: "during the late 350s the appearance of Aetius' theology within the Homoian alliance was a significant factor in prompting some to reject it. To some who had broadly supported the emerging Homoian position, Aetius came to seem its logical and unacceptable end" (l.577–580). When Eudoxius became bishop of Antioch in 357, "Aetius followed him from Alexandria and began teaching there" (l.829–830), provoking the council Basil of Ancyra convened at Ancyra in 358. Constantius subsequently wrote condemning Eudoxius "for promoting Aetius and his teaching," and Aetius was exiled along with Eudoxius and Eunomius (l.982–986).

After the Homoian settlement of 360, Aetius and Eunomius "began setting up an alternative episcopate in areas where they had supporters. This 'Eunomian' Church survived well into the next century" (l.653–655).

## Influence on Eunomius

[[eunomius|Eunomius]] functioned "for some years as a secretary to him (from about 355)" (l.648–649). Ayres traces how Eunomius's *Apology* both repeats and develops Aetius's positions: the argument that the Son cannot come from the Father's essence (because anything generated from an essence shares that essence, yet the Son is generated by will); the centrality of *ingenerate*; and the soteriological significance of knowable divine names. Aetius's influence on Eunomius is described as "clear" at the point where Eunomius gives "great weight to ingenerate as a term summing up the character of God's essence" (l.676–677).

The Cappadocians became the principal opponents of both Aetius and Eunomius, but Ayres notes they "do not engage in an 'Athanasian'-style attempt to cast Aetius and Eunomius as new Ariuses" (l.586–588).
