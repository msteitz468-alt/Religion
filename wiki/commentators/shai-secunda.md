# Shai Secunda

**Shai Secunda** is a contemporary academic **Talmudist trained in Iranian studies**, and a leading
voice in the comparative subfield he helped systematize, **[[talmudo-iranica|Talmudo-Iranica]]** — the
reading of the **[[talmud-bavli|Babylonian Talmud]]** within its **Sasanian Iranian** context, alongside
**Middle Persian (Pahlavi)** and other Zoroastrian literature. His first book,
[[secunda-the-iranian-talmud|*The Iranian Talmud: Reading the Bavli in Its Sasanian Context*]] (2014),
is the wiki's foundational source for the field.

## Biographical and Scholarly Context

Secunda earned his PhD at **Yeshiva University**, where he studied under **Yaakov Elman** — the scholar
who at the turn of the 21st century revived the question of Persian cultural impact on the Bavli, and
from whom Secunda takes his cue. His Iranian-philology work was shaped by **Prods Oktor Skjærvø** (whose
[[skjaervo-spirit-of-zoroastrianism|*Spirit of Zoroastrianism*]] is also on file here), whom Secunda
thanks for correcting his Middle Persian readings. He has held positions at the Hebrew University of
Jerusalem (Martin Buber Society) and at **Bard College**, where he holds the Jacob Neubauer Chair of
Jewish Studies. His later work extends the program to menstrual-purity law (*The Talmud's Red Fence*,
2020), the topic of his doctoral dissertation.

## Hermeneutical Method

Secunda combines three modes that the wiki's [[talmudic-hermeneutics|talmudic-hermeneutics]] and
[[skjaervo-spirit-of-zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian-philology]] pages each touch separately:

- **Modern critical Talmud study** — text-critical, **source-critical**, and literary-critical analysis
  that separates the tannaitic and amoraic strata from the anonymous redactional layer (the **Stam**),
  and treats the Bavli as "an assemblage of different works composed in various times, places, and
  circumstances" (the legacy of *Wissenschaft des Judentums*), not as a seamless eternal text.
- **Iranian / Pahlavi philology** — close reading of Middle Persian texts (the *Hērbedestān*, *Dēnkard*,
  *Bundahišn*, *Ardā Wirāz nāmag*, the *Mādayān ī Hazār Dādestān*) and of the **Aramaic incantation
  bowls** that mix Jewish and Persian names and formulae.
- **Comparative-religions theory** — drawing on **Jonathan Z. Smith** (the self-aware "magic" of
  comparison) and **Michael Satlow** ("Beyond Influence") to move from chasing one-directional
  **"influence"** toward an **intertextual** reading of the Bavli and Pahlavi literature across a shared
  Sasanian **"text-scape."**

## Characteristic Positions

1. **The Bavli is a Sasanian document.** Just as the Palestinian Talmud is read against Greco-Roman
   Palestine, the Bavli must be read against Sasanian Iran; ignoring this "has come at the expense of a
   deep appreciation of the Talmud's laws, narratives, and other forms of discourse."
2. **"Beyond influence."** The search for clean channels of borrowing imagines two "self-contained,
   impervious" entities that "meet, intercourse, and part." Reality is messier — *reception,
   incorporation, rejection, reaction*, **ecotypification**, and **joint participation in a broader
   cultural project**. Where direct transmission is irretrievable, read the corpora as co-producing
   meaning in one discursive space.
3. **Mutual "discourses of the Other."** Rabbis and [[magi|Zoroastrian priests]] each built sharply
   dualistic images of the other (the rabbinic Jew/*goy* binary mirrors the Zoroastrian *ēr*/*an-ēr*) —
   yet the very vehemence of these polemics, and a *concurrent* set of "familiarizing" texts, testify to
   how **proximate and entangled** the two communities actually were.
4. **Dating discipline.** Because much fully articulated Zoroastrian eschatology survives only in **late
   (9th-c., post-Islamic) Pahlavi** texts, both "Zoroastrian origins of everything" claims and their flat
   denial are methodologically naïve; each parallel must be argued case by case.
5. **Stakes beyond the academy.** A Bavli understood as a product of a plural Iranian world reframes how
   later (even contemporary) Judaism is understood — and quietly rebukes the modern Iran–Israel
   antagonism that has occasionally dragged the Talmud into its polemics.
