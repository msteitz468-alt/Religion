# Scot McKnight

American NT scholar and theologian, professor of NT at Northern Seminary (Lombard, IL). Widely published in Pauline theology, Gospels scholarship (particularly Jesus and Kingdom of God), and ecclesiology. His commentary on Colossians for the NICNT series (Eerdmans, 2018) is his major Pauline commentary; Joel B. Green (Asbury) is the current general editor of NICNT. McKnight is broadly post-conservative evangelical: he accepts historical-critical method as a tool while insisting on the theological coherence of the canonical text.

## Hermeneutical Method

McKnight's approach is grammatico-historical with heavy emphasis on Second Temple Jewish background (1 Enoch, Philo, DSS, 4 Ezra, Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs), close engagement with the NPP conversation (Dunn, Wright, Barclay, Campbell, Gorman), and a consistently ecclesiological focus. Several methodological commitments shape his reading:

- **Christological entry point**: Paul's theology is entered through the Damascus-road Christophany, not through the forensic-soteriology door of Romans 3. The Messiah-Lordship of the risen Christ is the prior reality; soteriology, ecclesiology, and ethics all flow from it.
- **Community ethics over individual virtue**: Pauline ethics is ethics-for-a-community, not individual virtue formation. He explicitly contrasts Paul with Aristotelian virtue ethics, arguing that Paul's concern is not how individuals become virtuous but how a new-creation fellowship embodies cosmic reconciliation.
- **Post-critical doctrinal engagement**: Reads commentaries across the evangelical-critical spectrum; does not treat pseudonymity arguments as settled but engages them seriously. Classified as an evangelical critical scholar rather than a traditional conservative apologist.

Framework: Christian grammatico-historical (modern critical); theologically attentive to Christological monotheism, ecclesiology, and the Pauline "in Christ" formation pattern.

## Characteristic Positions

**1. The "Known-to-Unknown" Methodology Critique (Authorship)**
McKnight's most distinctive methodological contribution to Pauline studies is his demolition of the comparative method underlying pseudo-Pauline hypotheses. The standard argument for pseudonymity compares a letter's style and theology to "authentic Paul" and, finding deviations, infers a different author. McKnight's counter: there is no such thing as a fixed baseline of "pure Paul." Every Pauline letter was produced collaboratively — with co-workers (Timothy, Sosthenes), dictated to secretaries (Tertius in Romans), and shaped by situational pressures, oral performance, and varying emotional registers. Galatians, written in white-hot anger, sounds nothing like Romans. If stylistic deviation disqualifies Colossians, it disqualifies Galatians too. Conclusion: Colossians is *Pauline as much as Galatians* — not a stronger defense, but an epistemological challenge to the debate's foundations. See [[colossians-authorship|Colossians: Pauline or Deutero-Pauline?]].

**2. Halakic Mystics — Jewish, Not Syncretistic (Opponents)**
McKnight identifies the Colossian opponents as Jewish Christians who combine Torah-observance (food laws, Sabbath, calendar observances) with an asceticism-based program aimed at heavenly ascent and participation in angelic liturgy. He follows Sappington, Francis, and Bandstra but is more emphatic: the opponents are *not* Hellenistic syncretists, proto-Gnostics, or mystery-religion devotees. The evidence is internal to Colossians — the specific practices of 2:16 (festivals, new moons, Sabbath) are characteristically Jewish; the "worship of angels" (2:18) is best understood as *angel-like worship* (ascetic-mystical entry into heavenly liturgy), not veneration of angels as such. Uses Sumney's methodological caution (distinguish certain evidence from probable from possible) throughout. See [[colossian-opponents|The Colossian Opponents ("Colossian Philosophy")]].

**3. Christology as Port of Entry (Pauline Theology)**
Paul's theology enters the stage through the door of Christology, not soteriology. The Damascus encounter revealed Jesus as the risen Lord-Messiah, the cosmic Creator-Reconciler; Paul's subsequent theological development is an origami-like unfolding of that Christological core. The narrative structure: *theocracy* (Gen 1–1 Sam 8) → *monarchy* (1 Sam 8–Gospels) → *christocracy* (the present age) → the eschatological handover (1 Cor 15; Rev 20–22). This sequence positions Paul's ethics, ecclesiology, and soteriology within a christocentric historiography, not as solutions to an Augustinian guilt crisis. Explicit critique of the Lutheran *ordo salutis* as a misorientation.

**4. Christoformity (Formation Theology)**
McKnight's preferred term for the shape of Christian moral formation is *Christoformity* rather than Gorman's *cruciformity*. Gorman's term is too narrow: it risks reducing the template for Christian formation to the cross alone, omitting the resurrection, ascension, and return. Formation into Christ means formation into the *whole* Christ-event. The Christoform life is embodied in the new-creation fellowship of the church, expressed in household relations (3:18–4:1) and ethics (3:5–17). See [[christoformity|Christoformity]].

**5. Christological Anthropology (Col 1:15 — Image of God)**
On the *eikōn* of 1:15, McKnight inverts the common Adam Christology direction. The usual move: Jesus is the "Second Adam," a better version of the prototype. McKnight: this is *not* Adamic Christology but *christological anthropology*. If Christ is the prior Eikōn-template according to which creation was made (1:16: all things created "in him, through him, and for him"), then Adam was modeled *on Christ*, not Christ modeled on Adam. Christ is not Adam 2.0; Adam is a secondary *prōtotokos*-eikōn instantiation. Creation theology is thus christo-teleological: to contemplate creation is to encounter a manifestation of the Son.

**6. Powers as Polluted Structures (Col 2:15)**
McKnight's excursus on "The Powers as Polluted Structures" (at Col 1:16; 2:15) reads the powers not as a celestial hierarchy of supernatural beings (the Pseudo-Dionysius/Aquinas tradition) but as *earthly, systemic manifestations of (perhaps fallen) angelic powers* — the sociopolitical structures through which cosmic/angelic rebellion against God takes concrete, institutional form. "Thrones" and "dominions" are heavenly, invisible potentates; "rulers" and "authorities" are their earthly, visible servants. The cross did not just defeat spiritual beings; it stripped the systemic structures through which those powers operate. See [[principalities-powers|Principalities and Powers]].

**7. Household Code as Anti-Aristotle (Col 3:18–4:1)**
McKnight reads the household code as subversive of Roman honor-shame structures, not as their theological legitimation. His principal argument: in the Colossian code, the husband is *never told to lead*. He is told to *love* (3:19) and not to be harsh. Submission is grounded not in the husband's authority or in any creation-order hierarchy but in Christoform living ("as is fitting in the Lord," 3:18). McKnight explicitly charges most complementarian commentary with being "more Aristotelian than Christian," substituting Aristotle's "natural hierarchy" language for the gospel's Christoform mutuality. The husband's love is defined through the Song of Solomon (covenantal presence, advocacy, flourishing), not Aristotle's *Politics*. The key equality statement at 4:1 ("provide what is *right and fair*") grounds the entire code in justice, not hierarchy. Direct contrast with [[frank-thielman|Thielman]]'s complementarian reading of the same Haustafel tradition. See [[ephesians-household-code|The Ephesians Household Code]] for the contested interpretation.

**8. Realized Eschatology as Cosmic Vision**
McKnight acknowledges Colossians' more present-focused eschatology but reframes the standard diagnosis. The usual claim: Colossians has "shifted" from Paul's genuine inaugurated eschatology to a more realized one (hence pseudonymity). McKnight: this misses the *cosmic vision* at work. The future is not displaced but swallowed into a cosmic frame — "the immanent making immediate the future." Hope is not just what will happen; it is the force driving the ecclesial community toward the cosmic reconciliation already accomplished in Christ.

**9. Anti-Empire as Supra-Imperial**
McKnight engages but moderates the anti-empire reading. He endorses Galinsky's "supra-imperial" over Walsh/Maier's "anti-imperial" — Paul envisions the cosmic lordship of Christ that transcends and relativizes all earthly empire without engaging in direct political subversion. He warns against circular reasoning in empire-critical hermeneutics: reading Roman sources, then reading Paul in light of those sources, and concluding Paul is anti-imperial is circular. The issue is not whether Paul saw tension between the kingdom of God and Rome (he obviously did) but whether his primary concern is political subversion.

**10. Ephesian Imprisonment**
McKnight favors Ephesian rather than Roman origin for Colossians (~52–57 CE): the distance argument (Onesimus more plausibly traveled to Ephesus than Rome), Aristarchus's arrest in Ephesus (Acts 19:29), Philemon 22's implied proximity. The earthquake at Colossae (61 or 64 CE) sets the terminus ante quem.

## Relationship to Other Commentators

- **Thielman** (BECNT Ephesians): Agrees on Pauline authorship of Colossians; disagrees sharply on the household code (complementarian vs. anti-hierarchical). Both are within the evangelical Protestant tradition but represent significantly different interpretive positions on the Haustafel.
- **Dunn** (NIGTC Colossians): Engages extensively; appreciates Dunn's Jewish background work; differs on whether the opponents are more broadly syncretistic (Dunn) vs. purely Jewish (McKnight).
- **Wright** (Colossians/Philemon, Tyndale): Appreciates Wright's christological-historiographical framework; generally aligned.
- **Gorman**: Appreciates "cruciformity" but corrects it to "Christoformity."
- **Barclay**: Significant influence; McKnight's emphasis on grace-shaped ethics that subvert Roman honor-worth structures draws heavily on Barclay's *Paul and the Gift* (2015).

## Known Controversies

- The *household code* reading is directly in tension with complementarian interpretations prevalent in evangelical commentary (Thielman, O'Brien, Wright on Ephesians).
- His authorship methodology critique has been influential but is disputed: some argue the co-authorship argument does not fully explain the degree of Colossians' theological divergence from Romans/Galatians.
