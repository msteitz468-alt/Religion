# John Philoponus

John Philoponus (c. 490–c. 570), the Alexandrian Aristotelian commentator and miaphysite theologian, is for Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], Ch. 5) "both the most remarkable and the most idiosyncratic figure in sixth-century Patristic philosophy." A major philosopher of late antiquity in his own right (his physics influenced Galileo), he was condemned by the Byzantine Church on two counts: his miaphysite Christology and his role in the [[tritheist-controversy|tritheist controversy]]. Zachhuber rejects the view that he merely "applied" philosophy to doctrine or was insincere: "it is hard to deny the sincerity and commitment he brought to the topics under investigation."

## Physis Used in Two Ways

Philoponus's key distinction: *physis* is used "in a twofold manner" — (1) for the universal species "on its own," its common *logos*, "which does not exist in any of the individuals"; and (2) for "the same common nature which exists in the individuals and assumes a particular existence in each of them" — the **particular nature** (ἰδικωτάτη φύσις) (*Arbiter* 7). Particular nature differs from hypostasis only in that "hypostasis in addition also signifies those properties... by which they are separated from each other" (idiomata).

## Particular Natures and the Division of the Universal

In the *Arbiter* (Diaitetes, c. 552, addressed to Justinian) Philoponus writes of an actual **division of the nature itself**: the common *logos* of human nature, "albeit it is in itself one, but when realized in many subjects, becomes many, existing in each completely and not partially." The particular natures are genuinely many — here "Philoponus directly contradicts Gregory of Nyssa's notorious claim that plural use of universal terms was catachrestical." His analogies (the shipbuilder's idea realized in many ships; knowledge multiplied in students; the ring producing many seals) point toward Platonic *ante rem* universals. He endorses the miaphysite **WT/WHI argument**: the common divine nature did not become incarnate, "for in that case... God the Logos was united with all human beings."

## Rejection of the Concrete Universal

"The major observation... is the complete absence in Philoponus of the concrete universal." He never treats ousia as the totality of all individuals — without arguing against it, "there can be no doubt that he did not consider it a philosophically viable understanding of the universal." In this he "practically sided with the Chalcedonians of the sixth century" who had also discarded it, and equated hypostasis with the **individual (atomon)** in Peripatetic fashion: that "in which the division into genera and species comes to an end."

## Reconstruction of the Classical Theory

Philoponus introduced two changes to the Cappadocian theory: (1) particular substances/natures; (2) abandonment of the concrete universal in favour of an **abstract universal treated as a merely conceptual entity** — the Godhead one only "in mind and reason." Drawing on Aristotle (via Alexander of Aphrodisias): "the universal is either nothing or posterior" (*De An.* A.1); "one can find an individual without a universal, for example the single sun." Zachhuber's verdict: Philoponus "rigorously drew the conceptual conclusions from recent doctrinal developments resulting in a shift of the ontological focus from the universal to the particular," perceiving the underlying tension "more than anyone else did in the sixth century."

## The Tritheist Controversy

See [[tritheist-controversy|the dedicated page]]. Philoponus's earlier *Against Andrew* "took an entirely traditional line on the trinitarian issue"; he was drawn in (c. 567) after Athanasius (grandson of Empress Theodora) sent him John Ascoutzanges's book. He came to accept "three individual substances" in the Trinity, homoousios but distinct: "we do not speak of three divinities of different substances... but call those divinities homoousioi." His argument: a common nature is *ipso facto* divisible — "if it was not divisible, it was not common." Zachhuber stresses the change was not a new theory of universals but "his new willingness to speak of 'three substances, natures, and divinities'" as "a direct consequence of the Severan postulation of particular natures."

## Major Works

- ***Arbiter (Diaitetes)*** — irenic Christological treatise, preserved in Syriac and (partly) in Greek via John of Damascus and the *Doctrina Patrum*.
- ***Tmemata*** — later, harder-line Christological work (fragments in Michael the Syrian).
- **Tritheist fragments** (*De trinitate*, *Contra Themistium*, etc., ed. van Roey).
- Numerous **Aristotelian commentaries** and *De aeternitate mundi contra Proclum*.
