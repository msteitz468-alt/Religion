# Russell Kirkland

Russell Kirkland was a professor of East Asian religions at the University of Georgia and one of
the leading revisionist scholars of Taoism in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. His
*Taoism: The Enduring Tradition* (Routledge, 2004) is a comprehensive critique of the
paradigms that governed 20th-century Western and Chinese sinological study of Taoism.

## Hermeneutical Method

Kirkland's method is a **corrective historical-critical approach**: returning to primary sources
in the Tao-tsang and the full range of historical evidence (inscriptions, court records, social
data) rather than privileging the two "philosophical classics" (*Lao-tzu* and *Chuang-tzu*) as
representing "authentic Taoism." He is particularly concerned with the ways in which Confucian
ideological biases — both premodern Chinese and modern Western — distorted all scholarly
discourse about Taoism.

Key methodological commitments:
- **People over texts**: the actual practices and communities of Taoists, not textual idealization
- **The full Tao-tsang**: the proper data set for Taoism, not a selection of two "classics"
- **Social history**: who practiced Taoism, of what class and gender, across what periods
- **Political history**: Taoism's deep entanglement with imperial legitimation across centuries

## Major Arguments

1. **The tao-chia/tao-chiao dichotomy is false**: There was never a group of people who identified
   as practitioners of "philosophical Taoism" (*tao-chia*); this category was invented by modern
   scholars following Confucian prejudices. See [[taochia-taochiao-dichotomy]].

2. **The Nei-yeh is the foundational text**: The *Nei-yeh* (Kuan-tzu ch. 49), focused on
   [[biospiritual-cultivation|biospiritual cultivation]], predates the *Tao te ching* and is a
   more accurate index of what actual early practitioners valued. See [[nei-yeh]].

3. **Taoism as organized community dates from the 5th c. CE**: No social group calling itself
   "Taoism" existed before [[lu-hsiu-ching|Lu Hsiu-ching]] organized the Ling-pao texts into the
   first Taoist canon (~471 CE).

4. **The Tao te ching is a composite** with three distinct textual strata: the Guodian bamboo slips
   (~300 BCE), the Ma-wang-tui silk texts (~200 BCE), and the received Wang Pi text. The political
   and feminine-imagery passages are later additions (possibly the work of Huan Yüan or chi-hsia
   academy scholars). See [[daodejing]].

5. **Biospiritual cultivation is the continuous Taoist core**: Cultivation of *ch'i*, *ching*,
   and *shen* — not wu-wei naturalism or immortality-seeking — is the thread connecting Nei-yeh,
   T'ien-shih, Shang-ch'ing, Ch'üan-chen, and Lung-men.

6. **Taoism was never anti-political**: Taoists from the T'ien-shih through the T'ang literati
   actively engaged in imperial legitimation (the T'ang dynasty's founding was legitimized by
   the Taoist master Wang Yüan-chih).

## Relation to Other Scholars

Kirkland acknowledges the work of Anna Seidel, Michel Strickmann, and Stephen Bokenkamp as
formative for the modern study of Taoism. He critiques the earlier generation (Maspero, Creel,
Welch, Fung Yu-lan) for perpetuating the tao-chia/tao-chiao framework. He also critiques
Isabelle Robinet's characterization of Taoists as nature-loving hermits as unfounded.
