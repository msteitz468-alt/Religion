# Moses de León

Spanish kabbalist, by overwhelming scholarly consensus the principal author and compiler of the [[zohar|Zohar]] — the foundational work of [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] — which he composed and circulated in Castile in the last quarter of the 13th century while attributing it to the 2nd-century Tanna [[shimon-bar-yochai|Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai]]. The question of the Zohar's authorship is one of the central controversies of Jewish intellectual history; see [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]].

---

## Biographical Context

Little is known of his life with certainty. Born around 1240, probably in León (Castile), Moses ben Shem Tov lived in various Castilian towns — Guadalajara (where he produced Zoharic material in the 1280s), Valladolid, and Ávila, where he died in 1305 while returning home from a journey. He was a member of the vibrant circle of Castilian kabbalists active c. 1270–1310 that included the brothers Isaac and Jacob ha-Kohen and Todros Abulafia, a circle whose interest in the demonic "left emanation" ([[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]]) deeply shaped the Zohar's mythology.

De León was learned in:
- **Rabbinic literature** — Talmud, Midrash, and the aggadic tradition the Zohar everywhere presupposes and reworks
- **Medieval Jewish philosophy** — he owned and was influenced by a copy of Maimonides' *Guide of the Perplexed*; the Zohar both absorbs and combats Maimonidean rationalism
- **Earlier Kabbalah** — the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, the Geronese kabbalists (Naḥmanides, Azriel), and Neoplatonic emanationist sources

In addition to the Zohar (written in an artificial, archaizing Aramaic), de León wrote about twenty works in Hebrew under his own name — among them *Sefer ha-Rimmon* (*The Book of the Pomegranate*, on the mystical reasons for the commandments), *Shekel ha-Qodesh*, and *Mishkan ha-Edut*. These Hebrew works share the Zohar's distinctive doctrines and vocabulary, which is a principal piece of evidence for his authorship.

## Hermeneutical Method

De León / the Zohar operates at the **Sod** (secret) level of [[pardes|PaRDeS]], reading the Torah as an encoded account of the inner life of God — the dynamic interplay of the ten [[sefirot|sefirot]]. His characteristic moves:

- **Theosophical exegesis**: every verse, name, and ritual detail decodes to sefirotic reality
- **Mythic narrative**: in place of the philosophers' abstractions, de León restores a vivid, dramatic, often erotic portrait of the divine inner life (the union of Tif'eret and [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]]; the war against the Sitra Aḥra)
- **Pseudepigraphy as method**: by setting his teaching in the mouth of Rabbi Shim'on bar Yoḥai and his Companions wandering through 2nd-century Galilee, de León clothes radical innovation in the authority of ancient tradition — fully consistent with Kabbalah's self-understanding as *qabbalah* ("received tradition"), not invention

---

## Characteristic Positions

- **Theurgy**: the commandments (*mitsvot*) have cosmic functions; their performance with proper intention maintains the union of the sefirot. This is the heart of de León's *Sefer ha-Rimmon* and the Zohar's halakhic sections
- **The divine feminine**: de León gives the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] her fullest development as an independent divine persona, the Bride of Tif'eret
- **Evil as the Other Side**: he inherits and dramatizes the Castilian doctrine of the [[sitra-ahra|Sitra Aḥra]] as a parasitic shadow of the holy emanation
- **Torah as the body of God**: the Torah is not a book about God but a living manifestation of God; its letters are divine names

## The Authorship Question

After de León's death in 1305, his widow reportedly told the kabbalist Isaac of Acre (who was investigating the Zohar's origins) that her husband had written the entire work himself and attributed it to Shim'on bar Yoḥai to increase its value and salability — "because if I wrote under my own name, no one would buy it." The reliability of this report (preserved in Isaac of Acre's partly-lost *Divrei ha-Yamim*) is itself contested, but it crystallizes the modern critical consensus. The linguistic, conceptual, and historical evidence — anachronistic Aramaic, dependence on medieval sources, doctrines unattested before the 13th century, shared vocabulary with de León's signed Hebrew works — converges on his authorship. See the full treatment in [[zohar-authorship|Zohar Authorship Controversy]].

Whether "author" is even the right category is debated: some scholars (Yehuda Liebes) argue the Zohar emerged from a circle of kabbalists engaged in shared mystical-literary creativity, with de León as the central but not sole figure.

---

## Influence

The Zohar de León produced became, within two centuries, the third canonical pillar of Judaism alongside the Bible and the Talmud — studied, venerated as ancient and quasi-revealed, and the foundation for all later Kabbalah (Cordovero, Luria) and for Ḥasidism. Few pseudepigraphical authors in history have had a comparable impact. The irony is profound: a work of the 1280s, presented as a work of the 180s, reshaped Judaism's conception of God.
