# Cyril of Alexandria

Cyril (c. 376–444), Patriarch of Alexandria, was the most influential Greek theologian of the fifth century and the central protagonist of the Christological controversy against [[nestorius|Nestorius]]. In Johannes Zachhuber's [[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], Cyril is key evidence that [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|Cappadocian philosophy]] had, within two generations, become the **"classical theory"** — a shared intellectual *koine* — even for a thinker rooted in the rival Alexandrian tradition. He is venerated across Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian churches alike; his "one incarnate nature of the Word" formula became the banner of [[oriental-orthodoxy|miaphysite]] Christology.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Cyril's theological formation was Alexandrian: his anti-Arian Trinitarian argument depends heavily on [[athanasius|Athanasius]] (and Didymus the Blind), and in the heat of the Nestorian controversy he drew — unknowingly — on [[apollinarius-of-laodicea|Apollinarius]]'s writings (transmitted under orthodox names) for his anti-Antiochene Christology. His vast corpus is matched in size only by Chrysostom's in the East and Augustine's and Jerome's in the West. His Trinitarian works (the *Thesaurus on the Holy and Consubstantial Trinity*, the *Dialogues on the Trinity*, the *Commentary on John*) date to the early 420s, only ~40 years after Gregory of Nyssa.

## The Classical Theory in Cyril (Zachhuber's argument)

Zachhuber treats Cyril as an "unlikely candidate" for Cappadocian influence — which makes the evidence all the more telling:

- **Terminology.** Cyril unapologetically deploys *ousia*, *physis*, *hypostasis*, *idion*/*idiōma*, and (unlike the more reluctant Athanasius and Cappadocians) *homoousios* freely — the rapid normalization of the once-controversial technical vocabulary.
- **Univocal predication → co-ordination.** Cyril takes over the specifically Cappadocian argument (built on Aristotle's *Categories*: synonyms share a "formula of being," *Cat.* 1; substances admit "no more or less," *Cat.* 4) that univocal predication entails ontological equality: "a human being is not more or less a human being than another"; so the Son is *homoousios* with the Father, called "greater" only *oikonomikōs* (in salvation history, glossing John 14:28).
- **Universal and particular.** He distinguishes universal names ("human being" = the totality of the race, the *ousia* itself) from proper names ("Peter," "Paul," dividing humanity into particulars) — echoing the *Epistle 38*.
- **From logic to ontology.** Crucially, Cyril takes the further step of treating universal natures as concrete realities (*pragma*) — wholes that comprehend their individuals. He speaks of the unity of human nature as **identity** (not mere likeness), grounded in a "natural bond" from Adam, and of "the whole nature" affected by Adam's fall and Christ's resurrection — importing Gregory of Nyssa's concrete account into a soteriological key.

## Christology and the Conceptual Crisis

Cyril's defense of the personal unity of Christ pushed the classical theory to its breaking point:
- He insisted on a **union *kath' hypostasin*** (according to hypostasis) and condemned "dividing the hypostases of the one Christ after the union" (third anathema against Nestorius).
- Zachhuber shows Cyril **accepted the Cappadocian correlation** that two natures, existing as individuated, imply two hypostases — which is why he polemicized against confessing "two" after the union and pressed instead for one incarnate nature (*mia physis*). His move toward "no *physis* without *hypostasis*" is not an abandonment of the Cappadocian framework but a development of its tenet that the universal exists only in concrete hypostases.
- He embraced the **[[double-homoousion|double homoousion]]** (Christ consubstantial with the Father in divinity, with us in humanity) in the *Formula of Reunion* (433), insisting on its symmetric form against Nestorius — more than mere political acquiescence.
- His second *Letter to Succensus* preserves the sharpest early statement of the conceptual objection: if Christ is consubstantial with us, must not his human nature continue to "subsist" (*hyphestēken*) — i.e. exist as a hypostasis — after the union?

## Major Works

- ***Thesaurus on the Holy and Consubstantial Trinity*** and ***Dialogues on the Trinity*** — anti-Arian/anti-Eunomian Trinitarian theology.
- ***Commentary on John*** — exegesis with a strong Trinitarian focus.
- ***Against Nestorius***, the **twelve anathemas**, letters to Nestorius, to Succensus, and the ***Formula of Reunion*** (433) — the Christological corpus.

## Reception

His second *Letter to Nestorius* was accepted as doctrinally normative at the Council of Chalcedon (451), even as the Council's "two natures in one hypostasis" formula diverged from Cyril's preferred *mia physis* — the fault line that produced the [[chalcedonian-definition|Chalcedonian schism]]. Miaphysites (esp. Severus of Antioch — covered in a later scope) claimed Cyril as their authority; Chalcedonians (the "neo-Chalcedonians") worked to show Cyril and Chalcedon compatible.
