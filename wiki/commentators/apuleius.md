# Apuleius of Madaura

Latin Platonist, rhetorician, and author from Roman North Africa (c. 124 – after 170 CE) — "African
Apuleius, who was learned both in the Greek and Latin tongues." He is the chief target of
[[city-of-god|*City of God*]] **Books VIII–IX**, where [[augustine|Augustine]] uses Apuleius's own
treatise **_De Deo Socratis_ ("On the God of Socrates")** to demolish the doctrine of demons as
mediators.

## Doctrine of Demons (as Augustine reports it)

Apuleius divides all rational animals into three kinds by their dwelling: **gods** in heaven, **men** on
earth, **demons** in the air between. Demons "have immortality of body in common with the gods, but
passions of the mind in common with men." His formal definition: demons are "in nature animals, in soul
subject to passion, in mind reasonable, in body aerial, in duration eternal." On this scheme demons
carry men's prayers up to the gods and the gods' answers back down — they are the necessary mediators.

## Augustine's Critique

- Nothing in Apuleius's definition is a genuine good: rationality joined to passion without virtue only
  makes demons *more* capable of misery and vice.
- Demons are therefore worse mediators than any good man — "a chaste man must use as patrons… demons who
  love these crimes." It is incoherent to worship what one ought not to imitate, "when it is the highest
  duty of religion to imitate Him whom thou worshippest."
- Apuleius's own admissions collapse any distinction between *good* and *evil* demons (Book IX): their
  mind is "a sea tossed with tempest."
- Augustine notes the irony that Apuleius was himself accused of magic in his lifetime and had to defend
  himself. He judges Apuleius, however, more cautious than [[porphyry|Porphyry]] in shielding the
  celestial gods from the passions.
