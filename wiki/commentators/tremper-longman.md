Tremper Longman III was a colleague of [[bruce-waltke|Bruce Waltke]] at Westminster Theological Seminary before moving to Westmont College. He received his Ph.D. from Yale University with a dissertation on Akkadian fictional autobiography — a genre study that became the foundation of his approach to Ecclesiastes. He co-authored *An Introduction to the Old Testament* with the late Ray Dillard and co-edited the *Baker Commentary on the Old Testament Wisdom and Psalms* series.

Longman represents a more historically critical strand of evangelical OT scholarship than Waltke — more willing to accept non-Solomonic authorship of Wisdom texts, more attentive to ANE genre analysis, and more prepared to read biblical speakers as theologically unreliable voices requiring canonical correction.

## Background

Longman spent fifteen years on this commentary, offering it in a doctoral seminar at Westminster. His doctoral dissertation analyzed 15 Akkadian fictional autobiography texts, establishing the genre that he applies to Ecclesiastes' body (1:12-12:7). This genre work is unique in the wiki — the only commentary whose interpretive framework derives directly from the commentator's own primary academic research in ANE literature.

He dedicates the commentary to psychologist Dan Allender, a boyhood friend and professional collaborator; the Ecclesiastes commentary reflects a pastoral and psychological attentiveness to suffering and meaninglessness alongside the philological rigor.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Two-Voice Canonical Reading

Longman's central interpretive move: the book has two speakers — Qohelet (1:12-12:7) and the Frame Narrator (1:1-11; 12:8-14) — and the book's normative theology belongs to the latter, not the former. The body of the book is a *quotation*, a long teaching example used by the Frame Narrator to warn his son about the dangers of skeptical wisdom.

This reading strategy parallels the structure of Job: both books place theologically problematic speeches in the mouths of human characters (Job's friends; Qohelet) and correct them with a normative voice at the end (Yahweh's speech; the Frame Narrator's epilogue). Recognizing this structure is the prerequisite for canonical interpretation of both books.

### 2. Genre Analysis: Akkadian Fictional Autobiography

The genre of Qohelet's speech (1:12-12:7) parallels 15 Akkadian fictional autobiography texts: three-part structure (first-person introduction + extended narrative + wisdom admonitions), with the first-person speaker being a historically later author placing his words in the mouth of a famous predecessor. The genre was not considered deceptive — the fictional nature was transparent. This explains and validates the Solomonic persona: Qohelet speaks *as* Solomon to establish that even the richest and wisest human being found no ultimate meaning "under the sun."

### 3. Grammatico-Historical Exegesis with ANE Contextualization

Longman does not reject traditional exegetical methods but uses them within a genre-conscious framework. He takes the unusual Hebrew of Ecclesiastes seriously (interacting extensively with Daniel Fredericks's linguistic study) without treating linguistic peculiarities as decisive for dating. He is attentive to the Septuagint and other ancient versions.

### 4. Reader Identification and Pastoral Sensitivity

The commentary reflects Longman's interest (developed with Dan Allender) in the psychology of human despair and meaning-seeking. He reads Qohelet as accurately diagnosing the human condition apart from God's redemption — not as a theological error but as a reliable witness to what life "under the sun" feels like. This grounds the book's canonical utility: it is the Bible's honest voice for those experiencing meaninglessness.

## Key Theological Positions

**Qohelet is not the book's normative voice**: The body of the book presents a skeptical wisdom teacher; the Frame Narrator's epilogue corrects him. Identifying Qohelet's theology with the book's message is the most common interpretive error — the same error as identifying Job's speeches with the book's message.

**Non-Solomonic authorship**: The internal evidence (past tense "I was king," Eccl 1:12; "all who ruled Jerusalem before me," 1:16; the drop of the Solomonic persona after ch. 3; the non-royal perspective on oppression) and the genre evidence (Akkadian fictional autobiography) both require that Qohelet is *not* Solomon. Even conservative evangelical scholars (Delitzsch, Young) accepted this.

**Qohelet's God as distant and dangerous**: Qohelet uses *Elohim* (transcendence/power), never *YHWH* (covenant/relationship). His "fear God" means dread before a powerful being — the opposite of the warm covenantal reverence that *yir'at YHWH* means in Proverbs 1:7.

**The *carpe diem* passages as resignation, not affirmation**: Qohelet's guarded recommendations of pleasure (eating, drinking, work) are the best available in a meaningless world — resignation to the minimum, not celebration of God's gifts.

**The epilogue's frame narrator is *critical* of Qohelet**: The goads and nails metaphors (12:11) are *harmful*, not positive. "No end to the making of many books" includes Qohelet's writings in the warning. The Frame Narrator's stance toward Qohelet is ambivalent praise moving to implicit warning.

**Romans 8 as the NT key**: The Septuagint's *mataiotes* (= *hebel*) in Romans 8:18-21 ("the creation was subjected to futility") is an allusion to Ecclesiastes. Qohelet describes the world under the curse accurately. Jesus Christ redeems from the *hebel* world by entering and overcoming it.

## A Key Tension With Waltke

Waltke and Longman were colleagues at Westminster and share a Reformed evangelical tradition, but their approaches to the Wisdom literature create a significant tension:

- **Waltke on "fear of the LORD"** (Prov 1:7): A compound expression of covenantal love, trust, and reverence; the foundation of wisdom; rational + non-rational; *positive*
- **Longman on "fear God"** (Eccl 5:6; 7:15-18; 8:12-13): Dread before a powerful and dangerous being; no covenantal warmth; *negative*
- **Waltke**: Solomonic authorship of Proverbs defended; pseudepigraphy rejected
- **Longman**: Non-Solomonic authorship of Ecclesiastes accepted; fictional autobiography genre validates literary persona

This tension is precisely the tension between Proverbs and Ecclesiastes *within the Wisdom canon itself* — the Frame Narrator's epilogue resolves it by reasserting Prov 1:7 (12:13) as the corrective to Qohelet's despair.

## Ecclesiastes (NICOT 1998) — Fidelity Reingest (2026-07-18)

Source page: [[longman-ecclesiastes-nicot]]. Prefaces + Intro I–XIV + Text and Commentary 1:1–12:14 + Final Word re-read with line loci (~258 grounded claims).

**Method (Ecclesiastes-specific, confirmed on body):** Genre = **framed wisdom autobiography**; body 1:12–12:7 // Akkadian fictional autobiography (Cuthaean Legend of Naram-Sin et al.). Two voices: Qohelet = virtual quotation and **foil**; Frame Narrator = author of the finished book and normative theology. Solomonic **persona** for the search (chs. 1–3 only), not historical Solomonic authorship. Language unusual but not a secure date barometer (Fredericks; Zimmermann/Dahood rejected). MT largely stable; Aquila-like LXX often secondary.

**Theology (load-bearing loci):** *Hebel* = Longman’s preferred “**meaningless**” (30+ times; 1:2 // 12:8 inclusio). Twin sources of anguish: **death** and inability to know the **right time** (time and chance, 9:11). God = distant *Elohim*, never *YHWH*; Qohelet’s “fear God” = fright. Six *carpe diem* passages = **resignation** / anodyne, not Kaiser–Whybray affirmation. Epilogue 12:9–12: reserved praise → *sought to find* failure → goads/nails as **sting/harm** (Fox) → warn son including Qohelet’s books. Normative close 12:13–14: fear God + keep commandments + eschatological judgment (wisdom + law + prophets). **A Final Word:** Christ redeems from vanity under the sun (Rom 8:18–21 *mataiotes*; Gal 3:13; self-emptying entry into the cursed world).

**Commentary structural outline (Longman):** I. Frame prologue 1:1–11; II. Qohelet 1:12–12:7 (intro; “Solomon’s” quest 1:13–2:26; quest continues 3:1–6:9; wise advice 6:10–12:7 ending in death meditation); III. Frame epilogue 12:8–14.

## Song of Songs (NICOT 2001) — Fidelity Reingest (2026-07-18)

Source page: [[longman-song-of-songs-nicot]]. Preface + Intro I–XII + Poems 1–23 re-read with line loci.

**Method (Song-specific):** Genre is the “key” (Saadia): the book is an **anthology of 23 lyric love poems** (“erotic psalter”), not allegory, drama, or *hieros gamos*. Superscription *lĕšĕlōmōh* = “**concerns** Solomon,” not full authorship. Egyptian Ramesside love poetry is the primary ANE parallel. Rejects Provan/Delitzsch plot constructions for lack of stage directions and uncertain speakers (~10%).

**Theology (three dimensions, Intro §XII):** (1) human eros primary — mutual, exclusive, total, beautiful; marriage-in-canon (Childs); “do not awaken” thrice; (2) Gen 2–3 → Song *tĕšûqâ* (7:11 ET 7:10) as already–not-yet redemption of intimacy (Trible-shaped); (3) divine–human marriage metaphor (Hosea–Eph 5–Rev 19) **illuminated** without suppressing human love or arbitrary detail-pressing. Carr’s **functional decanonization**: literal recognition without use is half-defeat.

**Commentary climaxes:** garden of love 4:10–5:1; love strong as death / flame of Yah (8:6–7); Solomon vineyard foil (8:11–12); open ending (8:13–14).
