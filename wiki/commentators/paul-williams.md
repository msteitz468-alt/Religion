# Paul Williams

**Paul Williams** (b. 1950) is a British scholar of Buddhism, long at the University of
Bristol, and one of the foremost anglophone authorities on **[[mahayana|Mahayana]]**
Buddhist philosophy. He is the wiki's principal *academic* (rather than confessional) voice
on Indian Buddhist thought — complementing the practitioner-scholars
[[bhikkhu-bodhi|Bodhi]], [[walpola-rahula|Rahula]], and [[thich-nhat-hanh|Thich Nhat Hanh]].

## Context and Works

Williams's *Mahāyāna Buddhism: The Doctrinal Foundations* (1989) is a standard survey of
the Great Vehicle; with Anthony Tribe and Alexander Wynne he wrote
**[[williams-buddhist-thought|*Buddhist Thought: A Complete Introduction to the Indian
Tradition*]]** (2000; 2nd ed. 2012), the work ingested here. He also wrote the General
Introduction to the Crosby & Skilton translation of the
**[[bodhicaryavatara|Bodhicaryāvatāra]]** already in the wiki (see
[[crosby-skilton-bodhicaryavatara|that page]]) — so his reading of
[[sunyata|emptiness]] as the negation of inherent existence, and of the
**emptiness of emptiness**, was already in play.

A personal note relevant to method: Williams, long a Buddhist, converted to Roman
[[catholicism|Catholicism]] (recounted in *The Unexpected Way*, 2002). He continues to write
as a rigorous historian of Buddhist philosophy; the wiki treats his exposition as scholarly
description, not advocacy.

## Hermeneutical Method

Williams reads the Indian texts **historically and philosophically** — reconstructing what
the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*, the Abhidharma analysts, [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]], the Yogācāra
masters, and the Buddha-nature literature were actually *arguing*, and submitting those
arguments to analytic scrutiny. He is known for pressing the texts' logic hard: e.g. his
insistence that the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* really does deny any Self whatever (against the
"the Buddha only showed what is *not* the Self" reading), and his careful exposition of
**[[yogacara|Yogācāra]]**'s "three natures" as an *antidote to the nihilist reading of
emptiness*.
