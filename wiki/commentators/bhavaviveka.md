# Bhāvaviveka

**Bhāvaviveka** (c. 6th c.; also **Bhāviveka**, **Bhavya**) is the commentator on
[[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]] whom the Tibetan tradition treats as the founder of the
**[[svatantrika-prasangika-distinction|Svātantrika]]** stream of [[madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]]. In the
[[lamrim-chenmo|Lamrim Chenmo]] (Vol. III) he is cited both as an authority (e.g. on serenity, the
elephant-taming simile, the threefold etymology of *paramārtha*) and as the principal foil whose
position [[candrakirti|Candrakīrti]] — and [[tsongkhapa|Tsong-kha-pa]] following him — refutes.

## Works (as cited in the Lamrim Chenmo)

- **Prajñāpradīpa** (*Lamp for the Fundamental Treatise*) — his commentary on the
  [[mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] (the *Tarkajvālā*'s root).
- **Tarkajvālā** (*Blaze of Reasons*) — autocommentary on his *Madhyamakahṛdaya*.
- **Madhyamakahṛdaya** (*Heart of the Middle Way*).

## The Svātantrika Position

The defining difference, as Tsong-kha-pa frames it: Bhāvaviveka accepts that phenomena exist **"by
way of their own character" (*svalakṣaṇa*) conventionally**, even while denying intrinsic existence
ultimately. Because a non-mistaken valid cognition can then establish a subject "appearing in common"
to both disputants, he holds that the Mādhyamika must — and may — use **autonomous (*svatantra*)
syllogisms** with subject and reason established for both parties. He criticized
[[buddhapalita|Buddhapālita]]'s mere-consequence method as powerless, and added the qualifier
"ultimately" to refutations of production (which Candrakīrti calls "senseless"). At the conventional
level he sides with the Sautrāntikas (accepting substantially existent minute particles and external
objects).

Per the same source, the **Yogācāra-Svātantrika** masters Śāntarakṣita and [[kamalasila|Kamalaśīla]]
form a later branch (external objects do not exist even conventionally; *blue* exists in
consciousness). [[candrakirti|Candrakīrti]]'s refutation — that nothing has intrinsic character even
conventionally, so no commonly-established subject exists — is the heart of the Prāsaṅgika critique
of autonomous argument.
