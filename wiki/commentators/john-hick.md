# John Hick

## Biographical Context

John Harwood Hick (1922–2012) was an English philosopher of religion and theologian, the
most influential 20th-century advocate of **[[religious-pluralism|religious pluralism]]**.
He began as a conservative evangelical, was trained in philosophy at Edinburgh and
Oxford, and was ordained in the Presbyterian Church of England. Over a long career —
Cambridge, then a contested chair at Birmingham, then Princeton Theological Seminary and
finally the Claremont Graduate University — his theology moved steadily away from
confessional Christian exclusivism toward a global, cross-tradition framework.

Two biographical facts shaped the trajectory. First, his early work in epistemology
(*Faith and Knowledge*, 1957) framed religious belief as an interpretive "experiencing-as"
rather than as propositional inference — a move that later let him treat *all* the
traditions as interpretive responses to a common reality. Second, his years in religiously
plural Birmingham, working alongside Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, and Jewish communities, gave the
abstract problem of competing truth-claims a concrete, civic urgency. His heresy was
formally complained of within Presbyterian circles (over his rejection of a literal
incarnation), but never disciplined.

His magnum opus, ***An Interpretation of Religion: Human Responses to the Transcendent***
(1989), delivered as the 1986–87 Gifford Lectures, is the systematic statement of the
pluralistic hypothesis and the primary source for this page.

## Hermeneutical Method

**Discipline**: Analytic philosophy of religion, working cross-traditionally rather than
within any single scriptural-hermeneutical system (not the Quadriga, not PaRDeS, not
tafsir). Hick treats the doctrines, scriptures, and mystical reports of all the major
traditions as **data** for a second-order, religion-spanning theory.

**Framework — a "religious interpretation of religion."** Hick rejects both the
naturalistic reduction of religion (religion as projection/illusion — Feuerbach, Freud)
and the confessional claim that one tradition alone is true. His method is to ask what
hypothesis best accounts for the data of religious life *taken as a whole* on the
assumption that the traditions are not simply mistaken.

Distinctive methodological moves:

- **Critical realism about religion.** Religious language *intends* a transcendent reality
  and is not merely expressive or regulative — against the non-realism of Braithwaite,
  D. Z. Phillips, and Don Cupitt. See [[religious-realism|religious realism vs.
  non-realism]]. But that reality is known only as humanly interpreted.
- **Neo-Kantian epistemology.** Borrowing Kant's distinction between the *noumenon* (thing
  in itself) and *phenomenon* (thing as it appears), Hick distinguishes **[[the-real|the
  Real *an sich*]]** from the Real as humanly experienced. The traditions' gods and
  absolutes (Yahweh, the Trinity, Allah, Brahman, the Dharmakaya, Nirvana, the Tao) are
  *phenomenal manifestations* of one noumenal Real, schematised through the conceptual and
  cultural "lenses" of each community.
- **The soteriological criterion.** Religions are to be assessed not by their unverifiable
  metaphysics but by their fruits — their capacity to effect the **transformation of human
  existence from self-centredness to Reality-centredness**, evidenced in the production of
  saints and in the cross-traditional **Golden Rule**. By this criterion the great
  traditions appear to be (so far as we can judge) *roughly equally* effective contexts of
  salvation/liberation.
- **Mythological vs. literal truth.** Conflicting doctrines that cannot be settled by
  evidence (incarnation, reincarnation, the eternity of the universe) are reread as
  **mythologically true** — true insofar as they evoke an appropriate practical response to
  the Real — rather than as literal factual assertions. This is how he dissolves the
  [[#The Conflicting-Truth-Claims Problem|conflicting-truth-claims problem]].

## Characteristic Positions

1. **The pluralistic hypothesis.** The great post-axial traditions are culturally
   conditioned but soteriologically equivalent responses to one ultimate reality, **the
   Real**, which is itself beyond the reach of human concepts. No tradition has privileged
   access; each is a valid "lens." This is the position the whole of *An Interpretation*
   is built to defend. See [[religious-pluralism]].

2. **The Copernican revolution in theology.** Hick's earlier image (1973): theology must
   move from a Ptolemaic model (one's own religion at the center, others orbiting) to a
   Copernican one (the Real/God at the center, all traditions — including one's own —
   orbiting). This reframes the [[religious-pluralism-debate|exclusivism / inclusivism /
   pluralism]] trichotomy.

3. **The Real is ineffable.** The Real *an sich* is beyond the personal/impersonal
   distinction; "personal God" and "non-personal Absolute" are its two basic phenomenal
   ranges. The named deities are **personae of the Real** (Yahweh, Shiva, the Heavenly
   Father); Brahman, the Tao, Nirvana, Sunyata are its **impersonae**. See [[the-real]].

4. **The axial age as the watershed.** Following Jaspers, Hick dates the decisive turn to
   soteriological religion — "salvation/liberation" as transformation — to the **[[axial-age|axial
   age]]** (c. 800–200 BCE). Pre-axial religion is cosmic maintenance; post-axial religion
   is the quest for a limitlessly better state. Jesus, Muhammad, and Mahayana are read as
   later developments *within* axial streams.

5. **The incarnation as metaphor.** That "Jesus is God incarnate" is, taken literally,
   both philosophically incoherent (the Chalcedonian "two natures") and exclusivist in its
   implications; it is better understood as a *mythological/metaphorical* expression of the
   experienced significance of Jesus. This is his most controversial Christian-internal
   claim.

## The Conflicting-Truth-Claims Problem

The standard objection to pluralism is that the traditions plainly *contradict* one
another. Hick sorts the conflicts into three kinds and argues none refutes the hypothesis:

- **Historical** (Did the Exodus happen? Was Jesus crucified — cf. [[crucifixion-in-the-quran|Qur'an
  4:157]]?): in principle settleable by evidence, but mostly under-determined; not
  soteriologically decisive.
- **Trans-historical** (Is the universe eternal? Do we reincarnate or face a single
  judgment?): genuine questions with real answers we cannot reach — analogous to the
  Buddha's *avyakata* ("unanswered questions"). Believing rightly about them is **not
  necessary for salvation**.
- **Conceptions of the ultimate** (personal God vs. non-personal Absolute): not a real
  contradiction once both are seen as phenomenal manifestations of the one Real (the
  wave/particle complementarity analogy).

The single belief the hypothesis *cannot* accommodate as literally true is the claim that
**one tradition alone is salvific** — such claims can be "mythologically true" for a
community but not literally true for all.

## Known Controversies and Criticisms

**The Real collapses into the unknowable.** Critics (Keith Ward, Gavin D'Costa, William
Alston, Paul Eddy) argue that a Real of which literally *no* substantial predicate holds
— not personal, not good, not one-or-many — is indistinguishable from nothing, and cannot
ground the value judgment that the traditions are "in touch" with it. The neo-Kantian
move, they say, either says too little (pure agnosticism) or smuggles in content.

**Covert exclusivism / a meta-theology of its own.** D'Costa's influential charge:
pluralism is not neutral but is itself a substantive (broadly liberal-Protestant,
post-Kantian) theological position that *relativizes every tradition's self-understanding*
— it does to the traditions exactly what it accuses exclusivism of doing, conscripting
them into a framework none of them holds.

**It dissolves what believers actually mean.** Treating incarnation, Trinity, Torah's
election, or rebirth as "mythologically true" is, to confessional critics, a polite way of
saying they are *false* — the pluralist saves the traditions only by gutting their literal
claims. This is the core of the **particularist** reaction (and the line later pressed,
from a comparative-religion angle, against perennialism generally).

**The soteriological criterion is question-begging or unmeasurable.** Judging traditions
"equally productive of saints" presupposes a cross-traditional standard of sainthood and
an empirical claim Hick himself admits is only "impressionistic."

## Influence and Legacy

Hick set the terms of late-20th-century debate in the **theology of religions**. Alan
Race's threefold typology — **exclusivism / inclusivism / pluralism** — became standard
largely as a map of where Hick stood relative to Karl Barth (exclusivism) and Karl Rahner's
"anonymous Christians" (inclusivism). His pluralism became, in Stephen Prothero's words, a
"new orthodoxy" in the academy and in interfaith circles — and precisely for that reason the
chief target of the particularist backlash. Within Christian theology he provoked sustained
rebuttal (D'Costa, Ward, Plantinga-influenced exclusivists); within comparative religion he
is the most philosophically rigorous cousin of the **perennialist** stream (Huston Smith,
the Traditionalists), though Hick's neo-Kantian, historically-conditioned Real differs
sharply from the perennialists' claim of a directly accessible common mystical essence.
