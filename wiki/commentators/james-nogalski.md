James D. Nogalski is Professor of Old Testament at Baylor University and one of the world's leading scholars of the Book of the Twelve (the Minor Prophets as a canonical collection). His doctoral dissertation on *Literary Precursors to the Book of the Twelve* (BZAW, 1993) and subsequent *Redactional Process in the Book of the Twelve* (BZAW, 1993) established him as a major figure in this field. His NICOT volumes on Joel, Obadiah, and Jonah (2023) and Micah (2024) are among the wiki's most recent commentaries.

## Key Contributions

**Joel as scribal prophecy**: Nogalski's most distinctive contribution — Joel is a postexilic literary compilation by a temple-associated scribal prophet, not the record of an 8th-century oral prophet. The book was designed for its canonical location between Hosea and Amos.

**Joel as "literary anchor" of the Twelve**: Every recurring theme across the Twelve is present in Joel. The book functions programmatically, foreshadowing what will unfold across the whole collection.

**The Book of the Twelve as canonical unit**: Nogalski's career-long argument — the twelve Minor Prophets were collected, edited, and arranged as a canonical unit with intertextual connections, shared vocabulary, and recurring theological themes. This is the most significant methodological commitment in his commentary.

**Three Day of Yahweh scenarios in Joel**: Systematic mapping of the book's three distinct temporal scenarios (judgment against Judah; near-term deliverance; long-term transformation with Spirit outpouring and judgment of nations).

**Introduction to the Book of the Twelve**: The commentary opens with a substantial introduction to the Twelve as a collection — its ancient attestation as a unit, recent scholarship, canonical and theological implications — before treating Joel specifically.

**Micah three-phase composition (NICOT 2024)**: Early core (chs. 1–3; 722/701 horizon); exilic/postexilic lawsuit–lament–liturgy (chs. 6–7, entering before 4–5); postexilic anthology of future Zion material (chs. 4–5). Mic 3:12 as Masoretic midpoint of the Twelve; Mic 4:1–3 // Isa 2:2–4 with priority insoluble and function read via divergent continuations; Mic 6:8 as prophet’s rejoinder inside a *rîb* (not free-floating slogan), intertextual with Hos 12:6; Mic 7:18–20 Exod 34:6–7 web plus proposed Jonah 2:3(4) citation when Jonah entered the Twelve.

## Hermeneutical Method

Nogalski works as a **redaction and canonical critic** whose distinctive tool is the close analysis of **inner-biblical citation and allusion**. His method reads each Minor Prophet both discretely and as an editorially shaped component of the Twelve, tracing the "literary breadcrumbs" (catchwords, shared formulas, structural parallels) that he argues invite cross-book reading. Two representative demonstrations in the 2023 volume:

- **Obadiah's dual dependence.** Nogalski argues Obadiah simultaneously reworks **Jeremiah 49:7–22** (its source, adapted with "emphatic expansions") and mirrors **Amos 9** — the shared phrase "from there I will pull you down" (Amos 9:2; Obad 4; Jer 49:16), five *ʾim* ("though") clauses matching Amos 9:1–4, the doubled *hălôʾ* of Obad 5 against Amos 9:7, and the *yrš* ("possess") link of Obad 17 to Amos 9:12 that "explains why Obadiah was placed after Amos." He draws here on his own *Redactional Process in the Book of the Twelve*.
- **The vetitive argument (Obad 11–14).** He reads the eight *ʾal* + imperfect forms as genuine future-oriented **vetitives**, not past indictments, resolving the tension through Obadiah's canonical placement among the eighth-century prophets (a *vaticinium ex eventu*).

His governing category for the postexilic prophets is **scribal prophecy** — books produced by temple-associated scribes who combined and adapted prior scripture rather than transcribing oral oracles. He is careful about **voice**: his redaction-critical and dating theses are argued as positions against named interlocutors (Duhm, Jeremias, Barton, Wolff, Bergler, Wöhrle, Sweeney, Ben Zvi), and he repeatedly cautions against over-literal theological application (the "angry God" of Jonah 1; equating ancient Edom with a modern people).
