# Origen of Alexandria (*Origenes Adamantius*)

**Origen** (c. 184/185–253/254 CE) is the most prolific and intellectually ambitious biblical commentator of the patristic era — the founder of systematic Christian biblical scholarship. A student of [[clement-of-alexandria|Clement of Alexandria]] and subsequently head of the Alexandrian catechetical school, he produced thousands of works (homilies, commentaries, scholia, and apologetics) of which only a fraction survives, mostly in Latin translations by Rufinus and Jerome. His system is a bold synthesis of Platonist cosmology with Christian scripture, generating both the most sophisticated allegorical hermeneutic of antiquity and the most contentious doctrinal speculations in the history of Christian orthodoxy.

## Biographical Overview

Born to a Christian family in Alexandria c. 184/185 CE; his father Leonidas was martyred under Septimius Severus (202 CE), leaving Origen to support the family. He became head of the Alexandrian catechetical school around 202 CE and began his prodigious exegetical output. Around 215–216 CE he visited Rome; c. 230 CE he was ordained a presbyter in Caesarea Maritima without the permission of his bishop Demetrius of Alexandria, leading to his expulsion from Alexandria. He settled in Caesarea, where his school produced Eusebius among others. Tortured under the Decian persecution (250–251 CE), he died at Tyre c. 253/254 CE of injuries sustained.

He reportedly castrated himself, taking Matthew 19:12 literally — a biographical claim repeated by Eusebius (*HE* VI.8.2) and contested by modern scholars. The claim became emblematic of both his radical commitment to the text and the extremes of Alexandrian allegory.

## Major Works

- **[[de-principiis|De Principiis (*Peri Archon*, On First Principles)]]** — 4 books; the first systematic Christian theology; doctrine of God, Trinity, rational souls, cosmology, Scripture; survives mostly in Rufinus's free Latin translation (Schaff/ANF text)
- **[[contra-celsus|Contra Celsus (Against Celsus)]]** — 8 books (c. 248 CE); the most substantial early Christian apologetic, responding point-by-point to [[celsus|Celsus]]'s *True Doctrine* (c. 178 CE) — and the **sole vehicle through which Celsus survives**, since Origen "cited Celsus's book at length, sometimes sentence by sentence, before offering his own views" (Wilken). Reason and faith, divinity of Christ, miracles, ethics. Note Origen's still-**subordinationist** reply on worship: "we do not hold that the son is mightier than the Father, but inferior" (*c. Cels.* 8.15). See [[wilken-christians-as-romans-saw-them|Wilken]].
- **[[commentary-on-john-origen|Commentary on the Gospel of John]]** — 32 books (Books I, II, IV–VI, X, XIII, XIX, XX, XXVIII, XXXII survive); allegorical reading of John's Prologue and Passion; the most sophisticated patristic Johannine commentary
- **[[commentary-on-matthew-origen|Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew]]** — originally 25 books (Books X–XIV survive in Greek; Series Commentariorum in Latin); ethical and typological exegesis; marriage, celibacy, law
- **Homilies** — on Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Joshua, Judges, Kings, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Song of Songs, Luke; mostly in Rufinus's/Jerome's translations
- **Commentary on Romans** — 15 books; survives in Rufinus's abbreviated Latin; justification, law, election
- **Commentary on Song of Songs** — the most influential patristic allegorical reading (bride=soul/church; bridegroom=Logos)
- **Hexapla** — synoptic edition of the OT in 6 columns (Hebrew, LXX, Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, Origen's recension with critical signs); lost except fragments
- **[[letters-of-origen|Letter to Africanus (and Africanus to Origen)]]** — exchange on the Greek additions to Daniel (Susanna, Bel and the Dragon); the Hexapla methodology; LXX authority vs. *hebraica veritas*
- **[[letters-of-origen|Letter to Gregory (Thaumaturgus)]]** — on Christian use of Greek philosophy (*spoils of Egypt*)

## Hermeneutical Method

Origen's interpretive system is the most systematically articulated allegorical hermeneutic in Christian antiquity, directly comparable in structure to Philo's Jewish allegory (which he read) and anticipating the later [[fourfold-sense|Quadriga]].

### The Three Senses of Scripture (Origen's Trichology)
In *De Principiis* IV, Origen elaborates a **three-level reading** corresponding to the human constitution (body–soul–spirit):

1. **Somatic / Literal (Historical) sense** — the plain narrative; useful for simpler believers (*somatikoi*); often presents impossibilities or offensive statements when read alone (the earth resting on waters; God walking in the garden; the sun standing still)
2. **Psychic / Moral sense** — the soul's applicability; behavior, virtue, progress; the pastoral reading for growing Christians
3. **Pneumatic / Spiritual (Allegorical) sense** — the highest, typological/mystical/eschatological reading; reserved for the *pneumatikoi*; the "true" meaning to which the letter points

Origen's key hermeneutical claim: Scripture contains intentional "stumbling blocks" (*skandala*) — passages that cannot be read literally without absurdity or impiety — placed there by the Spirit to compel the reader to ascend to the spiritual sense. This is not a deficiency of Scripture but a pedagogical strategy of the Logos.

### Allegory and Typology
Origen distinguishes **typology** (historical events in the OT prefigure NT events) from **allegory** proper (the literal narrative encodes a timeless spiritual truth with no reference to history). He uses both, and the distinction is not always maintained cleanly. His Logos Christology organizes all allegorical reading: every OT text ultimately refers to Christ (the "spiritual law," the "eternal gospel"), every soul's journey mirrors Christ's.

### Cross-Tradition Comparison
Origen's allegorical method is structurally parallel to:
- [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]]'s allegorical reading of the Torah (Stoic concepts; soul as imago Dei)
- [[pseudo-dionysius|Pseudo-Dionysius]]'s symbolic theology (sensible signs mediating transcendent reality)
- [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]]'s *ta'wil* (inner interpretation of Quranic images)

## Major Theological Positions

### Trinitarian Theology
Origen's Trinity is **subordinationist**: the Father is the ultimate *Monad* (ungenerated, absolutely simple); the Son/Logos is eternally generated (*agenetos* vs. *genetos*) and subordinate; the Spirit subordinate to both. The Son is the "second God" (*deuteros theos*). The eternal generation of the Son is Origen's key contribution to Trinitarian theology, later refined by Nicaea's *homoousion*.

### Pre-existence of Souls (*Peri Archon* I–II)
All rational creatures (*logika*) were created equal and spiritual. They **fell** from their original contemplation of God by varying degrees of freedom — the degree of fall determines the kind of body (angel, human, demon). This is Origen's distinctive account of the origin of souls and the diversity of existence. **Condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553 CE).**

### Universal Restoration (*Apokatastasis*)
At the end of the ages, all rational creatures — including the Devil — will be restored to original unity with God. This is Origen's most controversial doctrine, derived from 1 Corinthians 15:28 ("God will be all in all") and his reading of divine love as ultimately irresistible. **Condemned as heresy.** See [[apokatastasis|apokatastasis]].

### Spiritual Cosmology
The physical cosmos is a school (*paideuterion*) for fallen souls; embodiment is remedial, not punishment for sin (unlike Gnosticism). Multiple world-ages (eons) precede and follow the present one; the plurality of worlds is compatible with divine pedagogy. This proto-evolutionary cosmological vision draws on Stoic *ekpyrosis* and Middle Platonist cosmology.

## Reception and Controversy

### Orthodox Reception
- Deeply influential on the **Cappadocian Fathers** (Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa): they published a *Philocalia* of selected Origen passages (c. 360 CE)
- [[jerome|Jerome]] initially admired Origen as the greatest exegete; later vigorously attacked him
- [[john-chrysostom|John Chrysostom]] drew on Origen's Commentary on Matthew
- In the West, [[ambrose|Ambrose]] and indirectly [[augustine|Augustine]] absorbed Origenian insights via mediation

### Condemnation
- **Origenist controversy (c. 393–415 CE)**: Jerome vs. Rufinus; bishops divided; the *anathema* of Origen circulated
- **First Origenist crisis**: Theophilus of Alexandria condemned Origenism at local synods (400 CE); Jerome aligned with Theophilus
- **Second Origenist crisis (543 CE)**: Emperor Justinian condemned 9 Origenist theses; the **Fifth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople II, 553 CE)** formally condemned Origen and 15 anathemas, including pre-existence of souls and *apokatastasis*
- Origen's works survive because even opponents could not stop reading him; Rufinus's Latin translations bowdlerize the most controversial passages

### Modern Assessment
Historical-critical scholarship regards Origen as the founder of Christian biblical hermeneutics and apologetics, and his condemnation as largely politically motivated. The *De Principiis* is treated as a speculative *protreptikon* ("first principles as a sketch") rather than a dogmatic pronouncement. His allegorical method remains the primary alternative to historicist-critical reading in patristic exegesis.

## Relation to Neoplatonism

Origen and Plotinus were reportedly both students of [[ammonius-saccas|Ammonius Saccas]] in Alexandria (Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* 14) — though "Origen the Christian" and "Origen the Platonist" may be two different people. The Christian Origen's system is unmistakably shaped by Middle Platonism: the hierarchical Trinity mirrors the Platonic One–Intellect–Soul (*Nous*–*Psyche*) triad; the fall and return of souls mirrors Platonic *periagoge*; the non-literal reading of Homeric myths (Porphyry on allegorical reading) is adapted for Scripture. His relationship to Neoplatonism proper (post-Plotinus) is one of parallel development rather than direct dependence.

## Christology in Ehrman's account

[[bart-ehrman|Ehrman]] ([[ehrman-how-jesus-became-god|*How Jesus Became God*]]) calls Origen "the
greatest Christian theologian before the debates of the fourth century" and his *On First
Principles* (c. 229) the first systematic theology. Christ is God's ever-existing
**[[logos-christology|Wisdom and Word]]**. Origen's distinctive (later-condemned) move is the
**pre-existence of souls**: of the many souls created to contemplate the Son, one never fell but
clung to the Word with "absolute devotion" and became divine "like iron placed in fire" — and
that soul became the man Jesus. Ehrman notes Origen was orthodox in his day but later faulted for
ideas that "led to … the Arian controversy." See [[arian-controversy]], [[trinity]].
