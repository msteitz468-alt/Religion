# Walpola Rahula

**Walpola Rahula** (1907–1997) was a Sri Lankan Theravada monk and scholar, among the
first Buddhist *bhikkhus* to hold a university professorship and the author of *What the
Buddha Taught* (1959) — for half a century the standard concise English introduction to
the core teaching, and a primary lens for the wiki's [[theravada|Theravada]] doctrine.

## Biographical Context

Ordained young in the Sri Lankan monastic tradition, Rahula trained at the Vidyalankara
Pirivena and took advanced degrees (including a doctorate at the University of Ceylon and
study at the Sorbonne under the great sinologist **Paul Demiéville**, who wrote his
foreword). He held academic posts in Ceylon, at Northwestern University in the United
States, and elsewhere — embodying the modern **scholar-monk** who addresses both the
*saṅgha* and the secular university. He was also a noted advocate of the socially and
politically engaged bhikkhu (*The Heritage of the Bhikkhu*).

## Hermeneutical Method

Rahula reads the [[tipitaka|Pali suttas]] in a rigorously **[[theravada|Theravada]]**, text-
centered mode, but with two characteristic emphases:

1. **Doctrinal minimalism and rationalism.** He foregrounds the Buddha's invitation to
   *come and see* (*ehipassika*) and the *Kālāma Sutta*'s appeal to investigation over
   authority, presenting the Dhamma as a teaching to be tested, not believed.
2. **Theravada–Mahayana convergence.** Unusually for a Theravada monk, Rahula argued that
   on fundamentals the two great vehicles agree, and he engaged respectfully with
   [[mahayana|Mahayana]] (he wrote on [[abhidharma|Abhidharma]] and on the
   [[bodhisattva|bodhisattva]] ideal as common ground). This makes him a useful
   cross-sectarian voice in the wiki.

His exposition is built around the **[[four-noble-truths|Four Noble Truths]]** as the
organizing frame, with a celebrated chapter on **[[anatta|anatta]]** (the "doctrine of
no-soul") that has shaped anglophone understanding of non-self.

## Influence

*What the Buddha Taught* became the most widely assigned single introduction to Buddhism in
English, valued for compressing the doctrine — [[dukkha|dukkha]], the
[[five-aggregates|five aggregates]], [[anatta|non-self]], [[dependent-origination|dependent
origination]], [[nirvana|nibbāna]], and *bhāvanā* (meditation) — into a short, accurate,
accessible compass, with an appendix of his own translations of key Pali texts.
