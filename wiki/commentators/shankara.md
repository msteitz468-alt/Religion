# Shankara

**Adi Shankaracharya** (8th c.) is the most influential philosopher-commentator in Hindu history and
the systematizer of [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedanta]] (non-dualism). In a short life (traditionally
32 years) he wrote the definitive commentaries on the entire **prasthana-trayi** and founded a network
of monasteries (*mathas*) that still anchor the tradition.

## Major Works

- The **Brahma-Sutra-Bhashya** (*Śārīraka-mīmāṃsā-bhāṣya*) — the foundational commentary establishing
  Advaita; ingested via [[thibaut-vedanta-sutras|Thibaut's SBE translation]]. Prefaced by the
  **[[adhyasa|Adhyāsa-bhāṣya]]** — a freestanding philosophical essay on superimposition that is the
  system's diagnostic core (see below). SBE 34 covers Adhyāyas I–II Pādas I–II; SBE 38 covers
  Adhyāyas III–IV.
- The **Gītā-bhāṣya** on the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]] — ingested via
  [[gambhirananda-bhagavad-gita|Gambhirananda's translation]]. Śaṅkara begins his comment only at
  **2.10**, reads the Self as **actionless**, and argues that **knowledge alone liberates** — refuting
  the combination of knowledge and action ([[jnana-karma-samuccaya|jñāna-karma-samuccaya]]) and treating
  the Gita's devotional/active teaching as provisional. Action serves only to **purify the mind**
  ([[karma-yoga|karma-yoga]] as preparation, not consummation).
- Commentaries on the principal [[upanishads|Upanishads]].
- Independent works (the *Upadeshasahasri*; popular hymns and the *Vivekachudamani* attributed to him).

## The Adhyāsa-Bhāṣya — The Diagnostic Preamble

Śaṅkara does not begin with sūtra I.1.1 but with a freestanding essay on **[[adhyasa|adhyāsa]]**
(superimposition). Its argument:

- **Subject** (the Self; sphere of the "I") and **Object** (the not-Self; sphere of the "Thou") are as
  opposed as darkness and light — they cannot be identified, nor can their attributes be attributed to
  each other.
- Yet this very confusion is the **natural, beginningless human condition**: we superimpose bodily
  attributes ("I am stout, lean"), sense-organ attributes ("I am deaf"), mental attributes ("I doubt,
  I determine") onto the Self; and we superimpose the Self's consciousness and reality onto the
  not-Self.
- This mutual superimposition **= avidyā** (Nescience). It is the presupposition on which ALL practical
  activity rests — including the use of Vedic injunctions and prohibitions.
- "All the practical distinctions — those made in ordinary life as well as those laid down by the Veda
  — between means of knowledge, objects of knowledge, and knowing persons… are based on the mutual
  superimposition of the Self and the Non-Self."
- The means of right knowledge (perception, inference, scripture) can only operate because there is a
  "knowing personality" — but this knowing personality *itself* presupposes the superimposition: "nor
  does anybody act by means of a body on which the nature of the Self is not superimposed."
- **Liberation** = ascertainment of the true nature of the Self "by means of the discrimination of
  that which is superimposed" = ***vidyā*** (knowledge). The Vedānta-texts are studied *for the purpose
  of this liberation*.

The Self's non-objecthood: the Self cannot be a normal object of superimposition because it is
self-luminous (*svayamprakāśa*), never brought into sensory contact. Śaṅkara's reply: the Self is not
an absolute non-object — it is the object of the "I"-notion (*asmat-pratyaya*), known through immediate
intuition (*aparokṣa*); and non-sensory superimposition is possible (e.g., "men superimpose dark-blue
colour on the ether, which is not the object of sensuous perception").

## The Commentary on the Opening Sūtras

**I.1.1** ("Then therefore the enquiry into Brahman"): The four prerequisites for Brahma-enquiry —
*viveka* (discrimination of eternal/non-eternal), *vairāgya* (renunciation of fruits), the
six-fold auxiliaries (*ṣaṭ-sampat*: tranquility, self-restraint, etc.), and *mumukṣutva* (desire for
release). The enquiry into Brahman does NOT depend on prior Pūrva Mīmāṃsā.

**I.1.2** ("From which the origin, subsistence, and dissolution of this world"): Brahman defined by
causal omnipotence: that omniscient, omnipotent cause from which proceeds the origin, subsistence, and
dissolution of "this world — differentiated by names and forms, containing many agents and enjoyers,
the abode of the fruits of actions, whose arrangement cannot even be conceived by the mind."

**I.1.4**: Extended rebuttal of Mīmāṃsā. Key results:
- Mokṣa is eternal — *kūṭastha-nitya* (unchanging eternity), not an effected fruit of action
- Knowledge ≠ action: action can be done, not-done, or modified; knowledge depends on the object
  only and cannot be enjoined or modified
- The *mahāvākyas* (especially *tat tvam asi* and *aham brahmāsmi*) express **real identity**, not
  figurative combination or devotional meditation
- "He who knows Brahman *becomes* Brahman" — immediate; no intervening action

## Characteristic Positions

[[atman|Atman]] **is** [[brahman|Brahman]]; the world of plurality is **[[maya|maya]]**, superimposed on
Brahman by ignorance (*avidya*); Brahman is **nirguna** (without attributes); [[moksha|liberation]] is
**jnana** (knowledge that dispels ignorance), attainable in this life (*jivanmukti*). He deploys the
**two levels of truth** (ultimate / conventional) to honor dualistic and theistic scripture as
provisionally true.

## Hermeneutical Method

Shankara reads the Upanishadic **mahavakyas** (*tat tvam asi*) as literal identity statements and
subordinates difference-texts to them. His rationalist, text-critical *bhashya* style set the model for
all later Vedanta — including his opponents.

## Controversies and Influence

Charged by [[ramanuja|Ramanuja]] and others with being a **crypto-Buddhist** (*pracchanna-bauddha*) —
his maya and two-truths resembling [[sunyata|emptiness]] and the Buddhist
[[neyartha-nitartha|neyartha/nitartha]] move. The chief antagonist of both later Vedanta schools (see
[[brahman-atman-vedanta-schools|the triad]]); the dominant voice of classical and modern Hindu
philosophy.
