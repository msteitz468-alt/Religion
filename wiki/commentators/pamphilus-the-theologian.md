# Pamphilus the Theologian

Pamphilus the Theologian (fl. later 6th c.), author of a single surviving work — the ***Solution to different topics or queries*** (*Solutio*) — is for Johannes Zachhuber ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]], §8.1) "the first of a 'Leontian School'" and the author of "a momentous and rather original attempt to transform Patristic philosophy." Against Marcel Richard's portrayal of a "dim-witted scholastic," Zachhuber finds "considerable sophistication."

## The Substance–Accident Model

Pamphilus's decisive novelty is to make the **substance–accident binary foundational**, where the history of Patristic philosophy had rested on the ousia/hypostasis binary. He defines ousia by its ontological independence: "ousia is whatever subsists on its own (τὸ κατ' ἰδίαν ὕπαρξιν ὑφεστώς) and does not have its existence in another. This is said in distinction from all accidents." Zachhuber traces this to the single passage in [[leontius-of-byzantium|Leontius of Byzantium]] (*CNE* 1) that introduced the [[enhypostaton|*enhypostaton*]] — which Pamphilus "identified as the systematic core of this 'father's' thought," building a "philosophy of the *enhypostaton*": ousia as self-subsisting, accidents inhering in it, the *enhypostaton* as "placeholder of ousia at the level of the individual."

## Hypostasis Diminished

Pamphilus "never refers to hypostasis as that which subsists," transferring "the ontological responsibility of independent existence from hypostasis to ousia": "ousia signifies the existence of each thing, while hypostasis [signifies] the multitude of characteristic properties." Hypostasis is therefore "no longer a basic ontological concept; instead, it is explained as the... compound of the two foundational building blocks of his ontology: substance and accident." Christ's hypostasis is "a composite, substantial thing... or an individual in two natures." In reducing the role of hypostasis to answer the [[tritheist-controversy|tritheist]] threat, Pamphilus resembles [[damian-of-alexandria|Damian of Alexandria]].

## Individual Nature as a Third Option

Against tritheism, Pamphilus rejects "particular substances or individuals" in the Trinity (a particular ousia = a hypostasis) and offers a three-way distinction for Christ's humanity: universal nature (→ the whole Trinity united to humanity), particular nature (→ two hypostases), and a third — the **individual nature** "as seen in the species, *enhypostaton* but not with a hypostasis of its own (ἰδιοϋπόστατον)." This differs from Leontius: Leontius's individual nature includes individuating properties, whereas Pamphilus's individual nature carries **generic/universal properties only**; particular properties belong to the hypostasis. Zachhuber notes Pamphilus "may have been the first Chalcedonian to articulate this response," later used by [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] (EF 55).

## Not a Particularist

Krausmüller argues Pamphilus's "individual substance" is effectively identical to [[john-philoponus|Philoponus]]'s "particular substance," making tritheism inevitable. Zachhuber counters that "two things that look the same are not always in fact the same": miaphysites introduced particular natures to preserve the continuity of nature and hypostasis, while Chalcedonians introduced individual natures "as an abstraction of what was common within the concrete individual." Pamphilus retains a realist universal — species as "unity and identity in the many" (*ταὐτότης*, numerical sameness), Aristotle's priority of individuals read as "only epistemic, not ontological."
