# Pelagius

The British ascetic and moral teacher (habitually called *monachus*) whose insistence on unconditional free will and human moral capacity provoked [[augustine|Augustine]]'s doctrine of grace and original sin, giving rise to the [[pelagian-controversy|Pelagian controversy]]. A fashionable spiritual director at Rome until Alaric's invasion (409) drove him to Africa and the East. Drawn from [[kelly-early-christian-doctrines|J.N.D. Kelly]], ch. XIII.

## Doctrine

- **Free will absolute.** God gave man "the unique privilege of being able to accomplish the divine will by his own choice." Kelly distinguishes Pelagius's three factors: *posse* (the power to act — God's gift), and *velle* and *esse* (willing and realisation — ours).
- **No [[original-sin|original sin]].** Since each soul is created immediately by God ([[traducianism|creationism]]), it "cannot come into the world soiled by original sin." Adam's trespass introduced death and "a habit of disobedience" propagated "not by physical descent, but by custom and example." Infant baptism is therefore benedictory, not for the remission of inherited guilt.
- **[[grace|Grace]] as external aid.** Grace is, in the main, *ab extra* — "a grace of knowledge": free will itself, the revelation of God's law through reason, and the law of Moses and example of Christ. There is no special interior action of God on the soul, and predestination follows God's foreknowledge of the lives men will lead.
- **Impeccantia.** "A man can, if he will, observe God's commandments without sinning" — not a fixed state but the fruit of strenuous effort. Augustine's prayer *"da quod iubes et iube quod vis"* ("give what Thou commandest, and command what Thou wilt") particularly distressed Pelagius as making men "puppets" of grace.

Kelly's verdict: Pelagius was "primarily a moralist, concerned for right conduct." His more aggressive disciples — **Caelestius** (the practical leader, condemned at Carthage 412) and **Julian of Eclanum** ("the ablest thinker in the group") — pressed the harder consequences.
