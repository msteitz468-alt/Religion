# Theodoret of Cyrus

Theodoret (c. 393–c. 458/466), bishop of Cyrus (Cyrrhus) in Syria, was the most learned representative of the **Antiochene** school and, in Johannes Zachhuber's account ([[zachhuber-rise-of-christian-theology|*The Rise of Christian Theology*]]), arguably the chief intellectual antagonist of [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril of Alexandria]] in the fifth-century Christological controversy. Alongside Cyril, he is Zachhuber's prime witness that [[ousia-hypostasis-distinction|Cappadocian philosophy]] had become the shared **"classical theory"** across otherwise opposed parties: Antiochene and Alexandrian combatants agreed on the terminological foundations laid by Basil and the Gregories even as they disagreed about everything else.

## Biographical and Textual Context

A prolific author — apologetics, biblical commentaries, a *Church History*, and letters — many of whose works were lost or transmitted under other names (ironically including Cyril's) after his eventual condemnation by the Second Council of Constantinople (553, the "Three Chapters"). His Trinitarian thought is reconstructed from the *Eranistes* (a dialogue of 447/448 against miaphysite Christology) and three works of disputed transmission: an *Exposition of the True Faith* (ascribed to Justin Martyr) and the pseudo-Cyrilline *On the Trinity* and *On the Incarnation* — all attributed to Theodoret by Severus of Antioch and accepted as genuinely his.

## The Classical Theory in Theodoret

- **Christian philosophy, distinct from pagan.** The *Eranistes* opens by demanding that the terms *ousia*, *hypostasis*, *prosopon*, *idiotes* be defined. Theodoret is unapologetic about the *philosophical* character of doctrinal definition while insisting it is an autonomous **Christian** philosophy ("true gnosis"), distinct from the pagans — for whom, he claims, *ousia* and *hypostasis* have no discernible difference, whereas "in the teaching of the Fathers" they do: "the same difference the universal has to the particular... *ousia* has to *hypostasis*."
- **Names, natures, individuals (the concrete account).** Echoing the *Epistle 38*: "man" is the common name of the nature, signifying "the Roman, the Athenian, the Persian... all who partake of this nature," while "Paul" or "Peter" signifies the individual. Hearing "human being," the mind turns to "the entirety of the human race." He even adds a biblical proof (Gen 6:7, God blotting out "countless multitudes") not found in the Cappadocians, and extends the analogy across the animal world (from one lamb, the genus of lambs).
- **Properties and predicates (the abstract account).** Common predicates ("God," "Lord," "creator") apply to all three persons; individual properties ("unbegotten" for the Father, "only-begotten" for the Son) to one — the distinction between the "indication of *ousia*" (identity by the formula of being) and the "modes of existence" (*tropoi hyparxeōs*).

Unlike Cyril, Theodoret rarely puts logical technicalities (the *Categories* argument) on display, and unlike Gregory of Nyssa he shows little speculative interest in the ideas for their own sake — which is exactly why Zachhuber finds him such clean evidence that the classical theory had become a taken-for-granted *koine*.

## Christology and the Two-Hypostases Problem

In his critique of Cyril's third anathema, Theodoret perceives the conceptual bind clearly: "he who assumes a union, presupposes separation." Holding firmly to **two natures** in the Incarnate and accepting the Cappadocian correlation of nature and hypostasis, he is — however reluctantly — willing to speak of **"two united hypostases, that is natures"** in Christ, while confessing one *prosōpon*, one Son, one Christ. This Antiochene willingness to follow the logic to two hypostases is the position later radicalized by the [[chalcedonian-definition|"Nestorian"]] Church of the East.

## Reception

Rehabilitated at Chalcedon (451) after condemning Cyril, but his anti-Cyrilline writings were anathematized in the Three Chapters at Constantinople II (553) as part of Justinian's attempt to reconcile the miaphysites — which is why so much of his corpus survives only pseudonymously.
