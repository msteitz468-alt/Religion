Barry G. Webb is a British-born, Australian-based OT scholar at Moore Theological College, Sydney — a conservative Anglican evangelical institution. His *Judges* commentary in the NICOT series (Eerdmans, 2012) represents the culmination of three decades of work that began with his Sheffield PhD (published as a monograph in 1987 and revised in 2008). It is the most explicitly literary of the NICOT volumes ingested into this wiki, and the most methodologically self-conscious.

## Hermeneutical Method

Webb's stated goal is to read Judges "as both literature and Scripture." He works at the intersection of two methodological streams:

**1. New Literary Criticism (Robert Alter tradition)**: Close attention to narrative technique — chiasm, ring composition, prolepsis, irony, parallelism, characterization, point of view, plot structure, and pacing. Webb treats Judges as a literarily crafted whole, not a compilation of sources to be peeled apart. His doctoral work was "the first attempt to apply the new literary approach to the book as a whole."

**2. Canonical Criticism (Brevard Childs tradition)**: The final form of the canonical text is the primary datum. Judges is read in its canonical setting — as the second panel of the Former Prophets, in dialogue with Joshua before it and 1 Samuel after; within the larger context of Old and New Testaments. Webb does not do typology arbitrarily but insists on textually grounded canonical connections (e.g., Samson's birth narrative ↔ Isaac, Samuel, John the Baptist; Judges 5:31 ↔ the "sun going forth" → Samson's name).

The combination makes Webb the most sophisticated literary exegete among the ingested commentators. He engages extensively with the scholarly literature — feminist (Bal, Exum, Trible, Ackerman), rhetorical-critical (O'Connell), social-scientific (Yee), liberation-theological (Hamlin), redaction-critical (Noth, Richter, Smend, Veijola, Boling, Auld) — synthesizing rather than dismissing.

### Relationship to Historical Criticism

Webb accepts a **modified Deuteronomistic History** position, distinguishing him from Woudstra (who rejects DH) and placing him closer to Bruckner:

- He acknowledges multiple redactional layers in Judges (DtrG, DtrP, DtrN; or Boling's Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic distinction)
- He argues against Noth that the epilogue (chs. 17-21) is integral to the book, not a secondary addition
- His primary concern is the canonical end-text, and redaction criticism serves as a *control* on literary reading rather than the primary goal
- He favors an early-date exodus (c. 1446 BCE) and defends the basic historicity of the judges period against minimalist scholarship, citing Provan/Long/Longman

### Feminist Scholarship

Webb is the first ingested commentator to engage feminist scholarship as a genuine dialogue partner. He cites Mieke Bal, J. Cheryl Exum, Phyllis Trible, Susan Niditch, and Susan Ackerman substantively. His position: Judges itself is neither a handbook of patriarchy nor a feminist text; it is "a book not of either/or but of both/and" (Ackerman). The abuse of women in the epilogue is explicitly condemned by the book's own theological logic — it is the fruit of idolatry and covenant unfaithfulness. This puts him in moderate tension with Woudstra, who addresses gender-related issues only in the context of defending patriarchal structures.

### Violence and Holy War

Webb addresses the violence of Judges more explicitly than any other ingested commentator. His framework:

1. The book itself distinguishes between **divinely authorized liberation wars** (condemned only by absolute pacifists) and **private vendetta / civil war** (condemned by the text itself — Gideon in ch. 8; the civil war of chs. 19-21)
2. The conquest wars of ch. 1 are the most theologically difficult; Webb makes a canonical argument: accepting Judges as canonical rules out strategies of avoidance (Marcionism, progressive-revelation dismissal, historical minimization) and commits the church to listening to what the text says about divine judgment, sin, and culture
3. The church now fights spiritual warfare (Eph 6:10-18; 2 Cor 10:4-5); the sword has been transferred to the state (Rom 13); the cross has focused divine wrath — but the theological content of the conquest material (culture is morally non-neutral; evil requires radical judgment; not all religion is good) remains authoritative

This is more theologically engaged than Woudstra's apologetic defense and more careful than Bruckner's liberation-hermeneutics engagement.

## Characteristic Positions

**On the downward spiral**: The "sin cycle" description of Judges flattens the book's design. It is a downward spiral: "after the forty years that follow Gideon's victory the land is never again said to enjoy rest." The book's structure enacts its theology.

**On Gideon**: Starts as Moses (diffident, obedient, radically dependent on YHWH), ends as Aaron (making an ephod/idol from the people's gold). The name Jerubbaal ("let Baal contend") turns ironic — Baal does take up his own cause in Gideon's own lifetime. "The man who started by being a Moses ends here by being an Aaron."

**On Jephthah's vow**: The vow is an instance of Jephthah's characteristic move: turning religion into politics. "He has tried to conduct his relationship with God in the same way he has conducted his relationships with men." The theme of the Jephthah narrative is "the tendency of people to turn religion into negotiation with God instead of submission to him." Webb does not adjudicate whether the daughter was killed or consecrated to lifelong virginity.

**On Samson as Israel**: The controlling interpretive key: "His personal story is also the story of Israel as a whole in the judges period. As Israel had been set apart from other nations by God's covenant with her, so Samson is set apart from other men by his calling as a Nazirite. As Israel went after foreign gods, Samson goes after foreign women."

**On Samson's birth narrative and Christology**: The structural parallel between Samson's birth announcement (barren woman, divine messenger, Nazirite destiny) and Luke 1 (John the Baptist's birth) is not accidental. Luke 1:15 alludes to Judg 13:4-5; Luke 1:42 (the blessing on Mary) parallels Judg 5:24 (Jael). The NT places the judges within the single arc of salvation history that climaxes in Christ.

**On kingship**: Neither simply pro- nor anti-monarchical. The epilogue's refrain ("no king in Israel") registers both the need for kingship (given the moral chaos) and its limits. Judah's prominence at both opening and close anticipates David; the Davidic dynasty is the legitimate culmination of Israel's need for human leadership, but even David will fail. Only YHWH is the true and permanent Judge.

**On Jotham's fable (9:7-21)**: One of the most penetrating political allegories in the OT. The only trees willing to become king are the useless ones — the bramble. The worthy trees (olive, fig, vine) decline, knowing that leadership requires giving up what makes you valuable. Abimelech is the bramble — dangerous precisely because he is worthless.

Webb is the most literarily sophisticated of the three ingested Former Prophets commentators, and the one most willing to engage with non-evangelical scholarship as a genuine dialogue partner rather than an opponent to refute.
