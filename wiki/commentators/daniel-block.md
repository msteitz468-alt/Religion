Daniel I. Block is a Canadian evangelical OT scholar, Professor Emeritus of Old Testament Interpretation at Wheaton College Graduate School. Born in 1943, he received his doctoral training in the UK and has also taught at Southern Baptist Theological Seminary. His two-volume NICOT commentary on Ezekiel (1997 and 1998) is the most extensive commentary in the series by page count and represents the culmination of his career as an Ezekiel specialist. He has also published widely on Deuteronomy, Judges, Ruth, and ancient Near Eastern national identity.

Block represents a careful evangelical exegete who takes seriously both the Hebrew text and the ANE background, defends Ezekielic authorship of the book, and reads the restoration oracles as predicting literal events while acknowledging that many descriptions are ideological and symbolic rather than straightforwardly literal.

## Background

Block's nationality (Canadian) and Baptist tradition give him a distinct positioning in the NICOT series, which has been dominated by British Anglicans (Goldingay), American Wesleyans (Oswalt, Hartley), and Reformed scholars (Waltke, Craigie, Webb). His doctoral work on ancient Northwest Semitic perceptions of national identity fed directly into his reading of Ezekiel's theology of Israel, land, and divine patronage.

## Hermeneutical Method

### 1. Single Authorship and Ezekielic Recording

Block defends substantial involvement by Ezekiel himself in recording his oracles. He constructs a seven-stage model of prophetic book production (prophetic event → rhetorical event → transcriptional event → narratorial event → compilation event → editorial event → nominal event) and argues that Ezekiel is likely responsible for at least the transcriptional, narratorial, and compilation stages. Key evidence: the autobiographical first-person style throughout; the care in dating oracles; the scroll-eating vision (2:9-3:3) as anticipation of a written record; explicit divine commands to write (24:1-2; 37:16); the difficulty of imagining anyone else caring enough about a rejected prophet to record his messages for future verification.

### 2. The "Four Pillars" Framework

Block's most distinctive structural contribution. He identifies four theological "pillars" on which the exiles had based their false sense of security and shows how Ezekiel's pre-587 preaching (chs. 4-24) systematically demolishes each pillar, while his post-586 restoration oracles (chs. 34-48) rebuild each on a proper covenantal foundation. This framework provides both an interpretive grid for the whole book and a theological argument: Israel's claims to divine protection were not based on the eternal promises per se (which are indeed eternal), but on an illegitimate, unconditional appropriation of those promises in the absence of covenant faithfulness.

### 3. The *Kābôd* as Theological Spine

Block makes the departure and return of YHWH's *kābôd* the interpretive center of the book. Reading chs. 1, 8-11, and 40-48 as a coherent theological narrative about divine presence — departure because of defilement, return in the eschatological restoration — gives structural and theological coherence to what might otherwise seem a disparate collection of visions.

### 4. "That They May Know That I Am YHWH"

Block insists on this formula (over 60 occurrences) as the book's governing epistemological and theological purpose. All divine action — judgment and restoration alike — aims at the recognition of Yahweh's person and character through his performance. This prevents a reductively punitive reading of the judgment oracles: their purpose is revelation, not merely retribution.

### 5. Holistic Reading (Resumptive Exposition)

Block documents a pattern of "resumptive exposition" throughout the book: themes are briefly introduced in early chapters, dropped, and then picked up later for full development. This pattern suggests either a conscious editorial principle or a deliberate rhetorical device used by Ezekiel in organizing his own material. Block uses this to argue for the book's substantial unity and against the scissors-and-paste redaction-critical approaches that have dominated Ezekiel scholarship.

### 6. Comparative ANE Philology, Radically Transformed

(From the Vol. 1 re-ingest, 2026-07-18.) Block's programmatic statement: Ezekiel's experience
"must be interpreted within their ancient Near Eastern cultural milieu" — Akkadian cognates
and rituals (the *pīt pî* mouth-opening ritual behind "I will open your mouth"; *Ludlul bēl
nēmeqi* confinement motifs; the tablet of destinies behind the scroll vision; Mari, the Erra
Epic, adoption formulae, treaty terminology), iconography, and Aramaic/Elephantine parallels
— yet borrowed forms are "radically transformed" and "thoroughly demythologized" in orthodox
Yahwism. Signature example: Yahweh making a "living idol" of Ezekiel, so that the exiles
"acknowledge that Yahweh does not speak through sculptures of wood and stone, but through his
'human idol.'"

### 7. Rhetoric over System: "A Preacher, Not a Systematic Theologian"

Block repeatedly resolves Ezekiel's apparent contradictions rhetorically rather than
doctrinally: the determinism of ch. 20 vs. the individual ethic of ch. 18, the righteous
slain with the wicked in 21:8 (a "deliberately offensive rhetorical device intended to
shock"), and the 20:25–26 "no-good laws" ("The force of Ezekiel's rhetorical strategy lies
precisely in its nonconformity with human reason"). Textual roughness (chs. 1, 7, 21) he
attributes to the prophet's emotional excitement, not scribal corruption or editorial layers.

### 8. The Reluctant Prophet

Against most scholarship, Block reads Ezekiel as fundamentally resistant to his call: the
unmatched length of the call narrative, the scroll ingestion, the sevenfold binding, the
"bitter, in the rage of my spirit" departure, and the seven stunned days ("For a week he
struggles inwardly with Yahweh") until the stern watchman induction — one year of imposed
speechlessness per day of resistance, on his reading of the seven-year dumbness. Ezekiel's
effectiveness therefore "says less about him as a person than about Yahweh, who is able to
take the most unlikely vessel and transform it into an agent of his glory."

### 9. Text-Critical Conservatism and the Contingency of Prophecy (Vol. 2)

(From the Vol. 2 re-ingest, 2026-07-18.) Across chs. 25–48 Block consistently defends MT and
Ezekielian unity against emendation and accretion criticism: he retains "Assyria" in 31:3
against the near-universal cypress emendation, distinguishes "inauthentic" from "secondary"
("The prophet may himself have supplemented an earlier oracle"), rejects Lust's Papyrus 967
priority thesis for 36:23b–38 with seven arguments, and resists the Wellhausen school's
Levite-demotion reading of ch. 44 ("Linguistic and conceptual considerations point to the
chronological priority of P over Ezekiel"). On the Tyre nonfulfillment problem (29:17–21) he
argues prophetic outcomes were **contingent**: "The efficaciousness of the word lay not in the
word itself but in the power of the divine speaker" — against Carroll's *vaticinium ex eventu*
and honest-error readings alike.

### 10. Satire and the "Literary Cartoon" (Vol. 2)

Block's signature genre move in the second volume: prophetic satire with "intensified
reversal" (the sea monster reduced to a fish; the cosmic tree felled to Sheol), and the
"literary cartoon" label applied to the Gog oracle ("a satirical literary cartoon strip
consisting of eight frames"), the Sheol tableau of ch. 32 ("a literary cartoon rather than a
literary photograph"), and the river of 47:1–12 ("an impressionistic literary cartoon with an
intentional ideological aim") — in each case a hermeneutical brake on overliteral reading.
Companion move: the **ideational** interpretation of chs. 40–48 ("The issue for the prophet is
not physical geography but spiritual realities… Where God is, there is Zion") and of the
*nāśîʾ* as "Yahweh's appointed lay patron and sponsor of the cult." The Exodus-pattern mapping
of chs. 33–48 makes Ezekiel a **second Moses** — mediator of a new Torah revealed on a high
mountain, whose glory-entry scene (43:1–7) mirrors Exod 40:34–38.

## Key Theological Positions

**The *qinʾâ* as legitimate passion, not pathology**: Against modern readings that treat YHWH's response to Israel's infidelity as psychologically disordered, Block argues that divine *qinʾâ* is the entirely appropriate response of a covenant partner when an exclusive relationship is threatened. Its intensity is proportionate to the depth of YHWH's covenantal love.

**Individual moral responsibility (ch. 18)**: One of Ezekiel's most distinctive contributions; YHWH is fundamentally on the side of life; repentance is always available; inherited guilt is not the operative principle.

**Restoration as literal but described ideologically**: Against both amillennialism (which spiritualizes all OT restoration promises into NT church realities) and dispensational literalism (which reads every detail of chs. 40-48 as a future physical reality), Block argues that Ezekiel genuinely predicted literal events (restoration to the land, a Davidic king, a period of peace and prosperity) but that many of his *descriptions* employ ideological, symbolic, and fantastic imagery that is not meant to be taken architecturally.

**The final temple vision (*YHWH šammâ*)**: Not a blueprint for a physical building but a "theological constitution" for the new Israel. The primary concern is divine presence, not political structure. The city name ("YHWH is there") is the hermeneutical key to the entire vision.

**Ezekiel as "prophet of the Spirit"**: The *rûaḥ* as the agent of animation (ch. 37), conveyance, inspiration, and ownership. The restoration promise of 36:27 (Spirit placed within to enable obedience) is the Ezekielian counterpart to Jeremiah's new covenant promise.

**Characteristic exegetical calls (Vol. 1)**: the "thirtieth year" = Ezekiel's age (with
Origen); the second lion of ch. 19 = Jehoiakim (against Jehoiachin/Zedekiah); 21:32 =
Nebuchadrezzar, not the Messiah (Gen 49:10 deliberately inverted); the Daniel of 14:14 = the
biblical Daniel, not the Ugaritic Danʾel; 16:60's everlasting covenant = reinstitution of the
old covenant, not a new one; the sour-grapes proverb = fatalism, not theodicy protest;
divine deception in 14:9 = "Yahweh answers insincerity with insincerity"; 24:1–14 = "a
hitherto unrecognized disputation speech." He engages feminist criticism of chs. 16 and 23
head-on in a formal excursus — conceding the offense, defending the rhetoric ("Ezekiel's aim
is to shock his audience, not to titillate"), and warning that rejecting the portrayal means
"we sit in judgment over God — a dangerous inversion indeed."
