# Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen (646–735 CE)

Ssu-ma Ch'eng-chen was the most important Taoist figure of the T'ang dynasty — a
[[shang-ching|Shang-ch'ing]] master, court advisor to multiple emperors, renowned calligrapher
and painter, and author of major texts on [[biospiritual-cultivation|biospiritual cultivation]].
He embodied what Kirkland calls "literati Taoism": the integration of Taoist practice with
aristocratic culture and imperial politics.

## Social Position and Background

Ssu-ma came from a noble bloodline: his father and grandfather held government posts, and the
family belonged to a collateral branch of the clan that had ruled China as the emperors of the
Chin dynasty (266–420). This imperial lineage made him an especially potent figure for the
T'ang rulers' legitimatory program. He was also famed in his own lifetime as "one of history's
greatest calligraphers" and "a painter expressive and brilliant in all the arts."

## Political Role

Ssu-ma fulfilled the ancient Chinese paradigm of the **sagely advisor** (*ti-wang shih*,
"instructor of emperors and kings"): a figure whose spiritual attainment elevates the ruler
who consults him to near-mythical heights.

Key interactions with T'ang emperors:
- **Emperor Jui-tsung** (r. 710–712): summoned Ssu-ma in extraordinary fashion (sending an
  imperial kinsman to personally invite him, comparing the act to legendary sage-kings consulting
  great worthies of antiquity). One text says Jui-tsung "was going to entrust the governance
  of the realm to Ssu-ma with confidence."
- **Emperor Hsüan-tsung** (r. 712–756): conferred sacred texts and ritual formulae upon the
  emperor in a lay ordination ceremony. Ssu-ma also successfully persuaded Hsüan-tsung to revise
  imperial state cult ceremonies to honor the Shang-ch'ing "Perfected Lords" of the sacred
  mountains — a remarkable instance of a Taoist master redirecting the imperial ritual calendar.

A 742 inscription hails Ssu-ma as having "revived the Virtue (*te*) of Kuang-ch'eng" — referring
to a legendary worthy whom the Yellow Emperor (the ideal imperial archetype) had consulted in
highest antiquity.

## Major Works

- ***Tso-wang lun*** ("On Sitting in Forgetfulness"): a guide to the gradual progression toward
  union with Tao through seven stages of self-transformation; widely known among T'ang literati.
- ***Fu-ch'i ching-i lun*** ("On the Essential Meaning of the Absorption of Ch'i-Energy"):
  an organized explication of biospiritual reality — the nature of *ch'i*, *ching*, *shen*,
  and the practical means of cultivating them.
- ***T'ien-yin-tzu*** ("The Master of Heavenly Seclusion"): likely edited or secretly authored
  by Ssu-ma; outlines "five progressive gateways" to spiritual transcendence, beginning with
  balanced diet and culminating in "entering into Suchness." States explicitly: "even in spiritual
  transcendence, one is still human" — confirming that the Taoist goal is a this-life
  transformation, not merely an afterlife hope.
- ***Hsiu-chen mi-chih*** ("Esoteric Instructions for Cultivating Reality"): a manual that was
  widely known among T'ang literati and "remained current in the world" even after the fall
  of T'ang — though it is now lost.

## Hermeneutical Method

Ssu-ma's method is **gradual biospiritual cultivation** within the Shang-ch'ing framework:
- The *Tso-wang lun* outlines seven progressive stages of releasing ordinary concerns
- The *Fu-ch'i ching-i lun* provides systematic instruction in *ch'i* cultivation
- He incorporated Buddhist concepts (the *T'ien-yin-tzu* borrows "entering into Suchness"
  from Mahayana Prajnaparamita) while insisting on a distinctively Taoist framework: gradual
  path, bodily engagement, holistic transformation

He explicitly rejected "sudden awakening" (*wu*) in favor of staged cultivation, contrasting
Taoist practice with Ch'an/Zen. He also incorporated proto-"Tao-nature" ideas (from the
*Pen-chi ching*) but this direction was not sustained in later Taoism.

## Posthumous Legend

The mausoleum of Ssu-ma was reportedly opened after his death, yielding only a staff and a
pair of shoes — the customary Taoist sign of "mortuary liberation" (*shih-chieh*), suggesting
that he had ascended while appearing to die. This followed the same pattern as Huang Ling-wei
and Yeh Fa-shan.

From 1423, Taoists at Mao-shan compiled records claiming Ssu-ma as "the Grand Master in the
twelfth generation" of the Shang-ch'ing lineage — a retroactive fabrication, since the concept
of such a lineage did not exist in Ssu-ma's own day.
