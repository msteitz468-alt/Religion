## Biographical Overview
Zedekiah (originally named Mattaniah) was the last king of Judah. A son of Josiah, he was placed on the throne by the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar in 597 BCE as a puppet ruler following the deposition and exile of his nephew, Jehoiachin. Despite taking an oath of loyalty to Babylon, Zedekiah eventually rebelled, seeking an alliance with Egypt. This led to the final Babylonian siege of Jerusalem. Zedekiah attempted to flee as the city walls were breached but was captured near Jericho. He was brought before Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, where he was forced to watch his sons executed before his own eyes were gouged out. He was then bound in bronze chains and taken to Babylon, where he died.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **2 Kings 24-25 / 2 Chronicles 36**: Provide the historical summary of his reign, rebellion, capture, and tragic end, judging him negatively for doing "evil in the sight of the LORD."
- **Jeremiah (various, esp. 21, 34, 37-39)**: Zedekiah repeatedly consults Jeremiah but lacks the courage to obey the prophet's command to surrender. He allows his officials to imprison Jeremiah but secretly rescues him and moves him to the courtyard of the guard.

## Tradition-Specific Reception
- **Judaism**: Remembered as a weak and tragic figure who brought about the destruction of the First Temple due to his vacillation and failure to heed prophetic warnings. His blinding is seen as a fulfillment of prophecies regarding his exile.
- **Christianity**: Serves as a cautionary tale of the consequences of covenant unfaithfulness and the fear of man.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Zedekiah stands in stark contrast to the messianic ideal of the Davidic king. His very name, meaning "The LORD is my righteousness," is ironically contrasted by Jeremiah with the future "Righteous Branch" who will actually fulfill that name (Jer 23:5-6). He embodies the paralysis of Judah's leadership—wanting to hear the word of God but being too crippled by political fear to act upon it.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
Zedekiah's existence and the general events of his reign are firmly attested by both biblical texts and Babylonian chronicles. Interpretive focus often lies on assessing his character: whether he was inherently wicked or simply a weak, tragic ruler caught in an impossible geopolitical vise.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Commentators like John Goldingay and Hetty Lalleman highlight his ambivalence. Lalleman notes that Zedekiah is not as willfully "bad" as Jehoiakim, as he continues to seek God's word and shows some grace to Jeremiah, yet his ultimate failure is his fear of his own officials, which paralyzes him from making the right choice.
