## Biographical Overview
Jeremiah ben Hilkiah was born into a priestly family in Anathoth, in the territory of Benjamin. Called to prophesy as a young man in the 13th year of King Josiah (627 BCE), his ministry spanned the final decades of the Kingdom of Judah. He witnessed Josiah's reforms, the successive reigns of Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah, and the catastrophic fall of Jerusalem to the Babylonians in 587 BCE. He faced constant opposition, imprisonment, and threats to his life from kings, false prophets, and his own townsmen. After the fall of Jerusalem, he remained in Judah briefly before being taken to Egypt against his will by a fleeing remnant.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **The Book of Jeremiah**: The central figure; his call (Jer 1), his "Confessions" or laments (Jer 11-20), his conflict with King Jehoiakim including the burning of his scroll (Jer 36), his imprisonment by King Zedekiah and rescue by Ebed-melech (Jer 37-38), and his forced exile to Egypt (Jer 43-44).
- **2 Chronicles 35-36**: Mentioned as lamenting the death of King Josiah and prophesying the 70-year exile.
- **Daniel 9**: Daniel reads the scroll of Jeremiah to understand the 70 years of desolation.

## Tradition-Specific Reception
- **Judaism**: Remembered as the great prophet of the destruction of the First Temple. Traditionally considered the author of the Book of Jeremiah, Lamentations, and 1-2 Kings. The Talmud emphasizes his lineage from Rahab and Joshua and views him as a tragic figure who suffered deeply for his people.
- **Christianity**: Viewed as a "type" or foreshadowing of Christ, especially as the "Man of Sorrows" and a rejected prophet. His prophecy of the New Covenant (Jer 31) is foundational for Christian theology (quoted extensively in Hebrews 8).
- **Islam**: Although not mentioned by name in the Quran, Islamic tradition (Qisas Al-Anbiya) reveres him as the prophet Aramiya, who warned the Israelites of the coming destruction of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Jeremiah is the embodiment of the prophetic word; his life and suffering are integral to his message. His "Confessions" reveal the immense personal cost of bearing God's word to a resistant people. He represents the transition from the Sinai Covenant to the promised New Covenant, signifying that God's ultimate plan requires an internalized Torah and divine initiative in salvation.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
Most scholars accept the historical existence of Jeremiah and the basic outline of his life, but debate the extent to which the biographical narratives (often attributed to his scribe Baruch) have been shaped by later Deuteronomistic redactors. There is also debate over the "two versions" of the book of Jeremiah (MT and LXX) and how they reflect the development of the Jeremiah tradition.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Commentators like John Goldingay highlight Jeremiah as a "metaphor for God's word," whose suffering parallels God's own pathos. Hetty Lalleman emphasizes his role in developing the theology of "turning" (*šûb*) and his lonely, faithful witness amid national apostasy.
