## Biographical Overview
Baruch ben Neriah was the dedicated scribe, disciple, and companion of the prophet Jeremiah. Coming from a prominent scribal family (his brother Seraiah was a high royal official), Baruch faithfully recorded Jeremiah's prophecies. He famously wrote down the scroll dictated by Jeremiah in 605/604 BCE, read it to the public and officials, and, after King Jehoiakim burned it, wrote a second, expanded scroll (Jer 36). He shared in Jeremiah's sufferings and was forcibly taken to Egypt along with Jeremiah after the fall of Jerusalem (Jer 43).

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Jeremiah 36**: Baruch writes, reads, and rewrites the scroll of Jeremiah's prophecies.
- **Jeremiah 43**: Accused by Judean leaders of inciting Jeremiah against them; forced to accompany the refugees to Egypt.
- **Jeremiah 45**: Receives a personal prophetic word from God addressing his despair, promising that his life would be spared as "loot" wherever he goes.

## Tradition-Specific Reception
- **Judaism**: Rabbinic tradition views Baruch as a prophet in his own right and a disciple of Jeremiah. Some traditions suggest he later traveled from Egypt to Babylon, where he became the teacher of Ezra.
- **Christianity**: Varies by denomination. The deuterocanonical/apocryphal Book of Baruch is included in the Catholic and Orthodox canons, leading to his veneration and liturgical use (especially Baruch 3:37 as a prophecy of the Incarnation).

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Baruch represents the vital role of the scribe in the preservation and transmission of the prophetic word. He embodies the faithful remnant who suffer alongside the prophet. The inclusion of his personal lament and God's response (Jer 45) highlights that God intimately sees and addresses the despair of his faithful servants, even amid global catastrophe.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
Baruch's historicity is widely accepted, bolstered by the discovery of two clay bullae (seal impressions) bearing the inscription "Belonging to Berechiah, son of Neriah, the scribe." A major interpretive controversy surrounds his association with the pseudepigraphal literature bearing his name (1, 2, 3, and 4 Baruch), which were written centuries later to address the destruction of the Second Temple by attributing apocalyptic visions to him.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
Scholars like Hetty Lalleman point to Baruch's essential role in the formation of the Book of Jeremiah, suggesting he may have been responsible for gathering the biographical narratives. John Goldingay notes that Baruch's story parallels Jeremiah's, and the promise he receives underscores the grace of survival in a time of absolute judgment.
