## Biographical Overview
Ebed-melech was an Ethiopian (Cushite) eunuch or official serving in the royal palace of King Zedekiah during the final Babylonian siege of Jerusalem. When Judean officials threw the prophet Jeremiah into a muddy cistern to die of starvation, Ebed-melech bravely approached the king at the Benjamin Gate to advocate for Jeremiah's life. Granted permission, he took thirty men and used old rags to pad the ropes, safely pulling Jeremiah out of the cistern.

## Primary Source Appearances
- **Jeremiah 38**: The narrative of Ebed-melech interceding with King Zedekiah and orchestrating the rescue of Jeremiah from the muddy cistern.
- **Jeremiah 39:15-18**: God gives Jeremiah a special message for Ebed-melech, promising that because he trusted in the LORD, he would survive the imminent fall of Jerusalem and his life would be given to him as a prize.

## Tradition-Specific Reception
- **Judaism**: Recognized as a righteous Gentile. Rabbinic tradition (such as in the midrashic work Pirkei De-Rabbi Eliezer) sometimes lists him among the select few who entered Paradise alive, due to his righteous act of saving God's prophet.
- **Christianity**: Viewed typologically as a model of the inclusion of the Gentiles and an archetype of faith and courage. His story anticipates the conversion of the Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8.

## Theological and Narrative Significance
Ebed-melech functions as a striking foil to the leadership of Judah. While the Israelite king vacillates and the Judean officials seek to murder the prophet, this foreign official acts with decisive moral courage, compassion, and trust in Israel's God. He is an embodiment of the theme that faithful outsiders often recognize God's truth more readily than the insiders of the covenant community.

## Historicity and Interpretive Controversies
There is little dispute regarding the historicity of a Cushite official in the Judean court, as foreign mercenaries and palace servants were common in the ANE. "Ebed-melech" translates literally as "servant of the king," leading some scholars to debate whether it is a proper name or a title, though the narrative treats it as a personal identifier.

## Influence on Commentary Traditions
John Goldingay highlights Ebed-melech as "the kind of unambiguous hero who appears from time to time to play a bit part in the scroll's drama," contrasting his ethnic outsider status and moral clarity with the barbarism of the native Judean officials. Hetty Lalleman notes that his trust in God and practical kindness mark him as one of Jeremiah's very few allies in a society completely hostile to the prophetic word.
